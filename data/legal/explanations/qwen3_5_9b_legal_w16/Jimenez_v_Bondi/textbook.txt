Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# I. Factual and Procedural Background

## The Petitioners: Identity, Immigration Status, and Political Activities

To understand the claims for asylum, it is essential to first identify the individuals at the center of the dispute and their connection to the troubled country in question. Petitioners are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. Based on the record, the family are natives and citizens of Colombia living in Bogotá. To support their claims for protection, the Petitioners must demonstrate that they fall within the definition of a refugee, a status established under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). This definition requires that an applicant fear persecution in their country of nationality due to specific grounds, including race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

The Petitioners’ claim hinges heavily on their political participation in Colombia. Since 2012, they have been members of the Colombian Liberal Party. During the 2022 Colombian presidential election, the family actively supported Federico Gutierrez, a candidate for president. This involvement was not passive; the Petitioners participated in door-to-door campaigning in neighborhood by neighborhood to seek favorable votes. This constant engagement in public political activity served as the backdrop for the subsequent threats and harassment they faced. Furthermore, Jimenez holds a provisional law license and worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor’s office, assisting victims of domestic abuse and other crimes. This professional status is a critical factual detail because it explains Jimenez’s interaction with law enforcement and narco-trafficking elements; in the eyes of certain criminal elements within Colombia, his role as a provisional legal representative and his connection to the prosecutor’s office made him a target. Thus, the factual record establishes that the Petitioners were active public figures engaged in political and legal work before they relocated to the United States.

## Timeline of Harassment: From Campaigning to the Narco Dispute

The court’s opinion details a specific sequence of events in Colombia beginning during the 2022 election season that culminated in the family’s decision to flee. The timeline begins around the end of May 2022, when Jimenez started receiving threatening phone calls. These threats were often associated with his involvement in the Liberal Party. By the early June 2022, the situation escalated when a colleague of the family was kidnapped and murdered despite having reported his own threats. Despite this violence, the Petitioners continued their political campaigning.

A pivotal event occurred in late June 2022 regarding a private property dispute. Jimenez, leveraging his background as a lawyer, attempted to mediate a dispute between a client and a tenant who was withholding rent for over a year. During an investigation into the tenant’s identity, Jimenez discovered the tenant was an ex-husband of the client and, crucially, that the tenant was also known as "Don Rafa," a leader of a group affiliated with narco-trafficking and the current president of Colombia. Upon visiting the property, Jimenez found it to be a base for illicit activities, including prostitution and offering day-rental services to migrants. Although law enforcement had attempted to search the property, the Petitioners found no resolution, and a local police officer advised Jimenez to avoid the trouble he had created there.

Immediately following this encounter, the household faced a new threat. Jimenez received a "death to snitches" brochure targeting Liberal Party members, and shortly thereafter, he and his family were held at gunpoint in their furniture factory. The assailants explicitly linked their threats to Jimenez’s work with the prosecutor's office and his investigation into Don Rafa. Jimenez was treated as a “snitch” rather than a political activist. Days later, the harassing men targeted family members, with a man attempting to silence Jimenez’s younger son at a school. After reporting the criminal activity to a police officer, who responded that the police were complicit in the problem, the Petitioners decided to leave their home country within two months of the initial threats. This chronological progression is vital for establishing the causal link between the Petitioners' actions and the threats they received, which the lower courts ultimately found to lack the necessary connection to political opinion for asylum purposes.

## Travel and Entry: Unlawful Presence and Notice to Appear

The factual background does not end with the Petitioners' departure from Colombia; it must also account for their arrival in the United States to establish the procedural context of the case. The Petitioners arrived in the United States in December 2022, arriving without being admitted to the country or paroled by an immigration officer. Consequently, they are considered to be living in the United States unlawfully. This method of entry is procedurally significant because it triggers the enforcement mechanisms of the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). Approximately three months after their arrival, DHS served the Petitioners with individual Notices to Appear, which formally charged each of them as removable from the United States pursuant to the Immigration and Nationality Act.

The legal proceeding that followed this entry was bifurcated into two tracks: the immigration court proceedings and the asylum application process. In August 2023, the Petitioners appeared before an Immigration Judge (IJ). During this hearing, the Petitioners admitted the truth of the DHS's allegations, confirming their removability. Simultaneously, they applied for asylum and withholding of removal, with their minor children proceeding as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez's application. The circumstances of their entry—specifically that they were not admitted—made the Petitioners removable, but the application for asylum provided them the opportunity to defend themselves against those charges. The IJ ultimately directed removal to Colombia, finding that the Petitioners did not successfully overcome the presumptions of removability by showing a well-founded fear of persecution or grounds to block deportation.

## Lower Court Proceedings: Immigration Judge and Board of Immigration Appeals

Once the Immigration Judge rendered a final decision, the Petitioners appealed that denial to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), the administrative body within the Department of Justice that reviews decisions from Immigration Judges. The IJ denied the Petitioners' claims but credited their testimony as credible, finding instead that they failed to prove specific legal elements required for asylum. The IJ concluded that the Petitioners did not demonstrate that they suffered perse-cution in the past. The Judge also found no link between any harm suffered and any of the statutorily protected grounds, such as political opinion. Furthermore, the IJ determined that the Petitioners could legally relocate within Colombia to safety and that the Colombian government was capable or willing to protect them. Petitioners also raised claims regarding protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT) and argued that they were denied due process because the IJ violated their right to counsel, though the primary appeal focused on asylum and withholding of removal.

The IJ's decision was not final indefinitely; the Petitioners appealed to the BIA, which is under this circuit convention to review removal orders to ensure they comply with the record. The BIA affirmed the Immigration Judge's decision and dismissed the Petitioners' appeal. In doing so, the BIA affirmed the IJ's rejection of the Petitioners' due process claims and the denial of their detention relief. This procedural posture—where the BIA affirms the IJ and becomes the final administrative agency—is the one the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reviews. Because the BIA is the final administrative decision-maker on the record, the appellate court's review is focused on the administrative record that supports the BIA's and the IJ's findings. This establishes the procedural threshold for the appeal, grounding the court's analysis in whether the administrative record provided substantial evidence to support the lower court's determinations on past persecution, nexus, and government protection.

# II. Standards of Review and Legal Framework

Effective appellate analysis in immigration law requires a precise understanding of how the Federal Circuit Court reviews Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) decisions. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit outlines the procedural boundaries that govern the appeal process. These boundaries determine what information the court considers, how heavily it weights the administrative agency's conclusions, and the level of proof the petitioner must afford to succeed. This chapter breaks down the jurisdictional basis, the burden of proof placed on the asylum applicant, the restricted scope of evidentiary review, and the statutory definition of asylum and its protected grounds as applied by this opinion.

## Jurisdiction Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252: Consolidation of Review

The threshold requirement for any case before the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit is proper subject matter jurisdiction. In this matter, the court expressly exercises jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. This statute provides the statutory basis for judicial review of final orders of removal. While removal proceedings are final administrative actions, the federal courts retain authority to grant consolidated review of asylum claims alongside the removal order. The court cites *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848 (10th Cir. 2025), to establish that the jurisdiction extends specifically to appeals involving the BIA’s denial of asylum relief.

This jurisdictional grant is critical because it allows the appellate court to look at the asylum claim in conjunction with the administrative order rather than in isolation. When a petition for review is granted, the court is not limited to procedural defects but may engage with the merits of the underlying application. However, the court notes that it is bound by the statutory limit of reviewing "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." This means the jurisdiction under § 1252 does not permit the court to accept new evidence or evidence outside the record compiled during the BIA proceedings. Consequently, the scope of the court's authority is strictly defined: it has the power to review but is restricted to the materials that the BIA actually considered when affirming the immigration judge’s decision.

## The Burden of Proof: Preponderance of the Evidence

Once jurisdiction is established, the substantive burden of the applicant shifts to the petitioner. In *Jimenez*, the court clarifies that asylum is a "discretionary form of relief" but requires the applicant to meet a specific evidentiary threshold. Under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), the burden rests on the noncitizen applicant to prove by a "preponderance of the evidence" that they qualify as a refugee. The court cites *Singh* and 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1) to articulate that this burden is strict in the sense that the applicant must affirmatively demonstrate the facts, rather than relying on speculation.

The standard "preponderance of the evidence" signifies that the facts sought to be established must be more likely true than not true. This standard applies to all claims for asylum. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners attempted to prove that they faced persecution, that the persecution had a nexus to a protected ground, and that the government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The court of appeals analyzes the petitioner’s performance against this standard. Specifically, to qualify for relief based on past persecution, the applicant must present proof sufficient to tip the scale of probability. The opinion notes that while the Immigration Judge found the petitioners credible, credibility alone is not sufficient; the applicant must still satisfy the evidentiary burden regarding the specific elements of asylum. Thus, a successful appeal requires demonstrating that the petitioner has met this burden, or that the administrative record does not support the finding that they have failed to do so.

## Standard of Review: Deference to the Administrative Record

Because the BIA is the final administrative authority before the court, the appellate court does not conduct a de novo review of facts. Instead, the *Jimenez* panel applies the "substantial-evidence standard." Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." Citing *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012), the opinion establishes that this is a highly deferential standard. This means the Tenth Circuit will not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment for that of the BIA if there is reasonable support in the record.

Furthermore, the court emphasizes that its review is limited to the administrative record. The panel notes that because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, the court is not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of the grounds, but the review remains tethered to the evidence developed in the lower proceedings. If reasonable adjudicators could find that the evidence is substantial enough, the appellate court must uphold the BIA’s decision. If, however, the evidence is scant or contradictory, the BIA’s findings may be vacated. In *Jimenez*, the court finds that the record contains "substantial evidence" supporting the BIA’s conclusion that the petitioners did not prove persecution, reinforcing the legal principle that a petitioner cannot succeed on appeal simply by arguing against the character of the evidence in the record.

## Definition of Asylum and Protected Grounds Under the INA

To satisfy the standards of review and the burden of proof discussed above, the petitioner must define their claim within the statutory framework of the INA. The *Jimenez* court references *Niang v. Gonzales*, 422 F.3d 1187 (10th Cir. 2005), to outline the legal definition of a refugee. To be eligible for asylum status, an applicant must be outside their country of nationality and be unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution. This persecution must be based on one of five statutorily protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In applying this framework, the *Jimenez* panel sets out the specific elements required to show past persecution. The court describes "persecution" as the infliction of suffering or harm offensive to the core of the refugee concept, requiring more than mere threats or restrictions on liberty. Additionally, the harm must be on account of a protected ground and committed by the government or forces the government is unable or unwilling to control. The court notes that past persecution also creates a rebuttable presumption regarding the fear of future persecution, but the initial showing must meet these definition requirements. For mixed-motive cases, where victims claim persecution for multiple reasons, the political opinion cannot be incidental but must be at least one "central reason" for the harm. This statutory requirement provides the structural baseline against which the courts measure all subsequent factual determinations in asylum appeals.

# III. Analyzing the Threshold Elements of Asylum

## Defining 'Persecution': Suffering Beyond Restrictions and Threats

The first hurdle in any asylum claim is establishing "past persecution." To understand *Jimenez v. Bondi*, a student must first grasp how the law defines this term. Persecution is not a technical term in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA); rather, it is interpreted through judicial precedent. In the Tenth Circuit, as established in *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ from the persecutor in a way regarded as offensive. Crucially, this standard dictates that the harm must "entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty."

When reviewing the Petitioner's history, the court applied two key limitations on what constitutes persecution. First, threats alone are insufficient. In *Karki v. Holder*, the Tenth Circuit explicitly held that threats alone cannot constitute persecution, even when regarding a well-founded fear of future harm. Second, psychological or emotional distress alone is rarely enough. In *Jimenez*, while the petitioners argued that the cumulative threats caused severe anxiety and panic attacks for their children, the court noted that threats and emotional distress are often "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling" without rising to the level of persecution. The court relied on prior precedent, such as *Brandy v. Holder*, which distinguished between mere "restrictions on life and liberty" and the "offensive harm" required for asylum. Therefore, even if an applicant was undeterred from threats or suffered emotional trauma, the threshold is not met if the harm was limited to the petitioners' subjective fear rather than objective, offensive suffering inflicted by the persecutor.

## The Nexus Requirement: Political Opinion as a Central Reason

Once the threshold of harm is cleared, an asylum applicant must satisfy the "nexus" requirement. This means the persecution must occur "on account of" a statutorily protected ground, such as political opinion. In *Jimenez*, the appellate court clarified that the burden is not merely showing the persecutor acted for *a* reason, but that the protected ground was "at least one central reason" for the harm.

The standard for proving a nexus claim is rooted in the "central reason" test. As the Tenth Circuit explained in *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, an applicant's political opinion "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." While a persecutor may have mixed motives—targeting someone for economic reasons while also harboring political hatreds—the protected ground must play a substantial role. If the protected ground plays only a "minor role," the claim for asylum based on political opinion fails. The court emphasized that when reviewing complex cases, the question is never whether the persecutor had *any* political objection, but rather whether that political objection was a primary driver of the attack. This distinction is vital for students: proving a persecutor disliked a claimant's political views is not the same as proving the persecutor targeted the claimant *because* of those views.

## Fact Application: The Role of the Narco-Dispute ('Don Rafa')

To apply the legal standard for persecution and nexus, the court in *Jimenez* dissected the specific timeline of the threatening incidents against the Petitioners. This section is critical for students learning to distinguish between timeline and causation. The critical factual pivot in this case was the escalation of threats coinciding with a professional dispute, rather than the Petitioners' political campaigning.

Prior to the escalation, Petitioners were active members of the Liberal Party and campaigned for a presidential rival. They received some threats during this period, but the court noted there was no evidence to suggest "Don Rafa" (a narco-trafficking leader) was behind those initial politically affiliated threats. The narrative shifted only after the Petitioner Jimenez intervened in a property dispute. As a provisional lawyer, Jimenez visited a property to negotiate rent, only to discover the tenant was ex-husband of a client and a known gang leader. Jimenez then learned the property was a base for crimes and reported this to law enforcement. It was after this specific incident involving the illicit property that the men with guns threatened Jimenez, accusing him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor and noting the threats came from an order from "Don Rafa."

The court treated this timeline as decisive evidence. Because the significant physical threats and the three-day standoff at the furniture factory occurred specifically after the property investigation, the court focused on the expansion of direct conflict with a source of narco-trafficking, rather than the general political activity. This distinction allowed the court to isolate whether the harm was political in nature or personal/professional in nature.

## Classifying Motive: Tangential Political Opinion vs. Personal Animosity

The final analysis in this section required the court to weigh the Petitioners' arguments against the substantive record. Petitioners argued that Don Rafa targeted them for their political alliances. However, the appellate court concluded the government's "unable-or-unwilling" protection status (and consequently the nexus for asylum) depended on whether the *source* of the animosity was political or personal.

The record showed that the escalation of persecution correlated with Jimenez's concrete conflict with Don Rafa regarding the rent-paying and the closure of the property, coupled with the "revenue and security" impact of a federal investigation. While Petitioners claimed their membership in the Liberal Party drove the persecution, the IJ and BIA found that the persecutors were acting out of professional animosity. The court found substantial evidence that the political opinion was merely "tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm."

This distinction illustrates the "mixed-motive" bar in asylum cases. Even if a persecutor holds a political view against an applicant, the asylum claim fails if the *action* taken against them is motivated primarily by something else, such as professional rivalry or self-defense. Here, the "snitch" accusation and the warning to leave the property were framed as personal retribution for interference with criminal operations, indicating that the underlying animosity was personal. Consequently, the court declined to treat the political affiliation as a "central reason," leading to the dismissal of the Petitioners' asylum threshold claim.

# IV. Government Protection and Internal Relocation

## The 'Unable or Unwilling' Standard: Government Responsibility

In evaluating whether a noncitizen qualifies for asylum or withholding of removal based on well-founded fear of future persecution, courts must determine if the sovereign government is acting effectively to mitigate that risk. Under the precedent set forth by the Tenth Circuit in *Singh v. Bondi*, the analysis of government protection considers how the responding state reacted to an applicant's past persecution. The court identifies two types of information that weigh heavily during this inquiry: first, whether the government stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors, and second, whether the government offered specific protection to the applicant, such as by placing them in protective custody. This inquiry is not abstract; it requires evidence regarding the government's practical response to dangerous conditions faced by the noncitizen. The standard is grounded in the legal proposition that a risk of future persecution cannot be established if the state demonstrates it is either willing or able to control the destabilizing forces. In the context of *Jimenez*, the court utilized this framework to assess whether Colombia lacked the capacity or willingness to intervene on behalf of the Petitioners, despite allegations that their persecutors were aligned with the government.

## Evidentiary Requirements: Reporting Past Persecution to Authorities

A critical procedural component of the 'unable or unwilling' analysis is the applicant's interaction with state authorities. The Tenth Circuit has established that a key fact in this analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government. Without such notice, the court lacks the empirical basis to determine how police would have responded or how the government would have attempted to help. Consequently, a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors, as there is no record of a missed opportunity for intervention. However, the opinion clarifies that this requirement is not absolute. An applicant may still satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous. In *Jimenez*, the Third Circuit citation (*Aviles-Gonzalez*) supports the principle that without reporting, the court cannot assess government reaction, creating a strict evidentiary hurdle for applicants who claim their persecutors are effectively part of the state, as they must provide evidence that reporting itself was impossible or unsafe.

## Internal Relocation: Evaluating Safe Areas and Family Ties

Even if a noncitizen demonstrates a fear of persecution in their current location, their claim may be rebutted by the government showing that the individual could avoid future mistreatment by relocating to another part of their home country. This requirement is governed by the standard that the noncitizen could avoid persecution by moving to a safer area within the country. The record in *Jimenez* was viewed under a preponderance of the evidence standard to determine if safe relocation was feasible. The court noted that the presence of family in a country does not always establish safety for a petitioner seeking asylum or withholding of removal; hypothetical hardships regarding family are insufficient to block a claim. Furthermore, administrative or economic burdens associated with moving do not preclude a finding of safe area availability. The court specifically looked at whether the Petitioners had lived safely elsewhere before departing for the United States and whether perceived threats were tied to a specific neighborhood rather than the entire nation.

## Application to Case Facts: Police Inaction vs. Systemic Unwillingness

Applying these standards to the record, the court found substantial evidence that the Petitioners could not establish that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. While Petitioners argued that individual police officers advised them to leave and that Don Rafa was aligned with the government, the court distinguished between isolated apathy and systemic inability. The opinion notes that while individual police conversations can indicate unwillingness, a one-off interaction does not necessarily prove the government is unable to protect a victim. To establish a systemic failure, the record should show a broader inability to function, which was not supported by the facts. In *Jimenez*, evidence showed that the government was aware of the specific property at issue and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the criminal conduct there, contradicting the claim that the state could not intervene. Additionally, the Petitioners failed to report wrongdoing or corruption despite Jimenez and his father having worked for the government in Colombia. Finally, regarding internal relocation, the court observed that the threats directed at the Petitioners were to stay out of the neighborhood, not to leave the country. The fact that family members and the Petitioner's father continued to live and work unharmed in Colombia supported the conclusion that the Petitioners could mitigate any potential for future persecution by moving to another location within Colombia.

# V. Conclusion, Holding, and Significance

## Summary of the Holding: Denial of Petition for Review

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit issued a decision that ultimately denies the petition for review filed by Colombian asylum applicants. The court exercises jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 to review the final orders of the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and the Immigration Judge (IJ). The central holding of the opinion is that the BIA did not err in denying the petitioners' applications for asylum and withholding of removal. The court determined that the petitioners failed to meet the necessary statutory criteria under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Specifically, the Tenth Circuit found that there was substantial evidence in the record supporting the Board's findings that the petitioners had not suffered past persecution, that their harm lacked the required nexus to a statutorily protected ground, and that the Colombian government remained willing and able to protect them. Consequently, the court affirmed the IJ's and BIA's consilidated decision, ruling that the applicants are not eligible for the discretionary relief of asylum or the mandatory protection of withholding. Because the petitioners failed to establish a well-founded fear of persecution required for asylum, the court also reasoned that they necessarily failed to meet the "higher standard for withholding of removal," which requires a "clear probability" of future persecution if a claim for asylum is denied. This conclusion reinforces the procedural requirement that a clear showing of past persecution is the most efficient way to satisfy the burden of proof for asylum claims, absent a specific showing of future fear that does not rely on that presumption.

## Key Distinctions: Past Persecution and Future Fear of Persecution

When analyzing asylum claims, it is critical for a law student to distinguish how "past persecution" interacts with the "well-founded fear of future persecution" requirement. In the INA, an applicant may obtain refugee status through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. However, this is not automatic; the petitioner must still prove the harm amounted to past persecution on account of a protected ground. In *Jimenez*, the court clarified that if an applicant cannot establish the first prong—past persecution—and cannot establish an independent well-founded fear of future persecution, the claim fails entirely. The court notes that asylum is a discretionary form of relief granted to eligible noncitizens, whereas withholding of removal is a mandatory form of relief that applies when an applicant meets a higher standard of proof. Because *Jimenez* failed to demonstrate they suffered past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution under asylum standards, they were consequently ineligible for withholding. This distinction highlights the strategic importance of the "past persecution" element; if an applicant can prove they were harmed in the past, the burden shifts to the government to show internal relocation is possible or that the persecutor has ties to officials within the government that make protection unlikely. Failing this initial burden often renders subsequent arguments regarding government inability to protect redundant.

## Implications for Future Asylum Claims and Nexus Arguments

The *Jimenez* decision places significant weight on the element of "nexus," which requires that the persecution be on account of a statutorily protected ground, such as political opinion, race, religion, or membership in a particular social group. For applicants claiming political opinion, the 10th Circuit requires that the political opinion be "at least one central reason" for the persecution. The opinion explicitly rejects the notion that a protected ground can be incidental or subordinate to another motive. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners argued they were targeted because they supported a candidate who opposed the president, but the court found the BIA correct in determining the persecutors were motivated primarily by the petitioner's professional conflict with a narcotrafficking leader and his involvement in a property dispute. The court emphasized that the petitioners' political activities occurred after the initial escalation of threats and were not the primary driver of the danger. This precedent serves as a warning for future litigants attempting to frame mixed-motive claims. To succeed, an applicant must provide evidence that the persecutor's targeting cannot be explained solely by criminal, economic, or personal grievances. If the political opinion is merely tangential or a minor role in the harm, as it was in *Jimenez*, the nexus requirement is not satisfied, and the claim for asylum collapses. This establishes a high bar for applicants claiming political persecution where the factual background suggests private criminal acts or professional retaliation unrelated to political dissent.

## Limits of Threat-Based Claims Under 10th Circuit Precedent

A critical legal takeaway from *Jimenez* is the limitation on claims based solely on threats of future harm without physical injuries. The court cites established Tenth Circuit precedent stating that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." While the court acknowledges that threats must be considered when assessing the cumulative impact of mistreatment, it remains clear that emotional or psychological harm resulting solely from threats does not rise to the level of persecution required for asylum. In this case, the petitioners conceded that they were not physically harmed and experienced no lasting physical injury. The court referenced cases such as *Karki v. Holder*, *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, and *Chhetri v. Rosen*, noting that even fact patterns involving more extreme psychological distress or multiple threats have previously been found insufficient to grant asylum. The opinion stresses that persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm that is "offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." This doctrinal limitation means that litigants relying on "threats" as the central evidence of persecution will likely face denial unless they can corroborate threats with actual physical acts of violence, inability to secure their freedom, or a degree of harm that goes beyond the immediate emotional impact of the intimidation. For a law student studying this case, the lesson is that the severity of the *actual* harm endured—not merely the fear of potential harm—determines the threshold for the first prong of an asylum claim. The dissenting factors of the 10th Circuit generally require concrete, objective evidence of harm beyond the subjective impact of a phone call or a threatening statement.
