Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Threats Without Stars: The Tenth Circuit's Strict Enforcement of the Persecution Standard

Supreme immigration law is often defined by the intersection of statutory text and the practical interpretation of what constitutes "persecution" under the Immigration and Nationality Act. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit reaffirmed a rigorous doctrinal hurdle that has long troubled petitioners confronting administrative removal orders: fear of future persecution, even when supported by a credible narrative of threats and danger, cannot substitute for a statutory threshold of "suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive." While the court acknowledged that physical injury is not strictly necessary, *Jimenez* solidifies a precedent wherein psychological distress and death threats must yield to the tangible reality of past prosecution to qualify under the administrative record.

The facts in *Jimenez* present a harrowing narrative that closely parallels the profiles rejected in prior Tenth Circuit jurisprudence. Joseph Leojaz and his family, involved in Colombian politics and legal work, faced escalating violence linked to narco-trafficking groups and individual officers. They encountered men at gunpoint, witnessed their colleague murdered, and experienced direct physical threats against their family. Despite presenting evidence of this cumulative harassment, the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) affirmed the Immigration Judge's denial. The court's analysis serves as a reminder to immigration attorneys and scholars that the "reasoning of the IJ is not an inquiry into whether a noncitizen *survived* violence, but whether that violence reached the statutory definition of persecution." By grounding this decision in the administrative record and eschewing any broad humanitarian extrapolation, the Tenth Circuit demonstrates that asylum claims are bound by the strict record-inference rules that govern the entire system.

Central to the court's reasoning is its application of precedent regarding the definition of harm. The opinion explicitly cites *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, noting that a finding of persecution requires harm that is more than just "restrictions or threats to life and liberty." Although the court clarifies that physical harm is not constitutionally mandated to satisfy the prong, it emphasizes that the record must demonstrate suffering that is "offensive." In this instance, the Petitioners conceded they suffered no lasting physical harm and relied heavily on emotional and psychological impact, including sleep issues and panic attacks. The Tenth Circuit noted this failure to cite body damage as problematic, recounting its jurisprudence in *Karki v. Holder*, which found threats alone insufficient. While the court acknowledged that multiple incidents could be considered cumulatively, it concluded that the combination of phone calls, threats of kidnapping, and a single physical assault on a family member did not rise to the level required by the INA. This aligns with a long-standing tendency in the circuit to demand concrete evidence of tangible loss rather than reliance on the cumulative weight of danger.

The court’s dismissal of the nexus argument further underscores the precision required at the intersection of motive and statutory grounds. *Jimenez* claimed their harm was based on their political opinion, specifically their support for the Liberal Party during a 2022 election. The Tenth Circuit, however, found that the persecutor, Don Rafa, was motivated principally by economic interference and the Petitioners' professional activity as lawyers for the National Prosecutor's Office, rather than a political belief per se. The court emphasized the "political opinion is not a catch-all" for mixed motives, requiring the petitioner to prove the opinion was a "central reason" for the harm. Because the threats emerged only after the Petitioners intervened in a property dispute involving a narco-trafficking group leader, the nexus to the Liberal Party status was deemed incidental. This reasoning creates a potential complexity for future petitioners entering the asylum system where government actors are intertwined with criminal enterprises, forcing them to untangle the specific motivations behind violence rather than grouping it under broad political persecution.

Finally, the opinion addresses the requirement that the government in the Petitioners' home country be unwilling or unable to provide protection, which is crucial for asylum but distinct from withholding of removal. The Petitioners argued that the narco-trader Don Rafa was linked to the President's administration, rendering the Colombian state useless. The court, however, required evidence of reporting to the authorities or a showing of systemic failure based on the record. Because the Petitioners had worked within the justice system in Colombia but did not report the violence to investigators, and because police records showed the state had attempted seizures at the property in question, the failure to utilize national mechanisms undermined their claim. Tenth Circuit jurisprudence suggests that without evidence of a refusal to help or a demonstrated futility of the reporting system, the claim that the state is population-uncontrollable is unlikely to survive scrutiny. The court noted that relocation within Colombia to relatives' homes was a viable option that did not carry the same risks of prosecution or retaliation, further dismissing the argument that the country could not protect them.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a cautionary case study for legal professionals focused on asylum applications in the Western Hemisphere. It reinforces the textualist approach to the term "persecution" that has been gaining traction across federal circuits in recent years. The decision highlights a gap between the humanitarian logic of asylum and the evidentiary rigors of the administrative process. While 8 U.S.C. § 1158 is designed to protect the vulnerable, the Tenth Circuit's ruling suggests that fear alone, regardless of its depth or reasonableness, will not secure relief without a demonstrable history of tangible harm. This precedent incentivizes applicants to gather evidence of physical safety risks over life instability, potentially narrowing the scope of asylum to those who have crossed into the realm of physical violence rather than those merely threatened by it.



# The Nexus Paradox: Distinguishing Political Opinion from Professional Friction in Criminal Disputes

In the crowded landscape of immigration jurisprudence, few requirements are as scrutinized—or as difficult to prove—as the nexus between fear of persecution and a statutorily protected ground. Asylum and withholding of removal claims fundamentally turn on whether the government is unwilling or unable to protect an applicant from harm that stems from their specific identity or political expression. The Tenth Circuit's opinion in *Jimenez v. Bondi* (Oct. 7, 2025) provides a stark illustration of the consequences when that nexus is severed by ordinary criminal disincentives. For asylum litigants who operate within conflict zones or engaged in local activism, the case reinforces a critical √ shade of gray: political affiliation alone does not shield a litigant from retaliation when professional feuds and criminal enterprises are at play.

## The Thin Line Between Activism and Crime

At the heart of this contentious decision is the court's application of the "central reason" test for political opinion claims. Petitioners in *Jimenez*, Liberal Party members and campaigners, argued they were targeted by narco-trafficking groups aligned with the current Colombian president because of their political views. The Tenth Circuit, agreeing that the petitioner must show the political opinion motivated the harm, rather than inheriting it, found no such causality here. The court viewed the political component as merely "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate" to a more immediate motive: financial disruption and criminal investigation.

This application of the "central reason" test highlights a significant doctrinal hurdle for litigants whose political activities inevitably place them in the crosshairs of organized crime. Jimenez’s conflict did not start with_office hate mail; it began with a routine property dispute where he acted as a voice for a tenant against a narco-linked businessman known as "Don Rafa." As the opinion notes, threats escalated specifically after Jimenez investigated the property, which the men accused him of threatening for its involvement in narco-trafficking activities. While supporting the ruling that the persecutors' citation of the Liberal Party was a post-hoc justification or secondary motive, the nuance is crucial in distinguishing systemic persecution from targeted retaliation out of personal vendetta.

Analytically, this ruling suggests that asylum seekers must isolate their fear from the underlying cause with surgical precision. If the harm results from a specific act of investigation or confrontation that is viewed as an attack on economic or criminal interests, the political activity preceding the act may be deemed "unprotected" by the statute. This creates a perilous burden: litigants must prove that their political identity was the predicate, not the incidental feature, of the harm. In this case, the fact that Jimenez was a law student and intern for the National Prosecutor’s office complicated his political defense, eventually framing him as a rival rather than a refugee.

## The Height of Persecution and the Reporting Requirement

While the nexus issue drives the denial of asylum, the court's rejection of the claim was bolstered by its interpretation of the definition of "persecution" and the government's willingness to protect. Under Tenth Circuit precedent, threats alone rarely suffice for a past persecution finding unless they are combined with other substantial harm. The Petitioners' evidence consisted of threats, emotional distress, and the death of a colleague, yet the court found no "lasting physical harm" to the Petitioners themselves, concluding that the cumulative impact of threats did not rise to the statutory bar.

This evidentiary bar intersects with the "unable or unwilling" analysis of government protection. The Tenth Circuit places weight on whether an applicant formally reported persecution to authorities. Jimenez, who had connections to the local police, told a patrol officer that he was safe in Colombia anyway—you did not seek problems. The court noted that while "a failure to report can undercut a claim," it relies heavily on evidence that reporting would have been futile. Because the Petitioners did not report the threats or the corruption they witnessed, the court found it impossible to assess whether the government was truly unwilling to intervene.

This creates a downstream effect where litigants are forced to prove they *should* have pursued an avenue for help before fleeing, despite the danger inherent in engaging the very state apparatus accused of protection failure. The court here was unwilling to accept the petitioner's argument that contacting the government was dangerous or signaling futile without concrete evidence linking the state to the specific persecutor in a way that would render the attempt dangerous.

## Conclusion: Complications in Defining Asylum Eligibility

The Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez v. Bondi* ultimately denies the petition, maintaining that the Petitioners could safely relocate within Colombia. The court reasoned that the threats were local and area-specific, and the family members living in the countryside demonstrated that interior relocation was feasible. This part of the decision reflects a willingness to treat "safe internal relocation" as a viable defense if the threat appears geographically constrained, even in a country with pervasive governance issues.

However, the doctrinal takeaways for the field are sharper. *Jimenez* serves as a warning for advocates of activists embroiled in local criminal politics: political affiliation is shield only when it is the bedrock of the harm, not merely the backdrop. The court's reasoning forces litigants to chart the precise path from the perpetuated harm that took place. But for many displaced individuals, the line between political expression and professional friction is blurred by necessity.

In the future, this ruling might limit the success of cases where persecutors leverage a victim's professional standing to impose harm on grounds of ideology. It reinforces that an asylum system should not grant refuge based on reputational risk alone, but requires proof that the state's failure to protect is both tied to the harm and driven by an animus toward the victim's protected status. *Jimenez* stands as a sober reminder that in the asylum process, the identity of the enemy and the motive of the crime are not always the same.

With these Elements in mind, the Court's decision will shape many future asylum claims involving political activists operating within conflict zones where criminal and political self-interest overlap.



# Reporting the Unreportable: The *Jimenez* Precedent on Exhaustion and the 'Unable or Unwilling' Standard

The intersection of private violence and state apathy remains one of the most volatile flashpoints in asylum law. When a claimant alleges they have fled a country precisely because that nation’s government is unable or unwilling to protect its citizens, the legal inquiry becomes equally complex: does the claimant have a duty to seek local protection before claiming the state is failing them? In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit issued a ruling that sharpens this knife, reinforcing a rigorous evidentiary burden for applicants who claim systemic government failure. While the court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals on multiple metrics—specifically regarding the threshold of past persecution and the nexus requirement—the decision offers a critical precedent regarding the functional requirement of reporting to state authorities.

The underlying facts of this case involve Colombian applicants alleging threats tied to political activism mixed with a specific property dispute against a narco-trafficking leader. The appellate panel, in denying the petition for review, found multiple failures in Petitioner’s claim. Crucially for future practitioners, the court scrutinized the “unable or unwilling” inquiry not merely as a passive determination of local security conditions, but as an affirmative check on the applicant’s domestic efforts to survive. The Tenth Circuit’s reasoning suggests that without a contemporaneous effort to engage with law enforcement, or at least proof that such engagement was futile, an applicant cannot satisfy the statutory requirement that the government is unwilling or unable to protect them.

This posture of the decision reflects a longstanding tension in asylum jurisprudence: the need to verify that the state has actually had the chance to act before a claimant can conclude it will not. In Section II.E of their opinion, the judges articulated that the answer to whether the government is unwilling depends heavily on evidence of its response to past persecution. The Court highlighted that "a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government." While the court acknowledged that a failure to report is "not necessarily fatal," it indicated that in many instances, the failure to generate a record of interaction with authorities effectively cuts off the claim. In *Jimenez*, the applicants had interacted with local police, but the court found that the officers’ advice to "leave the neighborhood" was insufficient to prove a systemic unwillingness, particularly given that law enforcement had previously attempted searches at the disputed property.

From a doctrinal standpoint, this ruling emphasizes that "unable or unwilling" is not a self-evident fact derived from the reputation of a country. Rather, it is a case-by-case factual query where the applicant must demonstrate that the specific state apparatus failed them where it allegedly should have acted. By tethering the "unwillingness" of the government to the specific report of the petitioner, *Jimenez* imposes an element of exhaustion on the asylum process. This means that in cases involving corruption or unreliable police, claimants cannot simply assume a vacuum of protection exists; they must navigate that vacuum or prove it is impassable through the same channels they claim failed them.

The downstream implications for litigants and immigration practitioners are significant. In jurisdictions characterized by corruption—a common element in Colombian cases as well as many others in Latin America—litigants will likely face heightened skepticism when they fail to provide documentation of police reports. The Tenth Circuit signaled that even individual conversations with unhelpful officers do not automatically equate to government unwillingness absent broader context. Furthermore, the decision limits arguments based on hypotheticals. The court rejected the proposition that family members living safely in the same country prove the entire system is capable of protecting the asylum seeker, noting that the safety of relatives does not establish safety for the applicant. This reinforces the preponderance of the evidence standard by requiring granular detail rather than general assertions regarding national security conditions.

Ultimately, *Jimenez* serves as a reminder that the "unable or unwilling" standard is not merely a question of the law in the books, but of the practical reality as seen through the lens of the victim. The court rejected the claim because the Petitioners could not bridge the gap between their fear and the state’s inaction with sufficient evidence. As constitutional and humanitarian concerns regarding safe harbors remain paramount, the Tenth Circuit’s insistence on specific reporting mechanisms ensures that asylum relief remains grounded in particularized facts rather than generalized assumptions about the rule of law in the home country.
