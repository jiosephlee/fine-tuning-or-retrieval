Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 2: # II. Standards of Review and Legal Framework

Effective appellate analysis in immigration law requires a precise understanding of how the Federal Circuit Court reviews Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) decisions. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit outlines the procedural boundaries that govern the appeal process. These boundaries determine what information the court considers, how heavily it weights the administrative agency's conclusions, and the level of proof the petitioner must afford to succeed. This chapter breaks down the jurisdictional basis, the burden of proof placed on the asylum applicant, the restricted scope of evidentiary review, and the statutory definition of asylum and its protected grounds as applied by this opinion.

## Jurisdiction Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252: Consolidation of Review

The threshold requirement for any case before the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit is proper subject matter jurisdiction. In this matter, the court expressly exercises jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. This statute provides the statutory basis for judicial review of final orders of removal. While removal proceedings are final administrative actions, the federal courts retain authority to grant consolidated review of asylum claims alongside the removal order. The court cites *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848 (10th Cir. 2025), to establish that the jurisdiction extends specifically to appeals involving the BIA’s denial of asylum relief.

This jurisdictional grant is critical because it allows the appellate court to look at the asylum claim in conjunction with the administrative order rather than in isolation. When a petition for review is granted, the court is not limited to procedural defects but may engage with the merits of the underlying application. However, the court notes that it is bound by the statutory limit of reviewing "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." This means the jurisdiction under § 1252 does not permit the court to accept new evidence or evidence outside the record compiled during the BIA proceedings. Consequently, the scope of the court's authority is strictly defined: it has the power to review but is restricted to the materials that the BIA actually considered when affirming the immigration judge’s decision.

## The Burden of Proof: Preponderance of the Evidence

Once jurisdiction is established, the substantive burden of the applicant shifts to the petitioner. In *Jimenez*, the court clarifies that asylum is a "discretionary form of relief" but requires the applicant to meet a specific evidentiary threshold. Under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), the burden rests on the noncitizen applicant to prove by a "preponderance of the evidence" that they qualify as a refugee. The court cites *Singh* and 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1) to articulate that this burden is strict in the sense that the applicant must affirmatively demonstrate the facts, rather than relying on speculation.

The standard "preponderance of the evidence" signifies that the facts sought to be established must be more likely true than not true. This standard applies to all claims for asylum. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners attempted to prove that they faced persecution, that the persecution had a nexus to a protected ground, and that the government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The court of appeals analyzes the petitioner’s performance against this standard. Specifically, to qualify for relief based on past persecution, the applicant must present proof sufficient to tip the scale of probability. The opinion notes that while the Immigration Judge found the petitioners credible, credibility alone is not sufficient; the applicant must still satisfy the evidentiary burden regarding the specific elements of asylum. Thus, a successful appeal requires demonstrating that the petitioner has met this burden, or that the administrative record does not support the finding that they have failed to do so.

## Standard of Review: Deference to the Administrative Record

Because the BIA is the final administrative authority before the court, the appellate court does not conduct a de novo review of facts. Instead, the *Jimenez* panel applies the "substantial-evidence standard." Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." Citing *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012), the opinion establishes that this is a highly deferential standard. This means the Tenth Circuit will not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment for that of the BIA if there is reasonable support in the record.

Furthermore, the court emphasizes that its review is limited to the administrative record. The panel notes that because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, the court is not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of the grounds, but the review remains tethered to the evidence developed in the lower proceedings. If reasonable adjudicators could find that the evidence is substantial enough, the appellate court must uphold the BIA’s decision. If, however, the evidence is scant or contradictory, the BIA’s findings may be vacated. In *Jimenez*, the court finds that the record contains "substantial evidence" supporting the BIA’s conclusion that the petitioners did not prove persecution, reinforcing the legal principle that a petitioner cannot succeed on appeal simply by arguing against the character of the evidence in the record.

## Definition of Asylum and Protected Grounds Under the INA

To satisfy the standards of review and the burden of proof discussed above, the petitioner must define their claim within the statutory framework of the INA. The *Jimenez* court references *Niang v. Gonzales*, 422 F.3d 1187 (10th Cir. 2005), to outline the legal definition of a refugee. To be eligible for asylum status, an applicant must be outside their country of nationality and be unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution. This persecution must be based on one of five statutorily protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In applying this framework, the *Jimenez* panel sets out the specific elements required to show past persecution. The court describes "persecution" as the infliction of suffering or harm offensive to the core of the refugee concept, requiring more than mere threats or restrictions on liberty. Additionally, the harm must be on account of a protected ground and committed by the government or forces the government is unable or unwilling to control. The court notes that past persecution also creates a rebuttable presumption regarding the fear of future persecution, but the initial showing must meet these definition requirements. For mixed-motive cases, where victims claim persecution for multiple reasons, the political opinion cannot be incidental but must be at least one "central reason" for the harm. This statutory requirement provides the structural baseline against which the courts measure all subsequent factual determinations in asylum appeals.
