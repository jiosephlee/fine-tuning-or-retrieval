Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # I. Factual and Procedural Background

## The Petitioners: Identity, Immigration Status, and Political Activities

To understand the claims for asylum, it is essential to first identify the individuals at the center of the dispute and their connection to the troubled country in question. Petitioners are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. Based on the record, the family are natives and citizens of Colombia living in Bogotá. To support their claims for protection, the Petitioners must demonstrate that they fall within the definition of a refugee, a status established under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). This definition requires that an applicant fear persecution in their country of nationality due to specific grounds, including race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

The Petitioners’ claim hinges heavily on their political participation in Colombia. Since 2012, they have been members of the Colombian Liberal Party. During the 2022 Colombian presidential election, the family actively supported Federico Gutierrez, a candidate for president. This involvement was not passive; the Petitioners participated in door-to-door campaigning in neighborhood by neighborhood to seek favorable votes. This constant engagement in public political activity served as the backdrop for the subsequent threats and harassment they faced. Furthermore, Jimenez holds a provisional law license and worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor’s office, assisting victims of domestic abuse and other crimes. This professional status is a critical factual detail because it explains Jimenez’s interaction with law enforcement and narco-trafficking elements; in the eyes of certain criminal elements within Colombia, his role as a provisional legal representative and his connection to the prosecutor’s office made him a target. Thus, the factual record establishes that the Petitioners were active public figures engaged in political and legal work before they relocated to the United States.

## Timeline of Harassment: From Campaigning to the Narco Dispute

The court’s opinion details a specific sequence of events in Colombia beginning during the 2022 election season that culminated in the family’s decision to flee. The timeline begins around the end of May 2022, when Jimenez started receiving threatening phone calls. These threats were often associated with his involvement in the Liberal Party. By the early June 2022, the situation escalated when a colleague of the family was kidnapped and murdered despite having reported his own threats. Despite this violence, the Petitioners continued their political campaigning.

A pivotal event occurred in late June 2022 regarding a private property dispute. Jimenez, leveraging his background as a lawyer, attempted to mediate a dispute between a client and a tenant who was withholding rent for over a year. During an investigation into the tenant’s identity, Jimenez discovered the tenant was an ex-husband of the client and, crucially, that the tenant was also known as "Don Rafa," a leader of a group affiliated with narco-trafficking and the current president of Colombia. Upon visiting the property, Jimenez found it to be a base for illicit activities, including prostitution and offering day-rental services to migrants. Although law enforcement had attempted to search the property, the Petitioners found no resolution, and a local police officer advised Jimenez to avoid the trouble he had created there.

Immediately following this encounter, the household faced a new threat. Jimenez received a "death to snitches" brochure targeting Liberal Party members, and shortly thereafter, he and his family were held at gunpoint in their furniture factory. The assailants explicitly linked their threats to Jimenez’s work with the prosecutor's office and his investigation into Don Rafa. Jimenez was treated as a “snitch” rather than a political activist. Days later, the harassing men targeted family members, with a man attempting to silence Jimenez’s younger son at a school. After reporting the criminal activity to a police officer, who responded that the police were complicit in the problem, the Petitioners decided to leave their home country within two months of the initial threats. This chronological progression is vital for establishing the causal link between the Petitioners' actions and the threats they received, which the lower courts ultimately found to lack the necessary connection to political opinion for asylum purposes.

## Travel and Entry: Unlawful Presence and Notice to Appear

The factual background does not end with the Petitioners' departure from Colombia; it must also account for their arrival in the United States to establish the procedural context of the case. The Petitioners arrived in the United States in December 2022, arriving without being admitted to the country or paroled by an immigration officer. Consequently, they are considered to be living in the United States unlawfully. This method of entry is procedurally significant because it triggers the enforcement mechanisms of the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). Approximately three months after their arrival, DHS served the Petitioners with individual Notices to Appear, which formally charged each of them as removable from the United States pursuant to the Immigration and Nationality Act.

The legal proceeding that followed this entry was bifurcated into two tracks: the immigration court proceedings and the asylum application process. In August 2023, the Petitioners appeared before an Immigration Judge (IJ). During this hearing, the Petitioners admitted the truth of the DHS's allegations, confirming their removability. Simultaneously, they applied for asylum and withholding of removal, with their minor children proceeding as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez's application. The circumstances of their entry—specifically that they were not admitted—made the Petitioners removable, but the application for asylum provided them the opportunity to defend themselves against those charges. The IJ ultimately directed removal to Colombia, finding that the Petitioners did not successfully overcome the presumptions of removability by showing a well-founded fear of persecution or grounds to block deportation.

## Lower Court Proceedings: Immigration Judge and Board of Immigration Appeals

Once the Immigration Judge rendered a final decision, the Petitioners appealed that denial to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), the administrative body within the Department of Justice that reviews decisions from Immigration Judges. The IJ denied the Petitioners' claims but credited their testimony as credible, finding instead that they failed to prove specific legal elements required for asylum. The IJ concluded that the Petitioners did not demonstrate that they suffered perse-cution in the past. The Judge also found no link between any harm suffered and any of the statutorily protected grounds, such as political opinion. Furthermore, the IJ determined that the Petitioners could legally relocate within Colombia to safety and that the Colombian government was capable or willing to protect them. Petitioners also raised claims regarding protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT) and argued that they were denied due process because the IJ violated their right to counsel, though the primary appeal focused on asylum and withholding of removal.

The IJ's decision was not final indefinitely; the Petitioners appealed to the BIA, which is under this circuit convention to review removal orders to ensure they comply with the record. The BIA affirmed the Immigration Judge's decision and dismissed the Petitioners' appeal. In doing so, the BIA affirmed the IJ's rejection of the Petitioners' due process claims and the denial of their detention relief. This procedural posture—where the BIA affirms the IJ and becomes the final administrative agency—is the one the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reviews. Because the BIA is the final administrative decision-maker on the record, the appellate court's review is focused on the administrative record that supports the BIA's and the IJ's findings. This establishes the procedural threshold for the appeal, grounding the court's analysis in whether the administrative record provided substantial evidence to support the lower court's determinations on past persecution, nexus, and government protection.
