Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # III. Analyzing the Threshold Elements of Asylum

## Defining 'Persecution': Suffering Beyond Restrictions and Threats

The first hurdle in any asylum claim is establishing "past persecution." To understand *Jimenez v. Bondi*, a student must first grasp how the law defines this term. Persecution is not a technical term in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA); rather, it is interpreted through judicial precedent. In the Tenth Circuit, as established in *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ from the persecutor in a way regarded as offensive. Crucially, this standard dictates that the harm must "entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty."

When reviewing the Petitioner's history, the court applied two key limitations on what constitutes persecution. First, threats alone are insufficient. In *Karki v. Holder*, the Tenth Circuit explicitly held that threats alone cannot constitute persecution, even when regarding a well-founded fear of future harm. Second, psychological or emotional distress alone is rarely enough. In *Jimenez*, while the petitioners argued that the cumulative threats caused severe anxiety and panic attacks for their children, the court noted that threats and emotional distress are often "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling" without rising to the level of persecution. The court relied on prior precedent, such as *Brandy v. Holder*, which distinguished between mere "restrictions on life and liberty" and the "offensive harm" required for asylum. Therefore, even if an applicant was undeterred from threats or suffered emotional trauma, the threshold is not met if the harm was limited to the petitioners' subjective fear rather than objective, offensive suffering inflicted by the persecutor.

## The Nexus Requirement: Political Opinion as a Central Reason

Once the threshold of harm is cleared, an asylum applicant must satisfy the "nexus" requirement. This means the persecution must occur "on account of" a statutorily protected ground, such as political opinion. In *Jimenez*, the appellate court clarified that the burden is not merely showing the persecutor acted for *a* reason, but that the protected ground was "at least one central reason" for the harm.

The standard for proving a nexus claim is rooted in the "central reason" test. As the Tenth Circuit explained in *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, an applicant's political opinion "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." While a persecutor may have mixed motives—targeting someone for economic reasons while also harboring political hatreds—the protected ground must play a substantial role. If the protected ground plays only a "minor role," the claim for asylum based on political opinion fails. The court emphasized that when reviewing complex cases, the question is never whether the persecutor had *any* political objection, but rather whether that political objection was a primary driver of the attack. This distinction is vital for students: proving a persecutor disliked a claimant's political views is not the same as proving the persecutor targeted the claimant *because* of those views.

## Fact Application: The Role of the Narco-Dispute ('Don Rafa')

To apply the legal standard for persecution and nexus, the court in *Jimenez* dissected the specific timeline of the threatening incidents against the Petitioners. This section is critical for students learning to distinguish between timeline and causation. The critical factual pivot in this case was the escalation of threats coinciding with a professional dispute, rather than the Petitioners' political campaigning.

Prior to the escalation, Petitioners were active members of the Liberal Party and campaigned for a presidential rival. They received some threats during this period, but the court noted there was no evidence to suggest "Don Rafa" (a narco-trafficking leader) was behind those initial politically affiliated threats. The narrative shifted only after the Petitioner Jimenez intervened in a property dispute. As a provisional lawyer, Jimenez visited a property to negotiate rent, only to discover the tenant was ex-husband of a client and a known gang leader. Jimenez then learned the property was a base for crimes and reported this to law enforcement. It was after this specific incident involving the illicit property that the men with guns threatened Jimenez, accusing him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor and noting the threats came from an order from "Don Rafa."

The court treated this timeline as decisive evidence. Because the significant physical threats and the three-day standoff at the furniture factory occurred specifically after the property investigation, the court focused on the expansion of direct conflict with a source of narco-trafficking, rather than the general political activity. This distinction allowed the court to isolate whether the harm was political in nature or personal/professional in nature.

## Classifying Motive: Tangential Political Opinion vs. Personal Animosity

The final analysis in this section required the court to weigh the Petitioners' arguments against the substantive record. Petitioners argued that Don Rafa targeted them for their political alliances. However, the appellate court concluded the government's "unable-or-unwilling" protection status (and consequently the nexus for asylum) depended on whether the *source* of the animosity was political or personal.

The record showed that the escalation of persecution correlated with Jimenez's concrete conflict with Don Rafa regarding the rent-paying and the closure of the property, coupled with the "revenue and security" impact of a federal investigation. While Petitioners claimed their membership in the Liberal Party drove the persecution, the IJ and BIA found that the persecutors were acting out of professional animosity. The court found substantial evidence that the political opinion was merely "tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm."

This distinction illustrates the "mixed-motive" bar in asylum cases. Even if a persecutor holds a political view against an applicant, the asylum claim fails if the *action* taken against them is motivated primarily by something else, such as professional rivalry or self-defense. Here, the "snitch" accusation and the warning to leave the property were framed as personal retribution for interference with criminal operations, indicating that the underlying animosity was personal. Consequently, the court declined to treat the political affiliation as a "central reason," leading to the dismissal of the Petitioners' asylum threshold claim.
