Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 4: # IV. Government Protection and Internal Relocation

## The 'Unable or Unwilling' Standard: Government Responsibility

In evaluating whether a noncitizen qualifies for asylum or withholding of removal based on well-founded fear of future persecution, courts must determine if the sovereign government is acting effectively to mitigate that risk. Under the precedent set forth by the Tenth Circuit in *Singh v. Bondi*, the analysis of government protection considers how the responding state reacted to an applicant's past persecution. The court identifies two types of information that weigh heavily during this inquiry: first, whether the government stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors, and second, whether the government offered specific protection to the applicant, such as by placing them in protective custody. This inquiry is not abstract; it requires evidence regarding the government's practical response to dangerous conditions faced by the noncitizen. The standard is grounded in the legal proposition that a risk of future persecution cannot be established if the state demonstrates it is either willing or able to control the destabilizing forces. In the context of *Jimenez*, the court utilized this framework to assess whether Colombia lacked the capacity or willingness to intervene on behalf of the Petitioners, despite allegations that their persecutors were aligned with the government.

## Evidentiary Requirements: Reporting Past Persecution to Authorities

A critical procedural component of the 'unable or unwilling' analysis is the applicant's interaction with state authorities. The Tenth Circuit has established that a key fact in this analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government. Without such notice, the court lacks the empirical basis to determine how police would have responded or how the government would have attempted to help. Consequently, a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors, as there is no record of a missed opportunity for intervention. However, the opinion clarifies that this requirement is not absolute. An applicant may still satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous. In *Jimenez*, the Third Circuit citation (*Aviles-Gonzalez*) supports the principle that without reporting, the court cannot assess government reaction, creating a strict evidentiary hurdle for applicants who claim their persecutors are effectively part of the state, as they must provide evidence that reporting itself was impossible or unsafe.

## Internal Relocation: Evaluating Safe Areas and Family Ties

Even if a noncitizen demonstrates a fear of persecution in their current location, their claim may be rebutted by the government showing that the individual could avoid future mistreatment by relocating to another part of their home country. This requirement is governed by the standard that the noncitizen could avoid persecution by moving to a safer area within the country. The record in *Jimenez* was viewed under a preponderance of the evidence standard to determine if safe relocation was feasible. The court noted that the presence of family in a country does not always establish safety for a petitioner seeking asylum or withholding of removal; hypothetical hardships regarding family are insufficient to block a claim. Furthermore, administrative or economic burdens associated with moving do not preclude a finding of safe area availability. The court specifically looked at whether the Petitioners had lived safely elsewhere before departing for the United States and whether perceived threats were tied to a specific neighborhood rather than the entire nation.

## Application to Case Facts: Police Inaction vs. Systemic Unwillingness

Applying these standards to the record, the court found substantial evidence that the Petitioners could not establish that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. While Petitioners argued that individual police officers advised them to leave and that Don Rafa was aligned with the government, the court distinguished between isolated apathy and systemic inability. The opinion notes that while individual police conversations can indicate unwillingness, a one-off interaction does not necessarily prove the government is unable to protect a victim. To establish a systemic failure, the record should show a broader inability to function, which was not supported by the facts. In *Jimenez*, evidence showed that the government was aware of the specific property at issue and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the criminal conduct there, contradicting the claim that the state could not intervene. Additionally, the Petitioners failed to report wrongdoing or corruption despite Jimenez and his father having worked for the government in Colombia. Finally, regarding internal relocation, the court observed that the threats directed at the Petitioners were to stay out of the neighborhood, not to leave the country. The fact that family members and the Petitioner's father continued to live and work unharmed in Colombia supported the conclusion that the Petitioners could mitigate any potential for future persecution by moving to another location within Colombia.
