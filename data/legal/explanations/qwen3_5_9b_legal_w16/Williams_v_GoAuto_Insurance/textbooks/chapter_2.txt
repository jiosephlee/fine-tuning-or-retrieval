Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 2: # II. Louisiana's Insurance Cancellation Measures: Statutory Framework and 'Strict Adherence'

## Overview of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)
This chapter explores the specific provisions of the Louisiana statute that govern how insurance policies financed through third parties may be cancelled for nonpayment. Under Louisiana law, when an insurance-premium finance company requests the cancellation of an insurance policy due to an uncured default, specific procedural steps must be met. According to the text of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv), the finance company must send to the insurer, by depositing it in the mail, with a private carrier, or via electronic mail. The entity sending the notice must provide a copy of the notice of cancellation accompanied by a statement certifying compliance with the law. This statutory framework was the central focus of the plaintiffs' challenge in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, where the core dispute turned on whether the insurance company could lawfully rely on a notice sent electronically by the finance company without a physical signature before the cancellation occurred.

## The Four-Prong Certification Requirement of the Premium Finance Company
The statute imposes a rigorous burden on the premium finance company when invoking the power of attorney to effectuate a cancellation. The finance company must transmit a "statement certifying" four specific elements to the insurer to trigger the cancellation process. First, the company must certify that the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as provided in Paragraph (1) of the statute. Second, the company must certify that the premium finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured. Third, the certification must establish that a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as provided in the relevant subsection, specifying the date of sending. Fourth, the company must certify that copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur under the policy. In *Williams*, the plaintiffs argued that the finance company failed to meet these requirements, specifically contending that the absence of a signature on the notice rendered the certification incomplete.

## The Insurer's Duty Upon Receipt of Notice
Once the insurer receives the notice and the required statement of certification from the premium finance company, the statute dictates a specific duty. Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), "Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract." This provision clarifies that the insurer's obligation to consider the cancellation as requested is triggered by the act of receipt. The court analyzed the timing of this receipt in the context of modern technology, noting that a GoAuto deponent testified that the insurer's web service automatically performed the cancellation activity when the APAC process made a call to the system. The court concluded that the insurer takes possession, delivery, or custody of the request when its computer system receives the data, satisfying the statutory requirement for action.

## The Judicial Doctrine of Strict Adherence (Delatte, Eaglin)
Louisiana courts have established a judicial doctrine requiring strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy. As established in *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, strict adherence is a prerequisite for a valid cancellation. The review implies that "any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy." This high standard of compliance was emphasized in the district court’s analysis, which ultimately led to the dismissal of the plaintiffs’ claims. The doctrine prevents insurers from relying on vague or informal cancellation mechanisms, ensuring that the precise statutory procedures are followed without deviation. While the plaintiffs argued that the lack of a signature constituted a defect under this doctrine, the Fifth Circuit noted that Louisiana authorities did not explicitly require a signature, suggesting strict adherence does not mandate procedures the legislature did not prescribe.

## The Consequences of Procedural Defects: Ineffective Cancellation
The primary legal consequence of failing to adhere to the strict requirements of the cancellation statute is the rendering of the cancellation ineffective. If an insurer or finance company fails to comply with the substantive or procedural mandates of § 9:3550, the policy remains in force until such defects are cured or a new valid cancellation sequence is initiated. In *Williams*, the plaintiffs claimed this ineffectiveness supported allegations of misrepresentation and breach of the good faith and fair dealing obligation. Because the district court found no statutory violation, these claims were dismissed. However, the broader legal principle remains clear: following the statutory path is not merely an administrative formality but a substantive condition precedent to the termination of the coverage relationship.

## The Purpose of Strict Adherence: Notice and Cure Periods
The requirement of strict adherence serves two specific public policy functions identified in *Britten v. Reavis*. First, the rigid procedural requirements provide a clear method for fixing the exact time and date of cancellation. This certainty is essential for insurance risk management and premium calculation. Second, the processes grant a minimum time duration during which the default may be cured. In the context of the *Williams* opinion, this ten-day notice period was critical for the insured to make the necessary payments. By detailing these purposes, the court justified why the statute was interpreted so narrowly regarding certifications, ensuring that neither the insurer nor the insured loses the protections intended by the legislature.
