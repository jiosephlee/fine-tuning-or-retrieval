Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How do I determine whether to review the Board's likelihood-of-confusion findings de novo or for substantial evidence?
Question:
I am trying to understand the standard of review applied in this case. The opinion states on page 2 that the court reviews the Board's legal conclusions de novo but its findings of fact for substantial evidence. However, the DuPont factors are described as both 'findings of relevant underlying facts' and 'questions of law.' Can someone clarify the interactive relationship between these two standards when analyzing the DuPont factors?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, here is the applicable standard of review and how it interacts with the *DuPont* analysis.

### The General Standard of Review per the Opinion

Under the opinion’s Section II, the Court adheres to the Federal Circuit precedent established in *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc.*: the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (**Board**’) **legal conclusions are reviewed de novo**, while its **findings of fact are reviewed for substantial evidence**.

To determine whether a reviewing court should apply *de novo* or substantial evidence, you must first categorize the specific issue under the *DuPont* framework.

**1. Likelihood of Confusion is a Question of Law**
The opinion explicitly states that while *likelihood of confusion* is the ultimate legal question (reviewed *de novo*), it is a question of law **"based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the *DuPont* factors."** Thus, the *DuPont* factors act as the bridge between the ultimate legal conclusion and the factual record.

**2. The *DuPont* Factors are Questions of Fact**
Citing *Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc.*, the opinion confirms that **"Each of the [thirteen] *DuPont* factors presents a question of fact."** Consequently, when a party challenges a specific factor explicitly (e.g., the similarity of goods under the second factor), the Board’s factual finding regarding that factor is reviewed for **substantial evidence**.

### Clarifying the "Interactive Relationship"

The confusion in your question arises because the Board applies legal standards to factual inquiries. In *Apex Bank*, the Court illustrates this interplay in two distinct ways:

**A. Standard Application (Substantial Evidence)**
In Section III.A, the Court reviews the **second DuPont factor** (similarity of services). The Court upholds the Board’s finding that the services were "highly similar" for substantial evidence review because the Board "carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services" and the evidence was adequate to support the conclusion. Here, the Board acted as a fact-finder.

**B. Legal Conclusion Within a Fact-Finding Factor**
The interactive relationship becomes critical when the Board makes a legal error in *applying* a factual factor.

*   **Section III.B (Sixth Factor):** The sixth factor considers the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." While this factor involves counting marks (fact), the Board must also determine legally what constitutes "similar goods."
*   The Board initially defined the "relevant public" narrowly to "consumers of credit card services," excluding broader "banking and financing services."
*   The Court identified this as a **legal flaw**, stating: "The sixth *DuPont* factor requires the Board to consider similar marks for similar goods... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."
*   **Result:** Because the Board's definition was legally inconsistent with its factual finding in the second factor, the Court treated the Board's conclusion on this factor as legally erroneous and **vacated** it (applying *de novo* scrutiny to the legal conclusion regarding the scope of similarity).

### Summary for Application

When analyzing a court opinion like *Apex Bank*:

1.  **Default:** Treat the application of *DuPont* factors as **questions of fact** subject to the **substantial evidence** standard.
2.  **Exception:** Apply **de novo** review if the Board’s analysis of a factor constitutes a **legal error**, such as incorrectly defining the scope of goods/services or misinterpreting the legal significance of the factors.
3.  **Ultimate Inquiry:** Remember that **likelihood of confusion** is always the underlying question of law. If the Board makes an error that affects the ultimate legal conclusion of confusion (e.g., remanding the first factor because the sixth factor might change the "commercial impression"), the reviewing court will vacate those findings.

### Does a dictionary definition alone justify a finding that 'banking' and 'credit card services' are legally identical under the Second DuPont factor?
Question:
Apex argues in its brief that it is a retail bank not a credit card issuer, yet the Board ruled their services were 'legally identical, in part' based on dictionary definitions for 'banking' and 'finance' extending credit. I'm confused about how relying on dictionary definitions can overcome the factual reality that Apex didn't currently offer credit cards and CC Serve didn't issue them either, but rather serviced accounts for banks. Is this a precedent for how the Board handles service similarity, or did they overlook the actual business model?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

Based on the Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, a court upheld the Board’s finding that dictionary definitions could establish "legal identity" between banking services and credit card services under the Second DuPont factor, even where the parties’ specific business models differed. The Court affirmed that the dictionary definition of "banking" and "finance"—as encompassing the extension of credit—was sufficient to support a finding of highly similar services despite one party not currently issuing cards.

**Relevant Legal Doctrine: The Second DuPont Factor**

Under trademark law, the Second DuPont factor assesses the similarity of the parties’ goods or services. The Federal Circuit states in *Apex Bank* that:
> "The services need not be identical—the evidence need only establish that 'the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.'"

The focus is on the nature and definition of the services to the consumer, not the internal contractual mechanics of how the service is delivered by the entity itself.

**Application in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* (2025)**

Your confusion regarding the "factual reality" of the business models (i.e., Apex is a bank, CC Serve is a servicer) is precisely the distinct argument addressed in Section III.A of the opinion. The Court explicitly rejected Apex’s assertion that the services were "not the same and . . . are not directly competitive" based on these distinctions.

The Board reasoned that dictionary definitions for "banking," "bank," and "finance" encompass "extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards." Consequently, the Board concluded the services were "legally identical, in part." When the Federal Circuit reviewed this under the substantial evidence standard, it held:
> "We find Apex's argument unavailing... substantial evidence supports the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar."

Importantly, the opinion notes that the Board did not rely solely on the Oxford or Webster entries; it used the definitions to categorize the *services* generally. While the Board also cited third-party registrations to show the services could emanate from a single source, the Federal Circuit affirmed that the dictionary-based classification was the primary driver for the "legal identity" finding.

**Precedential Value for Service Similarity**

This opinion provides strong precedent that consumers define services broadly by regulatory or dictionary categorization rather than specific operational roles (e.g., merchant-of-record vs. servicing entity). The Court implies that the "legal description" of the service controls over the "functional reality" of the business model.

However, remember that the Federal Circuit *vacated* the Board's findings on the First and Sixth DuPont factors (similar marks and commercial impression) on June 25, 2025, because the narrow definition of the relevant public used under the Sixth Factor should have been broader given the services' similarity. Thus, while this ruling upholds the *nature* of the services, it vacates the analysis of similar marks based on that nature.

**Conclusion**

In summary, *Apex Bank* establishes that for the Second DuPont factor, a dictionary definition of "banking" and "finance" is competent evidence to establish legal similarity with credit card services, even if neither party actually issues the cards in their specific business arrangement. The Court prioritized the high-level definition of the market ("financial services") over the specific transactional roles.

### Why did the Board incorrectly limit the 'relevant public' solely to consumers of credit card services in its analysis of the Sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
The court vacated the Board's finding on the sixth DuPont factor because it felt the 'relevant public' should match the scope found in the second factor. In the second factor, services were deemed 'highly similar' and 'partially legally identical.' If they are legally identical regarding the relevant public, why couldn't the Board consider the 42 other third-party marks using 'Aspire' in the broader banking and financing sector? Isn't it standard to define 'similar goods' narrowly to avoid irrelevant comparisons in crowded markets?
Answer:
**The Board erred because it applied a narrower definition of "goods/services" for the sixth DuPont factor than it applied for the second factor, creating an internal inconsistency in its likelihood-of-confusion analysis.**

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, Hughes, C.J. clarifies that the definition of "similar goods" established in the second DuPont factor controls the scope of the relevant market for the sixth factor.

### The Legal Standard: Consistency Across Factors

The second DuPont factor assesses the **similarity of the parties' goods and services**, determining the scope of protection due to the degree of relatedness. The sixth DuPont factor evaluates the **number and nature of similar marks** used on similar goods. The purpose of the sixth factor is to determine whether the field is "crowded," which might indicate a mark's weakness and narrow scope of protection (*Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC*).

Crucially, both factors rely on the same underlying factual premise: **what constitutes a "similar good."** If the Board defines two services as highly similar for the purpose of finding a likelihood of confusion under the second factor, it must apply that same definition when determining the relevant universe of third-party marks for the sixth factor.

### Application to the *Apex Bank* Opinion

In this case, the Board found in its analysis of the second factor that the services were **"highly similar"** and **"partially legally identical"** (*Apex Bank* at 6). The Board determined that Apex's "banking and financing services" overlapped legally with CC Serve's "credit card services."

However, when analyzing the sixth factor, the Board restricted its inquiry to a specific subset of that similarity. The Board explicitly defined the relevant public as "consumers of 'credit card services'" and excluded 42 third-party marks that used "Aspire" in the broader "banking and financing sector," deeming them "essentially irrelevant" (*Apex Bank* at 6).

Hughes, C.J. explains that this error prevents a correct application of the sixth factor:

> When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor (*Apex Bank* at 6).

### Why the Narrow Definition Failed

By limiting the relevant public to credit card consumers, the Board artificially narrowed the universe of "similar goods" to exclude the broader banking services that the Board itself had already classified as highly similar in the second factor. Because the 2nd Factor established that "banking and financing" services were legally identical to CC Serve's credit card services for the purpose of the likelihood-of-confusion test, the Board could not exclude third-party marks operating within that broader industry when assessing crowding.

As a result, the Court vacated the Board's findings on the sixth factor (and the first, given the interplay of commercial strength) to allow for a consistent analysis of the relevant public and relevant products.

***
**Sources cited in opinion:** *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, No. 2023-2143 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025); *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC*, 794 F.3d 1334; *Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot*, 396 F.3d 1369.

### What is the specific evidentiary threshold for a mark to be considered 'ubiquitous' or 'considerable' under the Sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
Under Juices Generation and Jack Wolfskin, there are different standards for marking marks as 'considerable' or 'ubiquitous.' In this case, the Board found 9 'notable' marks were not enough to show considerable use. The court on appeal notes there were '42 other third-party marks.' Based on the pre-emption calling 26 marks 'considerable' in Juices Generation, can someone explain how the court determines if a specific number (or set of examples) of third-party registrations reaches the threshold of 'sufficient commercial strength' to be ignored or considered 'ubiquitous' as a distraction?
Answer:
**There is no bright-line numerical threshold.** *Apex Bank* clarifies that the Sixth DuPont factor does not rely on a fixed number of third-party registrations (such as the 26 marks in *Juices Generation* or the 14 in *Jack Wolfskin*), but rather on the **scope of the relevant field** as defined by the "similar goods" analysis.

The Federal Circuit in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* vacated the Board’s finding on the Sixth DuPont factor because the Board was inconsistent in how it defined "similar goods" between the Second and Sixth factors. 

Here is how the decision frames the threshold for a mark to be considered "weak" or "ubiquitous" in a crowded field:

**1. The Doctrine of Consistent Scope**
Under the Sixth DuPont factor, evidence of third-party use is relevant to show a mark lacks commercial strength. However, the *Apex* court held that the scope of the inquiry into third-party marks must align with the scope of "similar goods" found under the Second DuPont factor. Because the Board found Apex and CC Serve’s services were "highly similar" (or partially legally identical) in the Second DuPont analysis, the Sixth DuPont analysis required the Board to consider that same universe of goods when counting third-party marks. The Board erred by restricting its count of relevant marks to only those involving credit card services, while excluding 42 other marks involving broader banking and financing services.

**2. The Evidentiary Standard**
The opinion states that evidence of third-party use "tends to indicate a lack of commercial strength," and a "crowded field" suggests that consumers are adept at distinguishing between them. While the Board cited *Juices Generation* (26 marks) and *Jack Wolfskin* (14 "notable" examples) as precedents for what constitutes a "crowded" field, *Apex* makes clear that these numbers are merely indicative of the Board's broad discretion, not statutory rules. The threshold for "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use is determined by whether the Board defined the field too narrowly to artificially limit the count of competing marks.

**3. Practical Application**
In this case, the Board relied on a count of nine "notable" marks to refuse to find the field crowded. *Apex* indicates that this number was insufficient because it was derived from an overly narrow definition of the relevant services. By remanding the case, the Court effectively instructs that the threshold is reached when the Board considers **all** third-party registrations that fall within the proper scope of "similar goods," rather than a selected subset. Thus, it may be the totality of the relevant registrations—both credit card and broader financial services—that pushes a count over the threshold of "commercial strength," provided the goods are legally consistent across the relevant factors.

**Conclusion**
The decision holds that no specific integer establishes insignificance. The weight of the Six DuPont factor depends on the totality of "similar marks in use on similar goods." Because the Board failed to retain the scope of its goods definition from the Second Factor to the Sixth Factor, its conclusion that nine marks were insufficient to demonstrate commercial weakness was legally flawed. The Court remands for reconsideration to ensure the Board accounts for the excluded marks within the correct scope of goods.

### Why did the court vacate the First DuPont factor analysis simply because of an error in the Sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
The First DuPont factor looks at the similarity of the marks (appearance, sound, connotation). The Sixth DuPont factor looks at crowded markets. The court says 'Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board's analysis here.' Can someone explain the doctrinal logic here? Does the presence of too many similar marks (Sixth Factor) automatically mean a mark has a 'weak commercial impression' (First Factor)? How is that causal link established?
Answer:
The court vacated the Board’s analysis of the First DuPont Factor because its determination regarding the "commercial impression" of the mark was dependent on the Board's proper evaluation of the Sixth Factor.

To understand the doctrinal logic, you must look at how the Sixth Factor feeds into the First Factor, specifically regarding the mark's "strength."

**The Doctrinal Link: Commercial Strength vs. Commercial Impression**
Under the *DuPont* factors, the Sixth Factor assesses the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." The relevant legal principle (citing *Palm Bay Imps.* and *Juice Generation* in the opinion) is that evidence of many similar marks may indicate that a mark is "weak" and entitled only to a narrow scope of protection. Conversely, a crowded field suggests customers are adept at distinguishing marks, which supports a finding of weakness.

However, the First Factor assesses the "similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties... as to commercial impression." This analysis requires an assessment of the mark's overall distinctiveness and the likely confusion to consumers.

**The Court's Reasoning**
In this opinion, the Board made an error in the Sixth Factor by defining the universe of "similar goods" too narrowly. While analyzing the Second Factor, the Board found the parties' services were "highly similar" or even "legally identical." Yet, when analyzing the Sixth Factor, the Board excluded marks for broader banking/financing services from the count because they did not explicitly match "credit card services."

The court held that the Board should have retained the same broad scope of similarity found under the Second Factor. The causal link the opinion describes is as follows:

1.  **Scope of Goods:** A broader definition of "similar goods" (under the Second Factor error acknowledged by the court) allows for a broader search for competing marks (Sixth Factor).
2.  **Commercial Strength:** The Ninth Circuit opinion states that the Sixth Factor may result in a "different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness."
3.  **Commercial Impression:** The First Factor focuses on the "overall commercial impression." The opinion explicitly states that commercial impression is informed by the strength of the mark.

Therefore, the court did not find that a crowded market *automatically* creates a weak commercial impression. Rather, the court recognized that you cannot accurately evaluate the mark’s **commercial strength** (which informs the First Factor's impression) without first evaluating the correct scope of the crowded field. Since the Board’s error in the Sixth Factor (the scope of goods compared) undermined the accuracy of assessing the mark's strength, the First Factor analysis—which relies on that strength to determine commercial impression—must also be vacated for reconsideration.

As the opinion puts it in Section III.C: "consideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."
