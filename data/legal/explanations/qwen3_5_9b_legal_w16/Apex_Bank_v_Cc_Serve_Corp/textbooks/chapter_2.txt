Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Standards of Review and the DuPont Framework

## 1. Statutory Basis: Lanham Act § 2(d) and Likelihood of Confusion

The core of the opposition in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* rests on a violation of Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). To register a mark, an applicant must demonstrate that their mark is not likely to cause confusion with a prior mark. The Federal Circuit in *Apex* reaffirms that when a prosecution applicant uses a mark that creates a likelihood of confusion with a prior user’s mark on the respective goods, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board must refuse registration. The statute requires a determination based on the concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user. While the Board sustains an opposition finding that confusion is likely based on those concurrent uses, the final liability determination depends on the application of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. This analysis serves as the legal framework through which the Federal Circuit tested the application, addressing whether the evidence supports a conclusion that the marks are confusingly similar.

## 2. Standard of Review: De Novo Review for Legal Conclusions

In reviewing Board findings on appeal, the Federal Circuit distinguishes between questions of law and questions of fact. Legal conclusions, such as whether the Board applied the correct legal standard in analyzing the evidence, are reviewed *de novo*. This means the appellate court gives no deference to the Board’s legal reasoning and decides the issue itself. In *Apex Bank*, the court explicitly stated, "We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo." This standard mandates that the circuit court independently evaluate the accuracy of the Board’s legal determinations regarding the inference of law drawn from the facts. It ensures that the statutory requirements of the Lanham Act are interpreted consistently by the appellate court rather than relying on the Board’s interpretation of the law.

## 3. Standard of Review: Substantial Evidence for Facts

While the law is reviewed de novo, the factual findings made by the Board are reviewed for "substantial evidence." Substantial evidence is defined in the opinion as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This standard represents a deferential inquiry into the evidence, asking whether the Board's opinions are supported by the record. The *M2 Software* rule emphasizes that the Board need not make every finding or record every piece of evidence that is available, but it must support its findings with relevant evidence that a reasonable mind would accept. If the Board’s factual findings regarding the party’s use of the mark or the nature of the services are supported by this standard of proof, the appellate court is barred from substituting its own judgment for that of the Board.

## 4. The DuPont Factors: Overview of the Likelihood-of-Confusion Analysis

To determine if confusion is likely, the Board utilizes the thirteen factors set forth in *Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Each of these factors presents a question of fact, and findings with regard to them are tested on appeal for substantial evidence. However, the analysis is not mechanical. As the *Apex* opinion notes, "Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors." The court has specific responsibility to assess these underlying findings. The thirteen factors cover various aspects of the marks and the market, including similarity of the marks, nature of the goods, and commercial strength. While the full thirteen factors are available for all cases, the Federal Circuit applies these flexibly to the specific context of the dispute.

## 5. Relevance of Factors: Which Factors Do the Parties and Board Consider?

The Board is not required to address every single DuPont factor in its analysis. It need only consider those factors that are "relevant and of record." In *Apex Bank*, the Board addressed specific factors—specifically the first, second, and sixth—to reach a conclusion on likelihood of confusion. The opinion notes that the Board focused on factors particularly relevant to the case, such as the similarity of goods and the similarity of the marks in their entireties. However, the parties and Board must ensure that the scope of their consideration is consistent across the analysis. In this case, the error found in the sixth DuPont factor, regarding the scope of third-party marks, required reconsideration of the first factor because the overall commercial impression could change. The guidance here is that the Board's selection of factors must remain consistent with its finding on related factors, particularly regarding the definition of the relevant public and the goods involved.
