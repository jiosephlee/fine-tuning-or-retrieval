Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Chapter 1: Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Introduction of the Parties: Apex Bank and CC Serve Corp.
To understand the dispute before the Federal Circuit, one must first identify the competing entities and their respective roles in the marketplace. CC Serve Corp. is a company that offers credit card services to its customers. As described in the judicial record, CC Serve is not a bank in its own right but rather operates by joining with banks to issue credit cards. Under this arrangement, the bank issues the ASPIRE-branded credit cards, while CC Serve and its affiliates service the accounts. In contrast, Apex Bank is a retail bank chartered in Tennessee with eighteen branch locations. Like many traditional banks, Auxiliary Bank offers personal checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and loans. Crucially, Apex Bank does not currently offer credit card services, distinguishing its current business model from CC Serve’s. However, Apex plans to expand its products to offer an internet banking platform, which will utilize a different brand name. The immediate factual conflict, therefore, arises from Apex’s intention to enter a business segment it does not currently occupy, involving services that compete directly or indirectly with those of CC Serve.

## Registration and Service Details: ASPIRE (CC Serve) vs. ASPIRE BANK (Apex)
The core of the trademark dispute involves the specific characters at issue and their registration history. CC Serve holds Registration No. 2126948 for the standard character mark ASPIRE. This registration was issued in 1998, providing CC Serve with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Conversely, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office in August 2019. Apex sought to register the word and design marks ASPIRE BANK for "banking and financing services." Although Apex’s application was approved for publication by the examining attorney on December 17, 2019, CC Serve raised a protest regarding the marks during the prosecution period. The procedural record highlights the distinction between the two marks: CC Serve’s mark is the single term ASPIRE, whereas Apex seeks to register a variation that includes the word BANK as part of the designation.

## Definition of Trademark Services: Disparity between Credit Card Services and Banking/Financing
Petitioners often frame their appeals around whether the goods or services at issue are related or distinct, and the facts of this case reflect the parties' differing definitions of their services. In its registration, CC Serve described its services as credit card services, noting that it joins with banks to issue ASPIRE-branded cards and manages associated accounts. Apex, however, defined its proposed services simply as "banking and financing services," excluding credit card issuance from its description. From the foregoing description of the business, it is apparent that the parties characterize their offerings differently. Apex argued it does not offer credit cards and thus participates in a distinct market. Nonetheless, the factual record establishes that CC Serve’s operations involve a partnership with banks to issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards, creating a scenario where one party manages the issuance and processing of the debt instrument while the other offers broader banking solutions.

## USPTO Timeline: Application, Publication, and Opposition Filing
The procedural history regarding the United States Patent and Trademark Office reveals a tight timeline of application, publication, and challenge. Apex submitted its intent-to-use applications in August 2019 seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK marks. Following internal prosecution, the marks were published for opposition on December 17, 2019. During this publication phase, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest to the examining attorney, asserting that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to its own registered mark ASPIRE. Initially, the examining attorney approved the marks for publication despite the protest. The next significant milestone occurred in February 2020, when CC Serve formally initiated an opposition to Apex’s marks with the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board. This opposition alleged a likelihood of confusion, initiating the formal administrative review process that preceded the appeal to the court and establishing the critical dates necessary for determining seniority and priority of rights.

## Procedural History: TTAB Opposition, Board Ruling, and Federal Circuit Review Jurisdiction
Once the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) resolved the opposition, the dispute escalated to the Federal Circuit. The Board sustained the opposition, refusing registration of Apex’s marks under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). The Board concluded that consumer confusion between the marks was likely based on its analysis of the relevant DuPont factors. Apex subsequently appealed this decision to the court. The Federal Circuit affirmed its jurisdiction over this appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B). In reviewing the Board's decision, the Federal Circuit applied a specific standard of review: it reviews the Board's legal conclusions, such as the application of section 2(d), de novo. Conversely, the court reviews the Board's findings of fact for substantial evidence, which is defined as relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. This bifurcated standard of review is fundamental to the appellate jurisdiction, requiring the court to independently evaluate the application of the law while deferring on factual determinations supported by the record evidence.

# Chapter 2: Standards of Review and the DuPont Framework

## 1. Statutory Basis: Lanham Act § 2(d) and Likelihood of Confusion

The core of the opposition in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* rests on a violation of Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). To register a mark, an applicant must demonstrate that their mark is not likely to cause confusion with a prior mark. The Federal Circuit in *Apex* reaffirms that when a prosecution applicant uses a mark that creates a likelihood of confusion with a prior user’s mark on the respective goods, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board must refuse registration. The statute requires a determination based on the concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user. While the Board sustains an opposition finding that confusion is likely based on those concurrent uses, the final liability determination depends on the application of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. This analysis serves as the legal framework through which the Federal Circuit tested the application, addressing whether the evidence supports a conclusion that the marks are confusingly similar.

## 2. Standard of Review: De Novo Review for Legal Conclusions

In reviewing Board findings on appeal, the Federal Circuit distinguishes between questions of law and questions of fact. Legal conclusions, such as whether the Board applied the correct legal standard in analyzing the evidence, are reviewed *de novo*. This means the appellate court gives no deference to the Board’s legal reasoning and decides the issue itself. In *Apex Bank*, the court explicitly stated, "We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo." This standard mandates that the circuit court independently evaluate the accuracy of the Board’s legal determinations regarding the inference of law drawn from the facts. It ensures that the statutory requirements of the Lanham Act are interpreted consistently by the appellate court rather than relying on the Board’s interpretation of the law.

## 3. Standard of Review: Substantial Evidence for Facts

While the law is reviewed de novo, the factual findings made by the Board are reviewed for "substantial evidence." Substantial evidence is defined in the opinion as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This standard represents a deferential inquiry into the evidence, asking whether the Board's opinions are supported by the record. The *M2 Software* rule emphasizes that the Board need not make every finding or record every piece of evidence that is available, but it must support its findings with relevant evidence that a reasonable mind would accept. If the Board’s factual findings regarding the party’s use of the mark or the nature of the services are supported by this standard of proof, the appellate court is barred from substituting its own judgment for that of the Board.

## 4. The DuPont Factors: Overview of the Likelihood-of-Confusion Analysis

To determine if confusion is likely, the Board utilizes the thirteen factors set forth in *Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Each of these factors presents a question of fact, and findings with regard to them are tested on appeal for substantial evidence. However, the analysis is not mechanical. As the *Apex* opinion notes, "Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors." The court has specific responsibility to assess these underlying findings. The thirteen factors cover various aspects of the marks and the market, including similarity of the marks, nature of the goods, and commercial strength. While the full thirteen factors are available for all cases, the Federal Circuit applies these flexibly to the specific context of the dispute.

## 5. Relevance of Factors: Which Factors Do the Parties and Board Consider?

The Board is not required to address every single DuPont factor in its analysis. It need only consider those factors that are "relevant and of record." In *Apex Bank*, the Board addressed specific factors—specifically the first, second, and sixth—to reach a conclusion on likelihood of confusion. The opinion notes that the Board focused on factors particularly relevant to the case, such as the similarity of goods and the similarity of the marks in their entireties. However, the parties and Board must ensure that the scope of their consideration is consistent across the analysis. In this case, the error found in the sixth DuPont factor, regarding the scope of third-party marks, required reconsideration of the first factor because the overall commercial impression could change. The guidance here is that the Board's selection of factors must remain consistent with its finding on related factors, particularly regarding the definition of the relevant public and the goods involved.

# Chapter 3: Analysis of the DuPont Factors

## DuPont Factor 2: Similarity of Goods and Services (Affirmed)

In the factual analysis provided by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board), the similarity of goods and services constitutes the second DuPont factor. This factor requires the Board to determine whether the respective products offered by the applicant and the prior user are related or if their marketing circumstances could give rise to a mistaken belief as to source. In *Apex Bank*, the Board focused on CC Serve's credit card services against Apex Bank's proposed banking and financing services. Central to the Board's finding was the definition of the services themselves. The Board utilized the Trademark ID Manual and third-party registrations to define CC Serve's entry for "credit card services." It concluded that these services included issuing cards for use in financing purchases, counseling on debt, and processing transactions.

For Apex's services, the Board looked to the dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance," noting that these terms encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. Consequently, the Board concluded that the parties' services were "legally identical, in part." The Federal Circuit affirmed this finding under the standard of substantial evidence. While Apex argued that the services were not directly competitive because CC Serve partners with banks rather than providing them itself, the Court deferred to the Board's analysis of the service descriptions. The opinion highlights that the Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services, and the evidence supported the determination of a high degree of similarity. This finding was critical because it set the baseline for the "universe" of services in which confusion could occur. Without establishing that the goods and services are related, the discussion of competing marks becomes irrelevant to a likelihood of confusion inquiry.

## DuPont Factor 6: Number and Nature of Similar Marks (Vacated/Erroneous Analysis)

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. The theory underlying this factor is that a crowded field indicates that a specific mark is relatively weak, affording it only a narrow scope of protection. Conversely, if the field is not crowded, the mark retains its normal scope of protection. In *Apex Bank*, the Board examined third-party uses of the word "Aspire" to determine commercial strength. The Board identified ninety-nine Aspire-formative marks but selectively applied this analysis. Specifically, the Board focused solely on nine marks for credit card services and deemed 42 other marks concerning broader financial services as "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's finding on this factor because this selective application represented a legal error. The sixth factor requires the Board to consider similar marks used on similar goods and services, not identical goods. The Third-in Circuit explicitly held in cases like *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation* that the scope of goods under the sixth factor need not be identical to trigger relevance. In *Apex*, the board restricted the relevant public to consumers of credit card services because of its initial classification of services. However, the Court found that excluding the additional banking and financing marks was too narrow. Since the rights to protect a mark depend on the commercial reality of the market, the Board's exclusion of relevant third-party marks weakened the accuracy of its assessment regarding CC Serve's mark strength.

## The Logical Link Between Factor 2 and Factor 6: Scope of Similarity

A critical error in this portion of the *Apex Bank* analysis was the lack of consistency between the Board's findings on Factor 2 and Factor 6. In Factor 2, the Board determined that the services were highly similar, noting they were "legally identical" in many respects. In Factor 6, however, the Board imposed a stricter standard of similarity, deciding to filter out any marks that did not relate strictly to credit cards, even though those broader services overlapped significantly with Apex's offerings. The Federal Circuit reasoned that if the Board has established that the services are highly similar under the second factor, it must retain that same scope in its consideration of similar marks under the sixth factor.

The logical linkage here teaches that the factual findings of one DuPont factor constrain the legal analysis of subsequent factors. By finding the services to be highly similar under Factor 2, the Board established that the relevant universe of commerce is broader than just credit card transactions. Therefore, applying a restrictive definition of "similar goods" in Factor 6 created an analytical inconsistency. The Court noted there is no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth factor than under the second. This consistency is necessary to ensure that the presumed strength of a mark is evaluated in the same market context in which the services are offered. When the market context shifts artificially between factors, the evidentiary weight of similar marks changes.

## DuPont Factor 1: Overall Commercial Impression (Vacated due to Error in Factor 6)

As part of this same opinion, the Federal Circuit also vacated the Board's finding regarding the first DuPont factor, which considers the similarity of the marks in their entireties regarding appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The Board's determination of commercial impression was not isolated; it relied heavily on the commercial strength of the senior mark, which is directly influenced by the sixth factor. When a mark is surrounded by crowded competition, as seen in the broader banking sector, its commercial impression may become more defined by that context than when evaluated in a narrower market.

In *Apex Bank*, the opinion explains that commercial impression informs the analysis of the first factor. Because the Board blindly applied the narrow scope found irrelevant in Factor 6 to its strength analysis, and subsequently ignored those strengths in Factor 1, the commercial impression finding became susceptible to vacatur. The remand instruction explicitly noted that reconsidering the sixth factor—specifically the number and nature of similar marks—would likely result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness. Because the strength of a mark is a component of the overall commercial impression, the error in defining the crowdedness of the field under Factor 6 rendered the existing analysis under Factor 1 legally flawed. The remand serves to allow the Board to recalibrate the commercial impression based on the correct scope of marks.

## Application of Law to Fact: How the Board's Error on Factor 6 Impacted Factor 1

The vacatur of Factor 1 underscores the pedagogical importance of the "logical link" between the DuPont factors. The Court made clear that the analysis of the first factor cannot proceed in a vacuum. If one assumes that the mark is unsupported by a finding of substantial similarity in services (Factor 2) or failed to account for a broader field (Factor 6), the overall commercial impression (Factor 1) is compromised. In *Apex Bank*, the Court was unmindful of the fact that the Board's decision to exclude the 42 financial marks skewed the determination of the field's crowdedness.

Therefore, the Board's failure to recognize that the "similar goods" analysis under Factor 6 must align with the "services" analysis under Factor 2 created a chain reaction. The initial error in defining the relevant goods in Factor 6 allowed the Board to underestimate the number of competing marks, which in turn inflated the perceived strength of CC Serve's mark. This led to an inaccurate assessment of the marks' overall commercial impression in Factor 1. When the Board is required to consider the additional marks in the financial sector, the scene of the market might shift. If the scene shifts, the similarity of the commercial impression changes. Consequently, the final decision cannot be left standing without the Board correcting this foundational analytical scope. This case illustrates how procedural consistency across multiple factors ensures that the ultimate conclusion regarding likelihood of confusion is supported by a coherent evidentiary record.

# Chapter 4: Holding and Case Significance

## The Final Holding: Affirmed-In-Part, Vacated-In-Part, and Remanded
In federal appellate litigation, the final disposition of an appeal determines whether a lower tribunal's decision stands, is completely overturned, or is sent back for further proceedings. This opinion illustrates a nuanced outcome where the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit employs three distinct dispositions: affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand. When the court affirms-in-part, it indicates that specific parts of the lower tribunal's reasoning and factual findings are correct and binding. Conversely, when the court vacates-in-part, it declares those specific parts to be legally incorrect without necessarily adopting specific corrective measures. Finally, remanding dictates that the case is returned to the trimming tribunal—here, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board—to pass judgment on the returned issues. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's finding regarding the second *DuPont* factor, concluded with "affirm-in-part." However, the court vacated the findings regarding the first and sixth factors, concluding "vacate-in-part." Because the corrected legal analysis for the vacated factors might change the ultimate result, the case was sent back to the Board via remand. This ruling structure teaches law students that appellate courts do not always seek to resolve every dispute in a single hearing; sometimes, a corrected application of the law requires a new factual review by the agency below.

## What the Remand Requires: Reconsidering Third-Party Mark Scope
The primary driver for the remand in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* was the scope of evidence the Board considered regarding third-party uses of similar marks. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board had initially focused its analysis of the sixth *DuPont* factor solely on marks used "credit card services." Apex argued this was too narrow because Apex's services were defined as "banking and financing." On appeal, the Federal Circuit held that the Board made a legal error by limiting the universe of considered marks based on a narrower definition of services than it had accepted earlier in the litigation. The court reasoned that once the services for the second *DuPont* factor were defined as "highly similar" and "legally identical," the Board could not artificially restrict the evidence for the sixth factor to a subset of those services. The remand specifically orders the Board to reconsider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods, ensuring that the scope of eligible third-party marks aligns with the definition of the services established for the second factor. Practitioners must therefore ensure that the "relevant public" defined in one part of their analysis is consistent with the definitions used to search for similar marks in another.

## Precedential Implications for Defining 'Similar Services'
This decision reinforces the importance of consistency in defining "goods and services" throughout a *DuPont* likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The *DuPont* factors require an examiner or tribunal to find relevant facts regarding the parties' marks and goods. If the tribunal finds the services are similar enough in Factor 2 to find that consumers might mistake the source, it logically follows that for Factor 6 (similar marks in use on similar goods), the comparison group should not be artificially narrowed. The Federal Circuit rejected the Board's practice of applying a stricter standard for similarity when analyzing evidence for commercial strength than when defining the services themselves. For future litigation, this means that when a tribunal finds services are related, it must acknowledge all third-party uses of similar marks on those related services, not just a subset that fits a narrow dictionary definition. This prevents the "cherry-picking" of evidence that might unfairly weaken a prior user's claim to commercial strength by excluding relevant market data.

## Summary of Key Legal Principles Established
Several procedural and substantive principles emerge from this opinion that are essential for understanding trademark appeals. First, the court applied a two-tiered standard of review: *de novo* review for legal conclusions and "substantial evidence" for findings of fact. This distinction allows the appellate court to scrutinize the Board's interpretation of the *DuPont* factors without overturning factual findings unsupported by the record. Second, the opinion clarifies the Lanham Act § 2(d) requirement that a registered mark should not be confused with another mark's use. Third, the court reiterated that the *DuPont* factors are guidelines, not a rigid formula; the Board is only required to consider factors that are "relevant and of record." In this case, the critical lesson is that the "relevant public" defined for the purpose of the analysis must remain constant across all factors. The court emphasized that the Board cannot define a broad relevant public for Factor 2 (mustaches of service) and then a narrow one for Factor 6 (number of similar marks) without justification.

## Significance for Trademark Registration and Opposition Proceedings
For practitioners engaged in trademark registration or opposition proceedings, *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp* serves as a critical warning regarding the consistency of factual construction. When filing an opposition or evaluating a risk of refusal, litigants must ensure that their arguments for the similarity of services are supported by evidence that covers all related goods, not just specific endpoints. If a litigant argues that "banking" encompasses credit card services to the point of identity, they risk having all third-party marks in the banking sector counted as prior uses for the strength of the challenged mark, rather than limiting the analysis to credit card marks alone. This opinion highlights that a finding of similarity in one context can dictate the breadth of evidence admissible in another. Ultimately, the decision reminds the legal community that the strength or weakness of a mark is influenced by the entire commercial impression of the marketplace, and limiting evidence based on inconsistent definitions of the product or service offers can be reversible error.
