# The Consistency Trap: Why the TTAB Must Align Its DuPont Scope

In the cutthroat world of trademark oppositions, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) acts as the first line of defense for existing registrants seeking to block new entrants. Legal practitioners know that the likelihood-of-confusion analysis is notoriously fact-sensitive, often turning on the nuanced application of the twelve DuPont factors. However, recently, the Federal Circuit signaled that internal consistency within that analysis might be as important as the individual factors themselves. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit has identified a critical procedural flaw: the TTAB’s tendency to define similarity narrowly to avoid a finding of crowdedness, then flip that definition to find identity in the next step. This sends a clear message to the Board and practitioners alike: you cannot play which-way street with the scope of your applicable market.

To understand the significance of *Apex Bank*, one must first understand the competitive landscape at play. CC Serve, a servicing company for various banks, held an early registration for the term "ASPIRE" in connection with credit card services dating back to 1998. Apex Bank, a retail bank without actual credit card services at the time, filed an intent-to-use application to use "ASPIRE BANK" for broader banking and financing services. Through an opposition proceeding, CC Serve argued that the marks were confusing, relying on the Tribunal’s rejection of the application. While the Board upheld the opposition, the Federal Circuit found reversible error in how they applied the factors where they weighed the services against prior owners and competing marks.

The crux of the Federal Circuit’s decision lies in the relationship between the Second and Sixth DuPont factors. The Second factor asks whether the parties’ goods and services are related, allowing for some flexibility beyond identity. In *Apex Bank*, the Board agreed that credit card services and banking services were essentially identical in legal terms, concluding that the Second factor heavily weighted finding a likelihood of confusion. However, when the Board turned to the Sixth DuPont factor—which examines the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods—they did the exact opposite. The Board restricted the universe of "similar goods" to only credit card services, excluding other banking and finance marks that use the word "Aspire" because Apex’s registration used the broader descriptor "banking and financing."

This inconsistency is where the legal error becomes evident. The Federal Circuit pointed out that if services are found to be "highly similar" under the Second factor, the relevant public must remain the same in the subsequent analysis. The court reasoned that when a Board member defines "services" as "legally identical" in one part of the opinion, they cannot later shrink that definition to exclude relevant comparisons in another. The court noted that the Sixth factor does not require identical goods, only similar ones, and since Apex and CC Serve's services were already found to be highly similar, the Board was legally bound to look at a broader range of Aspire-formative marks across the financial sector than it had previously specified as the commercial universe.

Critically, this isn't just a technicality in the abstract sense; it has downstream implications for trademark registration strategies. Historically, applicants have struggled to counter oppositions wherein the Board expands the relevant market to find infringement rights tailored to specific service needs at trial. By vacating the Sixth and First DuPont factors, the Federal Circuit has imposed a procedural constraint that forces the TTAB to maintain doctrinal consistency throughout the analysis. If the Second factor sets a broad standard for market identity, the Sixth factor cannot arbitrarily exclude potential confusion by narrowing the market too early. The court’s decision suggests that an inconsistent definition of similarity across the DuPont factors is not merely a policy preference but a substantive legal error under the Lanham Act.

From a practical standpoint, this ruling provides a remedy for the confusion often found in Board opinions where service definitions shift between factors. For applicants facing opposition, it argues that they should frame their distinction in the Second factor with the knowledge that the Board cannot retreat to a narrower definition later. Conversely, existing registrants like CC Serve must recognize that their commercial strength is not defined by a shrinking universe where third parties are quieted to protect their controls. The Federal Circuit’s clarification emphasizes that likelihood of confusion is a holistic inquiry where the relevance of competing marks should logically follow the initial service comparison.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank* serves as a reminder that trademark law requires internal coherence. While the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding regarding service similarity, it vacated the broader determinations on mark similarity and market crowdedness. This partial affirmance, partial vacatur approach ensures that the final judgment respects the factual findings while correcting the legal framework. As the TTAB considers more complex service landscapes, courts will likely look to this precedent to ensure that one factor does not inadvertently defeat another through inconsistent definitions. In the battle for brand identity, the rules of engagement must remain level.



# Banking or Credit Cards? How Dictionary Definitions Expand Trademark Protection

In the crowded marketplace of financial services, the boundary between a "bank" and a "credit card processor" can be perilously thin for trademark lawyers. The Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* illuminates this tension while reaffirming a pivotal role for lexicography in intellectual property law. The case centers on whether an established provider of credit card servicing (CC Serve) can block a competing retail bank (Apex Bank) from using the word "ASPIRE" in connection with its new banking services. The court’s mixed ruling—affirming the Board’s finding that the services are sufficiently related while vacating its analysis on the number of competing marks—highlights the difficulty of defining market niches in the fintech era and the consequences when those definitions are stretched to the breaking point.

## The Dictionary Problem: Expanding the Second Factor

The core of the dissension lies in the Second DuPont Factor, which asks whether the parties’ goods and services are related. In this case, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) concluded that CC Serve’s "credit card services" and Apex Bank’s "banking and financing services" were so similar that they likely emanated from a single source. The Board’s primary tool for this highly technical assessment was the dictionary. As the Federal Circuit noted in its affirmance, the examiner deleted the specific entry for "credit card services" from the Trademark ID Manual long before CC Serve registered, yet the Board turned to dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance" to fill the gap.

The court found substantial evidence that "banking" encompasses the issuance of credit cards and provided funds. Consequently, the Board described the services as "legally identical, in part." This interpretation is not merely technical; it fundamentally dictates the market into which a party might enter. By deferring to these broad linguistic definitions, the Federal Circuit accepted the Board’s view that a retail bank offering mortgages and loans is inherently operating in the same relevant universe as a firm processing credit card transactions. While this prevents consumer confusion where the service overlaps—like extending credit—it casts a wide net. This ruling suggests that new entrants seeking to carve out distinct market niches must carefully anticipate that the plaintiffs’ definition of "banking" will swallow their proposed defense of distinct goods, barring them from using similar marks unless they can prove a clearer separation.

## The Error of Scope: Restricting the Sixth Factor

While the court was satisfied that the services were similar enough to trigger a likelihood of confusion, it identified a fatal flaw in how the Board applied the Sixth DuPont Factor. This factor measures the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. The Board had limited its inquiry only to third-party registrations covering "credit card services." As the Federal Circuit shook down, the Board ignored marks using "Aspire" or similar terms connected to broader "banking and financing" services, deeming them "essentially irrelevant."

The appellate court’s vacatur here corrects a logic error that could grant excessive protection to weak marks. The Sixth Factor is designed to show whether a mark’s strength is diluted by competition. By narrowing the relevant public to just "consumers of credit card services," the Board artificially insulated CC Serve’s "ASPIRE" mark from the reality of a crowded generic field. The Federal Circuit held that if the Second Factor found the services to be highly similar, the Sixth Factor must review the universe of marks relevant to *that* similarity, not just the credit card subset. This correction is a significant procedural safeguard: it forces landscape analyses to be consistent. It prevents a defendant from winning on a narrow factual definition that contradicts their broader jersey strategy under the same copyright mark.

This decision leaves a lasting imprint on how IP practitioners approach Lanham Act proceedings, particularly in high-density sectors like fintech. For applicants, it serves as a warning that examiners and tribunals will apply dictionary definitions aggressively, potentially locking broad industry descriptions onto specific competitors.

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit’s handling of the First DuPont Factor signals a domino effect. Because the definition of a mark’s commercial strength affects whether consumers perceive the overall marks as confusing, the remand for the First Factor aligns the analysis of visual appearance with the flawed market scope. This suggests that trademark disputes will increasingly hinge on whether the relevant market was defined too narrowly. While the court held that CC Serve’s mark received "normal scope of protection" and was not weak enough to trigger a narrow ban, the process of reappraisal could ultimately tip the balance in favor of Apex. Ultimately, *Apex Bank* serves as a reminder that semantic precision in trademark law matters, but overreach in that precision risks invalidating the broader strength arguments necessary to defend your marks.



# Eroding Distinctiveness in a Crowded Field: The Standard for Commercial Strength

In the high-stakes world of trademark registration, the Battle of "Aspire" in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* offers a compelling lesson on how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board applies the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. While the Federal Circuit has long treated the DuPont factors as a flexible checklist, the court’s September 25, 2025, decision in this appeal provides a critical correction to how those factors relate to one another. Specifically, the panel, led by Circuit Judge Hughes, emphasized that excluding broader third-party usage in related sectors undermines the legal threshold needed to prove a mark’s inherent distinctiveness, effectively narrowing the scope of prior user protection when it should be broadening it.

For practitioners, the significance of *Apex Bank* lies in the fragmentation of the relevant market during opposition proceedings. The Board has historically managed oppositions by defining the relevant public and the scope of goods on a case-by-case basis. In this instance, the Board repeatedly defined the relevant services around the specific "credit card" label. CC Serve, the prior user, owns the "ASPIRE" mark for credit card services since 1998. Apex Bank, a regional bank planning to launch an internet banking service under "ASPIRE BANK," alleged confusion. The Board found confusion likely under the second DuPont factor, determining that the parties' services were "legally identical, in part." However, in analyzing the sixth factor—which assesses confusingly similar marks in use on similar goods—the Board allowed the definition of similarity to shrink from "highly similar" banking services down to just "credit card services."

This analytical inconsistency created a problem for DuPont Factor VI. The sixth factor is used to determine whether a field is crowded with similar marks. If the field is crowded, the mark is considered weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection; if the field is "considerably" or "ubiquitously" populated, it hurtens the new applicant less. The Board found nine relevant third-party marks for credit cards and decided that was not "voluminous" enough to impair CC Serve's standing. Apex argued the Board should ignore the four dozen other "Aspire" marks filed for general banking services. The court ultimately sided with Apex, vacating the Board's finding because the definition of "similar goods" must remain consistent across factors. If the services are legally identical for the purpose of confusion (Factor 2), they must remain functionally similar for the purpose of third-party dilution (Factor 6). By artificially subdividing the market, the Board implicitly insulated CC Serve from a crowded landscape that would otherwise weaken the commercial strength of the "ASPIRE" mark.

## The Fracture in the DuPont Formula

The Federal Circuit's reasoning underscores a recurring litigator's nightmare: the "moving target" of the relevant public. When the Board reviews the first, second, and sixth factors, it implicitly makes a finding about what the marketplace looks like. The court noted that the second factor, dealing with the similarity of services, was substantial. The Board held that Apex's banking services and CC Serve's credit card services were so similar that they could easily emanate from a single source. Yet, when facing the third-party marks analysis, the Board acted as if the universe of relevant services was much smaller, excluding the broader banking registrations from the count.

This inconsistency was the crux of the error. The court looked to precedent regarding *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*, where the Board found "voluminous evidence" of third-party registration to have established commercial weakness. Here, the Board admitted CC Serve's mark was entitled to the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." In a crowded field of Aspire-formative marks across the financial services industry, one would expect that scope to be narrower or subject to scrutiny regarding the propensity for confusion. By restricting the scope to only credit card services, the Board allowed CC Serve to benefit from a falsely pristine marketplace definition. The vacatur of the first and sixth factors sends a clear message to the PTTO Board: you cannot analyze the distinctiveness of a mark in isolation without ensuring your view on the field of goods is consistent from start to finish.

## Implications for Establishing Distinctiveness

The doctrinal ripple effect of *Apex Bank* is likely to be felt in high-volume opposition cases involving generic or descriptive marks like "Aspire." Trademark law recognizes that a term can lose strength if many others use similar marks to similar consumers. This case clarifies that evidence of that widespread use cannot be selectively excluded based on technical semantic distinctions that don't hold up logically. If the Board concludes that consumers see credit card services as part of banking (Factor 2), it cannot simultaneously say that consumers in the "other banking services" category are irrelevant for the purpose of establishing mark strength (Factor 6).

For the policy perspective, this approach prioritizes consumer expectation over rigid internal consistency of the applicant's definition. However, the court's opinion also tempers the first DuPont factor regarding commercial impression. By vacating the entire analysis that relies on the Board's narrow view of the third-party market, the court ensures that a mark's visual strength is not judged without the proper context of industry saturation. If the court were to remand and the Board reintegrates the 42+ other banking registrations, the texture of the "ASPIRE" marks might matter less. In a truly crowded field, even distinct designs may struggle to prove exclusivity.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* serves as a reminder that the DuPont factors are not siloed mathematical equations but interlocking components of a single legal standard. The Federal Circuit’s remand returns the burden of proof to the Board to justify why a third party should benefit from a narrower world definition than the plaintiff. As trademark law evolves, ensuring these factors do not fracture in ways that artificially proscribe prior rights or erroneously forbid new ones remains a central pillar for the Federal Circuit's developing jurisprudence.
