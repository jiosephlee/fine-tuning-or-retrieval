Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why did the court vacate the First DuPont factor analysis simply because of an error in the Sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
The First DuPont factor looks at the similarity of the marks (appearance, sound, connotation). The Sixth DuPont factor looks at crowded markets. The court says 'Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board's analysis here.' Can someone explain the doctrinal logic here? Does the presence of too many similar marks (Sixth Factor) automatically mean a mark has a 'weak commercial impression' (First Factor)? How is that causal link established?
Answer:
The court vacated the Board’s analysis of the First DuPont Factor because its determination regarding the "commercial impression" of the mark was dependent on the Board's proper evaluation of the Sixth Factor.

To understand the doctrinal logic, you must look at how the Sixth Factor feeds into the First Factor, specifically regarding the mark's "strength."

**The Doctrinal Link: Commercial Strength vs. Commercial Impression**
Under the *DuPont* factors, the Sixth Factor assesses the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." The relevant legal principle (citing *Palm Bay Imps.* and *Juice Generation* in the opinion) is that evidence of many similar marks may indicate that a mark is "weak" and entitled only to a narrow scope of protection. Conversely, a crowded field suggests customers are adept at distinguishing marks, which supports a finding of weakness.

However, the First Factor assesses the "similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties... as to commercial impression." This analysis requires an assessment of the mark's overall distinctiveness and the likely confusion to consumers.

**The Court's Reasoning**
In this opinion, the Board made an error in the Sixth Factor by defining the universe of "similar goods" too narrowly. While analyzing the Second Factor, the Board found the parties' services were "highly similar" or even "legally identical." Yet, when analyzing the Sixth Factor, the Board excluded marks for broader banking/financing services from the count because they did not explicitly match "credit card services."

The court held that the Board should have retained the same broad scope of similarity found under the Second Factor. The causal link the opinion describes is as follows:

1.  **Scope of Goods:** A broader definition of "similar goods" (under the Second Factor error acknowledged by the court) allows for a broader search for competing marks (Sixth Factor).
2.  **Commercial Strength:** The Ninth Circuit opinion states that the Sixth Factor may result in a "different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness."
3.  **Commercial Impression:** The First Factor focuses on the "overall commercial impression." The opinion explicitly states that commercial impression is informed by the strength of the mark.

Therefore, the court did not find that a crowded market *automatically* creates a weak commercial impression. Rather, the court recognized that you cannot accurately evaluate the mark’s **commercial strength** (which informs the First Factor's impression) without first evaluating the correct scope of the crowded field. Since the Board’s error in the Sixth Factor (the scope of goods compared) undermined the accuracy of assessing the mark's strength, the First Factor analysis—which relies on that strength to determine commercial impression—must also be vacated for reconsideration.

As the opinion puts it in Section III.C: "consideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."
