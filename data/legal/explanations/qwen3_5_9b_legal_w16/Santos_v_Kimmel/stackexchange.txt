Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Does a 'reasonable observer' standard apply when determining the 'purpose and character' factor of Fair Use, or does the user's subjective intent matter?
Question:
I'm reading *Santos v. Kimmel*, where the Second Circuit relies on *Andy Warhol Found. Inc. v. Goldsmith* to dismiss Santos's copyright claim. Kimmel argues he instigated the creation of the videos, implying a personal purpose, but the court dismisses this as 'subjective intent.' Why is the objective 'reasonable observer' standard the controlling metric for transformative use in the Second Circuit, and does this show I need to rethink how I apply the first fair use factor in case briefs?
Answer:
Yes, the Second Circuit in *Santos* confirms that the "reasonable observer" standard controls the analysis of the first fair use factor, while the subjective intent of the user is largely irrelevant. This is a crucial distinction for drafting case briefs, as it directs your analysis away from the internal motivations of the author and toward the public understanding of the work.

### The Reasonable Observer Standard Controls Transformativeness

Under the Copyright Act, the first factor analyzes whether a secondary use is transformative. As *Santos* reiterates following *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, the inquiry determines "whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." *Santos* at 4.

The decision explicitly rejects the argument that the user's internal knowledge or instigation of the creation dictates the nature of the use. Kimmel argued that because he "instigated the creation of the original Works," he was the "designer[] of their original purpose." *Santos* at 4. The Court dismissed this focus on Kimmel's subjective intent. Instead, the Court held:

> "Whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user." *Santos* at 5 (quoting *Andy Warhol* 598 U.S. at 544).

Because a reasonable observer would view the *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* segments as mockery designed to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money, rather than as a continuation of his original "videos of hope and strength," the use was found to be transformative under the objective perceived purpose.

### Practical Implications for Case Briefs

In briefing or writing argument, do not anchor the first fair use factor to the "bad faith" or strategic design of the defendant. While *Santos* acknowledges the Supreme Court's past statement in *Harper & Row* regarding the importance of "good faith," it notes that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself from this statement." *Santos* at 5.

Instead, ground your brief in the external perception of the work:
1.  **Audit Purpose:** Ask what a reasonable member of the audience receives from the new work.
2.  **Compare Character:** Determine if the secondary work adds a "new expression, meaning, or message" distinct from the original, regardless of how the original was prompted.
3.  **Ignore Subjective Design:** Discard arguments regarding the defendant's internal motivations (e.g., instigating creation) if they do not alter the *resultant* character of the work as perceived by the public.

As the Second Circuit noted in reviewing the complaint, "we only need to consider 'the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue' to decide 'the question of fair use.'" *Santos* at 5 (quoting *Cariou*). If the objective output creates a "lip service parody or social commentary" unavailable in the original, your analysis should highlight that external difference.

### Can bad faith or deceptive conduct, like using fictitious names on a service, negate a Fair Use defense?
Question:
The opinion mentions that Santos's complaint contains depictions of 'Kimmel's false representations' but notes the Supreme Court has 'distanced itself' from *Harper & Row*. However, *Harper & Row* held fair use 'presupposes good faith and fair dealing.' In a real-world litigation scenario, if the mock user used fake identities (Cameo platform), I would think this bad faith should be fatal to the claim. Did the Second Circuit's reasoning here overturn the traditional rule or simply limit the scope of what constitutes bad faith nullifying the defense?
Answer:
**Title: No, prospective bad faith or deceptive procurement does not negate fair use under *Santos v. Kimmel***

**Answer:**

Generally, deceptive conduct does not negate a fair use defense under current Second Circuit precedent, as established in *Santos*. While *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.* famously held that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing," the governing rule in *Santos* is that the Supreme Court has since "distanced itself from this statement," and bad faith allegations do not automatically nullify the defense at the motion to dismiss stage.

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit specifically addressed the Appellant's (Santos) argument regarding fictitious representations. The court stated:

> "Santos also contends that Kimmel’s false representations demonstrate bad faith and thus nullify the fair use defense. See *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.*, 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We disagree. It is true that the Supreme Court stated in *Harper & Row* that '[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.' Id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted). But the Court has since distanced itself from this statement."

**The Legal Doctrine**
The Second Circuit maintains that fair use is a "flexible concept" rooted in the transformative nature of the use rather than the subjective intent or honesty of the defendant's procurement methods. The court reasoned that the inquiry focuses on "the degree to which the use is transformative" and the effect on the market, not whether the defendant sought the material through deception. The opinion notes that to nullify fair use based on bad faith, a plaintiff must show an intent to "supplant" the original work's commercially valuable right, rather than simply to use it for criticism or commentary.

**Application to Facts**
In this specific scenario involving the Cameo platform, the Second Circuit did not apply a strict *Harper & Row* bad faith bar. Instead, the analysis focused on the fact that "the complaint paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary." Consequently, the court concluded that the first factor of fair use "strongly supports a finding of fair use" despite the alleged deceptive representations.

**Conclusion**
Mere bad faith or deceptive conduct (such as using fictitious names) does not automatically vitiate a fair use defense. The Second Circuit limits the scope of the *Harper & Row* good faith requirement, reserving "nullification" of the defense for cases where the primary purpose is to usurp the market for the original work, rather than to critique it. Therefore, the conclusion that "fair use bars Santos’s copyright claims even at the pleading stage" remains correct based on the analysis of transformation and market harm, not the procedural mechanisms used to obtain the video.

### How does the Second Circuit apply 'market usurpation' versus 'market devaluation' when deciding Factor 4 (Market Effect)?
Question:
In the fair use discussion, the court distinguishes 'devaluing' the first work through criticism from 'usurping' the market by offering a competing substitute. It quotes *Hachette* saying 'unlikelihood that creators... will license critical reviews... removes such uses from... potential licensing market.' Why does this distinction matter for a quoting satire where the secondary creator explicitly offers a different purpose? Is this relevant when the plaintiff argues the videos might still have commercial value?
Answer:
**Re: How does the Second Circuit apply 'market usurpation' versus 'market devaluation' when deciding Factor 4?**

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2d Cir. (2025), the Second Circuit clarifies the Fourth Factor of the fair use analysis by establishing a sharp distinction between "market usurpation" and "market devaluation." The Court explicitly states that the inquiry focuses on substitution rather than damage through criticism:

> "We ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute."  
> *Santos*, at 189 (quoting *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163, 189 (2d Cir. 2024)).

This distinction dictates why the secondary creator’s purpose is critical to determining market harm. By adopting the reasoning from *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, the *Santos* Court notes that "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market."

Consequently, this distinction protects creative works that offer a "different purpose" or transformative commentary. Even if the original worked (in this case, the personalized videos) possesses "commercially valuable right, in the videos," *Santos* indicates that authors cannot claim infringement simply because the second work has potential market value, provided it does not serve as a substitute. As the *Santos* Court observed, plaintiff Santos “has not suffered market harm within the meaning of the fourth fair use factor” because the use did not function as a competing substitute but rather operated within the realm of criticism and commentary. Thus, for a supervisor like Jimmy Kimmel acting as a secondary creator, the argument that the videos have commercial value is irrelevant if the purpose was transformative commentary, as it falls outside the potential licensing market the plaintiff can claim.

### Why did the Second Circuit avoid resolving the Preemption issue regarding Santos's state law claims?
Question:
Santos argued his contract claims were not preempted by the Copyright Act, whereas the court said they 'failed to state a claim.' The opinion states: 'We need not resolve this issue.' Procedurally, does this suggest that if a state law claim fails under state pleading standards, federal preemption is not a viable path of appeal? If I have a case where preemption is the defense, does failing to plead the contract effectively waive the need to analyze § 301(a) of the Copyright Act?
Answer:
Based on the Second Circuit’s opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 WL 8350679, the court avoided the federal preemption issue because the state law claims were independently insufficient under state pleading standards. Procedurally, if a claim fails under state law, affirming on those grounds renders a federal preemption analysis unnecessary for the desired result. Consequently, Santos’s failure to plead contract terms effectively rendered the preemption issue moot in this specific appeal.

The Second Circuit explicitly grounded its decision in judicial economy. The opinion states: "We need not resolve this issue [preemption], however, because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law." The appellate court determined that because the district court correctly applied state law to dismiss the claims, it "had no need to decide whether either claim would have survived on the merits if not for the federal standard." 

To reach this conclusion, the court analyzed the specific state pleadings required. First, regarding breach of contract, the court found Santos failed to allege he was a beneficiary of the Terms of Service, noting Illinois law's presumption against benefits to noncontracting third parties and New York law's requirements on "meeting of minds." Second, regarding fraudulent inducement, the court held Santos failed to plead the essential element of "out-of-pocket loss" as required under New York law. 

In your hypothetical scenario, if your client proposed preemption as an affirmative defense but the underlying failure to state a state law claim is dispositive, the plaintiff does not "waive" the defense to § 301(a); rather, the appellate court may elect to decide the case on state-law grounds alone. As articulated in *Santos*, if the state law claims "fail to state a claim for relief," the federal preemption question becomes "unnecessary to decide." Thus, a failure to plead the substantive elements of a contract may deprive the court of the obligation to analyze the federal preemption question, as the claim cannot proceed regardless of its federal viability.

### Under what circumstances can a party enforce a Terms of Service against a non-party user via 'express declaration'?
Question:
Santos claims a breach of the Cameo Terms of Service but notes he wasn't a party to it. The court cites *Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp.* and New York law which requires an 'express declaration' to allow non-contracting third parties to benefit. Even though the Terms apply to Kimmel, does the absolute requirement for an 'express declaration' apply strictly when invoking Illinois law or New York law in a contract dispute regarding platform terms? Does this mean users without accounts can rarely sue under ToS?
Answer:
Generally, a party cannot enforce a Terms of Service (ToS) against a non-signatory based on ToS provisions unless they fall within a specific exception that overcomes the presumption against third-party rights. As the Second Circuit explains in *Santos v. Kimmel, 24-2196 (2d Cir. 2025)*, this enforcement is severely limited when the plaintiff is not a party to the agreement, even if the defendant is bound by it.

### The Barriers to Third-Party Enforcement

The court identifies two primary doctrinal hurdles based on the cited state laws:

**1. The "Express Declaration" Standard (Illinois Law)**
Under the Illinois law governing the ToS interpretation in *Santos*, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties" (*Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp.*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016)). To enforce a covenant on a non-party, the document must contain language explicitly benefiting the third party.
*   **Specific Requirement:** The court notes that the implication must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration" (*Barry*, id., 976).
*   **Application:** The opinion states that while other provisions of the ToS did contain third-party beneficiary language, Santos's complaint failed to identify an "express declaration" that the specific provisions he alleged were breached applied to him. Without a clear intent to confer that specific benefit directly to a non-party like Santos, standing is lost.

**2. Implied Contract Requirements (New York Law)**
For claims based on implied contracts under New York law, the standard is equally strict. The court looks for "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to determine if an implied-in-fact contract existed (*Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc.*, 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep't 2002)).
*   **Essential Terms:** Furthermore, a complaint must "allege... the essential terms of the parties' . . . contract" (*Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.*, 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep't 1994)).
*   **Meeting of Minds:** There must be evidence of an "indication of a meeting of minds... constituting an agreement" that the specific obligations (like adhering to the ToS) fell on the party sued (*DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass'n Compliance & Legal Div.*, 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep't 2008)).
*   **Application:** Santos's complaint failed to plausibly allege that there was a mutual agreement that Kimmel would adhere to the ToS in a way that benefited Santos.

### Conclusion on Enforceability

Can a party enforce a ToS without privity? Extremely rarely. *Santos v. Kimmel* confirms that a third party can only sue for a breach of contract binding the other party (like Kimmel) if the contract explicitly intended to acknowledge the plaintiff's existence as a beneficiary or establish a direct meeting of minds on terms intended to benefit them.

Does this mean users without accounts (like Santos, the creator, or a user without a direct agreement with the platform) can rarely sue? The logic suggests yes. Unless the ToS contains "other provisions... contain[ing] third-party beneficiary language" directly applicable to you (*Santos*), you are unlikely to state a claim for breach of that ToS against a third-party user. The court emphasizes that without express third-party intent or a distinct implied agreement, the non-party is barred from enforcing the contract to which they are not a signatory.

### Why does New York law require an allegation of 'out-of-pocket loss' for a fraudulent inducement claim?
Question:
The court dismisses Santos's fraudulent inducement claim because he 'failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss.' I know fraud generally requires harm, but I thought fraudulent inducement might just need to show reliance plus damages. I am specifically confused if the court is requiring 'out-of-pocket loss' as a distinct element for New York fraudulent inducement, rather than just general compensatory damages or relational damages.
Answer:
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Santos's fraudulent inducement claim because New York law treats an "out-of-pocket loss" as an essential element of that specific cause of action. While general common law fraud often requires proof of reliance and damages resulting from that reliance, New York jurisprudence—as referenced in the opinion—specifically requires the plaintiff to allege actual out-of-pocket loss to state a valid claim for fraudulent inducement.

### The Essential Element of Out-of-Pocket Loss
Under New York state law, a fraudulent inducement claim is distinct from other fraud-related claims in that it necessitates proof of pecuniary harm defined as an out-of-pocket loss. The Second Circuit explicitly notes that Santos challenged the District Court’s dismissal on the grounds that he was required only to allege general damages, but the appellate court rejected this.

The opinion states:
> "Finally, Santos challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege any out-of-pocket loss as required under New York law. See *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We agree with the District Court’s conclusion that Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud. See *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017)."

This holding clarifies that for a fraudulent inducement claim to survive a motion to dismiss under New York law, the complaint must plead a specific type of injury—actual financial loss resulting from the fraud—rather than generalized relational or non-monetary damages.

### Application to the Case
The District Court had previously dismissed Santos's claim, concluding he "failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss." The Second Circuit agreed with the lower court, citing the precedent set out in *Lama Holding Co.* and *Connaughton*.

Because Santos’s complaint did not plead this specific requirement—he focused on the defendants' deceptive conduct rather than the corresponding financial loss he endured—the court concluded that his complaint failed to state a claim for relief. Thus, the requirement is not merely an evidentiary hurdle for calculating damages after the fact, but a substantive pleading element required to establish the cause of action under New York law.

### Conclusion
The requirement for an "out-of-pocket loss" is a pleading threshold imposed by New York state court decisions that define the boundaries of fraudulent inducement. As noted in *Santos v. Kimmel*, failure to allege this specific element renders the claim legally insufficient under the governing state law, even if general reliance or other potential harms exist.

### How does the pleading standard under Rule 12(b)(6) apply to complex fair use defenses like 'devaluation' vs. 'superseding'?
Question:
The opinion cites *TCA Television Corp.* saying fair use can be 'so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal.' Given that fair use factors are flexible and vary by context (*Andy Warhol*, *Hachette*), how does a judge in the 2nd Circuit draw the line between a 'plausibly argued defense' and a claim 'so clearly established' that it bars the suit at the 12(b)(6) stage, especially when market harm is objectively difficult to prove?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the line between a "plausibly argued defense" and one "so clearly established" for dismissal at the 12(b)(6) stage in fair use cases is drawn by evaluating whether the specific facts alleged in the complaint—read in the light most favorable to the defendant—demonstrate that the secondary work falls into a category of use that inherently negates market harm or lacks a transformative purpose, regardless of the flexible nature of the factors.

**The "Clearly Established" Standard for Fair Use**

In *Santos*, the Second Circuit clarified that the flexible nature of fair use does not preclude a motion to dismiss. The court relied on *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum* to establish that fair use can be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage. To survive a 12(b)(6) challenge, a plaintiff cannot assert a fair use defense; rather, the plaintiff must allege facts that would prevent the court from concluding that no reasonable jury could find the use was fair. In *Santos*, the court concluded that the complaint failed to allege sufficient market harm because the description of the use was so clearly commentary/criticism that the defense was barred without trial.

**Distinguishing "Devaluation" from "Superseding" in Market Harm Analysis**

Your question specifically concerns the market harm factor. While fair use is context-dependent, *Santos* applies a specific line of authority from *Hachette* to resolve the "plausibility" threshold for market harm. The court explicitly distinguishes between uses that merely **devalue** a work and uses that **usurp** the market for it:

> "We ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." (*Santos*, quoting *Hachette Book Grp.*)

For a plaintiff to state a claim for infringement, they must allege facts showing the use usurps the market. If the complaint shows the defendant created the work for criticism or parody (as Kimmel allegedly did to "mock" Santos), the defense becomes "clearly established" because, per *Campbell* (cited in *Santos*), the unlikelihood of creators licensing critical reviews removes such uses from the "very notion of a potential licensing market."

**Application to the Facts**

The District Court in *Santos* granted the motion to dismiss because the complaint's own allegations undercut the market harm claim. The court noted the plaintiff did not dispute that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was to "comment on the willingness of Santos... to say absurd things for money" (citation omitted). Where the complaint establishes the work is criticism rather than competition, the flexible "devaluation" concern is legally excluded from the market harm analysis.

Furthermore, the Second Circuit in *Santos* emphasized that the determination could be made on the face of the complaint and basic public documents:
> "Because we need only consider 'the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue' to decide 'the question of fair use,'" (*Cariou*, cited in *Santos*).

**Conclusion**

Therefore, as illustrated in *Santos*, the line is drawn when the complaint’s factual predicates—specifically the purpose and character of the use—align so clearly with established precedent (that criticism does not require licensing) that the flexible fair use factors actually harden into a bar to liability. If the defendant is a critic and the use does not offer a substitute for the original work, the "devaluation" through criticism does not count as market harm, rendering the infringement claim untenable at the pleading stage.
