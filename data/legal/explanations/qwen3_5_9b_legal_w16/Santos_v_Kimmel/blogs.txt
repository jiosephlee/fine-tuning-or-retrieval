Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## Reasonable Observer Overload: How *Santos* Nails Fair Use to the Objective Standard

* By [Your Name] | October 10, 2025

The tension between copyright protection and the robustness of parody remains one of the most fraught terrains in intellectual property law. In recent years, the Supreme Court’s re-engagement with fair use in *Authors Guild v. Google* and *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* has sought to balance commercial interests with free expression, but often by elevating the "transformative" nature of a work as the primary metric. Recently, the Second Circuit joined this conversation with a significant ruling in *Santos v. Kimmel*, one that reinforces an objective standard for fair use analysis while effectively dismantling the notion that a creator’s subjective malice or intent can override a transformative defense. In this case, former Congressman George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel and American Broadcasting Companies claiming copyright infringement and state law violations after Kimmel utilized personalized, positive-toned requests from the Cameo platform to construct satirical segments mocking Santos on his late-night show. The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Santos’s complaint on both primary, intellectual property and state law grounds, grounding its copyright analysis in a "reasonable observer" standard that makes the "subjective intent" of the parties largely irrelevant to fairness.

The crux of the controversy in *Santos* lay in the "purpose and character" of the use. Courts evaluate whether a secondary use transforms the original work or merely supersedes it to assess fair use. Here, Kimmel’s argument—or conversely, Santos’s counter-argument—touched upon the murky waters of who was the true designer of the content. The opinion details how Santos challenged the transformative nature of Kimmel’s segments, arguing they did not transform his videos but served Kimmel's commercial purpose of "mocking" him. However, Lohier, sitting as Circuit Judge, zeroed in on the distinction between the "subjective intent of the user" and the observable reality of the work itself. Drawing heavily on the 2023 *Andy Warhol* decision, the court held that transformative quality is a question not of "subjective intent," but of what a reasonable observer perceives. Historically, creators might have argued that if they commissioned the creation of a work, they could claim sole credit for its purpose. *Santos* clarifies that even if Kimmel instigated the request, the commercial value of the original videos and the satire of the new segments cannot be judged by the internal motives of the creator or the hostile means of the defendant.

Perhaps the most analytically fascinating portion of the opinion involves the treatment of "bad faith." In *Harper & Row*, the Supreme Court indicated that fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing. The Second Circuit acknowledged this principle theoretically but noted that the Court had since distanced itself from the strictness of this lineage. In this instance, Santos argued that Kimmel’s deceptive methods—using a fictitious persona to solicit the videos—constituted bad faith that should nullify any fair use protection. Lohier succinctly rejected this, noting that the complaint paints a picture of "sarcastic criticism and commentary" rather than an intent to supplant the market. This decision is significant because it signals to litigants that the "how" is less important than the "what" in fair use inquiries. While deception might be relevant to state law claims or actual damages later, it does not bar a finding of fair use at the pleading stage if the work objectively serves a different purpose than the original.

We must also look at the state law claims, as the court addressed them briefly but definitively. Santos brought claims for breach of contract and fraudulent inducement, relying on the Cameo Terms of Service. The court dismissed the contract claims for failing to state a claim for relief under both Illinois and New York law, noting the lack of a "meeting of minds" that would bind third parties to the original platform's terms. It is telling that the court did not need to resolve whether the Copyright Act preempts these claims because the state law claims were factually insufficient to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. This procedural efficiency underscores the Second Circuit’s preference for resolving cases on the merits where possible, though here the proceduralism cleared the way for the fair use analysis to dominate the discussion.

**Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects**

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Andy Warhol* has profound downstream effects for how media litigation will be framed. By tethering the transformative inquiry to a "reasonable observer" test rather than subjective intent, the court effectively closes a loophole that might allow creators to argue that they "owned" the purpose of a derivative work they solicited. For media defendants, this alleviates some anxiety about perceived deception in the production process; a critic or parody artist is not barred from fair use defense because they tricked a creator into making the content. For creators, however, it suggests that the integrity of their initial work's value is quite distinct from what happens later to that work in the public sphere.

Furthermore, the court’s treatment of market harm reinforces the principle that modification and parody do not need to compete commercially with the original to qualify as fair use. The Second Circuit reasoned that there is no market for a creator to license a license to a criticism or lampoon of their own productions. Willingness to license does not equal market value, a point the court hammered home by citing *Hachette* and *Campbell*. This aligns with the broader Copyright Act’s goal of encouraging creativity, not locking it behind a fortress of exclusive control that stifles commentary.

In summary, *Santos v. Kimmel* offers a crisp, if unsentimental, look at how copyright law navigates the collision between commerce and speech. By grounding the transformative analysis in objective reality rather than the subjective desires of the parties, the Second Circuit has provided a secure foothold for fair use arguments in satire cases. It reminds us that while the Producer might steal the spotlight on the intent of creation, the copyright is often just to the first shot, not the whole film.



# Killing the 'Good Faith' Guardrail: The End of *Harper & Row* as a Hard Constraint

## The *Harper & Row* Moment

The intersection of celebrity life, satire, and intellectual property law often produces winners when courts are willing to look at transformation rather than technicality. However, hybrid cases like *Santos v. Kimmel* reveal deeper fissures in the affirmative defenses available to First Amendment actors. When George Santos appealed his copyright infringement suit against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC, he hoped to leverage the "petty" nature of the content to the court's view of the damages, but he faced a stronger wall: the possibility that his original videos were simply fair game for commentary. The Second Circuit’s decision on October 6, 2025, is particularly notable not just for affirming a dismissal, but for how explicitly it treated the "good faith" requirement of fair use as manageable rather than mandatory. This ruling effectively signals that ethical misconduct by the user of the derivative work does not automatically vitiate the protection of fair use, even when that user deceived the original copyright holder to create the content in the first place.

## Transformation Over Malice

A significant portion of the opinion addresses Santos's contention that Kimmel's deceptive behavior regarding the Cameo platform negated the fair use defense. Specifically, Santos argued that Kimmel's "false representations" demonstrated bad faith, which he claimed could nullify the doctrine. This argument rests on the Supreme Court's language in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, which stated that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." Judge Lohier, writing for the Second Circuit, acknowledges the *Harper & Row* holding but pivots on the subsequent trajectory of copyright jurisprudence. The court notes that the Supreme Court has since "distanced itself" from that particular statement.

This move is critical because it deprioritizes the subjective intent of the secondary user in favor of an objective evaluation of the work's purpose. The opinion relies heavily on the prevailing standard that fair use turns on what a reasonable observer perceives as the intent of the user, not the actual mental state of the creator. As the court noted, the complaint admitted that a reasonable observer would perceive the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity," rather than a willingness to say absurd things for money. By taking this statutory focus on the "purpose and character of the use," the Second Circuit effectively equates the transformative nature of the secondary work with the primary question, rendering the bad-fact of the original creation a minor footnote. The court also cites *Authors Guild v. Google* to suggest that "critical reviews or lampoons" fall outside the very notion of a potential licensing market, reinforcing that criticism does not need to be done with "clean hands" to qualify.

## What This Means for Satirical Speech

While the opinion is concise, the doctrinal implications are substantial for the industry of political satire. Platforms like Cameo (and the technology allowing for "personalized" videos) often blur the line between commercial transactions and creative expression. If the Second Circuit establishes that good faith is no longer a hard constraint upon fair use, comedians and late-night hosts gain significant insulation against frivolous suits aimed at monetizing their criticism of public figures. The court's reasoning suggests that the moment a work is transformative, the inquiry becomes about how the new work functions in the marketplace rather than why the actors entered the transaction.

However, this ruling also highlights the importance of the procedural posture. The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal at the pleading stage (Rule 12(b)(6)) by treating the fair use doctrine as sufficiently "clearly established by a complaint." This means defendants might win on summary judgment or even motions to dismiss only if their transformative nature is inherent in the description of the work being contested. In this specific case, the "fans of hope" versus "price for absurdity" contrast created a clear record. For future litigants, this suggests that the *Santos* decision may bolster claims where the user's deception is clearly irrelevant to the transformative purpose, but it leaves open questions where bad faith might still be seen as a relevant factor in the remaining factors of the § 107 analysis.

The dismissal of state law claims further underscores the federal dominance in this arena. The court declined to reach the complex question of copyright preemption, noting instead that the contract claims failed because they lacked essential terms or a clear meeting of the minds. This deference to copyright claims as the exclusive vehicle for regulation is a side effect worth noting, suggesting that New York law will likely be put aside when federal courts find federal precepts sufficient to decide the equity and transformation spectrum. Ultimately, the Second Circuit has reinforced that while *Harper & Row* garden-fences the definition of fair use, it has not set up a permanent gatekeeping mechanism regarding the morality of the secondary user.



# Cameo Contracts and Third-Party Beneficiaries: The Privity Wall

## Why this Case Matters

In an era where content creation is increasingly divorced from traditional broadcasting relationships, the intersection of digital platforms, contract law, and copyright has become a minefield for creators. George Santos v. Kimmel is significant not just because it involves a featured performer and a late-night host, but because it serves as a potent reminder of the legal walls erected around digital terms of service. While the core of the dispute involves fair use in the context of parody, the Second Circuit’s disposition of the claims offers a hard look at "non-signatory" vendor liability. The ruling reinforces the strict privity requirements that usually shield digital platforms from serving as damages defendants for terms they draft solely for their buyers, potentially leaving content creators in a precarious legal position where their work can be repurposed by media entities without redress under state contract law.

## The Third-Party Beneficiary Wall

The most intricate part of the decision concerns the state law claims—specifically, the breach of contract claim based on the Terms of Service of the Cameo platform. Here, Judge Lohier navigated the tension between platform liability and contractual privity. The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of these claims largely because Santos, the creator, was not a signatory to the Terms of Service. Under Illinois law, which governs the agreement, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties."

This legal standard establishes a robust privity wall. The court noted that for a third party to successfully sue under a contract they did not sign, the implication that the contract applies to them must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration." In this instance, the Terms of Service allowed for third-party beneficiaries in specific instances, but when the parties intended to grant such rights, they were explicit. Justice Lohier’s panel found no such declaration in the provision governing the video generation. Furthermore, the court dismissed the fraudulent inducement claim by the "failing to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss," underscoring the high bar required to pierce established contract defenses even in cases of alleged misconduct by a platform’s instigator. This sets a formidable precedent for situations where platforms facilitate transactions but exclude non-signatories from legal recourse, relying heavily on the "meeting of minds" and "essential terms" to defeat implied contract claims.

## The Fair Use Barrier

While the contract analysis blocked the state law claims, the copyright claims faced a different, albeit related, doctrinal hurdle. The court determined that Kimmel’s segments constituted fair use. The reasoning leans heavily on the "purpose and character of the use," emphasizing that transformative uses are those that add something new, such as comment or criticism, rather than supplanting the original. A key victory for fair use here was the application of the "reasonable observer" test. Santos argued that the videos were created for subscription purposes and positivity, claiming this objective was subjective to him as a creator. The court correctly rejected this, noting that fairness depends on what the use conveys to the world—a willingness to say absurd things for money—rather than the creator’s internal intent.

## Doctrinal Implications and Future Litigation

The downstream effects of this ruling could reshape the landscape of content condemnation. By affirming that "commercially valuable" rights can be overwatched by parody under fair use and by upholding strict privity rules in contracts, the court may render it more difficult to monetize criticism of famous persons as a basis for specific state law claims. For content creators on platforms like Cameo, this case suggests that generating one-off content for platforms which then utilize those works for media production exposes creators to significant risks.

The interplay between these two doctrines is critical. If the contract claims fail because there was no privity, and copyright claims fail because the use was transformative, creators face a situation where their intellectual labor is appropriated for derision without a contractual mechanism for compensation or redress. This is not merely a procedural dismissal; it reflects a substantive choice by the courts that the creative market operates on placement, not contract. If this becomes the standard interpretation of beneficiary status in digital ToS agreements, platforms may find themselves immune from plaintiff suits for "sabotaging" their terms by using third-party intellectual property. However, this leaves a gap for rights holders who cannot bypass the privity shield through false facts or creative copyright pleading.
