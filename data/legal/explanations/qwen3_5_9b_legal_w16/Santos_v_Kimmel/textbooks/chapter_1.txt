Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Procedural Posture and Standard of Review

## Parties and Background Facts of Copyright Claims
To understand the legal analysis in this case, one must first identify the litigants and the factual foundation underlying their appeal. The appellant is George Santos, a former Congressman, who sued the appellees—Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (ABC), and The Walt Disney Company. The lawsuit arose from interactions on the Cameo video platform. According to the opinion, Jimmy Kimmel, utilizing various fictitious names, submitted requests to Santos for personalized videos through this service. Santos fulfilled each of these requests. However, Kimmel subsequently redirected the videos, airing them on his late-night show, *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*, as part of a mocking series titled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court treated these facts as the factual predicate for the case, meaning the appellate court is not evaluating the truth of these events based on a trial record, but rather how the plaintiff has framed them within their legal pleadings.

## District Court Dismissal Under Rule 12(b)(6)
The procedural history begins with the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, where Judge Cote entered a final judgment dismissing the entirety of Santos’s complaint. In civil procedure, a dismissal under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) occurs when a defendant moves to dismiss a case because the plaintiff has failed to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. In this instance, the District Court concluded on the face of the complaint that Santos’s claims were legally insufficient in two distinct ways. First, regarding the claim for copyright infringement, the court concluded it was barred by the affirmative defense of fair use. Second, regarding the state law claims—specifically breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement—the court concluded that they either failed to state a claim for relief or were preempted by the federal Copyright Act. This dismissal marks the procedural posture on which Santos’s appeal rests, shifting the case from the sufficiency of initial pleadings to appellate review of those determinations.

## De Novo Standard of Review for Pleadings Dismissals
When a party appeals a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal to an appellate court, they request a new review of whether the complaint was legally adequate. To guide this evaluation, the Second Circuit applies a *de novo* standard of review. This means the appellate court reviews the legal questions raised by the dismissal without deference to the District Court’s decision, treating the legal sufficiency of the complaint as a matter of law. Under this standard, the Second Circuit does not look at whether the facts will be proven at trial or weigh the strength of the evidence; rather, it asks whether, assuming the facts alleged in the complaint to be true, the plaintiff could still recover. Because the dismissal is treated as a question of law, the appellate court decides the issue independently based purely on the principles of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and relevant substantive law.

## Scope of the Complaint and Judicial Notice
The *de novo* standard of review defines the scope of materials the appellate court may use to determine whether a complaint states a claim. Unlike a trial, where evidence is presented under oath, the appellate court is limited to the contents of the pleadings themselves. Specifically, the court considers the facts alleged in the complaint, any documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and any other documents incorporated by reference into the complaint. In this specific case, the Second Circuit noted that it could also take judicial notice of copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry. Judicial notice allows a court to accept the existence and accuracy of certain facts, such as public records, without requiring formal proof. This expansion of materials beyond the complaint proper is significant because it provided the court with authenticated records of the actual videos in question, which aided in assessing the fair use defense without needing a trial transcript of media testimony.

## Introduction to the Substantive Claims
The complaint in *Santos v. Kimmel* contained two categories of legal claims: federal copyright claims and state common law claims. The federal claim for copyright infringement presented a question of federal statutory law under the Copyright Act. To survive a 12(b)(6) dismissal, Santos needed to plausibly allege that the Second Circuit might find infringement after weighing the statutory fair use factors under 17 U.S.C. § 107. The state law claims included causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement, grounded in New York and Illinois law. Santos argued that the court should have allowed the breach of contract claims to proceed, asserting they were unique enough to avoid preemption by the federal Copyright Act. However, the appellate court treated these substantive claims with varying degrees of scrutiny determined by the pleadings; where the copyright claim was dismissed on meritorious grounds, the state claims were dismissed on procedural grounds related to their failure to meet state pleading standards, rendering the preemption issue unnecessary to resolve at this stage.
