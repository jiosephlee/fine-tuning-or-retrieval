Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Defense

## Copyright Infringement as an Affirmative Defense

In examining claims of copyright infringement, courts often address the relationship between copyright protection and the incentives for creative expression through the doctrine of fair use. In *Santos*, the Second Circuit clarified that fair use functions as an affirmative defense. This means that while a defendant may commit acts that fall within the scope of copyright protection, the law excuses this behavior under specific circumstances to ensure that the application of the Copyright Act does not stifle the very creativity the Act is designed to promote. The District Court in Roberts and Circuit Court in Second Circuit employed this framework to analyze the case at hand. The opinion emphasizes that fair use allows courts to avoid rigid application of rights when doing so would improperly restrict creativity. Therefore, an analysis of fair use is required even when the taken portion of a work is technically infringing, as the doctrine serves as a safety valve to balance the rights of the copyright holder with the broader public interest.

## Application of 17 U.S.C. § 107 Factors

To determine whether a particular use excused from infringement, courts must consider the factors set forth in 17 U.S.C. § 107. These factors are non-exclusive, meaning they serve as a flexible guide rather than a rigid checklist. Under the Copyright Act, the analysis focuses on four distinct dimensions of the use. The first factor asks about the purpose and character of the use. The second considers the nature of the copyrighted work itself. The third factor evaluates the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the work as a whole. The fourth and final factor examines the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. The Second Circuit noted that of these factors, the "purpose and character of the use" often carries significant weight, particularly when a plaintiff claims their content was distorted or mischaracterized by the defendant. The District Court applied this statutory framework to the facts of the case to determine the presence of infringement.

## Factor Analysis: Purpose and Character of Use

When analyzing the purpose and character of the use, courts focus chiefly on whether the specific use is "transformative." As stated in *Santos*, a use is transformative if it adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, rather than merely superseding the objects of the original creation. In this specific case, the District Court found that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was to comment on the willingness of George Santos to say absurd things for money. This standard ensures that the legal analysis actually measures the impact of the work in context, rather than getting bogged down in the private motivations of the parties involved.

## Assessment of Transformative Nature and Factual Context

The factual context surrounding the creation and dissemination of the content is critical to the transformative analysis. In *Santos*, Kimmel aired the videos as part of a mocking series of segments titled "Will Santos Say It?" on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*. The Second Circuit accepted the District Court's finding that a reasonable observer would see these videos as conveying messages of hope, strength, and positivity, contrasting with any argument that the defendant was simply repeating the original content. The opinion highlights that the District Court was entitled to dismiss the copyright claim when the plaintiff—who was also a consumer of the Cameo platform—could not alter the objective characterization of the use. The Court reasoned that the use was sufficiently transformative because Kimmel inserted the videos into a specific critique and satire, changing the character of the original cameos into commentary.

## Dismissal of Bad Faith and Market Harm Arguments

The Second Circuit further addressed Santos's arguments regarding bad faith and market harm, rejecting the claim that bad faith could nullify the fair use defense. While the Supreme Court has stated that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing," the opinion notes that the Court has since distanced itself from this statement. The Second Circuit supported the view that the litigation's complaint contrasted with the defendant's purpose to "supplant" the plaintiff's commercially valuable rights. Instead, the complaint portrayed defendants as motivated by criticism and commentary, which aligns with established precedents like *Authors Guild v. Google*. Additionally, the Court addressed market harm, noting that plaintiffs must show whether the second work usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute. Because critical reviews or lampoons are not typically licensed as commercial substitutes for the original imaginative work, Santos failed to allege sufficient market harm in the pleading stage.

## Subjective Intent Versus Transformability

A final and critical aspect of the *Santos* opinion concerns the disconnect between the author's intent and the transformative nature of the resulting work. Santos argued that the original allegation acknowledged the purpose of his videos was to say absurd things for money, which he claimed matched the purpose of the infringement. However, the District Court and the Second Circuit disagreed. The opinion emphasizes that the subjective intent of the user does not determine the purpose of the use; rather, the question is how the use appears to a reasonable observer. This distinction is vital for the doctrine of fair use, as it prevents the copyright holder from controlling the secondary uses of their work simply by defining their own intent during creation. The Court affirmed the dismissal because the use was deemed transformative based on the objective nature of the segments aired on JKL, regardless of Santos's intent in creating the initial videos. The Court concluded that fair use barred the copyright claims even at the pleading stage, requiring only the comparison of the original version of the videos and the segments at issue.
