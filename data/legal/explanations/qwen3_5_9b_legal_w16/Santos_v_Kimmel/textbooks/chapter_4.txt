Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 4: # Fraudulent Inducement and Final Disposition

## Elements of Fraudulent Inducement under New York Law

The second section of the Second Circuit’s analysis addresses the state law claims included in the complaint, specifically focusing on fraudulent inducement under New York law. While fraudulent inducement generally involves an allegation that a party was induced to enter into a contract through fraudulent misrepresentations, the appellate court was able to define the scope of this claim principally through the damages requirement. The opinion cites New York precedent, specifically *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, to establish that a plaintiff must allege they suffered an "out-of-pocket loss" as a requirement of the claim. Because the District Court had already granted summary judgment based on this foundational element, the Second Circuit’s focus was on whether the complaint adequately alleged this specific component of the tort. This pedagogical focus highlights that even if a plaintiff establishes complex relationships of negotiation and communication, the legal viability of a fraudulent inducement claim often rests on the demonstration of actual economic harm.

## Failure to Allege Out-of-Pocket Loss

The central analytical work in this portion of the chapter is the application of the out-of-pocket loss requirement to the facts of the complaint. George Santos argued that Jimmy Kimmel engaged in a scheme that, while beginning with requests for personalized videos, constituted fraudulent inducement. However, the Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal because the complaint failed to allege that Santos suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as a victim of the alleged fraud. The court cites *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* for the proposition that New York law requires this specific showing. In the absence of such allegations, the complaint was deemed insufficient to state a claim for relief. Even if Santos had considered Kimmel’s use of fictitious names or his refusal to compensate him to be deceptive, the pleading standard under Rule 12(b)(6) could not be satisfied without alleging the requisite financial detriment. This illustrates the high pleading burden for state law fraud claims, where mere deception must be coupled with proven or plausibly alleged financial injury to survive a dismissal at the pleading stage.

## Review of the District Court's Disposition

To understand how the Second Circuit reached its decision regarding these claims, students must understand the standard of review applied. The opinion explicitly states that the court reviews the District Court’s dismissal of the complaint de novo pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. This standard of review is critical because it dictates that the appellate court considers the facts alleged in the complaint, along with documents attached or incorporated by reference, accepting them as true. The court clarified that it may also take judicial notice of documents such as copyright registrations from the Copyright Office’s registry. This procedural framework means that the District Court’s factual determinations were not binding on the Second Circuit, but rather the defendant must have the initial burden of clarifying that the complaint fails to state a claim as a matter of law. The Second Circuit found that even accepting the allegations in the complaint as true, the necessary elements for fraudulent inducement were missing, confirming that the District Court was correct in its procedural application.

## Summary of Appellate Holding and Affirmation

The disposition of the case relied on the convergence of the copyright analysis and the state law dismissal. The case summary indicates that while the District Court dismissed the copyright claims based on the fair use doctrine and dismissed state claims on preemption or failure to state a claim, the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment for separate but consistent reasons. Under the appellate analysis, the fair use doctrine barred the copyright claims, while the state law claims failed to state a claim because Santos did not plausibly allege essential terms of an agreement or show out-of-pocket loss. Specifically, for the fraudulent inducement claim, the court affirmed the dismissal because the complaint lacked the necessary allegations of financial loss. Consequently, the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment of the District Court, upholding the dismissal of both the federal and state claims against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC.

## Broader Implications for Public Figures and Media Claims

While the opinion is technically an affirmation of pre-trial dismissals, it provides guidance on the interplay between media criticism and liability. In this case, the Second Circuit distinguished between Kimmel’s motive to criticize or comment on Santos and any potential liability for fraud. The court noted that Kimmel’s use of fictitious names and the context of the videos were part of a "sarcastic criticism and commentary" rather than a "supplant" of Santos’s commercial rights. The broader implication for public figures and media claims is that allegations of deceptive solicitation by media entities, particularly those involving user-generated content, do not automatically constitute actionable fraud or copyright infringement. The decision reinforces that for a state law claim to succeed in this context, a public figure must still meet the rigorous pleading requirements of substantive New York law, such as demonstrating actual out-of-pocket loss, regardless of the notoriety or contentiousness of the public media discourse.
