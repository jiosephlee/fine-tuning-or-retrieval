Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # State Law Claims: Contract Theory and Preemption Analysis

## Overview of Breach of Contract and Implied Contract Claims
When reviewing the district court's dismissal of the Second Circuit's appeal, it is crucial to understand the procedural postures under which state law claims are evaluated. In *Santos*, George Santos brought claims for copyright infringement alongside state law causes of action, specifically breach of contract, breach of an implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The district court dismissed the entire complaint, finding the copyright claims barred by the fair use doctrine and the state law claims failed to state a claim for relief. On appeal, the Second Circuit reviewed these dismissals *de novo* for failure to state a claim under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). This standard of review requires the appellate court to view the facts alleged in the complaint as true and to decide whether those facts plausibly support the elements of the legal theory. In *Santos*, the appellate court analyzed whether the allegations met the necessary constitutional and statutory requirements to survive a motion to dismiss, particularly focusing on whether a contract existed and whether federal law preempted the state claims. This section focuses on the court's evaluation of the contract and related state claims, which were distinct from the copyright but dismissible under independent state law principles.

## Federal Preemption Under the Copyright Act
A significant legal concept in intellectual property litigation is preemption. Under the Copyright Act, specifically 17 U.S.C. § 301, certain state law claims are preempted if the subject matter is within the scope of copyright protection and the rights created are equivalent to copyright. In *Santos*, Santos challenged his dismissal, arguing that his state law claims were "qualitatively different" from a copyright infringement claim and therefore not preempted by the federal Copyright Act. This would allow state law claims to proceed even if federal copyright principles generally govern the underlying creative work. The district court had initially suggested these claims were preempted. However, the Second Circuit clarified its approach to preemption analysis in this opinion. Rather than resolving the complex question of whether the Copyright Act's preemption clause swallowing up state contract claims, the court employed a judicial economy rule often seen in appellate reasoning: if a state law claim fails on its own merits under state law, the court need not resolve the federal preemption issue. This means that preemption is a "last resort" analysis often only necessary when a plaintiff successfully states a plausible state law claim that turns out to be legally barred only because the state law would conflict with federal rights. By deciding the claims failed under state contract law first, the court avoided making a determination on the scope of federal preemption.

## Application of New York Privity and Third-Party Beneficiary Doctrine
The core of the issue regarding contract claims involved the document titled "Cameo Terms of Service." Santos argued that Kimmel was in breach of these terms. However, the relationship between Santos and the terms of service raises the foundational concept of privity of contract. Privity dictates that a contract is binding only on the parties who signed it. In *Santos*, the Second Circuit looked to choice-of-law rules to determine which state's contract law applied. The court noted that the Terms of Service were governed by Illinois law, as that is where the Cameo platform is likely incorporated or where the service agreement is hosted. The court applied Illinois jurisprudence, noting a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." This rule illustrates that under Illinois law, if a user (Kimmel) agrees to a contract but a non-user (Santos) sues claiming a benefit under that agreement, or if the terms are characterized as prohibiting the use of videos in a way Santos did not consent to, courts will not treat them as binding the third party unless there is an "express declaration" that the contract provisions apply to them.

The Second Circuit distinguished this from the second contract claim, which dealt with breach of implied contract. Here, the court applied New York law. Under New York's contract theory, a court looks to "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to determine if an "implied-in-fact contract was formed." This doctrine relies on the objective actions of the parties rather than written text. However, the court found Santos's pleading insufficient to meet the contractual requirements of New York law. The pleading did not allege, in specific detail, the "essential terms of the parties' contract." This highlights that simply asserting a contract existed is not enough; the plaintiff must define the deal's specifics (such as scope, price, or obligations) for the court to find that the conduct amounted to an agreement. Furthermore, Santos failed to plausibly allege that the parties demonstrated a "meeting of minds"—an agreement to adhere to the terms—which is necessary even for an implied-in-fact contract.

## Pleading Standards for Implied Contracts
When plaintiffs do not allege a written contract, the doctrine of an implied-in-fact contract often becomes the gap-filler in terms of liability. However, appellate courts, including the Second Circuit in *Santos*, are strict regarding the pleading standards required to assert these claims. Under New York state law, governing the implied contract claim, a complaint cannot be dismissed if it simply states a conclusion of law without factual support. The First Department Court cited in *Santos* established that a claim will fail if the plaintiff fails to "allege, in nonconclusory language... the essential terms of the parties' contract."

In *Santos*, the judge noted that the complaint failed to identify specific provisions of the Cameo Terms of Service that were implicitly agreed upon or breached. While Santos identified that Kimmel used fictitious names to request videos, he did not sufficiently connect these actions to a specific, agreed-upon contractual obligation that Kimmel promised to fulfill under those terms. The Second Circuit cited additional precedents, such as *DG & A Mgmt. Servs.*, to emphasize that without "an indication of a meeting of minds," the law cannot presumptively infer a contract existed. This teaching point is critical for students reading state law assertions in federal courts: compliance with Rule 12(b)(6) requires the plaintiff to plead the actual terms of the alleged deal. A plaintiff cannot simply assume the jury will find an implied contract exists; they must identify what terms, in the court's view, would bind the defendant, and why the facts viewed as a whole support that specific agreement.

## Court's Avoidance of the Preemption Issue via Merit Failure
Finally, it is instructive to analyze how the Second Circuit managed the relationship between federal preemption arguments and state law pleading deficiencies. The District Court had dismissed the first two state law claims (breach of contract and breach of implied contract) largely on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos appealed, arguing the claims were distinct enough from copyright infringement to survive preemption. The Second Circuit explicitly declined to rule on this statutory preemption issue, stating, "We need not resolve this issue... because Santos's state law claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law." This illustrates a practical aspect of judicial decision-making often referred to as decisional efficiency or ripeness. By resolving the dispositive issue first, the court prevented the unnecessary debate over the prescriptive reach of federal intellectual property law.

This approach also touches upon the legal standard for preemption itself, which requires that the state claim must exist independently of federal law. If the claim, even if plausible, would be preempted, the court usually proceeds to that branch of the law. However, if the claim fails of its own weight—for example, due to lack of privity, lack of terms, or lack of loss—the ground for the federal bar is removed. Here, the court found that the fraudulent inducement claim also failed because Santos alleged no "out-of-pocket loss." This is a specific New York pleading standard required for fraud cases, further reinforcing that the state law claims were insufficient on their face before the federal Copyright Act could be invoked. Consequently, the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment by strictly adhering to state law pleading failures, ensuring that federal preemption doctrines were not applied to a claim that never had standing as a state law claim in the first place.
