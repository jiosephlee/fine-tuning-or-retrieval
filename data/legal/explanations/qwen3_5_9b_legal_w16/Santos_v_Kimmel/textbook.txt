Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Procedural Posture and Standard of Review

## Parties and Background Facts of Copyright Claims
To understand the legal analysis in this case, one must first identify the litigants and the factual foundation underlying their appeal. The appellant is George Santos, a former Congressman, who sued the appellees—Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (ABC), and The Walt Disney Company. The lawsuit arose from interactions on the Cameo video platform. According to the opinion, Jimmy Kimmel, utilizing various fictitious names, submitted requests to Santos for personalized videos through this service. Santos fulfilled each of these requests. However, Kimmel subsequently redirected the videos, airing them on his late-night show, *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*, as part of a mocking series titled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court treated these facts as the factual predicate for the case, meaning the appellate court is not evaluating the truth of these events based on a trial record, but rather how the plaintiff has framed them within their legal pleadings.

## District Court Dismissal Under Rule 12(b)(6)
The procedural history begins with the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, where Judge Cote entered a final judgment dismissing the entirety of Santos’s complaint. In civil procedure, a dismissal under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) occurs when a defendant moves to dismiss a case because the plaintiff has failed to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. In this instance, the District Court concluded on the face of the complaint that Santos’s claims were legally insufficient in two distinct ways. First, regarding the claim for copyright infringement, the court concluded it was barred by the affirmative defense of fair use. Second, regarding the state law claims—specifically breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement—the court concluded that they either failed to state a claim for relief or were preempted by the federal Copyright Act. This dismissal marks the procedural posture on which Santos’s appeal rests, shifting the case from the sufficiency of initial pleadings to appellate review of those determinations.

## De Novo Standard of Review for Pleadings Dismissals
When a party appeals a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal to an appellate court, they request a new review of whether the complaint was legally adequate. To guide this evaluation, the Second Circuit applies a *de novo* standard of review. This means the appellate court reviews the legal questions raised by the dismissal without deference to the District Court’s decision, treating the legal sufficiency of the complaint as a matter of law. Under this standard, the Second Circuit does not look at whether the facts will be proven at trial or weigh the strength of the evidence; rather, it asks whether, assuming the facts alleged in the complaint to be true, the plaintiff could still recover. Because the dismissal is treated as a question of law, the appellate court decides the issue independently based purely on the principles of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and relevant substantive law.

## Scope of the Complaint and Judicial Notice
The *de novo* standard of review defines the scope of materials the appellate court may use to determine whether a complaint states a claim. Unlike a trial, where evidence is presented under oath, the appellate court is limited to the contents of the pleadings themselves. Specifically, the court considers the facts alleged in the complaint, any documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and any other documents incorporated by reference into the complaint. In this specific case, the Second Circuit noted that it could also take judicial notice of copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry. Judicial notice allows a court to accept the existence and accuracy of certain facts, such as public records, without requiring formal proof. This expansion of materials beyond the complaint proper is significant because it provided the court with authenticated records of the actual videos in question, which aided in assessing the fair use defense without needing a trial transcript of media testimony.

## Introduction to the Substantive Claims
The complaint in *Santos v. Kimmel* contained two categories of legal claims: federal copyright claims and state common law claims. The federal claim for copyright infringement presented a question of federal statutory law under the Copyright Act. To survive a 12(b)(6) dismissal, Santos needed to plausibly allege that the Second Circuit might find infringement after weighing the statutory fair use factors under 17 U.S.C. § 107. The state law claims included causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement, grounded in New York and Illinois law. Santos argued that the court should have allowed the breach of contract claims to proceed, asserting they were unique enough to avoid preemption by the federal Copyright Act. However, the appellate court treated these substantive claims with varying degrees of scrutiny determined by the pleadings; where the copyright claim was dismissed on meritorious grounds, the state claims were dismissed on procedural grounds related to their failure to meet state pleading standards, rendering the preemption issue unnecessary to resolve at this stage.

# Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Defense

## Copyright Infringement as an Affirmative Defense

In examining claims of copyright infringement, courts often address the relationship between copyright protection and the incentives for creative expression through the doctrine of fair use. In *Santos*, the Second Circuit clarified that fair use functions as an affirmative defense. This means that while a defendant may commit acts that fall within the scope of copyright protection, the law excuses this behavior under specific circumstances to ensure that the application of the Copyright Act does not stifle the very creativity the Act is designed to promote. The District Court in Roberts and Circuit Court in Second Circuit employed this framework to analyze the case at hand. The opinion emphasizes that fair use allows courts to avoid rigid application of rights when doing so would improperly restrict creativity. Therefore, an analysis of fair use is required even when the taken portion of a work is technically infringing, as the doctrine serves as a safety valve to balance the rights of the copyright holder with the broader public interest.

## Application of 17 U.S.C. § 107 Factors

To determine whether a particular use excused from infringement, courts must consider the factors set forth in 17 U.S.C. § 107. These factors are non-exclusive, meaning they serve as a flexible guide rather than a rigid checklist. Under the Copyright Act, the analysis focuses on four distinct dimensions of the use. The first factor asks about the purpose and character of the use. The second considers the nature of the copyrighted work itself. The third factor evaluates the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the work as a whole. The fourth and final factor examines the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. The Second Circuit noted that of these factors, the "purpose and character of the use" often carries significant weight, particularly when a plaintiff claims their content was distorted or mischaracterized by the defendant. The District Court applied this statutory framework to the facts of the case to determine the presence of infringement.

## Factor Analysis: Purpose and Character of Use

When analyzing the purpose and character of the use, courts focus chiefly on whether the specific use is "transformative." As stated in *Santos*, a use is transformative if it adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, rather than merely superseding the objects of the original creation. In this specific case, the District Court found that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was to comment on the willingness of George Santos to say absurd things for money. The落脚 points of the opinion include that whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks the use conveys, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user. This standard ensures that the legal analysis actually measures the impact of the work in context, rather than getting bogged down in the private motivations of the parties involved.

## Assessment of Transformative Nature and Factual Context

The factual context surrounding the creation and dissemination of the content is critical to the transformative analysis. In *Santos*, Kimmel aired the videos as part of a mocking series of segments titled "Will Santos Say It?" on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*. The Second Circuit accepted the District Court's finding that a reasonable observer would see these videos as conveying messages of hope, strength, and positivity, contrasting with any argument that the defendant was simply repeating the original content. The opinion highlights that the District Court was entitled to dismiss the copyright claim when the plaintiff—who was also a consumer of the Cameo platform—could not alter the objective characterization of the use. The Court reasoned that the use was sufficiently transformative because Kimmel inserted the videos into a specific critique and satire, changing the character of the original cameos into commentary.

## Dismissal of Bad Faith and Market Harm Arguments

The Second Circuit further addressed Santos's arguments regarding bad faith and market harm, rejecting the claim that bad faith could nullify the fair use defense. While the Supreme Court has stated that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing," the opinion notes that the Court has since distanced itself from this statement. The Second Circuit supported the view that the litigation's complaint contrasted with the defendant's purpose to "supplant" the plaintiff's commercially valuable rights. Instead, the complaint portrayed defendants as motivated by criticism and commentary, which aligns with established precedents like *Authors Guild v. Google*. Additionally, the Court addressed market harm, noting that plaintiffs must show whether the second work usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute. Because critical reviews or lampoons are not typically licensed as commercial substitutes for the original imaginative work, Santos failed to allege sufficient market harm in the pleading stage.

## Subjective Intent Versus Transformability

A final and critical aspect of the *Santos* opinion concerns the disconnect between the author's intent and the transformative nature of the resulting work. Santos argued that the original allegation acknowledged the purpose of his videos was to say absurd things for money, which he claimed matched the purpose of the infringement. However, the District Court and the Second Circuit disagreed. The opinion emphasizes that the subjective intent of the user does not determine the purpose of the use; rather, the question is how the use appears to a reasonable observer. This distinction is vital for the doctrine of fair use, as it prevents the copyright holder from controlling the secondary uses of their work simply by defining their own intent during creation. The Court affirmed the dismissal because the use was deemed transformative based on the objective nature of the segments aired on JKL, regardless of Santos's intent in creating the initial videos. The Court concluded that fair use barred the copyright claims even at the pleading stage, requiring only the comparison of the original version of the videos and the segments at issue.

# State Law Claims: Contract Theory and Preemption Analysis

## Overview of Breach of Contract and Implied Contract Claims
When reviewing the district court's dismissal of the Second Circuit's appeal, it is crucial to understand the procedural postures under which state law claims are evaluated. In *Santos*, George Santos brought claims for copyright infringement alongside state law causes of action, specifically breach of contract, breach of an implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The district court dismissed the entire complaint, finding the copyright claims barred by the fair use doctrine and the state law claims failed to state a claim for relief. On appeal, the Second Circuit reviewed these dismissals *de novo* for failure to state a claim under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). This standard of review requires the appellate court to view the facts alleged in the complaint as true and to decide whether those facts plausibly support the elements of the legal theory. In *Santos*, the appellate court analyzed whether the allegations met the necessary constitutional and statutory requirements to survive a motion to dismiss, particularly focusing on whether a contract existed and whether federal law preempted the state claims. This section focuses on the court's evaluation of the contract and related state claims, which were distinct from the copyright but dismissible under independent state law principles.

## Federal Preemption Under the Copyright Act
A significant legal concept in intellectual property litigation is preemption. Under the Copyright Act, specifically 17 U.S.C. § 301, certain state law claims are preempted if the subject matter is within the scope of copyright protection and the rights created are equivalent to copyright. In *Santos*, Santos challenged his dismissal, arguing that his state law claims were "qualitatively different" from a copyright infringement claim and therefore not preempted by the federal Copyright Act. This would allow state law claims to proceed even if federal copyright principles generally govern the underlying creative work. The district court had initially suggested these claims were preempted. However, the Second Circuit clarified its approach to preemption analysis in this opinion. Rather than resolving the complex question of whether the Copyright Act's preemption clause swallowing up state contract claims, the court employed a judicial economy rule often seen in appellate reasoning: if a state law claim fails on its own merits under state law, the court need not resolve the federal preemption issue. This means that preemption is a "last resort" analysis often only necessary when a plaintiff successfully states a plausible state law claim that turns out to be legally barred only because the state law would conflict with federal rights. By deciding the claims failed under state contract law first, the court avoided making a determination on the scope of federal preemption.

## Application of New York Privity and Third-Party Beneficiary Doctrine
The core of the issue regarding contract claims involved the document titled "Cameo Terms of Service." Santos argued that Kimmel was in breach of these terms. However, the relationship between Santos and the terms of service raises the foundational concept of privity of contract. Privity dictates that a contract is binding only on the parties who signed it. In *Santos*, the Second Circuit looked to choice-of-law rules to determine which state's contract law applied. The court noted that the Terms of Service were governed by Illinois law, as that is where the Cameo platform is likely incorporated or where the service agreement is hosted. The court applied Illinois jurisprudence, noting a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." This rule illustrates that under Illinois law, if a user (Kimmel) agrees to a contract but a non-user (Santos) sues claiming a benefit under that agreement, or if the terms are characterized as prohibiting the use of videos in a way Santos did not consent to, courts will not treat them as binding the third party unless there is an "express declaration" that the contract provisions apply to them.

The Second Circuit distinguished this from the second contract claim, which dealt with breach of implied contract. Here, the court applied New York law. Under New York's contract theory, a court looks to "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to determine if an "implied-in-fact contract was formed." This doctrine relies on the objective actions of the parties rather than written text. However, the court found Santos's pleading insufficient to meet the contractual requirements of New York law. The pleading did not allege, in specific detail, the "essential terms of the parties' contract." This highlights that simply asserting a contract existed is not enough; the plaintiff must define the deal's specifics (such as scope, price, or obligations) for the court to find that the conduct amounted to an agreement. Furthermore, Santos failed to plausibly allege that the parties demonstrated a "meeting of minds"—an agreement to adhere to the terms—which is necessary even for an implied-in-fact contract.

## Pleading Standards for Implied Contracts
When plaintiffs do not allege a written contract, the doctrine of an implied-in-fact contract often becomes the gap-filler in terms of liability. However, appellate courts, including the Second Circuit in *Santos*, are strict regarding the pleading standards required to assert these claims. Under New York state law, governing the implied contract claim, a complaint cannot be dismissed if it simply states a conclusion of law without factual support. The First Department Court cited in *Santos* established that a claim will fail if the plaintiff fails to "allege, in nonconclusory language... the essential terms of the parties' contract."

In *Santos*, the judge noted that the complaint failed to identify specific provisions of the Cameo Terms of Service that were implicitly agreed upon or breached. While Santos identified that Kimmel used fictitious names to request videos, he did not sufficiently connect these actions to a specific, agreed-upon contractual obligation that Kimmel promised to fulfill under those terms. The Second Circuit cited additional precedents, such as *DG & A Mgmt. Servs.*, to emphasize that without "an indication of a meeting of minds," the law cannot presumptively infer a contract existed. This teaching point is critical for students reading state law assertions in federal courts: compliance with Rule 12(b)(6) requires the plaintiff to plead the actual terms of the alleged deal. A plaintiff cannot simply assume the jury will find an implied contract exists; they must identify what terms, in the court's view, would bind the defendant, and why the facts viewed as a whole support that specific agreement.

## Court's Avoidance of the Preemption Issue via Merit Failure
Finally, it is instructive to analyze how the Second Circuit managed the relationship between federal preemption arguments and state law pleading deficiencies. The District Court had dismissed the first two state law claims (breach of contract and breach of implied contract) largely on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos appealed, arguing the claims were distinct enough from copyright infringement to survive preemption. The Second Circuit explicitly declined to rule on this statutory preemption issue, stating, "We need not resolve this issue... because Santos's state law claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law." This illustrates a practical aspect of judicial decision-making often referred to as decisional efficiency or ripeness. By resolving the dispositive issue first, the court prevented the unnecessary debate over the prescriptive reach of federal intellectual property law.

This approach also touches upon the legal standard for preemption itself, which requires that the state claim must exist independently of federal law. If the claim, even if plausible, would be preempted, the court usually proceeds to that branch of the law. However, if the claim fails of its own weight—for example, due to lack of privity, lack of terms, or lack of loss—the ground for the federal bar is removed. Here, the court found that the fraudulent inducement claim also failed because Santos alleged no "out-of-pocket loss." This is a specific New York pleading standard required for fraud cases, further reinforcing that the state law claims were insufficient on their face before the federal Copyright Act could be invoked. Consequently, the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment by strictly adhering to state law pleading failures, ensuring that federal preemption doctrines were not applied to a claim that never had standing as a state law claim in the first place.

# Fraudulent Inducement and Final Disposition

## Elements of Fraudulent Inducement under New York Law

The second section of the Second Circuit’s analysis addresses the state law claims included in the complaint, specifically focusing on fraudulent inducement under New York law. While fraudulent inducement generally involves an allegation that a party was induced to enter into a contract through fraudulent misrepresentations, the appellate court was able to define the scope of this claim principally through the damages requirement. The opinion cites New York precedent, specifically *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, to establish that a plaintiff must allege they suffered an "out-of-pocket loss" as a requirement of the claim. Because the District Court had already granted summary judgment based on this foundational element, the Second Circuit’s focus was on whether the complaint adequately alleged this specific component of the tort. This pedagogical focus highlights that even if a plaintiff establishes complex relationships of negotiation and communication, the legal viability of a fraudulent inducement claim often rests on the demonstration of actual economic harm.

## Failure to Allege Out-of-Pocket Loss

The central analytical work in this portion of the chapter is the application of the out-of-pocket loss requirement to the facts of the complaint. George Santos argued that Jimmy Kimmel engaged in a scheme that, while beginning with requests for personalized videos, constituted fraudulent inducement. However, the Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal because the complaint failed to allege that Santos suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as a victim of the alleged fraud. The court cites *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* for the proposition that New York law requires this specific showing. In the absence of such allegations, the complaint was deemed insufficient to state a claim for relief. Even if Santos had considered Kimmel’s use of fictitious names or his refusal to compensate him to be deceptive, the pleading standard under Rule 12(b)(6) could not be satisfied without alleging the requisite financial detriment. This illustrates the high pleading burden for state law fraud claims, where mere deception must be coupled with proven or plausibly alleged financial injury to survive a dismissal at the pleading stage.

## Review of the District Court's Disposition

To understand how the Second Circuit reached its decision regarding these claims, students must understand the standard of review applied. The opinion explicitly states that the court reviews the District Court’s dismissal of the complaint de novo pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. This standard of review is critical because it dictates that the appellate court considers the facts alleged in the complaint, along with documents attached or incorporated by reference, accepting them as true. The court clarified that it may also take judicial notice of documents such as copyright registrations from the Copyright Office’s registry. This procedural framework means that the District Court’s factual determinations were not binding on the Second Circuit, but rather the defendant must have the initial burden of clarifying that the complaint fails to state a claim as a matter of law. The Second Circuit found that even accepting the allegations in the complaint as true, the necessary elements for fraudulent inducement were missing, confirming that the District Court was correct in its procedural application.

## Summary of Appellate Holding and Affirmation

The disposition of the case relied on the convergence of the copyright analysis and the state law dismissal. The case summary indicates that while the District Court dismissed the copyright claims based on the fair use doctrine and dismissed state claims on preemption or failure to state a claim, the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment for separate but consistent reasons. Under the appellate analysis, the fair use doctrine barred the copyright claims, while the state law claims failed to state a claim because Santos did not plausibly allege essential terms of an agreement or show out-of-pocket loss. Specifically, for the fraudulent inducement claim, the court affirmed the dismissal because the complaint lacked the necessary allegations of financial loss. Consequently, the Second Circuit affirmed the judgment of the District Court, upholding the dismissal of both the federal and state claims against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC.

## Broader Implications for Public Figures and Media Claims

While the opinion is technically an affirmation of pre-trial dismissals, it provides guidance on the interplay between media criticism and liability. In this case, the Second Circuit distinguished between Kimmel’s motive to criticize or comment on Santos and any potential liability for fraud. The court noted that Kimmel’s use of fictitious names and the context of the videos were part of a "sarcastic criticism and commentary" rather than a "supplant" of Santos’s commercial rights. The broader implication for public figures and media claims is that allegations of deceptive solicitation by media entities, particularly those involving user-generated content, do not automatically constitute actionable fraud or copyright infringement. The decision reinforces that for a state law claim to succeed in this context, a public figure must still meet the rigorous pleading requirements of substantive New York law, such as demonstrating actual out-of-pocket loss, regardless of the notoriety or contentiousness of the public media discourse.
