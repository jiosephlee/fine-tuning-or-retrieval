Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## Reasonable Observer Overload: How *Santos* Nails Fair Use to the Objective Standard

* By [Your Name] | October 10, 2025

The tension between copyright protection and the robustness of parody remains one of the most fraught terrains in intellectual property law. In recent years, the Supreme Court’s re-engagement with fair use in *Authors Guild v. Google* and *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* has sought to balance commercial interests with free expression, but often by elevating the "transformative" nature of a work as the primary metric. Recently, the Second Circuit joined this conversation with a significant ruling in *Santos v. Kimmel*, one that reinforces an objective standard for fair use analysis while effectively dismantling the notion that a creator’s subjective malice or intent can override a transformative defense. In this case, former Congressman George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel and American Broadcasting Companies claiming copyright infringement and state law violations after Kimmel utilized personalized, positive-toned requests from the Cameo platform to construct satirical segments mocking Santos on his late-night show. The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Santos’s complaint on both primary, intellectual property and state law grounds, grounding its copyright analysis in a "reasonable observer" standard that makes the "subjective intent" of the parties largely irrelevant to fairness.

The crux of the controversy in *Santos* lay in the "purpose and character" of the use. Courts evaluate whether a secondary use transforms the original work or merely supersedes it to assess fair use. Here, Kimmel’s argument—or conversely, Santos’s counter-argument—touched upon the murky waters of who was the true designer of the content. The opinion details how Santos challenged the transformative nature of Kimmel’s segments, arguing they did not transform his videos but served Kimmel's commercial purpose of "mocking" him. However, Lohier, sitting as Circuit Judge, zeroed in on the distinction between the "subjective intent of the user" and the observable reality of the work itself. Drawing heavily on the 2023 *Andy Warhol* decision, the court held that transformative quality is a question not of "subjective intent," but of what a reasonable observer perceives. Historically, creators might have argued that if they commissioned the creation of a work, they could claim sole credit for its purpose. *Santos* clarifies that even if Kimmel instigated the request, the commercial value of the original videos and the satire of the new segments cannot be judged by the internal motives of the creator or the hostile means of the defendant.

Perhaps the most analytically fascinating portion of the opinion involves the treatment of "bad faith." In *Harper & Row*, the Supreme Court indicated that fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing. The Second Circuit acknowledged this principle theoretically but noted that the Court had since distanced itself from the strictness of this lineage. In this instance, Santos argued that Kimmel’s deceptive methods—using a fictitious persona to solicit the videos—constituted bad faith that should nullify any fair use protection. Lohier succinctly rejected this, noting that the complaint paints a picture of "sarcastic criticism and commentary" rather than an intent to supplant the market. This decision is significant because it signals to litigants that the "how" is less important than the "what" in fair use inquiries. While deception might be relevant to state law claims or actual damages later, it does not bar a finding of fair use at the pleading stage if the work objectively serves a different purpose than the original.

We must also look at the state law claims, as the court addressed them briefly but definitively. Santos brought claims for breach of contract and fraudulent inducement, relying on the Cameo Terms of Service. The court dismissed the contract claims for failing to state a claim for relief under both Illinois and New York law, noting the lack of a "meeting of minds" that would bind third parties to the original platform's terms. It is telling that the court did not need to resolve whether the Copyright Act preempts these claims because the state law claims were factually insufficient to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. This procedural efficiency underscores the Second Circuit’s preference for resolving cases on the merits where possible, though here the proceduralism cleared the way for the fair use analysis to dominate the discussion.

**Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects**

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Andy Warhol* has profound downstream effects for how media litigation will be framed. By tethering the transformative inquiry to a "reasonable observer" test rather than subjective intent, the court effectively closes a loophole that might allow creators to argue that they "owned" the purpose of a derivative work they solicited. For media defendants, this alleviates some anxiety about perceived deception in the production process; a critic or parody artist is not barred from fair use defense because they tricked a creator into making the content. For creators, however, it suggests that the integrity of their initial work's value is quite distinct from what happens later to that work in the public sphere.

Furthermore, the court’s treatment of market harm reinforces the principle that modification and parody do not need to compete commercially with the original to qualify as fair use. The Second Circuit reasoned that there is no market for a creator to license a license to a criticism or lampoon of their own productions. Willingness to license does not equal market value, a point the court hammered home by citing *Hachette* and *Campbell*. This aligns with the broader Copyright Act’s goal of encouraging creativity, not locking it behind a fortress of exclusive control that stifles commentary.

In summary, *Santos v. Kimmel* offers a crisp, if unsentimental, look at how copyright law navigates the collision between commerce and speech. By grounding the transformative analysis in objective reality rather than the subjective desires of the parties, the Second Circuit has provided a secure foothold for fair use arguments in satire cases. It reminds us that while the Producer might steal the spotlight on the intent of creation, the copyright is often just to the first shot, not the whole film.
