Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why does New York law require an allegation of 'out-of-pocket loss' for a fraudulent inducement claim?
Question:
The court dismisses Santos's fraudulent inducement claim because he 'failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss.' I know fraud generally requires harm, but I thought fraudulent inducement might just need to show reliance plus damages. I am specifically confused if the court is requiring 'out-of-pocket loss' as a distinct element for New York fraudulent inducement, rather than just general compensatory damages or relational damages.
Answer:
In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Santos's fraudulent inducement claim because New York law treats an "out-of-pocket loss" as an essential element of that specific cause of action. While general common law fraud often requires proof of reliance and damages resulting from that reliance, New York jurisprudence—as referenced in the opinion—specifically requires the plaintiff to allege actual out-of-pocket loss to state a valid claim for fraudulent inducement.

### The Essential Element of Out-of-Pocket Loss
Under New York state law, a fraudulent inducement claim is distinct from other fraud-related claims in that it necessitates proof of pecuniary harm defined as an out-of-pocket loss. The Second Circuit explicitly notes that Santos challenged the District Court’s dismissal on the grounds that he was required only to allege general damages, but the appellate court rejected this.

The opinion states:
> "Finally, Santos challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege any out-of-pocket loss as required under New York law. See *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We agree with the District Court’s conclusion that Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud. See *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017)."

This holding clarifies that for a fraudulent inducement claim to survive a motion to dismiss under New York law, the complaint must plead a specific type of injury—actual financial loss resulting from the fraud—rather than generalized relational or non-monetary damages.

### Application to the Case
The District Court had previously dismissed Santos's claim, concluding he "failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss." The Second Circuit agreed with the lower court, citing the precedent set out in *Lama Holding Co.* and *Connaughton*.

Because Santos’s complaint did not plead this specific requirement—he focused on the defendants' deceptive conduct rather than the corresponding financial loss he endured—the court concluded that his complaint failed to state a claim for relief. Thus, the requirement is not merely an evidentiary hurdle for calculating damages after the fact, but a substantive pleading element required to establish the cause of action under New York law.

### Conclusion
The requirement for an "out-of-pocket loss" is a pleading threshold imposed by New York state court decisions that define the boundaries of fraudulent inducement. As noted in *Santos v. Kimmel*, failure to allege this specific element renders the claim legally insufficient under the governing state law, even if general reliance or other potential harms exist.
