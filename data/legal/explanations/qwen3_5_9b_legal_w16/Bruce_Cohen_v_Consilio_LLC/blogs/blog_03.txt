Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The Procedural Cost of Casual Claims: Why 'New Relief' Does Not Save a Summary Judgment Appeal

Workplace wage disputes are among the most common litigation battles of the modern era, often turning on the nitty-gritty of overtime calculations and statutory definitions. Yet, frequently overlooked in the public record is another layer of the game: how a case is managed on the docket. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit offers a stark reminder that appellate advocates can lose significant ground on the merits because they also stumbled in the entryway. While Cohen successfully pressed a claim regarding overtime wages—which the employer voluntarily satisfied—the appellate court’s disposition of his remaining claims exposes a harsh reality for litigants who treat appellate briefing as a generic extension of trial advocacy. By unraveling Cohen’s attempt to bring new relief and statutory authority as if it were a proper addendum to a summary judgment appeal, the Eighth Circuit highlights the significant procedural costs of failing to align claims with formal pleading and argument.

For interested observers, the factual backdrop of *Cohen* was relatively straightforward but with sufficient stakes to warrant the court’s time. Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer, sued his employer, Consilio, alleging violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Prior to appeal, a key event changed the dynamics significantly: Consilio essentially made the bulk of Cohen’s money demands good with a stipulation that it had paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed. This stipulation meant that Cohen’s case had transformed into a technical battle over penalties and injunctive relief, with the district court granting summary judgment. Cohen then appealed, but the Eighth Circuit decided that even if he had presented a compelling argument on the MWTA penalties, the record and the briefing strategy kept these claims from landing.

The primary lesson of the case centers on the waiver of claims, specifically regarding penalties under the MWTA. Cohen pressed the district court and later the Eighth Circuit for civil penalties under § 181.032(f), arguing that Consilio had failed to provide statutory notice of the change in overtime policy. However, the Eighth Circuit pointed out that Cohen’s briefing offered little more than an assertion of entitlement without supporting legal authority. The court applied—and clearly emphasized—the principle that a plaintiff cannot simply build a case on a blank slate at the appellate level. As the majority noted, "inasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." This adherence to the standard established in *Milligan v. City of Red Oak* serves as a procedural warning: there is no backdoor for a claims waiver. Even if the substantive argument might have been viable, the failure to cite statutory backing during the briefing process, after having previously argued the claim to the district court, effectively extinguished it on appeal.

Beyond the statutory waivers, the court was equally firm when Cohen attempted to shift the contours of injunctive relief without a formal change to the pleadings. Cohen initially argued in his complaint for specific relief, but later, in his summary judgment briefing, he pivoted to request a different, affirmative injunctive order for the rescission of the overtime policy. The Eighth Circuit was unwilling to look away from this change in posture, noting that Cohen had earlier modified his request on the spot rather than through an amended complaint. Consistent with Rule 15(a) and the precedent set in *InfoDeli, LLC* (cited in the 8th Circuit’s own 2025 jurisprudence), new claims or changes in relief must be properly raised through amended complaints rather than opposition briefs. Because the district court never considered this specific change and it was not procedurally correct for Cohen to raise it via brief, the appellate court did not disturb the order on these grounds.

Analytically, the combination of these two holding points provides a cautionary tale for appellate counsel. The Eighth Circuit’s decision suggests that courts are prepared to stay within the "borders of the relevant controversy" as defined at the pleading stage, regardless of whether the litigant tries to retreat and retoken from inside the appellate process. The case demonstrates that calculating the "procedural cost of casual claims"—as the blog concept puts it—is a real financial risk. Cohen might have been entitled to more at trial, or he might have been permitted to introduce new evidence on penalties, but by treating the deluge of briefing for a different type of relief as a casual claim, he surrendered his right to argue for it on appeal.

Ultimately, *Cohen v. Consilio* will likely be cited in the lower federal courts that have jurisdiction over federal employment law claims and federal claims under Minnesota law as a reinforcement of the formalities of appellate procedure. While the lower courts remain free to determine, for instance, the standing and mooting of the MFLSA penalties claim—leading to the remand on that specific issue—the irony for Cohen is clear: he did not lose because the law was unclear on his side, but because he breached the procedural contract of appellate practice. Lower courts and practitioners who fail to anticipate that appellate briefing is as much about preservation as it is about persuasion are the ones who will find themselves remedying the system of justice without the relief they sought.
