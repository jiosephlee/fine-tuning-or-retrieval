Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Legislative Intent as a Litigator: Reading Section 181.101 Through the Commissioner's Lens

Workers' compensation and wage-and-hour law often operate through a duality of federal and state standards, creating a labyrinth where plaintiffs must navigate overlapping statutes and enforcement mechanisms. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit confronted this complexity in a wage theft case brought by a licensed attorney document reviewer. While the court affirmed that Consilio had not paid Cohen his back wages, the real doctrinal hook of the opinion lies in its narrow statutory interpretation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). By relying heavily on legislative silence and comparing sections of the Minnesota statutes, the Eighth Circuit taught litigators a valuable lesson: in the absence of explicit authorization, private plaintiffs may not secure the punitive penalties that the legislature arguably restricted to the state’s Labor Commissioner.

## The Limits of Private Enforcement under MPWA Section 181.101

The core of Cohen’s appeal hinged on whether he could recover statutory penalties for Consilio’s violation of the MPWA. Consilio had abruptly changed its overtime policy for Minnesota-based licensed attorney reviewers in 2019, eliminating premium overtime pay. While Consilio subsequently paid Cohen back wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Cohen sought additional statutory penalties under the MPWA under the premise that these were available to any aggrieved employee. The Eighth Circuit, however, sided with Consilio, holding that § 181.101(a) strictly reserves the authority to collect penalties to the Commissioner.

This holding rests on a strict textual reading of the MPWA. The court noted that § 181.101(a) stipulates that penalties are charged and collected by the Commissioner, and further provides that any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee. Crucially, for the employee, the statute only preserves the right to prosecute a claim for *wages*. The court reasoned that because the statute does not explicitly grant the employee the right to seek penalties, the legislature did not intend to grant it. This is a classic application of the canon of expressio unius: when the specific remedial power of the Commissioner is listed, the omission of similar power for the individual plaintiff is intentional.

## Statutory Comparison and the "They Know How" Doctrine

What makes this decision particularly instructive for legal writers and attorneys is the court’s method of statutory interpretation. In interpreting the "plain meaning" of § 181.101, the Eighth Circuit did not look in isolation; it looked across the statutory landscape to distinguish this section from just-title's § 181.13 and § 181.14. These sections explicitly allow discharged or resigned employees to "charge and collect a penalty" upon demand from the employee.

The court leaned on the precedent set by *Lagasse v. Horton* to make a critical point: if the legislature wants to empower an employee with a statutory penalty in one context (termination or resignation), but not in another (unpaid wages), the legislature "knows how" to accomplish that. This comparison allows the court to read an unspoken restriction into the statute. It prevents parties from arguing that the plain language of § 181.101 without more creates a private enforcement scheme. Instead, the opinion emphasizes that "aggressed parties" suing without reliance on the Commissioner are only entitled to the wages owed, not the additional financial whip typically associated with statutory penalties. This ensures that the deterrence strictures remain firmly in the public hand of the Commissioner.

## Doctrinal Implications: Textualism in State Law Litigation

The decision has significant downstream effects for wage theft practitioners operating in Minnesota. Historically, wage classes often bundle wage claims with liquidated damages or statutory penalties to increase leverage in settlements or to stricten the court’s findings on the employer’s bad faith. *Cohen v. Consilio* serves as a reminder that not all statutes designed to protect laborers grant private standing to sue for penalties. Even where a plaintiff has a statutory right to sue for unpaid wages and commissions under § 181.101, the remedy might not extend to penalties without a more explicit textual grant.

The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on this "plain meaning" approach aligns with the increasing trend in federal courts to defer to the specific text of state statutes rather than inferring a private cause of action where none is plainly written. This creates a tension between the policy goals of protecting workers—often best served by allowing private retaliation of statutory penalties—and the requirement of strict statutory construction. For the lower courts, it means they must be careful to distinguish between the substantive right to wages and the remedy for that right. In this case, the distinction was fatal to Cohen’s MPWA claim but left his status under the MFLSA intact.

## The Mootness Pivot and Final Relief

While the statutory interpretation of the MPWA was the analytical crux of the majority’s decision, the docket list also highlights the court’s attention to the MFLSA claim, where costs were paid under settlement stipulations. The Eighth Circuit vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA claim solely based on mootness concerns regarding whether Consilio’s internal policy had remained in place, noting that the voluntary cessation of the alleged violation does not necessarily guarantee mootness. This aspect of the ruling reinforces the procedural importance of the staying of judgment; without the policy continuing, Cohen had won back wages and liquidated damages but faced an uncertain path toward final enforcement.

Ultimately, *Cohen v. Consilio* is a powerful lesson on the art of reading state statutes through the lens of contemporaneous provision rather than assumed intent. It suggests to litigators that the road to full damages in employment disputes may be narrower than it appears when the employer successfully invokes the text of the MPWA. For the future of similar suits, this case dictates that plaintiffs must look closely for the specific statutory fuel that powers the penalty claim, rather than assuming it is available simply because the statute itself is violated. The Eighth Circuit ensures that, for the most part, enforcing the penalty remains the job of the state, not the citizen.
