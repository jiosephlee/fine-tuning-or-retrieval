### Does Minnesota Statute § 181.101(a) expressly authorize private employees to recover statutory penalties for wage violations?
Question:
In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment on the MPWA claim because the employee cannot seek 'average daily wage penalties.' The opinion relies heavily on the plain language of § 181.101(a) referencing 'the Commissioner.' However, I'm confused about the methodology here. If the statute says 'the Commissioner may charge and collect... any money collected... shall be paid to the employee,' does that imply the employee's right to suing is irrelevant to the penalty itself? Is this a 'jurisdictional' bar or a substantive one that precludes the employee from ever bringing this specific claim to federal court?
Answer:
No. Based on the Eighth Circuit’s analysis in *Cohen v. Consilio*, 8th Cir. (Oct. 6, 2025), Minnesota Statute § 181.101(a) does not express authorization for private employees to recover statutory penalties; instead, the court interpreted the statute to vest that exclusive authority in the Commissioner of Employment and Economic Security.

**Statutory Construction and Plain Language**
The Eighth Circuit applied a standard of plain textual interpretation. The opinion notes that § 181.101(a) explicitly provides that "the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." It further states that any money collected by the Commissioner "must be paid to the employee concerned." The court reasoned that because the statute only references the Commissioner as the party entitled to initiate the collection process and identify the penalty, the private employee does not possess an independent right to seek those specific penalties under this section. As the Eighth Circuit affirmed, "the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner], with any collected funds going to the employee" (*Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.*, 570 F. Supp. 3d at 695).

**Legislative Intent**
The court reinforced this conclusion by distinguishing § 181.101(a) from other sections of the same Act where the legislature explicitly empowered employees. Citing § 181.13(a) and § 181.14, subd. 2 (which allow employees to charge and collect penalties upon certain demands), the court applied the "legislative knows how" inference. Because the legislature contained in those sections but omitted it from § 181.101, the court concluded the omission was intentional. Therefore, an employee may prosecute a "claim for wages" under § 181.101, but they cannot seek the statutory average daily wage penalties as defined therein.

**Substantive Bar vs. Jurisdiction**
Regarding your methodology question, this operates primarily as a substantive bar to the specific claim rather than a jurisdictional defect. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the summary judgment on the MPWA claim because the district court correctly applied the law: when the unambiguous text of a state statute grants the right to seek a specific remedy (penalties) solely to a state official, a private plaintiff lacks the statutory entitlement to recover that remedy in federal court. 

The court distinguished this from the MFLSA claim (§ II.B) by addressing Zittig’s standing and mootness concerns specifically regarding the *continuing violation* required for civil penalties. However, for the MPWA claim, the ruling was dispositive: the employee cannot bring the claim regardless of the employer's conduct because the statute precludes the private party from requesting that specific relief. Thus, the employee is precluded from bringing the MPWA penalty claim to federal court, not because the federal court lacks power (jurisdiction), but because the claim itself is substantively noncause of action under the relevant state law.

### Can a plaintiff maintain standing to seek statutory penalties under the MFLSA after stipulating to the payment of all wages and liquidated damages?
Question:
The opinion vacates the district court's judgment on the MFLSA claim due to mootness concerns. Cohen stipulated he was paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, yet the court found a serious concern regarding jurisdiction to decide statutory penalties. But how does the stipulation affect *standing*? If the violation (underpayment) was 'cured' by payment, why does the court insist on the 'claim for civil penalties'? Is it because penalties are punitive/continuing rather than compensatory? I'm struggling to reconcile how payment of back wages leaves the threshold standing issue unresolved when the injury (unpaid wages) is seemingly mitigated.
Answer:
**Answer**

No, a stipulation that all wages and liquidated damages have been paid does not automatically moot a claim for statutory civil penalties, provided the alleged violative conduct (policy or practice) remains in place. Although the personal economic injury regarding back pay is cured, the Eighth Circuit explains in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* that standing to seek civil penalties is distinct from standing to recover compensatory wages.

**1. Distinction Between Compensatory Relief and Deterrent Penalties**
Under the MFLSA, a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each form of relief sought (*Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017)). While Cohen's claim for unpaid wages was cured by the stipulation, the claim for statutory penalties serves a different statutory purpose. Strictly speaking, these penalties are payable to the Minnesota Commissioner, not the plaintiff, and serve to "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." (*Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env't Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000), cited in *Cohen*).

**2. Continuing Interest and Mootness**
Under the *Friends of the Earth* standard, a plaintiff must maintain a "continuing interest" throughout the litigation (*Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189). The Eighth Circuit notes that although voluntary cessation of a practice does not automatically cure standing (*City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)), the issue turns on whether the "allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur" (*Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189).

In *Cohen*, the parties stipulated they were paid all overtime wages, yet whether Consilio's violative policy remained in place "remains in dispute." If the policy persists, the violation is ongoing, and Cohen may maintain standing to seek civil penalties under the MFLSA. However, if the policy is no longer in place and it is "absolutely clear" that wrongful conduct will not recur, the claim may be moot (*Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189).

**3. Procedural Outcome in *Cohen***
Because the district court granted summary judgment regarding the MFLSA claim, and the stipulated payments did not definitively resolve whether the violative policy remained in effect, the Eighth Circuit found a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction."

Consequently, the court vacated the grant of summary judgment regarding the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction (*Cohen*, id. at II.B, § III). The stipulation of wage payment did not kill the statutory penalty claim; rather, it shifted the legal analysis to whether the underlying violative policy remained in effect to support the claim for penalties.

### Why is a lack of legal authority or argument in the briefing grounds for waiver in a summary judgment appeal?
Question:

The opinion states, 'Cohen does not dispute that... he is entitled to penalties' but has 'no statutory authority' or 'meaningful argument' in support of reversal. Or is this an application of the rule that appellate courts are generally not 'correcting errors' if the appellant fails to marshal the necessary legal theory in the brief as per *Fed. R. App. P. 28*?
Answer:
The court's treatment of the MWTA claim in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* is a straightforward application of the appellate waiver doctrine. As you suggested in your second hypothetical, federal appellate courts generally do not review legal issues that have not been properly briefed or submitted for argument.

The relevant text is found in **Section II.C** of the opinion. There, the Eighth Circuit addresses the MWTA claim by noting that Cohen "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute." The court then explicitly states:

> "With no meaningful argument in support of reversal, we will not disturb the district court’s order dismissing this claim."

To support this decision, the opinion cites *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355 (8th Cir. 2000), stating: "[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it."

This citation confirms your intuition regarding *Fed. R. App. P. 28*: it is indeed an instruction that the appellant must marshal the necessary legal theory. The court's refusal to "guess" the law—i.e., to infer judicial authority for a penalty under an implicit statute—stems from the principle that appellate review is a process of evaluating the arguments presented below and during the appeal. If a party does not provide a legal basis for the reversal in the briefing, that issue is deemed waived, and the appellate court will not undertake *sua sponte* legal research or analysis for that specific claim.

Thus, the decision rests on procedural waiver based on the brief's contents, rather than a substantive determination of the MWTA's provisions. Because Cohen failed to brief the statutory interpretation necessary to support his penalties, the issue was waived regardless of whether implicit statutory authority might theoretically exist.

### Under what circumstances does a change in requests for injunctive relief during briefing constitute an impermissible 'new claim'?
Question:
The Eighth Circuit vacated the appeal without addressing Cohen's request for injunctive relief that Consilio rescind its policy. Cohen allegedly 'changed course' in his summary judgment briefing compared to the original complaint. The court applied the *InfoDeli* rule that 'new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.' But isn't injunctive relief involving the same cause of action? Why does the fact that it is an affirmative equitable remedy change it categorically compared to a legal claim for damages?
Answer:
**Short Answer**
The change in relief does not constitute a "new claim" in the substantive sense of creating a new cause of action; rather, under the *InfoDeli* rule applied in *Cohen*, a change in the specific request for relief constitutes a "new claim" for procedural purposes, requiring an amended complaint rather than a briefing amendment. The equitable nature of the remedy is not the categorical driver, but rather the failure to amend the pleadings under Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

**Detailed Explanation**

Under *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, 2025 WL (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (Docket No. 24-2079), the Eighth Circuit distinguishes between the "claim" itself (the legal theory or cause of action) and the specific "relief" sought within that claim. While students often conflate the two, the procedural rules treat them distinctly regarding pleadings.

**1. The *InfoDeli* Standard and Rule 15(a)**
The court explicitly applies the *InfoDeli, LLC* rule, citing 136 F.4th at 801 n.6, which states: "**'new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.'"** This is grounded in Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

The *Cohen* court reasoned that a claim's components—including the specific injunctive relief sought—must be preserved through the initial complaint or a subsequent amendment. "In his complaint, Cohen sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief, only to later change course and request different relief... in his summary judgment briefing." The court characterized this not merely as a change in argument, but as a request for relief that was not properly "presented" via the pleading process.

**2. Why the "Equitable" Distinction is Irrelevant**
Regarding your question on why the affirmative equitable remedy supposedly changes matters: The Eighth Circuit does not hold that equitable remedies are inherently subject to stricter pleading standards than legal ones. Instead, the standard applies to the *modification of the relief request itself*.

Because the request for relief is integral to the "claim" as defined for appellate preservation, Cohen’s failure to amend the complaint to reflect the "different relief" sought in the briefing meant the issue was not properly before the district court or the appellate court. The court states: "**The district court—understandably—did not consider it. Thus, the issue is not properly before us.**"

**3. Procedural Finality**
The *Cohen* opinion treats the specific scope of the injunctive relief as a matter of procedural bar. Under *InfoDeli*, courts cannot consider relief that effectively alters the scope of the originally pleaded claims without the procedural rigor of an amended complaint. Cohen’s argument that the injunctive relief was tied to the "same cause of action" is accurate regarding the *wage theft statute* (MFLSA), but the *specific equitable remedy requested during briefing* was treated as a new request that triggered the *InfoDeli* bar.

**Conclusion**
In *Cohen*, the barrier was not the equitable nature of the injunction, but the violation of Rule 15(a). The "new claim" doctrine prohibits parties from expanding the scope of relief—either by adding new claims or changing the specific relief sought—after the fact unless they file an amended complaint. Thus, Cohen’s briefing arguments were impermissible rather than a substantive expansion of the legal cause of action.

### What is the specific 'voluntary cessation' standard an employer must meet to moot a claim under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*?
Question:
The district court remanded for jurisdictional determination on the MFLSA claim because of the *Laidlaw* mootness concern. The opinion notes that while voluntary cessation does not always deprivewith jurisdiction, the employer must show 'it is absolutely clear that its wrongful conduct will not recur.' I need clarification: must the employer prove the policy is dead, or just that the practice is ended? Given Consilio paid Cohen all wages he alleged were owed, is the employer's 'cessation of a challenged practice' a sufficient affirmative defense to establish standing for the penalties if the policy remains official?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, 24-2079 (8th Cir. 2025), the "voluntary cessation" standard requires the employer to show that **it is "absolutely clear that its wrongful conduct will not recur."**

Simply putting an end to a specific practice or paying back wages is not sufficient to moot the claim if the underlying policy remains in place. The Eighth Circuit emphasizes that for the "ongoing unlawful conduct" associated with the Eighth Circuit's statutory penalties (which penalize future violations) to end, the employer must prove the **policy itself** is no longer in place.

The opinion states:
> "Prior to Consilio’s motion for summary judgment, the parties agreed that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, but whether Consilio’s policy is still in place remains in dispute."

Because the dispute centered on the policy rather than just the labor payments, the court noted that while redress for past harms (wages) occurred, that does not automatically remove the injury regarding future penalties. The court explicitly linked the mootness inquiry to the status of the policy:

> "If Consilio’s policy is no longer in place and it is 'absolutely clear' that its wrongful conduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may in fact be moot."

Conversely, the opinion warns that if the policy remains:
> "If Consilio’s policy remains, Cohen’s claim for civil penalties may still be viable."

Therefore, in *Cohen*, the employer could not rely on the cessation of the "practice" (i.e., paying the money) alone. The employer must demonstrate that the *violative overtime policy* is definitively dead. Because the status of the policy was "in dispute" and not "absolutely clear" from the record, the Eighth Circuit vacated the summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine the jurisdictional issue. Until the policy status is resolved, Cohen retains standing to seek penalties under the MFLSA.

### Why did the 8th Circuit remand specifically for a 'continuing interest' determination rather than simply ruling on the merits of the MFLSA penalties?
Question:
The Eighth Circuit held that the requisite personal interest must continue and vacated the grant of summary judgment regarding the MFLSA penalties, remanding for the district court to determine 'jurisdiction.' Why not resolve whether the case is moot de novo? If the employer stipulated to paying wages, shouldn't the continuing obligation to pay penalties be a question for the judge to decide rather than an appellate court to remand? In *Laidlaw*, the Supreme Court allowed officials to prove cessation, so why does this Eighth Circuit opinion require a jurisdictional 'light most favorable' review on remand rather than an appellate ruling?
Answer:
**Why the 8th Circuit Remanded for a "Continuing Interest" Determination Instead of Ruling on Merits**

In short, the Eighth Circuit remanded the MFLSA penalty claim for a jurisdictional determination (mootness/standing) rather than resolving the case on the merits because, under the *Laidlaw* standard, the court cannot entertain statutory penalty claims until the plaintiff demonstrates a "continuing interest" in the cessation of illegal conduct. The district court must find as a matter of fact whether the employer’s violative policy remains in place; only if that wrong is ongoing does the plaintiff maintain standing to seek penalties, which are intended to deter future violations.

Here is a breakdown of the legal reasoning in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* regarding this specific issue.

**1. Penalties Require Proof of Continuing Injury**
The Eighth Circuit’s explanation in Section II.B distinguishes between back wages (compensatory) and penalties. As the opinion notes:
> "Civil penalties... may 'afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct' because such penalties may 'encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.' *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000)."

Because statutory penalties are rooted in the necessity of relating to an *ongoing* or *threatened* violation to achieve deterrence, the court applied the mootness doctrine from *Laidlaw*. If the defendant has voluntarily ceased the practice (paid the back wages), the claim may be moot unless the plaintiff can show the wrongful practice is absolutely unlikely to recur. If the practice is still in place, the claim is not moot and the plaintiff has standing.

**2. The Threshold Issue is Factual, Not Legal**
The district court granted summary judgment on all claims. However, Cohen stipulated that back wages were paid, but whether the "violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute."

The Eighth Circuit recognized that determining mootness in this context is not purely a question of law for the appellate court to decide *de novo*. It is a threshold question regarding the Plaintiff's jurisdiction and the factual reality of the violation. The opinion states:
> "Whether Consilio’s policy remains [in place], Cohen’s claim for civil penalties may still be viable... However, if Consilio’s policy is no longer in place... Cohen’s MFLSA claim may in fact be moot."

Because the status of the policy determines whether the case is moot *at all*, the Eighth Circuit determined that it could not resolve the merits of the statutory interpretation (whether the MFLSA *allows* for penalties) without first establishing that the court had jurisdiction to decide the case in the first place.

**3. Mootness as a Jurisdictional Threshold**
The court framed this specifically as a jurisdictional matter because, without a continuing interest, the Article III requirement for standing is absent. *Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests. Inc.* and *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021), are cited for the proposition that the requisite personal interest must continue throughout the litigation.

The Eighth Circuit explicitly wrote:
> "Federal courts are not 'license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest.'"

Because the parties could not agree on the current state of the violation (policy in place vs. cancelled), the appellate court could not rule on the merits (which assumes jurisdiction exists) or declare definitive mootness. The 8th Circuit therefore vacated the grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim specifically to remand for the district court to determine its "jurisdiction"—meaning, did the plaintiff prove the violation was ongoing enough to sustain the status of a live case?

**Conclusion**
The Eighth Circuit refused to rule *de novo* on the merits of the MFLSA penalty statute because a jurisdictional prerequisite (standing/mootness) was not met based on the facts in the record. The court is not saying the MFLSA penalties are wrong; it is saying the court lacks the authority to rule on them because the employer has likely cured the violation, rendering the claim moot, or the ongoing violation status was a factual dispute requiring district court resolution before merits could be considered.

The distinction between the stipulated wages/liquidated damages and attorney’s fees is crucial here. The record establishes that Cohen stipulated to receiving all overtime wages and liquidated damages ($3,225.21 in overtime, $462 in liquidated damages). While the parties stipulated payment of these personal remedies, the question of whether Cohen has standing to seek *civil penalties* remains in dispute. The Majority “otherwise affirm[ed]" the fee award, implying that the fee entitlement could survive the remand regarding the penalty remand. Kobes suggests that if the plaintiff lacks standing or if the case is remanded for monet analysis to determine mootness, the fee award itself lacks a sufficient case or controversy to stand.

Thus, the disagreement is not on the existence of Article III standing generally, but on whether the attorney’s fees can survive a remand for mootness on the underlying penalties when a stipulation has already resolved the personal relief. Fees are typically considered a separate but interdependent form of relief; the Majority treated them as surviving the remand, while the Concurrence treated them as inseparable from the unresolved mootness question. This reflects a procedural difference in how the remedy is applied, not a conflict in the substantive Article III doctrine.
