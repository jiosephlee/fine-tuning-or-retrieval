Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Under what circumstances does a change in requests for injunctive relief during briefing constitute an impermissible 'new claim'?
Question:
The Eighth Circuit vacated the appeal without addressing Cohen's request for injunctive relief that Consilio rescind its policy. Cohen allegedly 'changed course' in his summary judgment briefing compared to the original complaint. The court applied the *InfoDeli* rule that 'new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.' But isn't injunctive relief involving the same cause of action? Why does the fact that it is an affirmative equitable remedy change it categorically compared to a legal claim for damages?
Answer:
**Short Answer**
The change in relief does not constitute a "new claim" in the substantive sense of creating a new cause of action; rather, under the *InfoDeli* rule applied in *Cohen*, a change in the specific request for relief constitutes a "new claim" for procedural purposes, requiring an amended complaint rather than a briefing amendment. The equitable nature of the remedy is not the categorical driver, but rather the failure to amend the pleadings under Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

**Detailed Explanation**

Under *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, 2025 WL (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (Docket No. 24-2079), the Eighth Circuit distinguishes between the "claim" itself (the legal theory or cause of action) and the specific "relief" sought within that claim. While students often conflate the two, the procedural rules treat them distinctly regarding pleadings.

**1. The *InfoDeli* Standard and Rule 15(a)**
The court explicitly applies the *InfoDeli, LLC* rule, citing 136 F.4th at 801 n.6, which states: "**'new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.'"** This is grounded in Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

The *Cohen* court reasoned that a claim's components—including the specific injunctive relief sought—must be preserved through the initial complaint or a subsequent amendment. "In his complaint, Cohen sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief, only to later change course and request different relief... in his summary judgment briefing." The court characterized this not merely as a change in argument, but as a request for relief that was not properly "presented" via the pleading process.

**2. Why the "Equitable" Distinction is Irrelevant**
Regarding your question on why the affirmative equitable remedy supposedly changes matters: The Eighth Circuit does not hold that equitable remedies are inherently subject to stricter pleading standards than legal ones. Instead, the standard applies to the *modification of the relief request itself*.

Because the request for relief is integral to the "claim" as defined for appellate preservation, Cohen’s failure to amend the complaint to reflect the "different relief" sought in the briefing meant the issue was not properly before the district court or the appellate court. The court states: "**The district court—understandably—did not consider it. Thus, the issue is not properly before us.**"

**3. Procedural Finality**
The *Cohen* opinion treats the specific scope of the injunctive relief as a matter of procedural bar. Under *InfoDeli*, courts cannot consider relief that effectively alters the scope of the originally pleaded claims without the procedural rigor of an amended complaint. Cohen’s argument that the injunctive relief was tied to the "same cause of action" is accurate regarding the *wage theft statute* (MFLSA), but the *specific equitable remedy requested during briefing* was treated as a new request that triggered the *InfoDeli* bar.

**Conclusion**
In *Cohen*, the barrier was not the equitable nature of the injunction, but the violation of Rule 15(a). The "new claim" doctrine prohibits parties from expanding the scope of relief—either by adding new claims or changing the specific relief sought—after the fact unless they file an amended complaint. Thus, Cohen’s briefing arguments were impermissible rather than a substantive expansion of the legal cause of action.
