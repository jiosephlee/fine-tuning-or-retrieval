Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why is a lack of legal authority or argument in the briefing grounds for waiver in a summary judgment appeal?
Question:
I find the court's treatment of the MWTA claim puzzling. The opinion states, 'Cohen does not dispute that... he is entitled to penalties' but has 'no statutory authority' or 'meaningful argument' in support of reversal. The court then cited *Milligan* stating, 'he has waived the issue.' Does this mean that if a state-law statute was implicit, the federal court can still guess it regardless of the lack of briefing? Or is this an application of the rule that appellate courts are generally not 'correcting errors' if the appellant fails to marshal the necessary legal theory in the brief as per *Fed. R. App. P. 28*?
Answer:
The court's treatment of the MWTA claim in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* is a straightforward application of the appellate waiver doctrine. As you suggested in your second hypothetical, federal appellate courts generally do not review legal issues that have not been properly briefed or submitted for argument.

The relevant text is found in **Section II.C** of the opinion. There, the Eighth Circuit addresses the MWTA claim by noting that Cohen "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute." The court then explicitly states:

> "With no meaningful argument in support of reversal, we will not disturb the district court’s order dismissing this claim."

To support this decision, the opinion cites *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355 (8th Cir. 2000), stating: "[I]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it."

This citation confirms your intuition regarding *Fed. R. App. P. 28*: it is indeed an instruction that the appellant must marshal the necessary legal theory. The court's refusal to "guess" the law—i.e., to infer judicial authority for a penalty under an implicit statute—stems from the principle that appellate review is a process of evaluating the arguments presented below and during the appeal. If a party does not provide a legal basis for the reversal in the briefing, that issue is deemed waived, and the appellate court will not undertake *sua sponte* legal research or analysis for that specific claim.

Thus, the decision rests on procedural waiver based on the brief's contents, rather than a substantive determination of the MWTA's provisions. Because Cohen failed to brief the statutory interpretation necessary to support his penalties, the issue was waived regardless of whether implicit statutory authority might theoretically exist.
