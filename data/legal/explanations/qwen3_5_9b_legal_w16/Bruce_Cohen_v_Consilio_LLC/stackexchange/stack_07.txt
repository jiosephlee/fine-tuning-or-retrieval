Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does the concurrence's disagreement with the majority regarding attorney's fees suggest a conflict in the 'Article III' standing requirement?
Question:
The concurring opinion by Judge Kobes is clear that he would vacate the judgment and remand for mootness but specifically 'disagrees' regarding attorney's fees, stating 'Neither party briefed the issue.' I am trying to understand if this implies that while the main holding focuses on mootness, the remedies are procedural rather than substantive. If Cohen had already stipulated to receiving all liquidated damages and wages under the stipulation, is the distinction between those and attorney's fees typically considered separately in class-action contexts? Does the concurrence suggest that standing is an independent bar to fees even if standing exists for the penalty claim?
Answer:
**Title:** Does the concurrence’s disagreement regarding attorney’s fees indicate a conflict in the Article III standing requirement?

**Body:**

No, the concurrence’s disagreement does not suggest a fundamental conflict in the *Friends of the Earth* Article III standing requirement for penalties versus fees. Instead, Judge Kobes’ opinion reveals a divergence regarding the severability of attorney’s fees from the underlying merits of the MFLSA penalty claim.

The relevant legal standard for Article III standing is explicitly stated in the majority opinion in Section II.B. Citing *Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc.* and *Davis v. Fed. Election Comm'n*, the Court notes that "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought" and that "the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." (Id. at 1181, citing *Friends of the Earth*).

The distinction between the stipulated wages/liquidated damages and attorney’s fees is crucial here. The record establishes that Cohen stipulated to receiving all overtime wages and liquidated damages ($3,225.21 in overtime, $462 in liquidated damages). While the parties stipulated payment of these personal remedies, the question of whether Cohen has standing to seek *civil penalties* remains in dispute. As Section II.B explains, penalties may afford redress for deterrence but require that the plaintiff demonstrate a continuing injury (*Friends of the Earth*). Because the parties stipulated the wages were paid, the standing for the wage claims was moot, but the standing for penalties depended on whether the underlying policy remained in place.

The Majority “otherwise affirm[ed]" the fee award, implying that the fee entitlement could survive the remand regarding the penalty remand. Judge Kobes, however, vacates the ruling as to attorney’s fees in the concurrence. This signals that Judge Kobes considers the fee award inextricably linked to the viability of the MFLSA claims, aligning with the *friends of Earth* language that standing must exist for each form of relief sought. Kobes suggests that if the plaintiff lacks standing or if the case is remanded for monet analysis to determine mootness, the fee award itself lacks a sufficient case or controversy to stand.

Thus, the disagreement is not on the existence of Article III standing generally, but on whether the attorney’s fees can survive a remand for mootness on the underlying penalties when a stipulation has already resolved the personal relief. Fees are typically considered a separate but interdependent form of relief; the Majority treated them as surviving the remand, while the Concurrence treated them as inseparable from the unresolved mootness question. This reflects a procedural difference in how the remedy is applied, not a conflict in the substantive Article III doctrine.
