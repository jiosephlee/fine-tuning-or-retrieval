Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Can a plaintiff maintain standing to seek statutory penalties under the MFLSA after stipulating to the payment of all wages and liquidated damages?
Question:
The opinion vacates the district court's judgment on the MFLSA claim due to mootness concerns. Cohen stipulated he was paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, yet the court found a serious concern regarding jurisdiction to decide statutory penalties. But how does the stipulation affect *standing*? If the violation (underpayment) was 'cured' by payment, why does the court insist on the 'claim for civil penalties'? Is it because penalties are punitive/continuing rather than compensatory? I'm struggling to reconcile how payment of back wages leaves the threshold standing issue unresolved when the injury (unpaid wages) is seemingly mitigated.
Answer:
**Answer**

No, a stipulation that all wages and liquidated damages have been paid does not automatically moot a claim for statutory civil penalties, provided the alleged violative conduct (policy or practice) remains in place. Although the personal economic injury regarding back pay is cured, the Eighth Circuit explains in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* that standing to seek civil penalties is distinct from standing to recover compensatory wages.

**1. Distinction Between Compensatory Relief and Deterrent Penalties**
Under the MFLSA, a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each form of relief sought (*Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017)). While Cohen's claim for unpaid wages was cured by the stipulation, the claim for statutory penalties serves a different statutory purpose. Strictly speaking, these penalties are payable to the Minnesota Commissioner, not the plaintiff, and serve to "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." (*Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env't Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000), cited in *Cohen*).

**2. Continuing Interest and Mootness**
Under the *Friends of the Earth* standard, a plaintiff must maintain a "continuing interest" throughout the litigation (*Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189). The Eighth Circuit notes that although voluntary cessation of a practice does not automatically cure standing (*City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)), the issue turns on whether the "allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur" (*Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189).

In *Cohen*, the parties stipulated they were paid all overtime wages, yet whether Consilio's violative policy remained in place "remains in dispute." If the policy persists, the violation is ongoing, and Cohen may maintain standing to seek civil penalties under the MFLSA. However, if the policy is no longer in place and it is "absolutely clear" that wrongful conduct will not recur, the claim may be moot (*Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189).

**3. Procedural Outcome in *Cohen***
Because the district court granted summary judgment regarding the MFLSA claim, and the stipulated payments did not definitively resolve whether the violative policy remained in effect, the Eighth Circuit found a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction."

Consequently, the court vacated the grant of summary judgment regarding the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction (*Cohen*, id. at II.B, § III). The stipulation of wage payment did not kill the statutory penalty claim; rather, it shifted the legal analysis to whether the underlying violative policy remained in effect to support the claim for penalties.
