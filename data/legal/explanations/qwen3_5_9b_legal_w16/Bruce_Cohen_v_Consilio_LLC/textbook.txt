Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# I. Factual Background, Procedural History, and Summary Judgment Standard

## Formation of the Employment Relationship

To understand the underlying dispute in *Cohen*, it is essential to first establish the context of the parties' relationship. Bruce Cohen, the plaintiff, served as an employee for Consilio, LLC, beginning in 2018. He held the specific role of an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer within the company. Consilio is identified in the record as a nationwide legal document review company that maintains operations with offices in Minnesota. This foundational employment relationship was significant because Cohen’s position as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer placed him at the center of the company’s internal compensation policies and overtime practices. For the courts to analyze whether a violation occurred, the record must show that Cohen was indeed an employee subject to the employment laws at issue. The district court and the Eighth Circuit did not dispute the existence or nature of this relationship, as it was the prerequisite for Cohen's claims to proceed. The clarity of the employment dates and role established the standing for Cohen to represent himself and other similarly situated employees, which became relevant when he filed the lawsuit on behalf of a putative class in August 2020.

## Implementation of the New Premium Overtime Policy

The central factual dispute regarding compensation began with a specific internal policy change announced by Consilio in 2019. In July 2019, Consilio sent a formal email notification to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers. This correspondence explained that the company would be instituting a new premium overtime policy starting in August 2019. Under this new policy, the company eliminated the requirement to pay overtime premium wages. Instead, the policy clarified that Cohen and his fellow employees would be compensated "at [their] base pay rate" for all hours worked, regardless of whether those hours exceeded the standard workweek restrictions. While the court notes the effective date of the policy, the enforcement tactics are critical to the case summary. This policy change directly precipitated the legal action because Cohen alleged that the failure to pay overtime constituted a violation of statutory wage requirements. The implementation of this policy was not applied retroactively to Cohen's pre-August 2019 hours, but the lawsuit specifically alleged violations occurring in the wake of this August 2019 shift in pay structure.

## Filing of the Lawsuit Alleging Violations of MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA

Cohen responded to the policy change by initiating litigation in August 2020. He sued Consilio on behalf of himself and a putative class, alleging that Consilio’s actions failed to pay owed overtime wages. In his complaint, Cohen asserted violations of three distinct statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). In seeking remedies for these alleged statutory violations, Cohen requested overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. The district court eventually granted summary judgment on these claims, but the Eighth Circuit review requires an understanding of the specific statutes Cohen alleged were violated. Central to this section is the distinction between the federal and state wage laws involved; the MFLSA claim was explicitly acknowledged in this opinion as a primary issue that would survive some scrutiny, while the MPWA and MWTA claims were subject to summary judgment regarding specific penalties. The filing of this lawsuit set the procedural posture of the case, creating a mediation path between stripping away the summary judgment and determining liability on the merits.

## Stipulated Payment of $256,010 in Wages and Liquidated Damages Prior to Appeal

A significant factual development occurred during the appellate proceedings that limited the scope of the dispute regarding actual wages. Following the filing of Cohen’s notices of appeal and Consilio’s motion for summary judgment, the parties engaged in a stipulated settlement of the core wage amounts. Prior to the appellate court hearing the case, Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers a total of $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded pursuant to the MFLSA claim. Specifically regarding Cohen, he personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages as part of these payments. The parties entered into a formal stipulation confirming that Cohen had been paid for all overtime wages he alleged were owed in the lawsuit, and that he did not dispute receiving any liquidated damages to which he was entitled under the MFLSA. This stipulation was crucial because it removed the dispute over the actual calculation of unpaid wages, shifting the focus of the legal battle to whether Cohen was still entitled to additional remedies, such as civil penalties, beyond these wage payments.

## Agreement Between Parties to Resolve Penalties as an Independent Issue

The primary remaining dispute between Cohen and Consilio concerned civil penalties rather than the underlying wages. After Consilio's payments were made, the parties could not agree on the specific penalties Cohen sought under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA. Cohen argued he was entitled to significant penalties under these various statutes, while Consilio argued the statutes did not permit Cohen to obtain penalties at all. To manage the litigation, the parties stipulated that the most efficient path forward would be for the Court to hear and decide Consilio’s summary judgment motion on these claims before any further discovery or litigation commenced. The parties acknowledged that if the district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment in full, no claims would remain and the case would be fully resolved. This procedural agreement was significant because it defined the legal battle as a question of law regarding penalties, rather than a question of fact regarding unpaid wages. It allowed the Eighth Circuit to review whether the statutory language actually authorized the employees to seek penalties as part of their independent remedy.

## District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment to Consilio

The legal proceedings concluded in the district court with a grant of summary judgment in favor of Consilio. After the parties’ stipulation and briefing, the district court granted Consilio’s summary judgment motion on all three claims: the MPWA, the MFLSA, and the MWTA claims. Cohen subsequently appealed this grant to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, challenging the district court’s decision to dismiss each claim. The district court’s ruling meant that Consilio was deemed to be entitled to judgment as a matter of law, implying that the evidence presented did not support Cohen’s claims of liability or statutory violation. When an appellate court reviews such a grant of summary judgment, it determines whether the legal standards were met based on the existing record. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in this case, which vacated the judgment in part related to the MFLSA claim, stems from its disagreement with the district court’s conclusion on this procedural and substantive issue.

## Eighth Circuit's Standard of Review: De Novo for Summary Judgment (View Evidence Most Favorable to Non-Movant)

For the purpose of legally analyzing the district court's grant of summary judgment, the Eighth Circuit applies a specific standard of review. When reviewing the district court's decision to grant summary judgment, the Eighth Circuit reviews the issue de novo. This means the appellate court reviews the case without deference to the lower court's decision and as if making the decision for the first time. Under this standard, the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn from the evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. The Eighth Circuit has established that summary judgment is proper only if there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, citing *Placzek v. Mayo Clinic*. In this case, the Eighth Circuit applied this standard to the MPWA and MWTA claims before vacating the summary judgment as to the MFLSA claim based on a jurisdictional standing issue regarding penalties.

# II. State Wage Statutes: MPWA and MWTA Analysis

## Breakdown of the MPWA Claim (§ 181.101): Employer vs. Commissioner Penalty Rights
In analyzing Cohen’s claim under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Eighth Circuit focused on the specific remedy Cohen sought: average daily wage penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Cohen argued that because Consilio violated the MPWA by paying employees without overtime premiums, he was entitled to enforce statutory penalties directly. However, the court distinguished between the statute of wages owed to an employee and the statute regarding civil penalties. While an employee may sue to collect wages, the authority to seek and collect statutory penalties under § 181.101 is textually reserved for a different actor. The court noted that when an employer is found to have violated § 181.101, the liability for "civil penalties or damages provided for in the section" is inconsistent with the procedural mechanism required to trigger them. The district court granted summary judgment on this claim because the statutory text limits who may bring the action for penalties, effectively creating a bifurcated enforcement scheme where the underlying wage debt may be contested by the employee, but the specific penalty provision requires Commissioner action.

## Statutory Construction: Plain Language Interpretation Requiring a Demand by the Commissioner
To resolve the dispute over penalty rights, the Eighth Circuit applied the canon of statutory interpretation that plain language and unambiguous meaning manifest legislative intent. The central subsection at issue, § 181.101(a), explicitly references the Commissioner regarding the enforcement of penalties. The court observed that the statute mandates the Commissioner to serve a demand for payment on behalf of an employee; only if payment is not made within ten days of that demand may the Commissioner charge and collect the wages and a penalty. It further instructed that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned. The court reasoned that if the legislature intended the employee himself to seek these penalties directly, the plain language of the statute would not have conditioned the availability of penalties on the Commissioner's service of a demand. This strict reading meant that Cohen's avoidance of the Commissioner's demand precluded his ability to recover these specific penalties from the employer under this section.

## Contrast with Other Sections: Legislative Intent Inferred from Statutory Inaction
A key component of the court's reasoning involved looking beyond the specific text of § 181.101 to the broader statute to infer legislative intent. The court highlighted a clear contrast between § 181.101 and other sections of the MPWA, specifically § 181.13(a) and § 181.14. In those sections, which address situations involving discharged or quitting employees, the legislature explicitly empowered the employee to "charge and collect a penalty" upon their own demand. Furthermore, the court pointed out that, unlike § 181.13 and 181.14, § 181.101 contains a specific section that states the current section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages, but it does not explicitly preserve the ability to sue for penalties. The court invoked the principle that the legislature knows how to accomplish an objective if it wishes to do so. The explicit inclusion of penalty rights in other sections, coupled with the silence and procedural restrictions in § 181.101, provided strong evidence that the omission was deliberate.

## Application of the Lagasse v. Horton Principle: Legislative Intent Inferred from Statutory Inaction
The Eighth Circuit relied on *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189 (Minn. 2022), to solidify the conclusion that Cohen lacked standing to seek penalties under § 181.101. *Lagasse* established that when the legislature has provided a specific remedy in one statute but not another, the legislative inaction is significant and indicates a conscious choice to exclude that remedy in the statute lacking it. The opinion explained that because the Minnesota legislature actively crafted specific provision for employees to recover penalties in the termination and resignation contexts (§ 181.13, § 181.14), the failure to do so in the non-termination context (§ 181.101) implied that the remedy was not intended to be available to the employee. Therefore, the district court's application of § 181.101 correctly deemed the statutory penalty unenforceable by the individual employee, reinforcing the summary judgment in favor of Consilio.

## Breakdown of the MWTA Claim (§ 181.032(f), (d)(4)): Notice Violations
Turning to the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), the court addressed Cohen’s claim that Consilio violated statute by failing to provide the required notice of a change in its overtime policy (§ 181.032(f), (d)(4)). The underlying facts showed that Consilio posted a communication regarding the new overtime policy, but Cohen alleged it was insufficient. This claim was distinct from the MPWA claim in that it turned on a specific notice requirement. However, in assessing the summary judgment, the court looked to the procedural posture agreed upon by the parties. Cohen had stipulated that Consilio had paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages apart from penalties. While Cohen argued that Consilio was required to rescind the policy, the court analyzed whether specific remedies were available under the MWTA text itself. The review indicated that Cohen was essentially asking for penalties for violating the notice statute, the analysis of which mirrored the strict statutory interpretation required for other wage claims.

## Procedural Waiver: Cohen Failed to Submit Legal Authority or Argument for MWTA Penalties on Appeal
The appellate court further considered Cohen’s request for penalties under the MWTA through the lens of appellate procedure. In the briefing before the Eighth Circuit, Cohen articulated a grievance that the district court provided little discussion of the MWTA claim and did not apply legal authority to it. However, the court noted that Cohen failed to provide his own legal authority or argument in his appellate brief to support the reversal. The Eighth Circuit applied a well-settled principle that a party forfeits an issue not supported with argument or legal authority. The court cited *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, explaining that "[i]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." Because Cohen did not present an argument showing that the district court erred in dismissing the claim, the court concluded it could not disturb the district court's order, and the issue was deemed waived on appeal.

## Outcome: Affirmation of Summary Judgment for Both State Claims
Following its analyses of the statutory construction and procedural posture, the Eighth Circuit reached its disposition regarding the state law claims. For the MPWA claim, the court affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment, holding that the scheme of § 181.101 limits penalty enforcement to the Commissioner. For the MWTA claim, the court affirmed the summary judgment based on the lack of proper argument and authority provided by Cohen. This section of the chapter illustrates that statutory rights to penalties are not guaranteed across all wage statutes in Minnesota and depend heavily on the precise text of the provision and procedural compliance by the plaintiff. The final result was an affirmation of the lower court's decision on the two Minnesota state statutes submitted for review, leaving only the federal MFLSA claim for remand and further jurisdictional analysis.

# III. Federal Statutory Claim: MFLSA Penalties and Mootness

## Distinction Between Statutory Penalties, Liquidated Damages, and Overtime Wages

To understand the Eighth Circuit’s refusal to affirm the district court’s decision regarding the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), a student must first grasp the specific types of relief available under the statute and how Cohen’s stipulation affected his case. Under the MFLSA, an employer aggrieved by a finding of liability faces multiple avenues for compensation. In this suit, Cohen alleged that Consilio failed to pay overtime wages. However, unlike other wage claims, Cohen stipulated in the record that Consilio had already compensated him for all overtime wages and liquidated damages due. The stipulation regarding liquidated damages and wages meant these specific liabilities were resolved, satisfying Cohen’s primary demand for restitution of lost money.

The distinction becomes critical when examining Cohen’s request for penalties. The parties agreed that MFLSA penalties are different from overtime wages and liquidated damages. Specifically, penalties are payable to the state Commissioner, not to the employee-plaintiff. Cohen could receive wages and liquidated damages directly into his own pocket, which creates a direct financial stake. However, penalties serve a different function: they are a sanction paid to the government regardless of whether the individual employee recovers monies for them. Cohen did not dispute the payments to the state regarding wages, but he argued he was entitled to these government-paid penalties. This raises a unique legal issue: if the employee receives no direct financial benefit from the penalty, does Cohen still have the right to seek it, and does his grievance extend if the employer stops paying overtime?

## The Concept of Standing and Personal Interest

The core of the Eighth Circuit’s analysis rests on the constitutional and statutory requirement that a plaintiff maintain standing throughout the litigation. Standing is not merely a hurdle at the start of a case; it must persist until judgment. A federal court adjudicates a live controversy, and that controversy requires an actual injury to the plaintiff. *Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433 (2017), establishes that a plaintiff "must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought." In Cohen’s case, the court identified a significant uncertainty regarding Cohen’s continuing interest in the claim for penalties. Because penalties go to the government rather than Cohen, the court asks: what is Cohen’s personal interest in ensuring those penalties are imposed?

If Cohen has already recovered all wages and liquidated damages, his personal financial injury regarding his past compensation seems to be cured. Cohen argued that the mere existence of penalties served a public interest by deterring Consilio from future illegal conduct. While civil penalties can technically afford redress to a plaintiff by encouraging defendants to discontinue unlawful conduct (*Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000)), Cohen still faces the jurisdictional question of whether the violation is still ongoing. If the violation is not ongoing, the "redress" of deterrence loses its relevance for the specific plaintiff suing. Consequently, the Eighth Circuit emphasized that the requisite personal interest must exist at the commencement of the litigation and continue throughout its existence.

## Application of the Voluntary Cessation Standard

The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning pivots on the doctrine of voluntary cessation. When an employer voluntarily stops the alleged illegal practice, such as changing an overtime policy, the plaintiff cannot simply assume the case is moot. An action claims is not moot solely because the defendant stops the alleged behavior; it remains live until it is absolutely clear that the behavior will not recur. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc.* outlines this standard: a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice "does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice." However, this rule acknowledges a limitation; a claim may become moot if subsequent events make it "absolutely clear" that the wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.

Cohen’s case falls into this gray area because the parties could not agree on the status of Consilio’s overtime policy. Prior to the summary judgment motion, Cohen and Consilio agreed Cohen had been paid all wages and liquidated damages. However, they could not agree on whether Consilio’s "violative overtime policy is still in place." If Consilio changed the policy and no longer violated the law, Cohen’s claim for penalties might be moot because he is no longer injured by the practice; the court would be issuing a judgment that served no purpose regarding his personal interest. Conversely, if the policy remained, Cohen retains a continuing stake in ensuring compliance through penalties. Because the Eighth Circuit could not resolve this factual dispute at the summary judgment stage, they could not definitively determine if Cohen had standing.

## Critical Question: Is the Violative Policy Still in Place?

The Eighth Circuit identified the critical factual uncertainty as the status of Consilio’s overtime policy. This inquiry is not merely a question of damages but a question of the existence of a live controversy. The court noted that federal courts are not "license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest." If Consilio maintained the policy, Cohen could argue the violation continues and the penalty is necessary. If Consilio abandoned the policy, Cohen’s claim for penalties loses its footing because the deterrent is no longer required for that specific employee.

The court’s willingness to remand stems from the fact that this factual dispute must be resolved to determine jurisdiction. The district court, by granting summary judgment on the merits of the MFLSA penalty claim, effectively bypassed the threshold question of whether the case was still live. The Eighth Circuit made clear that the viability of Cohen’s MFLSA claim for penalties depended on whether the alleged wrongful conduct had ceased. Without a factual determination on whether the policy remained, the Eighth Circuit concluded the district court had assumed its error.

## Mootness Analysis and Jurisdictional Constraints

Applying the mootness doctrine requires a student to distinguish between the "merits" of a case and the "jurisdiction" of a case. The Eighth Circuit held that Cohen’s MFLSA claim might be moot if the conduct ceased and would not recur. Even though Cohen argued the policy might change in the future, the court applied the strict standard that continued litigation requires a real, continuing injury. If the court concludes Consilio is compliant, the claim for penalties serves no remedial purpose for Cohen. The *Friends of the Earth* rule that voluntary cessation does not deprive the court of power is an exception to mootness, but the court noted the possibility that the case could become moot if the conduct truly ended.

The jurisdictional constraint highlighted here is that federal courts cannot adjudicate issues in favor of a plaintiff who no longer has a stake in the outcome. Because Cohen’s stipulated recovery of wages removed his personal financial stake in the past violations, the only remaining potential stake was the deterrence of future violations. If the alleged policy has already been eliminated, that stake is gone. The Eighth Circuit reasoned that because the parties could not agree on whether the policy remained, the district court could not properly dismiss or decide the claim. The preservation of the suit depends entirely on the existence of a live controversy, which turns on the status of the employer’s practices.

## Outcome: Vacatur and Remand

The Eighth Circuit’s disposition of the MFLSA claim demonstrates the importance of addressing jurisdiction before addressing the merits of a claim. The district court had granted summary judgment to Consilio on the penalties. The Eighth Circuit, by contrast, found that the district court may have erred by deciding the availability of penalties without first resolving whether the case remained live. Therefore, the Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s grant of summary judgment as to Cohen’s MFLSA claim.

The remedy was a remand specifically for the district court to determine the question of jurisdiction. The Eighth Circuit instructed the district court to resolve the mootness question first. The court recognized that if Consilio’s conduct had stopped and that cessation was absolute, the claim should be dismissed as moot. However, if Consilio had not abandoned the policy, the court would need to revisit the merits. By vacating the judgment, the Eighth Circuit preserved the possibility that Cohen could recover penalties, but only if the district court found a continuing violation that gave Cohen a continuing interest in the suit. This outcome underscores the procedural requirement that a plaintiff's standing is a prerequisite to any judicial decision on liability. KOBES, Circuit Judge, joined the vote and concurred specially, noting the distinction between *Laidlaw* penalties and MFLSA penalties, but agreeing on the vacatur of the attorney's fees and costs, and the MFLSA statutory penalties mootness issue. Thus, the chapter of this appeal concludes with the MFLSA claim remaining on hold, subject to the district court’s threshold jurisdictional analysis.

# IV. Injunctive Relief and Procedural Preservation

## Cohen's original claim for injunctive relief versus his summary judgment briefing request

When reviewing the procedural history of Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, it is necessary to distinguish between the relief initially sought in the complaint and the relief later articulated during the summary judgment briefing. The opinion notes that in Cohen’s original complaint, he sought "certain specific forms of injunctive relief." However, as the litigation progressed toward summary judgment, Cohen "changed course and request[ed] different relief" by withdrawing the 2019 policy in his summary judgment briefing. This discrepancy is critical because it creates ambiguity regarding exactly what relief the appellant is asking the appellate court to grant. The district court explicitly noted that it did not consider the new relief request found in the summary judgment briefing, as the record did not support considering a request that deviated from the original complaint without an amendment. Consequently, on appeal, Cohen argues only for an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy. This specific change in the scope of the requested relief raises a question of whether the appellate court has the authority to resolve a claim that was not formally preserved during the trial court proceedings. Understanding this shift helps the student grasp that procedural posture often dictates which issues are open for review, regardless of the substantive merit of the underlying legal theory.

## Application of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and Eighth Circuit precedent

The Court of Appeals grounded its decision on the requirements of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a). This rule governs the amendment of pleadings, including complaints and briefs, and ensures that parties have adequate notice of the claims being litigated so they may defend against them. The Eighth Circuit relies on this procedural framework to determine when a claim is properly before it. In this case, the Eighth Circuit cites *InfoDeli, LLC*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025), which reinforces the principle that new claims must be submitted through the formal mechanism of amended complaints rather than informal submissions within a briefing period. By referencing this precedent, the court establishes that the procedural rules of the district court have equivalent binding authority during the appellate phase. If a party fails to follow the standard preservation process—such as filing an amendment within the appropriate window—they cannot expect the appellate court to acknowledge those assertions as new substantive claims. This precedent serves as a reminder that litigation is a structured process where rights are defined by the timing and method of their assertion.

## Distinction between arguments and new claims: New claims require amended complaints

A key concept for lawyers to understand from this chapter is the legal distinction between "arguing" existing claims and "pleading" new claims. The court emphasizes that "new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs." This distinction exists to protect the integrity of the judicial process and to ensure that opposing parties receive fair opportunity to respond to allegations. When Cohen introduced a request to "withdraw the 2019 policy" in his summary judgment briefing, the court viewed this not merely as an argument on his existing claim for injunctive relief, but as a new claim because it altered the nature of the relief sought. If a plaintiff can switch the focus of the litigation through a brief without amending the pleadings, the opposing party may not be on notice that they are being asked to defend against a specific order. Therefore, the Eighth Circuit makes clear that changing the legal or equitable relief sought typically requires an amended complaint. Without that formal document, the claim remains waived in the eyes of the court, even if the party makes a consistent legal argument regarding it.

## Consequences of failing to preserve injunctive relief claims through proper procedure

The direct consequence of failing to preserve an injunctive relief claim through proper procedure is that the appellate court will dismiss the issue as not properly before it. In the opinion, the Court of Appeals states simply that "the issue is not properly before us." This disposition does not necessarily mean the court is ruling on the merits of whether the policy should be rescinded; rather, it rules that Cohen was procedurally barred from raising that specific request at this stage. If the court were to rehear the injunctive relief claim now, it would violate the principle that parties must present their cases coherently and in the correct order. By remanding only the substantive MFLSA claims and affirming the rest, the court signals that Cohen must have his procedural failures addressed at the trial level before seeking appellate review. This lesson teaches students that the failure to properly preserve a claim at the summary judgment stage often results in the claim being entirely removed from the appeal, leaving the substantive facts behind regardless of how strong the argument might have been.

## Consistency between the Eighth Circuit's ruling and the disciplinary legal principle regarding waiver

Finally, the Eighth Circuit’s ruling aligns with the widely accepted disciplinary legal principle regarding the waiver of claims. Waiver occurs when a party fails to assert a claim or defense at the appropriate time with sufficient specificity. In this case, Cohen effectively waived his specific request for injunctive relief when he failed to attach it to an amended complaint. The court’s reliance on Rule 15(a) and the cited precedent ensures consistency across Eighth Circuit jurisprudence. It reinforces that the system expects parties to define the scope of their litigation through written filings, not through informal arguments in appellate briefs. This consistency ensures predictability in judicial proceedings; if parties could raise any new claim at any point through an argumentative brief, the rules governing civil procedure would become meaningless. Therefore, the Eighth Circuit’s decision is consistent with the broader legal expectation that procedural due process requires claims to be formally pleaded, ensuring that all parties have fair notice of what is at stake. This final conclusion underscores that procedure is not merely a hoop to jump through, but a substantive right that protects the outcome of every lawsuit.
