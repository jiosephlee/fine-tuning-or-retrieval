Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 4: # IV. Injunctive Relief and Procedural Preservation

## Cohen's original claim for injunctive relief versus his summary judgment briefing request

When reviewing the procedural history of Cohen’s request for injunctive relief, it is necessary to distinguish between the relief initially sought in the complaint and the relief later articulated during the summary judgment briefing. The opinion notes that in Cohen’s original complaint, he sought "certain specific forms of injunctive relief." However, as the litigation progressed toward summary judgment, Cohen "changed course and request[ed] different relief" by withdrawing the 2019 policy in his summary judgment briefing. This discrepancy is critical because it creates ambiguity regarding exactly what relief the appellant is asking the appellate court to grant. The district court explicitly noted that it did not consider the new relief request found in the summary judgment briefing, as the record did not support considering a request that deviated from the original complaint without an amendment. Consequently, on appeal, Cohen argues only for an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy. This specific change in the scope of the requested relief raises a question of whether the appellate court has the authority to resolve a claim that was not formally preserved during the trial court proceedings. Understanding this shift helps the student grasp that procedural posture often dictates which issues are open for review, regardless of the substantive merit of the underlying legal theory.

## Application of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and Eighth Circuit precedent

The Court of Appeals grounded its decision on the requirements of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a). This rule governs the amendment of pleadings, including complaints and briefs, and ensures that parties have adequate notice of the claims being litigated so they may defend against them. The Eighth Circuit relies on this procedural framework to determine when a claim is properly before it. In this case, the Eighth Circuit cites *InfoDeli, LLC*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025), which reinforces the principle that new claims must be submitted through the formal mechanism of amended complaints rather than informal submissions within a briefing period. By referencing this precedent, the court establishes that the procedural rules of the district court have equivalent binding authority during the appellate phase. If a party fails to follow the standard preservation process—such as filing an amendment within the appropriate window—they cannot expect the appellate court to acknowledge those assertions as new substantive claims. This precedent serves as a reminder that litigation is a structured process where rights are defined by the timing and method of their assertion.

## Distinction between arguments and new claims: New claims require amended complaints

A key concept for lawyers to understand from this chapter is the legal distinction between "arguing" existing claims and "pleading" new claims. The court emphasizes that "new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs." This distinction exists to protect the integrity of the judicial process and to ensure that opposing parties receive fair opportunity to respond to allegations. When Cohen introduced a request to "withdraw the 2019 policy" in his summary judgment briefing, the court viewed this not merely as an argument on his existing claim for injunctive relief, but as a new claim because it altered the nature of the relief sought. If a plaintiff can switch the focus of the litigation through a brief without amending the pleadings, the opposing party may not be on notice that they are being asked to defend against a specific order. Therefore, the Eighth Circuit makes clear that changing the legal or equitable relief sought typically requires an amended complaint. Without that formal document, the claim remains waived in the eyes of the court, even if the party makes a consistent legal argument regarding it.

## Consequences of failing to preserve injunctive relief claims through proper procedure

The direct consequence of failing to preserve an injunctive relief claim through proper procedure is that the appellate court will dismiss the issue as not properly before it. In the opinion, the Court of Appeals states simply that "the issue is not properly before us." This disposition does not necessarily mean the court is ruling on the merits of whether the policy should be rescinded; rather, it rules that Cohen was procedurally barred from raising that specific request at this stage. If the court were to rehear the injunctive relief claim now, it would violate the principle that parties must present their cases coherently and in the correct order. By remanding only the substantive MFLSA claims and affirming the rest, the court signals that Cohen must have his procedural failures addressed at the trial level before seeking appellate review. This lesson teaches students that the failure to properly preserve a claim at the summary judgment stage often results in the claim being entirely removed from the appeal, leaving the substantive facts behind regardless of how strong the argument might have been.

## Consistency between the Eighth Circuit's ruling and the disciplinary legal principle regarding waiver

Finally, the Eighth Circuit’s ruling aligns with the widely accepted disciplinary legal principle regarding the waiver of claims. Waiver occurs when a party fails to assert a claim or defense at the appropriate time with sufficient specificity. In this case, Cohen effectively waived his specific request for injunctive relief when he failed to attach it to an amended complaint. The court’s reliance on Rule 15(a) and the cited precedent ensures consistency across Eighth Circuit jurisprudence. It reinforces that the system expects parties to define the scope of their litigation through written filings, not through informal arguments in appellate briefs. This consistency ensures predictability in judicial proceedings; if parties could raise any new claim at any point through an argumentative brief, the rules governing civil procedure would become meaningless. Therefore, the Eighth Circuit’s decision is consistent with the broader legal expectation that procedural due process requires claims to be formally pleaded, ensuring that all parties have fair notice of what is at stake. This final conclusion underscores that procedure is not merely a hoop to jump through, but a substantive right that protects the outcome of every lawsuit.
