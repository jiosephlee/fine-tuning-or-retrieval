Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # II. State Wage Statutes: MPWA and MWTA Analysis

## Breakdown of the MPWA Claim (§ 181.101): Employer vs. Commissioner Penalty Rights
In analyzing Cohen’s claim under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Eighth Circuit focused on the specific remedy Cohen sought: average daily wage penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Cohen argued that because Consilio violated the MPWA by paying employees without overtime premiums, he was entitled to enforce statutory penalties directly. However, the court distinguished between the statute of wages owed to an employee and the statute regarding civil penalties. While an employee may sue to collect wages, the authority to seek and collect statutory penalties under § 181.101 is textually reserved for a different actor. The court noted that when an employer is found to have violated § 181.101, the liability for "civil penalties or damages provided for in the section" is inconsistent with the procedural mechanism required to trigger them. The district court granted summary judgment on this claim because the statutory text limits who may bring the action for penalties, effectively creating a bifurcated enforcement scheme where the underlying wage debt may be contested by the employee, but the specific penalty provision requires Commissioner action.

## Statutory Construction: Plain Language Interpretation Requiring a Demand by the Commissioner
To resolve the dispute over penalty rights, the Eighth Circuit applied the canon of statutory interpretation that plain language and unambiguous meaning manifest legislative intent. The central subsection at issue, § 181.101(a), explicitly references the Commissioner regarding the enforcement of penalties. The court observed that the statute mandates the Commissioner to serve a demand for payment on behalf of an employee; only if payment is not made within ten days of that demand may the Commissioner charge and collect the wages and a penalty. It further instructed that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned. The court reasoned that if the legislature intended the employee himself to seek these penalties directly, the plain language of the statute would not have conditioned the availability of penalties on the Commissioner's service of a demand. This strict reading meant that Cohen's avoidance of the Commissioner's demand precluded his ability to recover these specific penalties from the employer under this section.

## Contrast with Other Sections: Legislative Intent Inferred from Statutory Inaction
A key component of the court's reasoning involved looking beyond the specific text of § 181.101 to the broader statute to infer legislative intent. The court highlighted a clear contrast between § 181.101 and other sections of the MPWA, specifically § 181.13(a) and § 181.14. In those sections, which address situations involving discharged or quitting employees, the legislature explicitly empowered the employee to "charge and collect a penalty" upon their own demand. Furthermore, the court pointed out that, unlike § 181.13 and 181.14, § 181.101 contains a specific section that states the current section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages, but it does not explicitly preserve the ability to sue for penalties. The court invoked the principle that the legislature knows how to accomplish an objective if it wishes to do so. The explicit inclusion of penalty rights in other sections, coupled with the silence and procedural restrictions in § 181.101, provided strong evidence that the omission was deliberate.

## Application of the Lagasse v. Horton Principle: Legislative Intent Inferred from Statutory Inaction
The Eighth Circuit relied on *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189 (Minn. 2022), to solidify the conclusion that Cohen lacked standing to seek penalties under § 181.101. *Lagasse* established that when the legislature has provided a specific remedy in one statute but not another, the legislative inaction is significant and indicates a conscious choice to exclude that remedy in the statute lacking it. The opinion explained that because the Minnesota legislature actively crafted specific provision for employees to recover penalties in the termination and resignation contexts (§ 181.13, § 181.14), the failure to do so in the non-termination context (§ 181.101) implied that the remedy was not intended to be available to the employee. Therefore, the district court's application of § 181.101 correctly deemed the statutory penalty unenforceable by the individual employee, reinforcing the summary judgment in favor of Consilio.

## Breakdown of the MWTA Claim (§ 181.032(f), (d)(4)): Notice Violations
Turning to the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), the court addressed Cohen’s claim that Consilio violated statute by failing to provide the required notice of a change in its overtime policy (§ 181.032(f), (d)(4)). The underlying facts showed that Consilio posted a communication regarding the new overtime policy, but Cohen alleged it was insufficient. This claim was distinct from the MPWA claim in that it turned on a specific notice requirement. However, in assessing the summary judgment, the court looked to the procedural posture agreed upon by the parties. Cohen had stipulated that Consilio had paid him all overtime wages and liquidated damages apart from penalties. While Cohen argued that Consilio was required to rescind the policy, the court analyzed whether specific remedies were available under the MWTA text itself. The review indicated that Cohen was essentially asking for penalties for violating the notice statute, the analysis of which mirrored the strict statutory interpretation required for other wage claims.

## Procedural Waiver: Cohen Failed to Submit Legal Authority or Argument for MWTA Penalties on Appeal
The appellate court further considered Cohen’s request for penalties under the MWTA through the lens of appellate procedure. In the briefing before the Eighth Circuit, Cohen articulated a grievance that the district court provided little discussion of the MWTA claim and did not apply legal authority to it. However, the court noted that Cohen failed to provide his own legal authority or argument in his appellate brief to support the reversal. The Eighth Circuit applied a well-settled principle that a party forfeits an issue not supported with argument or legal authority. The court cited *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, explaining that "[i]nasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." Because Cohen did not present an argument showing that the district court erred in dismissing the claim, the court concluded it could not disturb the district court's order, and the issue was deemed waived on appeal.

## Outcome: Affirmation of Summary Judgment for Both State Claims
Following its analyses of the statutory construction and procedural posture, the Eighth Circuit reached its disposition regarding the state law claims. For the MPWA claim, the court affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment, holding that the scheme of § 181.101 limits penalty enforcement to the Commissioner. For the MWTA claim, the court affirmed the summary judgment based on the lack of proper argument and authority provided by Cohen. This section of the chapter illustrates that statutory rights to penalties are not guaranteed across all wage statutes in Minnesota and depend heavily on the precise text of the provision and procedural compliance by the plaintiff. The final result was an affirmation of the lower court's decision on the two Minnesota state statutes submitted for review, leaving only the federal MFLSA claim for remand and further jurisdictional analysis.
