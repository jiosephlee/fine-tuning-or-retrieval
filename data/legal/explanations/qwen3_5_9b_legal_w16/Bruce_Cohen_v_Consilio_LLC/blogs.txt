Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The Mootness Minefield: Lasting Litigation Rights When Overtime Gets Paid Up Front

In the landscape of wage and hour litigation, settling for back pay and liquidated damages is often viewed as the musical note that signals the end of a song. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit suggested otherwise. When Consilio, a document review firm, voluntarily remedied its overtime policy by paying Cohen what he was owed in overtime and liquidated damages, Cohen expected the legal battle to retire along with it. However, because Cohen also sought statutory penalties payable to the state rather than himself, the Eighth Circuit vacated the summary judgment on the federal claim and remanded to determine if the ongoing violation had actually been cured. This split decision illuminates a critical tension in wage theft jurisprudence: the difference between private redress and public remedy, and the structural limits on corporate immunity when penalties go to the government rather than the aggrieved worker.

## The MPWA Trap: Statutory Limits on Penalty Recovery

Before reaching the federal mootness issue, the Eighth Circuit dismantled Cohen’s claim under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). Under the facts, Consilio had adopted a premium overtime policy stripping hourly licensed attorneys of premium pay. Cohen sought "average daily wage penalties" under Minn. Stat. ch. 181, § 181.101. The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning here is a classic exercise in statutory textualism. The court pointed out that § 181.101(a) reserves the authority to collect penalties exclusively to the Commissioner. If a plaintiff cannot recover penalties under a statute they argue is violated, the statutory text controls over general principles of remedial statute construction.

The court distinguished this section from other provisions within the MPWA, such as §§ 181.13 and 181.14, which explicitly allow discharged or resigning employees to charge themselves penalties. The Eighth Circuit noted that the Legislature “knows how” to empower individual employees with penalties when it chooses to do so. Since § 181.101(a) does not mention the employee, but instead refers to the Commissioner for the demand and collection process, the individual employee is precluded from seeking these damages. This ruling effectively closed a significant avenue for employees seeking public liability on the state statute side, forcing them to rely on other provisions or federal law. This is a sharp doctrinal win for employers who rely on non-payment statutes that technically situate enforcement with the state administrator rather than the individual worker.

## The MFLSA Mootness Puzzle: Private Redress vs. Public Remedy

The more intriguing legal landscape, however, exists in the court’s handling of the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim. Here, Cohen recovered all overtime wages and liquidated damages directly. Liquidated damages under federal law are essentially doubles the unpaid wages, sendable to the worker. If those are paid, the traditional equivalent harm is gone. But Cohen pressed on, seeking statutory penalties under the FLSA. This is where the Eighth Circuit found the friction point. Civil penalties under the FLSA are payable to the government or the Commissioner, not the employee.

This creates a standing puzzle: Can a plaintiff claim injury requiring a judicial hearing when their personal financial injury has been fully remedied? The Eighth Circuit declined to throw Cohen under the bus immediately but warned that Cohen’s claims faced a significant risk of mootness. The opinion cited *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC)*, the Supreme Court’s seminal mootness precedent. *Laidlaw* instructs that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not automatically deprive a court of power to determine the legality of the practice. However, the court also acknowledged that a plaintiff must maintain standing throughout the litigation—a continuing personal interest that is harmed by the wrongful conduct.

The Eighth Circuit remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction first. The reasoning is pragmatic and doctrinally sound. If Consilio’s policy remains in place, Cohen still has standing. If Consilio’s policy is absolutely clear as to being discontinued and likely not to recur, the case might be moot. The Eighth Circuit explicitly refused to rule on summary judgment because they could not presume the Constitutional jurisdiction to say Consilio acted illegally if no standing remained. This highlights a procedural minefield. For plaintiffs, this is a stark reminder that while the court cares about private injuries and the logic of the *Laidlaw* doctrine supports the ability to sue for penalties only after the harm of the *violation* is calculated, a clean slate on wage payments does not automatically moot a penalty claim against a company that might still be in violation.

For employers like Consilio, the path of least resistance is clear: pay the private damages (wages and liquidated damages) to clear the private record, but expect the legal fight to linger on the state or federal level if penalties are a factor. Yet, doing so does not convert a series of legally valid claims into a therapeutic gesture. The Eighth Circuit upheld the summary judgment on the MPWA claim, where statutory silence on employee penalties was fatal, but it could not resolve the MFLSA claim without first ensuring that the plaintiff actually retained the federal standing to sue for public penalties in light of the employer’s remedial steps.

This case finally settles that litigation is not automatically home on the victim’s receipt of back pay, but it demands strict adherence to the court rules that demand plaintiffs retain standing into the end. It is a necessary check on the tendency of employment disputes to become privatized, but it also signals that the law of standing will continue to filter out trivial or moot claims where the public penalty outweighs the private grievance. Practitioners must now weigh whether the administrative costs of carefully litigating mootness in wage theft cases exceed the benefits of the statutory penalty itself.



# Legislative Intent as a Litigator: Reading Section 181.101 Through the Commissioner's Lens

Workers' compensation and wage-and-hour law often operate through a duality of federal and state standards, creating a labyrinth where plaintiffs must navigate overlapping statutes and enforcement mechanisms. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit confronted this complexity in a wage theft case brought by a licensed attorney document reviewer. While the court affirmed that Consilio had not paid Cohen his back wages, the real doctrinal hook of the opinion lies in its narrow statutory interpretation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). By relying heavily on legislative silence and comparing sections of the Minnesota statutes, the Eighth Circuit taught litigators a valuable lesson: in the absence of explicit authorization, private plaintiffs may not secure the punitive penalties that the legislature arguably restricted to the state’s Labor Commissioner.

## The Limits of Private Enforcement under MPWA Section 181.101

The core of Cohen’s appeal hinged on whether he could recover statutory penalties for Consilio’s violation of the MPWA. Consilio had abruptly changed its overtime policy for Minnesota-based licensed attorney reviewers in 2019, eliminating premium overtime pay. While Consilio subsequently paid Cohen back wages and liquidated damages pursuant to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Cohen sought additional statutory penalties under the MPWA under the premise that these were available to any aggrieved employee. The Eighth Circuit, however, sided with Consilio, holding that § 181.101(a) strictly reserves the authority to collect penalties to the Commissioner.

This holding rests on a strict textual reading of the MPWA. The court noted that § 181.101(a) stipulates that penalties are charged and collected by the Commissioner, and further provides that any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee. Crucially, for the employee, the statute only preserves the right to prosecute a claim for *wages*. The court reasoned that because the statute does not explicitly grant the employee the right to seek penalties, the legislature did not intend to grant it. This is a classic application of the canon of expressio unius: when the specific remedial power of the Commissioner is listed, the omission of similar power for the individual plaintiff is intentional.

## Statutory Comparison and the "They Know How" Doctrine

What makes this decision particularly instructive for legal writers and attorneys is the court’s method of statutory interpretation. In interpreting the "plain meaning" of § 181.101, the Eighth Circuit did not look in isolation; it looked across the statutory landscape to distinguish this section from just-title's § 181.13 and § 181.14. These sections explicitly allow discharged or resigned employees to "charge and collect a penalty" upon demand from the employee.

The court leaned on the precedent set by *Lagasse v. Horton* to make a critical point: if the legislature wants to empower an employee with a statutory penalty in one context (termination or resignation), but not in another (unpaid wages), the legislature "knows how" to accomplish that. This comparison allows the court to read an unspoken restriction into the statute. It prevents parties from arguing that the plain language of § 181.101 without more creates a private enforcement scheme. Instead, the opinion emphasizes that "aggressed parties" suing without reliance on the Commissioner are only entitled to the wages owed, not the additional financial whip typically associated with statutory penalties. This ensures that the deterrence strictures remain firmly in the public hand of the Commissioner.

## Doctrinal Implications: Textualism in State Law Litigation

The decision has significant downstream effects for wage theft practitioners operating in Minnesota. Historically, wage classes often bundle wage claims with liquidated damages or statutory penalties to increase leverage in settlements or to stricten the court’s findings on the employer’s bad faith. *Cohen v. Consilio* serves as a reminder that not all statutes designed to protect laborers grant private standing to sue for penalties. Even where a plaintiff has a statutory right to sue for unpaid wages and commissions under § 181.101, the remedy might not extend to penalties without a more explicit textual grant.

The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on this "plain meaning" approach aligns with the increasing trend in federal courts to defer to the specific text of state statutes rather than inferring a private cause of action where none is plainly written. This creates a tension between the policy goals of protecting workers—often best served by allowing private retaliation of statutory penalties—and the requirement of strict statutory construction. For the lower courts, it means they must be careful to distinguish between the substantive right to wages and the remedy for that right. In this case, the distinction was fatal to Cohen’s MPWA claim but left his status under the MFLSA intact.

## The Mootness Pivot and Final Relief

While the statutory interpretation of the MPWA was the analytical crux of the majority’s decision, the docket list also highlights the court’s attention to the MFLSA claim, where costs were paid under settlement stipulations. The Eighth Circuit vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA claim solely based on mootness concerns regarding whether Consilio’s internal policy had remained in place, noting that the voluntary cessation of the alleged violation does not necessarily guarantee mootness. This aspect of the ruling reinforces the procedural importance of the staying of judgment; without the policy continuing, Cohen had won back wages and liquidated damages but faced an uncertain path toward final enforcement.

Ultimately, *Cohen v. Consilio* is a powerful lesson on the art of reading state statutes through the lens of contemporaneous provision rather than assumed intent. It suggests to litigators that the road to full damages in employment disputes may be narrower than it appears when the employer successfully invokes the text of the MPWA. For the future of similar suits, this case dictates that plaintiffs must look closely for the specific statutory fuel that powers the penalty claim, rather than assuming it is available simply because the statute itself is violated. The Eighth Circuit ensures that, for the most part, enforcing the penalty remains the job of the state, not the citizen.



# The Procedural Cost of Casual Claims: Why 'New Relief' Does Not Save a Summary Judgment Appeal

Workplace wage disputes are among the most common litigation battles of the modern era, often turning on the nitty-gritty of overtime calculations and statutory definitions. Yet, frequently overlooked in the public record is another layer of the game: how a case is managed on the docket. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit offers a stark reminder that appellate advocates can lose significant ground on the merits because they also stumbled in the entryway. While Cohen successfully pressed a claim regarding overtime wages—which the employer voluntarily satisfied—the appellate court’s disposition of his remaining claims exposes a harsh reality for litigants who treat appellate briefing as a generic extension of trial advocacy. By unraveling Cohen’s attempt to bring new relief and statutory authority as if it were a proper addendum to a summary judgment appeal, the Eighth Circuit highlights the significant procedural costs of failing to align claims with formal pleading and argument.

For interested observers, the factual backdrop of *Cohen* was relatively straightforward but with sufficient stakes to warrant the court’s time. Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer, sued his employer, Consilio, alleging violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Prior to appeal, a key event changed the dynamics significantly: Consilio essentially made the bulk of Cohen’s money demands good with a stipulation that it had paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed. This stipulation meant that Cohen’s case had transformed into a technical battle over penalties and injunctive relief, with the district court granting summary judgment. Cohen then appealed, but the Eighth Circuit decided that even if he had presented a compelling argument on the MWTA penalties, the record and the briefing strategy kept these claims from landing.

The primary lesson of the case centers on the waiver of claims, specifically regarding penalties under the MWTA. Cohen pressed the district court and later the Eighth Circuit for civil penalties under § 181.032(f), arguing that Consilio had failed to provide statutory notice of the change in overtime policy. However, the Eighth Circuit pointed out that Cohen’s briefing offered little more than an assertion of entitlement without supporting legal authority. The court applied—and clearly emphasized—the principle that a plaintiff cannot simply build a case on a blank slate at the appellate level. As the majority noted, "inasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." This adherence to the standard established in *Milligan v. City of Red Oak* serves as a procedural warning: there is no backdoor for a claims waiver. Even if the substantive argument might have been viable, the failure to cite statutory backing during the briefing process, after having previously argued the claim to the district court, effectively extinguished it on appeal.

Beyond the statutory waivers, the court was equally firm when Cohen attempted to shift the contours of injunctive relief without a formal change to the pleadings. Cohen initially argued in his complaint for specific relief, but later, in his summary judgment briefing, he pivoted to request a different, affirmative injunctive order for the rescission of the overtime policy. The Eighth Circuit was unwilling to look away from this change in posture, noting that Cohen had earlier modified his request on the spot rather than through an amended complaint. Consistent with Rule 15(a) and the precedent set in *InfoDeli, LLC* (cited in the 8th Circuit’s own 2025 jurisprudence), new claims or changes in relief must be properly raised through amended complaints rather than opposition briefs. Because the district court never considered this specific change and it was not procedurally correct for Cohen to raise it via brief, the appellate court did not disturb the order on these grounds.

Analytically, the combination of these two holding points provides a cautionary tale for appellate counsel. The Eighth Circuit’s decision suggests that courts are prepared to stay within the "borders of the relevant controversy" as defined at the pleading stage, regardless of whether the litigant tries to retreat and retoken from inside the appellate process. The case demonstrates that calculating the "procedural cost of casual claims"—as the blog concept puts it—is a real financial risk. Cohen might have been entitled to more at trial, or he might have been permitted to introduce new evidence on penalties, but by treating the deluge of briefing for a different type of relief as a casual claim, he surrendered his right to argue for it on appeal.

Ultimately, *Cohen v. Consilio* will likely be cited in the lower federal courts that have jurisdiction over federal employment law claims and federal claims under Minnesota law as a reinforcement of the formalities of appellate procedure. While the lower courts remain free to determine, for instance, the standing and mooting of the MFLSA penalties claim—leading to the remand on that specific issue—the irony for Cohen is clear: he did not lose because the law was unclear on his side, but because he breached the procedural contract of appellate practice. Lower courts and practitioners who fail to anticipate that appellate briefing is as much about preservation as it is about persuasion are the ones who will find themselves remedying the system of justice without the relief they sought.
