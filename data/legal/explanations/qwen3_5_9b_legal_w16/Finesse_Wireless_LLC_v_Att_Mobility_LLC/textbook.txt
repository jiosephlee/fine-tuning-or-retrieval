Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Procedural Posture, Jurisdiction, and Standards of Review

## Overview of Finesse Wireless LLC's Patents and the Nature of PIM/IMP Interference
To understand the substantive legal disputes in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, a law student must first grasp the technical foundation upon which the patent infringement claims rest. Finesse Wireless LLC holds ownership of two specific patents—U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775—which relate to methods for mitigating interference in radio transmissions. This interference arises when radios operate on different frequencies, yet their transmit signals combine when they encounter obstacles, such as metal fences or loose cable connections, to form new frequencies known as intermodulation products (IMPs). When these intermodulation products are caused by passive obstacles, they are classified as passive intermodulation products (PIM). The core technical dispute concerns whether the accused Nokia radios utilized the patented methodology to identify and cancel these specific passive signals. While the technical details of signal processing are central to the fact-finding, the legal focus remains on whether the accused hardware meets the specific limitations required by the claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents. Understanding this background is essential because the rest of the opinion details how the court evaluated the evidence regarding these technical limitations.

## Procedural History: Jury Verdict, District Court Decisions, and Notice of Appeal
The procedural path leading to the Federal Circuit’s decision demonstrates the typical lifecycle of a federal patent litigation appeal. Finesse Wireless LLC sued AT&T Mobility LLC and Nokia of America Corporation, alleging infringement of the asserted claims within both patents through the use of the PIM cancellation feature in Nokia radios. The dispute proceeded to a jury trial in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, where the jury rendered a verdict in favor of the plaintiff. Following this verdict, the jury assigned $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages covering the remaining life of the patents. After the verdict, the appellate steps began when the defendants, AT&T and Nokia (collectively the Appellants), filed motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement, JMOL regarding damages, and a new trial. The district court denied all three motions. Subsequently, the district court entered a judgment following the denial of these motions, which prompted Appellants to appeal to the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. This procedural posture establishes that the record includes the district court’s post-trial orders, which serve as the primary basis for the appellate court's standards of review.

## Jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)
A proper appellate review begins with establishing the court's authority to hear the case. In this instance, the court explicitly states that it has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). This specific statutory provision grants the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from United States district court decisions rendering final judgments in patent cases. Recognizing this statutory grant of authority is critical for a law student, as it distinguishes patent appeals from general civil appeals that might be heard by other regional circuits. Because the Federal Circuit is a specialized court for intellectual property, understanding this jurisdictional hook confirms why this specific opinion comes before a three-judge panel in the Federal Circuit rather than a circuit court like the Fifth Circuit where the trial occurred, even though the application of regional circuit law is required for certain procedural standards.

## Standard of Review for JMOL of Noninfringement on Federal Circuit and Fifth Circuit Law
The distinction between the Federal Circuit's jurisdiction and the standards applied is where the interplay between the regional circuit and federal patent law becomes clear. The opinion directs that the court reviews a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law under the law of the regional circuit, identifying that here, the Fifth Circuit applies. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, the review is conducted de novo, meaning the appellate court examines the issue anew without deferring to the district court. The Fifth Circuit will reverse a denial of JMOL only if, upon viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes reasonable jurors could not arrive at a contrary conclusion. However, the Federal Circuit opinion also notes that a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented. Understanding this dual review framework is vital because the appellate court must apply the stringent Fifth Circuit "strong and overwhelming" test for the denial of JMOL while applying the "substantial evidence" test for the factual findings supporting any infringement conclusions.

## Standard of Review for Factual Findings Supporting JMO and Damages
The standards of review shift depending on whether the appellate court is addressing claim construction, factual findings, or damages. The opinion clarifies that while claim construction is reviewed de novo, the underlying factual findings supporting the construction are reviewed for clear error. For the damages award specifically, the court reviews the denial of a motion for a new trial under the law of the regional circuit, which again is the Fifth Circuit. It specifies that the Fifth Circuit reviews a denial of a new trial for an abuse of discretion. This standard suggests that the appellate court is highly deferential when the district court orders a new trial or denies a motion to correct damages on the ground that the damages calculation was wrong. If the appellate court identifies no legal error in the district court's reasoning, the abuse of discretion standard makes it difficult to overturn the damages award absent a clear error in the court's application of the law or procedure.

## Burden of Proof and Allocation in Patent Infringement Cases
The allocation of the burden of proof dictates the analytical burden placed on the litigants and shapes the standard of review applied by the appellate court. In this case, as the plaintiff, Finesse bore the burden of proving that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims of the patents. This burden means that evidence must be sufficient to support a finding of infringement if the jury is to render a verdict in Finesse’s favor. During the summary judgment and trial phases, Appellants argued that Finesse failed to show the accused radios sample a passband of signals that includes both the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Because Finesse had the burden, the appellate court scrutinized whether the evidence presented—specifically expert testimony—was sufficient to persuade a reasonable jury that those technical requirements were met. When the party with the burden rests its case on self-contradictory testimony, or fails to produce clear evidence linking specific components to the claim elements, the appellate court may conclude the jury's verdict is unsupported. The opinion illustrates this principle when it notes that an expert's confusing change of course is insufficient to support a jury verdict when the burden was to prove substantial technical correspondence.

## Interplay between Noninfringement and Damages Claims
Finally, it is essential to understand how a ruling on noninfringement logically dictates the outcome for damages claims. If a patent is found not to have been infringed, there can be no liability, and consequently, no damages can be awarded for the alleged infringement. In this appeal, the appellate court vacates the damages award specifically because it reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL regarding noninfringement. Because the appal rote court determined that the jury’s crime-on-infringement verdict was not supported by substantial evidence, the legal basis for the damages award was eliminated. This disposition highlights the procedural efficiency of the JMOL mechanism: by determining that the evidence was insufficient to prove infringement regarding specific claims, the court rendered the calculation of damages moot. The decision to vacate the damages award rather than uphold a new trial on mere damages underscores the hierarchy of legal errors; an error regarding the core finding of infringement renders the subsequent calculation of remedies illegitimate.

# The '134 Patent: Claim Limitation and Expert Testimony Consistency

## Claim Construction of '134 Patent Claim 1: Limitation 1[a]
To understand the Federal Circuit’s reversal in this case, one must first grasp the specific limitation at issue within the '134 patent. Claim 1 recites a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference, and the court focused its analysis on limitation 1[a]. This limitation requires the accused device to perform a specific act regarding the passband of received signals: it must over-sample this passband to create a bit stream. Crucially, the claim stipulates that the "received signals" within this passband must include two distinct categories: "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The specification further clarifies that these interference generating signals must be capable of generating intermodulation products within the inband range of the signals of interest. For infringement to occur based on this language, the accused radios must physically sample both of these signal types as they arrive to create the bit stream. If the device fails to sample one of these categories, it does not meet the literal terms of the claim. This factual requirement established the burden of proof for the plaintiff, Finesse, who had to demonstrate that the Nokia radios' receiver operated in this dual-signal configuration.

## Dr. Jonathan Wells' Testimony and the Diagrammatic Evidence
At trial, the central evidence for Finesse regarding limitation 1[a] relied heavily on the testimony of its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios satisfied the limitation through a component called a radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC). He referenced a diagram from a Nokia technical document (Nokia document) to map the various signals. According to Dr. Wells' testimony, the RF ADC samples data on a path depicted in the diagram. On that specific path, he identified two components: the "Downlink TX reference" and the "modeled PIM path." While Dr. Wells initially used the language of the claim legend, Alice mapped the "Downlink TX reference" to the "signals of interest" and the "modeled PIM path" to the "interference generating signals." He asserted that the RF ADC samples this red path in the diagram, thereby capturing both required signal types. The jury accepted this mapping as sufficient evidence of infringement, finding that the claimed limitation was met.

## The Technical Dispute: Sampling Timing and Signal Generation
The conflict in the evidence centers on the physical reality of signal generation versus the timing of sampling. A fundamental principle of radio frequency operation is that a signal must exist before it can be sampled. Dr. Wells' testimony posited that the "interference generating signals" were the modeled PIM path. However, the technical evidence revealed a significant flaw in this mapping regarding timing. The underlying Nokia technology generates the "modeled PIM results" *downstream* of where the RF ADC samples the passband. In other words, the interference generating signals are not physically present at the RF ADC until *after* the radio has already performed its sampling phase. Citing the record, the court noted that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA downstream of the sampling point. Because the "interference generating signals" are generated after the sampling occurs, they cannot be the "interference generating signals" found in the "received signals" *during* the over-sampling step. Consequently, if Dr. Wells' mapping were correct regarding the signal path and timing, the accused device could not have satisfied the requirement of sampling a passband that includes those interference generating signals.

## Analysis of Cross-Examination and Contradictory Testimony
The legal strength of the trial evidence was severely undercut during cross-examination. When opposing counsel asked Dr. Wells to clarify his mapping of the Nokia diagram, the inconsistencies in his testimony became evident. He continued to identify the "modeled PIM path" as the "interference generating signal," which maintained the timing impossibility. However, upon further questioning, Dr. Wells shifted his testimony to refer to "x1 and x2" as the two input signals on the red path. He failed to explicitly state that x1 and x2 corresponded to the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals" required by the claim. He testified that x1 and x2 were represented by those signals on the red path but did not clarify that x1 or x2 equaled the specific claim terms. The Federal Circuit viewed this shift as a critical failure of the expert's case. An expert's opinion that is internally inconsistent, or where the expert fails to provide a clear, detailed explanation for their own contradictory testimony, cannot support a jury verdict. Because Dr. Wells never clearly testified that the specific inputs (x1/x2) were the specific claim limitations (signals of interest/interference generating signals), his opinion did not conclusively prove infringement.

## District Court's Reasoning on Sufficient Evidence
Despite these contradictions, the district court denied Finesse's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of noninfringement, rejecting Appellants' arguments. The district court reasoned that Dr. Wells' testimony was sufficient because it indicated he found two signals entering the RF ADC, which it interpreted as the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The court concluded that Dr. Wells was "clearly referring to x1 and x2" when he discussed the inputs to the ADC. The district court essentially glossed over the technical impossibility of sampling post-generated signals, instead accepting the narrative that the diagram's inputs were sufficient without a specific, unambiguous map from the x1/x2 terminology back to the claim language. This decision rested on a view of the evidence that found the expert's patchwork of testimony clear enough to support the jury's finding of infringement, even though the testimony failed to explicitly link the specific signal definitions required by the patent claims.

## Application of the Fifth Circuit's 'Strongly and Overwhelmingly' Standard
The Federal Circuit reviewed the district court's denial of JMOL using the legal standard of the Fifth Circuit, since the district court was in the Eastern District of Texas. Under this standard, a district court's denial of JMOL should only be reversed if the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not have arrived at any contrary conclusion. This standard essentially merges with the "substantial evidence" standard for factual findings in a patent infringement case. While a jury finding of infringement must only be supported by substantial evidence (meaning a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party), a JMOL standard imposes a higher burden on the party challenging the verdict to show that no reasonable jury could possibly have found infringement given the evidence. Here, the Federal Circuit determined that Dr. Wells' self-contradictory testimony did not cross this threshold. The court emphasized that a party bearing the burden of proof, like Finesse, cannot rest its case on expert opinion that is ambiguously contradictory regarding the core technical elements of the claim.

## Holding: Reversal of Denial of JMOL for the '134 Patent
Ultimately, the Federal Circuit held that the district court erred in denying the jury's finding that the Nokia radios did not infringe. The jury's verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. The district court accepted a testimony where the expert claimed the interference generating signals were the modeled PIM path, yet admitted those signals were generated after sampling, or where the expert's shift to "x1 and x2" was never explicitly defined to meet the claim language. The court concluded that when a party rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, the evidence may be insufficient to satisfy the substantial evidence standard. The Federal Circuit concluded that for the '134 patent, the claimed "interference generating signals" could not have been sampled by the RF ADC because they were generated downstream of the sampling step. As such, reasonable jurors could not have found infringement based on the record evidence presented. This necessitated a reversal of the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the '134 patent.

# The '775 Patent: Scope Disavowal and Insufficient Multiplication Evidence

## Claim Construction of '775 Patent Claim 1: The Three Signals and Seven Multiplications

To understand the Federal Circuit's analysis of the '775 patent, students must first grasp the specific limitations at issue in the converted district court judgment. The appeal required the Federal Circuit to evaluate whether the jury's finding that the accused radios infringed on U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 was supported by substantial evidence. The prior art analysis and claim construction lay the groundwork for this inquiry. At summary judgment, the district court addressed the extrinsic and intrinsic evidence to construct the critical terms of Claim 1. Specifically, the court construed the phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean signals that are "separately identifiable," but expressly declined to construe them as "unique" signals. This construction was crucial because it defined the boundaries of the claim scope regarding the inputs required to perform the interference cancellation. The Federal Circuit accepted this construction, noting that the intrinsic evidence did not limit the signals to unique inputs, and the inventor's testimony supported the "signals generally" interpretation. Thus, the legal threshold for infringement turned on whether the accused device could produce three separately identifiable signals, regardless of whether those signals were derived from unique sources.

## Prosecution History Considerations: Amendment to 'Three Signals' vs. 'Two or Three'

Appellants argued that the '775 patent should be interpreted more narrowly based on the prosecution history, specifically claiming that Finesse surrendered claim scope by amending the limitation during patent prosecution. According to the opinion, the original claims recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals," but Finesse amended this to "three signals" during the examination process to overcome an obviousness rejection based on the Filipovic reference. This reference taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs. Appellants contended that by dropping "two or three," Finesse disavowed any product using only two input signals, like the accused radios. However, applying the standards for claim surrender, the court held that Finesse did not surrender scope for products with two unique input signals. The court reasoned that Finesse's amendment was necessary to overcome a patent examiner's obviousness rejection regarding "passive" vs. "active" intermodulation interference, and it was not "clear and unambiguous" enough to disavow the ordinary meaning of the claims or narrow them to require unique inputs. Consequently, the revolutionary change did not mandate that the signals be distinct and unique, only that they be "separately identifiable" as construed by the district court.

## Analysis of Accused Infringement via Nokia Technical Documents

The bulk of the infringement finding turned on the testimony regarding the accused radios' operations. Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified that the accused radios triggered the PIM cancellation feature described in the '775 patent. His analysis relied heavily on a Nokia technical document which depicted the hardware architecture of the accused radios. Dr. Wells mapped the operations in the radios to the claims, arguing that the radios performed the necessary signal multiplications. On appeal, the Federal Circuit noted that Dr. Wells' testimony was the primary evidence supporting the jury's verdict of infringement. However, the court scrutinized whether the technical evidence provided a factual basis for the specific numerical requirements of the claim. The evidence relied upon by Dr. Wells described the internal workings of the Nokia equipment. While Dr. Wells argued that these configurations met the claim limitations, the factual accuracy of his mapping between the physical components in the Nokia document and the abstract requirements of Claim 1 remained contested. The appellate court found that the evidence presented at trial regarding the specific mathematical operations was insufficient to sustain the verdict.

## The 'Three Distinct Multiplications' vs. 'Seven Claimed Multiplications' Discrepancy

A central factual conflict in this case concerned the number of digital multiplications performed by the accused radios versus the number required by the patent. Claim 1 limits the method to generating seventh-order ICS by, given three signals, digitally multiplying and filtering specific combinations, including seven multiplications listed in the patent specification. Specifically, the claims require seven distinct operations such as S1×S1×S2 for modifying the third order intermodulation products. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios perform this cancellation by multiplying three signals. However, upon examining the Nokia document in the record, the Federal Circuit found that the document only listed three distinct multiplications: x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|. These three distinct mathematical operations totaled significantly less than the seven multiplications required by the claim limitation. The court pointed out that the Nokia document did not show any evidence that these three operations were repeated to generate the full seven claimed calculations, nor did it show that the device performed additional multiplications to reach the seven required outputs. Without this demonstration, the evidence failed to meet the "seven multiplications" limitation necessary for infringement.

## Lack of Expert Mapping Evidence on Appeal

The Federal Circuit also determined that subsequent testimony provided on appeal by Finesse did not satisfy the strict evidentiary standards for overturning the jury's decision. On appeal, Finesse sought to clarify Dr. Wells' mapping by suggesting a more complex relationship between the input signals x1 and x2. They proposed that the signals utilized in the claimed multiplications could include x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. This theoretical mapping attempted to solve the mathematical discrepancy between the three shown multiplications and the seven required by the claim. However, the court stressed that this clarifying evidence was never presented to the jury during the trial. Under the principle of substantial evidence, Finesse could not introduce new evidence of possibility to cure the lack of evidence possessed at trial. The Federal Circuit noted that Finesse failed to explain which subset of ten multiplications (from a broader review) corresponded to the seven claimed multiplications, nor did they provide a mapping from the ten multiplications to the three distinct ones shown in the Nokia document. The inability to map the physical evidence shown to the legal claim elements post-TRIAL period was fatal to their argument.

## Effect of Ambiguous Mapping and Flawed Integration of Evidence

The court further noted that the confusion between Dr. Wells' testimony compounded the evidentiary insufficiency. When cross-examined, the expert was found to be confusingly indistinct about which specific signals (x1, x2, or other paths) constituted "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The Federal Circuit observed that Dr. Wells repeatedly testified that the red path represented the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path, but when pressed, he struggled to identify how exactly these aligned with the claim requirements. This confusion meant that the link between the physical act of multiplication in the radios and the specific mathematical steps required by the patent remained out of evidence. The court applied the standard of review for denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), which requires that the evidence points "so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." Because the expert testimony was ambiguous regarding the number of multiplications, and the documentation only showed three distinct ones, a reasonable jury could not have found the seven were performed. The evidence was deemed insufficient to support the infringement verdict.

## Holding: Reversal of Denial of JMOL for the '775 Patent

Ultimately, the Federal Circuit granted the new trial regarding damages because the underlying infringement verdict was unsupported. The court reversed the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775. The court held that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications because the evidence only demonstrated three distinct multiplications, and the mapping required to bridge this gap was not established during the trial. This reversal effectively invalidated the jury's finding of infringement for the '775 patent. As a procedural consequence of this reversal regarding the '134 patent claim finding, the court vacated the entire damages award, as damages cannot be calculated without a valid finding of infringement underlying the patent. Thus, Finesse failed to meet its burden of proving that the accused product met the specific structural and numerical limitations of Claim 1, despite the prosecution history and expert testimony presented at trial.

# Damages, Concessions, and Final Case Disposition

## Review of the District Court's $166 Million Lump-Sum Damages Award

In the initial trial proceedings before the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, the jury returned a verdict in favor of the patentee, Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse), finding the jury that the accused products of AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia infringed the asserted patents. Based on this verdict of infringement, the jury awarded a substantial monetary recovery in the amount of $166,303,391. This award was structured as a lump-sum damages calculation intended to cover the remaining life of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Understanding the scope of this damages award is essential for the appellate review because, as demonstrated in the opinion, the fate of this award was inextricably linked to the findings of infringement made by the jury in the first instance. While the District Court allowed this calculation to stand following the verdict, the appellate court later analyzed whether the foundational basis for this award was legally sound. The background information provided in the opinion clarifies that the damages were computed specifically for the remaining lifespan of the patents, which highlights the importance of the infringement determination in establishing the period during which damages are recoverable.

## Standard of Review: Abuse of Discretion for New Trial on Damages

Following the jury's damage award, the district court denied AT&T's motion for judgment as a matter of law regarding the damages specification, as well as a subsequent request for a new trial. On appeal to the Federal Circuit, the court clarified the standard of review applicable to the district court's decision to deny a new trial on damages. Under Fifth Circuit law, which the Federal Circuit reviews under its jurisdictional grant, the standard for reviewing a denial of a new trial on damages is an abuse of discretion. This standard of review is distinct from the de novo review applied to legal conclusions, as the trial court is positioned best to evaluate the factual record and the propriety of the new trial motion in the context of the entire trial record. The appellate court cites the Fifth Circuit precedent established in *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.* to reinforce this deferential standard. It effectively instructs students that while legal theories are reviewed closely, the procedural decision of whether a new trial is warranted for damages issues is accorded significant deference to the trial judge. However, in this specific case, the Federal Circuit did not need to exercise its discretion to assess the reasonableness of the damages because the underlying verdict upon which they were based was overturned on the merits.

## Consequences of Reversal: Vacatur of Invalid Damages Underlying Junk Law

The most direct procedural outcome of the Federal Circuit's decision concerned the disposition of the $166 million award. In the Section regarding Damages, the court reiterated the precise consequence of its legal ruling on the liability issue: "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents, we vacate the damages award." This statement establishes the procedural mandate of vacatur as the necessary next step when the factual predicate for damages is removed. When a jury finds infringement, damages are assessed based on the expectation of earnings, license fees, or lost utilities associated with that infringement. If the patentee fails to prove infringement as a matter of law, the legal basis for the damage calculation is nullified. In this opinion, the Federal Circuit determined that the evidence presented was insufficient to prove infringement. Consequently, the damages award was vacated because it was based on a legal conclusion that the Court found unsupported. The opinion emphasizes that the damages could not simply be recalculated by the jury or reapplied; rather, the prohibition against the award was the direct result of the reversal of the infringement finding.

## Relationship Between Invalid Jury Verdict of Infringement and Monetary Awards

A fundamental concept in patent litigation that emerges from this opinion is the dependency of the monetary award on the finding of infringement. In the case of patent law, the jury finds that infringement has occurred, but the actual recovery of money is dependent entirely on that liability finding. This opinion illustrates that an invalid verdict of infringement makes any subsequent damages calculation legally defective. The Federal Circuit reasoned that because the jury's finding of infringement was not supported by substantial evidence regarding the operation of the accused radios, the jury could not have properly considered the value of those products to the patentholder. Therefore, the rhetorical flow of the opinion connects the failure of the infringement proof directly to the invalidity of the dollar amount awarded. A student must understand that damages are not an independent claim with its own foundation; they are a remedy for infringement. If the act of infringement is not established in the facts, the remedy (damages) remains unenforceable.

## Ordering Costs to the Defendants-Appellants

In its final disposition of the appeal, the Federal Circuit addressed the allocation of costs between the parties. The opinion concludes with a specific instruction: "COSTS / Costs to Defendants-Appellants." In federal appellate practice, this designation determines which party bears the prevailing costs of the appellate litigation. Notably, the Appellants were the Defendants, AT&T and Nokia, who appealed the district court's ruling. Usually, the party that prevails is awarded costs, but in this instance, the Court ordered costs to the Defendants-Appellants. This outcome reflects the complex procedural posture of the case where while the Appellants lost on the merits of the damages reversal and the no-vacatur decision, the final responsibility for the expenses of the appellate process rested with the party that filed the appeal. This section of the opinion serves as a practical reminder of how appellate courts handle the financial aspects of litigation once the substantive issues, including vacatur and costs, are resolved.

## Doctrine of Harmless Error in Noninfringement vs. Damages Context

While the opinion focuses on the strict requirements of evidence to support a finding, it implicitly addresses the concept of harmless error regarding the damages calculation. In the context of this appeal, the Federal Circuit treated the error in the infringement finding—specifically the ruling that the evidence was insufficient for JMOL—as a fatal error that necessitated a remedy. The court did not analyze whether the error was accidental or small enough to be harmless because the damages calculation cannot withstand the failure of the core requirement of infringement. The court effectively ruled that the error in allowing the infringement verdict was not harmless to the damages award; it was fatal. If a jury's infringement verdict is unsupported, the monetary award derived from it cannot stand, and the appellate court must reverse the errors rather than vacate the damages alone. This procedural choice underscores that damages errors are often contingent upon, rather than independent of, the validity of the infringement finding.

## Significance of the Decision for Patent Litigation Strategy

For the student of patent law, the reasoning in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* highlights critical strategic realities of expert testimony and capitalization of the jury process. The appellate court reversed the District Court's denial of judgment as a matter of law because the infringement experts presented to the jury were self-contradictory: they could not definitively map the technical signals within the accused device to the limitations of the patent claims. The costs to the defendants of this strategic failure were not only the monetary damages vacated but also the appellate loss of the JMOL. When experts testify that signals do not or cannot be sampled in the manner claimed, the risk to the patentee is that the entire damages architecture fails. This decision serves as a lesson that success in damages is anchored in the technical details of how the product allegedly infringes. If the technical recitation does not hold up under scrutiny of the evidence mapped to the claim language, the legal conclusion of infringement collapses, and the calculated damages for the remaining life of the patent vanish.
