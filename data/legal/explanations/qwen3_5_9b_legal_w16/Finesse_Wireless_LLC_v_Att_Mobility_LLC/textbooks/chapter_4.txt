Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # Damages, Concessions, and Final Case Disposition

## Review of the District Court's $166 Million Lump-Sum Damages Award

In the initial trial proceedings before the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, the jury returned a verdict in favor of the patentee, Finesse Wireless LLC (Finesse), finding the jury that the accused products of AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia infringed the asserted patents. Based on this verdict of infringement, the jury awarded a substantial monetary recovery in the amount of $166,303,391. This award was structured as a lump-sum damages calculation intended to cover the remaining life of U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775. Understanding the scope of this damages award is essential for the appellate review because, as demonstrated in the opinion, the fate of this award was inextricably linked to the findings of infringement made by the jury in the first instance. While the District Court allowed this calculation to stand following the verdict, the appellate court later analyzed whether the foundational basis for this award was legally sound. The background information provided in the opinion clarifies that the damages were computed specifically for the remaining lifespan of the patents, which highlights the importance of the infringement determination in establishing the period during which damages are recoverable.

## Standard of Review: Abuse of Discretion for New Trial on Damages

Following the jury's damage award, the district court denied AT&T's motion for judgment as a matter of law regarding the damages specification, as well as a subsequent request for a new trial. On appeal to the Federal Circuit, the court clarified the standard of review applicable to the district court's decision to deny a new trial on damages. Under Fifth Circuit law, which the Federal Circuit reviews under its jurisdictional grant, the standard for reviewing a denial of a new trial on damages is an abuse of discretion. This standard of review is distinct from the de novo review applied to legal conclusions, as the trial court is positioned best to evaluate the factual record and the propriety of the new trial motion in the context of the entire trial record. The appellate court cites the Fifth Circuit precedent established in *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.* to reinforce this deferential standard. It effectively instructs students that while legal theories are reviewed closely, the procedural decision of whether a new trial is warranted for damages issues is accorded significant deference to the trial judge. However, in this specific case, the Federal Circuit did not need to exercise its discretion to assess the reasonableness of the damages because the underlying verdict upon which they were based was overturned on the merits.

## Consequences of Reversal: Vacatur of Invalid Damages Underlying Junk Law

The most direct procedural outcome of the Federal Circuit's decision concerned the disposition of the $166 million award. In the Section regarding Damages, the court reiterated the precise consequence of its legal ruling on the liability issue: "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents, we vacate the damages award." This statement establishes the procedural mandate of vacatur as the necessary next step when the factual predicate for damages is removed. When a jury finds infringement, damages are assessed based on the expectation of earnings, license fees, or lost utilities associated with that infringement. If the patentee fails to prove infringement as a matter of law, the legal basis for the damage calculation is nullified. In this opinion, the Federal Circuit determined that the evidence presented was insufficient to prove infringement. Consequently, the damages award was vacated because it was based on a legal conclusion that the Court found unsupported. The opinion emphasizes that the damages could not simply be recalculated by the jury or reapplied; rather, the prohibition against the award was the direct result of the reversal of the infringement finding.

## Relationship Between Invalid Jury Verdict of Infringement and Monetary Awards

A fundamental concept in patent litigation that emerges from this opinion is the dependency of the monetary award on the finding of infringement. In the case of patent law, the jury finds that infringement has occurred, but the actual recovery of money is dependent entirely on that liability finding. This opinion illustrates that an invalid verdict of infringement makes any subsequent damages calculation legally defective. The Federal Circuit reasoned that because the jury's finding of infringement was not supported by substantial evidence regarding the operation of the accused radios, the jury could not have properly considered the value of those products to the patentholder. Therefore, the rhetorical flow of the opinion connects the failure of the infringement proof directly to the invalidity of the dollar amount awarded. A student must understand that damages are not an independent claim with its own foundation; they are a remedy for infringement. If the act of infringement is not established in the facts, the remedy (damages) remains unenforceable.

## Ordering Costs to the Defendants-Appellants

In its final disposition of the appeal, the Federal Circuit addressed the allocation of costs between the parties. The opinion concludes with a specific instruction: "COSTS / Costs to Defendants-Appellants." In federal appellate practice, this designation determines which party bears the prevailing costs of the appellate litigation. Notably, the Appellants were the Defendants, AT&T and Nokia, who appealed the district court's ruling. Usually, the party that prevails is awarded costs, but in this instance, the Court ordered costs to the Defendants-Appellants. This outcome reflects the complex procedural posture of the case where while the Appellants lost on the merits of the damages reversal and the no-vacatur decision, the final responsibility for the expenses of the appellate process rested with the party that filed the appeal. This section of the opinion serves as a practical reminder of how appellate courts handle the financial aspects of litigation once the substantive issues, including vacatur and costs, are resolved.

## Doctrine of Harmless Error in Noninfringement vs. Damages Context

While the opinion focuses on the strict requirements of evidence to support a finding, it implicitly addresses the concept of harmless error regarding the damages calculation. In the context of this appeal, the Federal Circuit treated the error in the infringement finding—specifically the ruling that the evidence was insufficient for JMOL—as a fatal error that necessitated a remedy. The court did not analyze whether the error was accidental or small enough to be harmless because the damages calculation cannot withstand the failure of the core requirement of infringement. The court effectively ruled that the error in allowing the infringement verdict was not harmless to the damages award; it was fatal. If a jury's infringement verdict is unsupported, the monetary award derived from it cannot stand, and the appellate court must reverse the errors rather than vacate the damages alone. This procedural choice underscores that damages errors are often contingent upon, rather than independent of, the validity of the infringement finding.

## Significance of the Decision for Patent Litigation Strategy

For the student of patent law, the reasoning in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* highlights critical strategic realities of expert testimony and capitalization of the jury process. The appellate court reversed the District Court's denial of judgment as a matter of law because the infringement experts presented to the jury were self-contradictory: they could not definitively map the technical signals within the accused device to the limitations of the patent claims. The costs to the defendants of this strategic failure were not only the monetary damages vacated but also the appellate loss of the JMOL. When experts testify that signals do not or cannot be sampled in the manner claimed, the risk to the patentee is that the entire damages architecture fails. This decision serves as a lesson that success in damages is anchored in the technical details of how the product allegedly infringes. If the technical recitation does not hold up under scrutiny of the evidence mapped to the claim language, the legal conclusion of infringement collapses, and the calculated damages for the remaining life of the patent vanish.
