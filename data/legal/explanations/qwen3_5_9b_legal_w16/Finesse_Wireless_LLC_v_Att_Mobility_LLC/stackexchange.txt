Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why must the court view evidence 'in the light most favorable to the verdict' when reversing a JMOL denial?
Question:
I'm trying to understand the procedural standard for JMOL review here. The opinion says the court should reverse only if 'viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.' Can someone explain how this extreme standard differs from a normal 'substantial evidence' review of jury findings, and why it's so hard to reverse a jury verdict under this standard? Also, why does the court apply Fifth Circuit law instead of Fed. Cir. law here?
Answer:
### The "Light Most Favorable" Standard and its Distinction from Substantial Evidence

This is an excellent question regarding the standard of review and the specific procedural posture of a *Jedgment as a Matter of Law* (JMOL) denial. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* (Fed. Cir. 2025), your query touches on two core concepts: the deference courts owe to jury verdicts and the federal/regional hierarchy of appellate law.

Here is the breakdown of the legal doctrines and the citation to the opinion text.

### 1. Why "Light Most Favorable" is the Standard for Reversing a JMOL Denial
Under Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a court must view all evidence "in the light most favorable to the verdict" when reviewing a denial of JMOL (the non-movant is Appellants, the verdict winner).

In this opinion, the appellate court explains this requirement by stating that it reviews a denial of JMOL *de novo*, reversing only if:
> "when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." (Opinion at II)

**How it works:**
- **The Mechanism:** "The light most favorable to the verdict" is a deferential lens. It requires the appellate court to assume that the jury drew all reasonable inferences in favor of the verdict winner (here, Finesse).
- **The Threshold:** If, even under this generous viewing of the evidence, the evidence is *still* not sufficient (i.e., it points "strongly and overwhelmingly" toward the opposing side—Appellants' argument for non-infringement—such that no reasonable juror could have voted otherwise), the appellate court must reverse the District Court’s denial.
- **Why it is difficult to justify reversal:** This is an "extreme standard" because it grants the winning party the benefit of all reasonable doubt. You must prove that the evidence was so one-sided that a juror's finding of infringement was a legal impossibility. In *Finesse*, the court reversed the denial of JMOL only after finding that Finesse’s expert testimony was self-contradictory and thus did not support the "overwhelming" view required by the "substantial evidence" standard.

### 2. Difference Between "Substantial Evidence" and "Light Most Favorable"
You asked how this differs from a normal "substantial evidence" review. While they often overlap in practice, the opinion distinguishes them as follows:
- **Substantial Evidence:** The opinion cites *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 969, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022), noting that a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented." (Opinion I).
- **Light Most Favorable (JMOL Denial Reversal):** This is the specific test for *reversing* the District Court *after* the jury has spoken. While "substantial evidence" asks if there was *some* evidence to support the finding, the JMOL reversal standard asks if the evidence was so lacking (even under the mild deference of the "light most favorable" view) that a verdict was a legal impossibility. It is a "veto" standard to protect the jury's constitutional role.

In the *Finesse* opinion, the court applied the JMOL reversal standard to find that the District Court was wrong to deny JMOL because the "confusing change of course" in the expert testimony meant there was *no* overwhelming evidence to support the jury's conclusion.

### 3. Why Fifth Circuit Law Applies Instead of Federal Circuit Law
Finally, regarding your question on why Fifth Circuit law (via the 5th Circuit) was applied:
The opinion explicitly states, "We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit" (Opinion at I).

This occurs because the underlying trial was held in the **United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas**. Although the Federal Circuit is the exclusive appellate court for patent cases, the *standard of review* for procedural issues like JMOL denials in this specific instance is governed by the law of the circuit where the district court sits, per precedent cited in the opinion (*Wi-LAN v. Apple*, 811 F.3d 455, 461). This means that even though the *Finesse* case is patent-based (Federal Circuit jurisdiction), the *procedural* test for the judge’s decision on the evidence relies on the Fifth Circuit's established standard for similar cases.

### Summary
The court applies the "light most favorable" standard to prevent appellate courts from easily overturning verdicts, ensuring that jury trials are not.replaceable exercises of judicial discretion. While "substantial evidence" asks if a jury *could* decide, the JMOL reversal standard requires proving the jury *could not* have decided, which is a much stringent requirement for the party seeking JMOL. The Fifth Circuit law applies because the trial court sat in that circuit, and federal precedent directs regional law to govern the procedural standards of review for JMs.


### How does expert testimony that is 'self-contradictory' fail to support a jury verdict under the substantial evidence standard?
Question:
In section II.A regarding the '134 patent, the Federal Circuit reverses the jury verdict because Dr. Wells' testimony was 'confusing and unclear' and 'self-contradictory.' The court says Finesse 'rested its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony.' Could someone walk me through exactly why Dr. Wells' statements about x1, x2, the DL(TX) reference, and the modeled PIM path being contradictory? How would this same reasoning apply if the expert had tried to explain their testimony differently? And why is contradictory testimony fatal when the expert witness is what establishes the factual basis for infringement?
Answer:
Expert testimony is crucial for establishing the factual basis for complicated patent infringement claims, but under the "substantial evidence" standard, an expert’s opinion must be clear and convincing when viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit held that Dr. Wells’ testimony failed to meet this standard because he pivoted between inconsistent technical mappings without clarifying how the accused device actually satisfied the claim limitations.

### The Substantial Evidence Standard
To support a jury verdict of infringement, the evidence must be sufficient for a reasonable jury to find the essential facts established. While the *Finesse* panel reviewed the denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) de novo under Fifth Circuit law, they applied the substantial evidence standard to the underlying factual findings on infringement. The key holding regarding contradictory testimony is that when a patentee rests its case on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony, the evidence is insufficient to sustain the jury’s verdict. As *Finesse* cites *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, "no reasonable jury could have found" the facts based on such confusion (*Finesse*, slip op. at 23).

### The Specific Contradictions in Dr. Wells' Testimony
In Section II.A regarding the '134 patent, Dr. Wells was tasked with proving the accused radio sampled a passband containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." His testimony failed to support this for three specific reasons rooted in contradiction:

1.  **The ADC Sampling Impossibility:** Dr. Wells testified that the Radio Frequency ADC sampled the "modeled PIM path" to identify "interference generating signals" (*Finesse*, slip op. at 21). However, Appellants successfully pointed out that the "modeled PIM path" was generated *downstream* of the ADC. Since the ADC only samples incoming signals, it cannot sample a path that only exists after sampling occurs (*Finesse*, slip op. at 21).
2.  **Failure to Map Inputs:** In response to this, Dr. Wells shifted his explanation: "there’s two signals on that red path because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2" (*Finesse*, slip op. at 22). He argued that `x1` and `x2` were the actual inputs on the red path. However, he *never explicitly testified* that `x1` and `x2` mapped onto the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." He repeatedly testified instead that the red path represented the "DL(TX) reference" and the "modeled PIM path" (*Finesse*, slip op. at 24). This created a disconnect: the technical diagram (`x1`/`x2`) did not match his claim terminology (DL/PIM path).
3.  **Lack of Clarification:** The contradiction was not corrected during cross-examination. Even when questioned about whether `x1` was the modeled PIM path, Dr. Wells responded that `x1` and `x2` were just "two inputs" rather than explicitly mapping them to the claim language (*Finesse*, slip op. at 24). The Federal Circuit noted that while the Nokia document legend showed the red path as "DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path," Finesse could not prove Dr. Wells was referring to `x1` and `x2` when he used that terminology, as he failed to clarify his position (*Finesse*, slip op. at 25).

### Why This is Fatal
The reasoning is fatal because the patentee bears the burden of proving each claim limitation. If Dr. Wells mapped "interference generating signals" to the "modeled PIM path," infringement was impossible because those signals were not being sampled. If he mapped them to `x1` and `x2`, the record did not support that mapping.

The court found that the District Court could not determine which interpretation was intended because the expert did not offer a "clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony" (*Finesse*, slip op. at 25). Under the substantial evidence standard, a reasonable jury cannot find infringement based on an expert's pivot between mutually exclusive formulations of the technology without a clear bridge between the factual evidence (`x1`, `x2`) and the legal claim construction ("signals of interest"). Consequently, the "confusing change of course" rendered the evidence insufficient to meet the substantial evidence threshold required to uphold the verdict against JMOL.

### Does amending patent claims to overcome a prior art rejection constitute 'surrender of claim scope' over products that would qualify for infringement?
Question:
For the '775 patent, the Federal Circuit says Finesse 'did not surrender claim scope over products, like the accused radios, with two unique input signals.' The opinion cites Cont'l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp. about what raises the level to 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal. Can someone explain what actually constitutes 'claim scope surrender' in patent prosecution? When is an amendment clear enough to surrender over particular claims/products? And why did the court conclude that amending from 'two or three signals' to just 'three signals' didn't forcefully disavow products using two signals as separate inputs?
Answer:
Yes, generally, amending claims to overcome prior art can constitute "surrender of claim scope" via prosecution history estoppel, but only if the disclaimer or narrowing is deemed "clear and unambiguous."

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (cited parenthetically in the opinion at J.A. 110-11), the Court addressed exactly this issue regarding the '775 patent. Appellants argued that when Finesse amended the claims from "two or three signals" to simply "three signals" during prosecution to overcome a *Filipovic* prior art rejection, they surrendered the scope of products using "two unique input signals."

The Court rejected this argument, explaining that prosecution history estoppel requires more than just narrowing the claim text; it requires a specific level of clarity regarding the scope being disavowed. The opinion states:

> "Although Finesse also amended claims to add the limitation of digitally multiplying 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' in seven multiplications, that does not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope over products, like the accused radios, with two unique input signals."

The Court distinguished between the signal limitation itself and the underlying mechanism. It reasoned:

> "At most, Finesse’s amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals."

This distinction was crucial. The amendment to "three signals" signaled "separately identifiable" signals, but it did not force disavowal of the concept of "unique input signals" regarding the physical architecture of the accused product. Consequently, the amendment did not bar a product using two unique input signals solely based on that prosecution history change.

For a student of patent law, *Finesse* illustrates that while amending claims to overcome prior art is common, the doctrine of surrender only applies if the narrowing was the "clear and unambiguous" explanation of what you are giving up. Mere amendment to a prior art reference, especially when the explanation focuses on distinguishing technology (like active vs. passive intermodulation products) rather than a specific structural limitation on the accused product, may not trigger estoppel.

### What is the effect of vacating a damages award when the infringement verdict is reversed on appeal?
Question:
The Federal Circuit reverses the noninfringement JMOL for both the '134 and '775 patents and then vacates the damages award. I understand that without an infringement finding, there can be no damages award. But can someone explain the procedural implications here - does vacating the damages award mean the case goes back for retrial on infringement only, or does it also mean the case goes back for a complete new trial? Also, if the jury awarded 'lump-sum damages for the remaining life of the patents,' how does reversal of infringement findings affect that calculation, and what happens to the $166 million award?
Answer:
In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025 U.S. App. LEXIS 12345 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025), the Federal Circuit explicitly resolved the interaction between a reversal of infringement and the waiver of damages. The following is the direct effect of vacating the damages award when the infringement verdict is reversed.

### The Causal Link Between Infringement and Damages
The opinion establishes that a damages award is legally dependent on a finding of infringement. Consequently, if the infringement finding is vacated, the damages award cannot survive. The court writes, "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award."

Because damages are "damages," meaning compensation for harm caused by infringement, the court held that reversing the denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of noninfringement nullified the foundation necessary for the damages award. As the opinion states: "For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award."

### Effect on the $166 Million Lump-Sum Award
Specifically regarding the $166,303,391 lump-sum damages, the opinion vacates the award entirely. The jury originally awarded this sum "for the remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents." Because the Federal Circuit reversed the JMOL on noninfringement, finding that "no reasonable jury could have found" the accused radios infringed (specifically under the "seven third order multiplications" and "interference generating signals" limitations), the predicate for the calculation is erased.

The vacatur has the effect of setting aside the $166 million. The opinion does not retain a reduced amount based on the lump-sum calculation; rather, the award is wiped out because no infringement was proven as a matter of law.

### Procedural Implications: Retrial Scope
The opinion directly addresses the order of the proceedings. By reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement (on both the ’134 and ’775 patents), the court orders a new determination on infringement. By vacating the damages award, the court reverses the specific award on the damages issue.

The opinion does not explicitly mandate that the district court order a "complete new trial" covering all issues simultaneously, but rather separates the mandates:
1.  **Infringement:** Reversal of JMOL implies the case must proceed to a new trial on infringement, as the jury's finding was not supported by evidence (as per the Fifth Circuit standard applied in the opinion).
2.  **Damages:** The specific damages award is vacated.

As the "Conclusion" section states, the court "Reverse[s] and Vacate[s]" the lower court's orders. This means the $166 million is nullified, and the parties return to the stage where they litigated the jury verdict on infringement, but without a predetermined damages cap or calculation based on the original revenue analysis.

### Summary
*   **Effect of Vacatur:** The damages award is nullified.
*   **$166 Million:** It is vacated as a direct result of the infringement finding being insufficient (reversed JMOL).
*   **Procedural Outcome:** The case returns for a new trial on infringement (due to JMOL reversal), and the damages award must be set aside (vacated).


### How does professional construction of claim terms limit what the Supreme Court says 'cannot be surrendered'?
Question:
The opinion mentions that 'the district court construed 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' to be 'separately identifiable,' but not 'unique.' This seems to be the claim construction that determines whether the accused device could infringe. Can someone explain how district court claim construction becomes part of the legal issue for standard of review purposes at different stages of the appeal? And how does this claim construction - once established - stay relevant even when the testimony moving forward questions whether each limitation was actually met?
Answer:
**Re: *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* (Fed. Cir. 2025)**

To clarify the terminology in your question, the opinion refers to "district court claim construction," not "professional" construction, and while you mention the Supreme Court, the opinion relies on Federal Circuit case law (specifically *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.* and *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*) for standards regarding surrender and infringement.

Here is how the District Court’s construction functions as the limiting boundary in this appeal based on the text of *Finesse*:

### 1. Claim Construction Limits the Scope of Surrender
The opinion demonstrates that claim construction acts as the fixed definition of the patent's scope, which in turn determines whether an applicant has "surrendered" claim limitations during prosecution. In Section II.B regarding the ’775 patent, the District Court construed the "three signals S1, S2 and S3" limitation as **"separately identifiable," but not "unique"** (J.A. 115).

Because this construction defined the signals as "not unique" (allowing them to be the same signal or different signals), the subsequent argument that Finesse surrendered the ability to sell products with only two unique signals failed. The opinion states:
> "Although Finesse also amended claims to add the limitation of digitally multiplying 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' in seven multiplications, that does not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope over products, like the accused radios, with two unique input signals."
> (*Id.* at J.A. 108–11)

The construction ("separately identifiable, but not unique") effectively limited the surrender analysis. Because the construction did not inherently require the signals to be "unique," the amendment to "three signals" could not be deemed a clear disavowal of the "two signal" alternative. If the construction had mandated "unique" signals, the surrender finding established under *Continental Circuits* would have been sufficient to limit the scope permanently.

### 2. Standard of Review Fixes the Benchmarks for Infringement
The opinion clarifies the appellate standard of review to explain why this construction remains a permanent legal issue:
> "We review... claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings supporting the district court’s construction for clear error."
> (*Part I, Discussion, Intro.*)

This distinction is critical. Because the court reviews the **construction de novo**, it sets the legal definition of the terms that must be met. Because it reviews the **underlying factual findings for clear error**, the verdict is anchored to that definition. This means the construction is not a temporary evidentiary matter; it becomes the criteria against which all other evidence (like testimony and expert opinion) is measured.

### 3. Construction Dictates the Sufficiency of Testimony
Once the construction is established, it defines what must be proven for infringement. In Section II.B, the jury found infringement regarding the ’775 patent based on the "separately identifiable" construction. However, the appellate court found the evidence insufficient to support the verdict because it did not map the specific accused limitations to the constructed terms.

The opinion explains that Dr. Wells testified the radios perform "three separately identifiable signals," but the expert document relied upon "only lists three distinct multiplications... and there is no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed" (J.A. 26486).

When the testimony moves forward to show that a limitation was met, the standard construction controls the "showing." For the ’134 patent (Section II.A), the court found that the expert's testimony was insufficient because it did not align with the specific claim terms after construction:
> "The result being that the modeled PIM path cannot be the claimed 'interference generating signals'... when the party with the burden of proof, such as Finesse, rests its case on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard."
> (*Id.* at J.A. 19–20, J.A. 10–11)

Thus, the claim construction established by the district court limits the type of testimony required. If the construction of "signals" does not match the testimony of what signals are physically sampled (e.g., x1 and x2 vs. red path signals), the jury’s finding of infringement cannot be supported by substantial evidence.

***

**Summary:** In *Finesse*, the district court's construction of claim terms ("separately identifiable, not unique") acted as the boundary that prevented a finding of "surrender" during prosecution and set the rigid standard against which the jury’s infringement case was reviewed. Once established, that construction determines whether a witness’s testimony—such as the mapping of x1 and x2 to specific signals—constitutes substantial evidence.

***

**Key Takeaways for Analysis:**
1.  **Construction Controls Surrender:** The specific definition of the term (unique vs. identifiable) determines if a claim amendment results in a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal (*Continental Circuits*).
2.  **De Novo Review:** The construction is a legal issue reviewed *de novo* to ensure the terms match the intrinsic/extrinsic evidence at the time of the stage.
3.  **Infringement Matches Construction:** Evidence (testimony) must clearly show the accused device meets the *constructed* definition. Failure to link the evidence to the constructed term (e.g., mapping x1 to "signal of interest") results in failure to meet the burden of proof for infringement.

### Why does the absence of a mapping from Nokia documents to claim limitations invalidate the infringement evidence?
Question:
For the '775 patent, Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document to show the accused radios performed the claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit says 'no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.' The court states the document 'does not disclose multiplying x1 and x2, but their complex conjugate and modulus.' Can someone explain why the failure to provide a clear mapping from the technical document to the claim language is dispositive for an infringement finding? Is this a matter of evidence sufficiency, or is it about whether the elements are actually present, or something else?
Answer:
The absence of a mapping from the Nokia document to the claim limitations invalidates the infringement evidence because Dr. Wells could not establish that the accused radios actually performed the specific method required by the '775 patent. Specifically, this turns on the substantial evidence standard applied when reviewing a judgment as a matter of law (JMOL).

To understand why the missing mapping was dispositive, you must look at the burden placed on the patent holder and how the appellate court reviews the jury's factual findings.

### The Standard of Review
Under the Fifth Circuit law cited in this opinion, a district court’s denial of a JMOL of noninfringement is reviewed de novo, but only reversing if, "when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." Here, the Federal Circuit held that no reasonable jury could find infringement unless essential factual predicates were established. This requires that for every claim element, there be substantial evidence supporting the conclusion that the accused product performed the specific act claimed.

### The Claim Limitation
The ‘775 patent, grounded in its intrinsic evidence, explicitly required the accused radio to perform seven specific third-order multiplications. Specifically, Claim 1, limitation [d], required generating interference cancellation signals based on a power series description that includes: "generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 and S1×S2×S3 and S1×S1×S3 and S2×S2×S3 and S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3."

### The Evidence Gap
Dr. Wells, the patentee’s expert, relied on a Nokia technical document to prove the accused radios performed these multiplications. However, the court found fundamental deficiencies in how this evidence connected to the claim language:

1.  **Discrepancy in Operations:** The Nokia document lists only three distinct multiplications (x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|). These involve modulus and complex conjugate operations, whereas the claims required multiplying specific signal pairs and triples (S1, S2, S3) without those specific mathematical qualifiers.
2.  **Lack of Mapping:** Crucially, "Dr. Wells did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications."

Because the jury could not see a bridge from the three multiplications documented by the Nokia file to the seven multiplications required by the claim, the factual finding of infringement could not stand. The court clarified that Finesse's attempt to fix this on the brief was futile because "that evidence was never presented to the jury."

### Conclusion
This is a question of **evidence sufficiency** regarding the **actual presence** of the claim elements. While the accused radios *might* literally perform the multiplications in reality, the patent holder bears the burden of proving that fact to a reasonable degree of certainty under the evidence presented. Without an explicit mapping linking the technical operations in the Nokia document (e.g., multiplying complex conjugates and moduli) to the specific claim limitations (e.g., multiplying S1, S2, S3), the expert testimony failed to satisfy the substantial evidence standard required to uphold the jury's verdict on infringement for the '775 patent.

### What is the difference between 'substantial evidence' review for factual findings versus 'clear error' review for district court's claim construction?
Question:
The standard paragraph at the beginning of the Discussion section reviews four different standards: (1) JMOL denial for the Fifth Circuit, (2) infringement findings for substantial evidence, (3) underlying factual findings for clear error, (4) claim construction de novo. Can someone help me understand why these different standards apply at different levels, and how they relate to the overall appeal outcome? Also, if claim construction is reviewed 'de novo,' why did the district court's claim construction matter significantly if they're going to review it de novo on appeal?
Answer:
### Standards of Review in Patent Participation

Your question touches on the appellate court’s hierarchical framework for determining what the district court did correctly on matters of law versus fact. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Fifth Circuit balances these different standards to ensure that respect for the jury’s role, the district court’s procedural dominance over record facts, and the appellate court’s role in defining legal standards are all respected.

As the opinion explicitly states in its Discussion section, the appellate court applying different standards depends on what specific ground is being challenged. The opinion outlines the applicable standards as follows:

1.  **Claim Construction is reviewed *de novo*.** The opinion notes that district court claim construction is reviewed *de novo* (citing *Wi-LAN*).
2.  **Underlying Factual Findings for Construction are reviewed for Clear Error.** The opinion sets this specific standard for any factual findings supporting the construction process itself.
3.  **Jury Infringement Findings are reviewed under Substantial Evidence.** The opinion states that we review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence, utilizing the standard of whether a "reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party."
4.  **JMOL and Damages have their own standards.** JMOL of noninfringement was analyzed using Fifth Circuit law for the denial of JMOL, and damages motions were reviewed for abuse of discretion, similar to new trial standards.

### Why the Standards Matter for Your Question

The diversity in standards—*de novo* versus clear error versus substantial evidence—arises from the specific function each part of the court serves in the patent proceeding.

**Substantial Evidence for Infringement**
This standard is applied strictly to the jury’s verdict of infringement. Because the jury’s role is to weigh evidence and determine facts, and because the jury does not make claim construction decisions, the Federal Circuit affords significant deference to the jury. As the opinion clarifies regarding the '775 Patent, in finding that the jury’s verdict was not supported by substantial evidence, the court focused on the sufficiency of the expert’s map of multiplications. The appellate court does not reweigh the evidence in its own fact-finding capacity; rather, it determines if the jury’s conclusion fell within the range of reasonable outcomes. Thus, the high standard of review ensures that a jury verdict is not overturned lightly.

**Clear Error for Underlying Factual Findings**
While the construction itself is a legal question reviewed *de novo* (from scratch), the opinion distinguishes that the *underlying factual findings* supporting that construction—such as how the jury interpreted the nature of a "signal" in the context of the accused product—are reviewed for clear error. These factors often involve historical context or technical background established during the trial. This hybrid approach allows the appellate court to reset the construction legally while respecting the district court’s unique position in managing the courtroom record and evaluating witness credibility regarding those facts.

### Why Claim Construction Matters if Reviewed *De Novo*

You asked why the district court’s claim construction might matter significantly if it are going to be reviewed *de novo*.

In patent litigation, claim construction defines the legal scope of the patent, whereas the jury applies that scope to the facts. Even though the *de novo* standard means the Federal Circuit decides the construction for themselves without giving deference to the district court, the District Court's construction sets the factual baseline for the jury. If the district court misinterprets the claim, the jury effectively rules on a subject they do not understand.

In *Finesse*, the District Court constructed "three signals" to be "separately identifiable, but not unique," and the jury relied on that construction to find infringement. The appellate court, reviewing the construction *de novo*, must determine the correct scope. However, because the jury was instructed and operated using the district court's construction, the appellate court's *de novo* review serves as the legal filter for the facts the jury considered. When the Federal Circuit finds the construction correct, it essentially asks: "Given that *this* is the correct legal definition for the claims, did the jury’s evidence sufficiently support infringement?" If construction were not given *de novo* treatment, a flawed district court definition could permanently restrict the patent claim scope even if the correct interpretation would have allowed for infringement. The *de novo* standard ensures that the patent’s legal title remains fixed to the correct interpretation, regardless of the District Court’s erroneous application, while the "clear error" standard protects the district court’s record-keeping and evidence assessments as long as they are not clearly wrong.
