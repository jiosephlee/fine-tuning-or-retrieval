Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Damages and Procedure: How a JMOL Reversal Erases the $166M Verdict

## Why This Matter Matters

The sheer scale of the damages awarded in a patent infringement case is often the news hook that captures market attention. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit addressed a verdict that included a lump-sum damages award of $166 million across the remaining life of two patents. Yet, the outcome of this appeal wasn't just a math adjustment; it was a procedural demolition. The Federal Circuit did not coddle the evidence it found. Instead, in a reversal presided over by Chief Judge Moore, the Court effectively tuned the standards for sufficiency of evidence in the Eastern District of Texas back to a higher level of scrutiny. The result is a stark reminder for patent owners that the rigidity of jury verdicts—particularly lump-sum damages—depends entirely on the integrity of the underlying infringement finding. This case serves as a cautionary tale for those viewing regional venue motion districts: even as trial courts in Texas frequently handle high-volume patent disputes, the appellate perspective remains skeptical of expert-led inferences born of imprecise testimony.

## The Eastern District Of Texas Procedural Pipeline

A critical takeaway from the opinion lies in the procedural framework the Federal Circuit applied. Because the district court for the Eastern District of Texas denied the motion for judgment as a matter of law, the Court of Appeals was required to defer to the regional standard of review. Citing *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the Federal Circuit adopted the Fifth Circuit's de novo review for JMOL, which demands a showing that evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not arrive at a contrary conclusion. This standard differs from the substantial evidence test applied to factual findings in patent infringement. The opinion reveals that the district court's reliance on piecemeal testimony to bridge gaps in an expert's timeline was insufficient to meet the high bar required for noninfringement. While Eastern District judges often face criticism for volume, the Federal Circuit is reinforcing that the burden of proof remains with the plaintiff, and legal arguments about the sufficiency of that burden are not lightly waived at the Rule 50 stage.

## Battle for The Wire: The '134 Patent Failure

The first pillar of the appellate upset involved the ’134 patent, where the core dispute centered on the physical reality of signal sampling. The patent claimed a method where the radio samples a passband of signals that includes “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” For infringement to be found, the radio must sample these signals *before* the interference generating signals are created. The district court relied on Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Wells, to map these signals to the defendant’s Nokia radios. The trouble was that Wells' testimony contained a paradoxical loop that no reasonable jury could have reasonably justified.

During cross-examination, appellate counsel pointed out that the radio could not sample modeled intermodulation products generated downstream after the analog-to-digital converter had already sampled data. Dr. Wells initially mapped the modeled PIM path to the claimed interference signals but later retreated to vague references to inputs x1 and x2 without explicitly tying them to the claimed terms. The Federal Circuit found the expert's pivot confusing and self-contradictory. If the modeled PIM path were the interference generating signal, limitation one of the claim could not be met because that signal was not in the passband before sampling. However, the district court held that because Dr. Wells had testimony on x1 and x2, there was sufficient evidence. The appellate Court found no reasonable jury could have bridged that gap between a confused expert witness and the clear physics of the alleged device.

## Multiplying The Doubt: The '775 Patent Failure

The second patent, the ’775, presented a mathematical failure that was arguably more discrete. This patent required the generation of intermodulation product cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals in seven specific multiplications. The evidence relied upon heavily relied on a specific Nokia technical document to show that the accused radios performed these multiplications. On its face, the technical document did not show seven multiplications but rather three distinct ones: `x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|`, `x1⋅x1⋅x2'`, and `x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|`. While Finesse argued on appeal that the radio performed ten multiplications shown in a different document, there was no record evidence presented to the jury mapping those ten multiplications to the seven claimed ones.

The court noted that while the plaintiff had amended claims to add a “three signals” limitation, that did not preclude the radio from using two unique input signals and copies. However, the math did not add up for the seven distinct multiplications required by the claim. Dr. Wells testified about the three distinct multiplications shown in the document but offered no explanation for how those map onto the seven claimed multiplications. Because the mapping evidence was never presented to the jury, the Federal Circuit ruled that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications. The inability of the expert to connect the technical document to the specific claim language created a evidentiary hole that was too wide to stand.

## The Mechanics Of Destroying A Jury Award

The final procedural point in this opinion is the automatic vacatur of the damages award because the underlying noninfringement verdict was reversed. The district court had found infringement and awarded $166 million in lump-sum damages based on the remaining life of the patents. While the Fifth Circuit allows discretion on the grant of a new trial on damages, the Federal Circuit is clear that the procedural domino effect of a JMOL reversal commands the vacation of the damages. This stands in contrast to cases where infringement is found but damages methodology is disputed; here, the grant of the license was illegal from the start. The result is that regardless of the complexities of calculating patent life or reasonable royalties, the total absence of liability means the full award cannot exist. This mechanical link means that any damages award in lump-sum form is inherently parasitic to the merits of infringement.

## Downstream Effects And Litigation Strategy

The implications for patent litigation in the Eastern District Of Texas are significant. Practitioners in this venue often rely on maintaining the benefits of local familiarity and the imperitability of jury benefits. However, *Finesse Wireless* suggests that the Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of the interference of evidence will tightly constrain the claims that rely heavily on expert inference where the roadmap is unclear. This opinion reinforces the Federal Circuit’s stance that expert testimony cannot be substitutive for the technical reality of the asserted device. Prospectively, this means that patent owners looking to seek lump-sum damages must ensure their expert's testimony reconstructs the accused device with absolute precision, making every claimed limitation a verifiable fact rather than a best guess. The opinion reminds us that procedural correctness on the merits is the single strongest defense against high-value verdicts, and that the damages verdict is merely a mirror of the infringement decision, reflecting its light or fancy.
