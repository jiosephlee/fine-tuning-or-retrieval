Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# The Wall of Expert Testimony: Why Finesse v. AT&T Shows the Risk of Ambiguous Mapping

Patent litigation often entrails abstract legal questions to high-stakes engineering realities. When *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* reached the Federal Circuit in September 2025, it came on the heels of a district court in the Eastern District of Texas awarding over $166 million in damages. For Finesse and the implementer AT&T, the outcome turned not on the validity of the technology regarding intermodulation products, but on whether the evidence added up to a "substantial evidence" standard for jury infringement. The Federal Circuit's decision to reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) provides a stark lesson for patentees and their expert witnesses: a claim construction that is supported by a technically confused explanation will not survive appellate scrutiny.

The case highlights the Federal Circuit’s commitment to ensuring that infringement verdicts are supported by more than just a "fig leaf" of testimony that falls apart under cross-examination. By focusing so heavily on the internal logic of the expert’s mapping of technology to claim terms, the court effectively lowereds the playing field for plaintiffs who rely on constructive ambiguity to bridge the gap between their patents and the accused products.

## The '134 Patent and the "Sampling" Contradiction

At the heart of this reversal lies the asserted '134 patent, which covers a method for mitigating interference by isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals." The crucial limitation at issue required the accused radio to sample a passband that included both categories of signals simultaneously. In the legal arena, Finesse struggled to prove that the physical architecture of AT&T’s Nokia radios actually performed this action.

The core problem emerged during the testimony of Finesse's expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. Wells initially mapped the claimed "interference generating signals" to a "modeled PIM path" depicted in a technical document. However, when cross-examined, the physical impossibility of the theory surfaced. As the expert noted, the radio’s Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) samples signals *before* they are generated downstream; therefore, the ADC could not sample a path that physically occurs later in the circuit. Dr. Wells attempted to pivot, declaring that the inputs "x1 and x2" represented the signals of interest and interference, respectively. The problem is that he never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to those terms. Instead, he continued to maintain that the "modeled PIM path" was the interference signal, a position that contradicted his own explanation of the sampling mechanism.

The Federal Circuit was unswayed by the jury's leap of logic. Applying the Fifth Circuit's standard—which requires evidence to point so strongly in favor of one party that no reasonable jurors could conclude otherwise—the court found the expert's testimony insufficient. The court noted that Piecing together testimony from ninety pages apart to create a map that was never explicitly drawn in the evidence was not enough to satisfy the burden of proof. This decision underscores that when an expert rests their entire case on a shifting explanation that contradicts basic circuit theory, the jury has no basis to find infringement.

## The '775 Patent and Missing Mathematical Links

The decision regarding the '775 patent is less about physics and more about mathematics. This patent claimed a process where a receiver generates cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals ($S_1, S_2, S_3$) in seven specific multiplications. While the court allowed the '775 patent's infringement verdict regarding the "number of signals" to stand—finding no disavowal of scope regarding "three signals"—it revoked the finding on the "seven multiplications."

Finesse's infringement expert relied on a Nokia document showing three distinct multiplications versus the seven required by the claim. The expert did not provide evidence of how those three multiplications mapped to the seven necessary for infringement. Notably, the document involved operations like complex conjugates and modulus, which are not identical to the simple digital multiplications claimed. Finesse attempted to argue that these operations represented a larger pool of ten multiplications, but failed to identify which subset satisfied the claim.

The Federal Circuit emphasized that this evidentiary gap prevents a reasonable jury from finding infringement. The court noted that while drill-in amendments can sometimes be clear and unambiguous, the mathematical gap between what the Nokia document actually disclosed and what the claims required was not bridged by inference. It is worth noting that the court distinguished this from the '134 patent regarding the "two signals" argument, where Finesse did not surrender scope. This distinction highlights the court's precision in separating what constitutes a voluntary waiver of patent scope versus what constitutes a mere necessity to make a legitimate product work within the bounds of law.

## The Precedent of "Substantial Evidence" and Downstream Effects

The significance of *Finesse* extends beyond the specific electrical signals involved. The decision reaffirms that the Federal Circuit applies the intense scrutiny of the Fifth Circuit regarding the "substantial evidence" standard. This is notable in the Federal Circuit's national appellate forum, where the regional circuit often sets the tone for the regional standard of review. In patent cases, where the Federal Circuit is accustomed to its own strict evidentiary standards, adopting the "strongly and overwhelmingly" clarity test signals a move toward greater skepticism of patentee theories that require needle-in-a-haystack logic.

For litigation strategies, this ruling suggests that the "verdict sheet" alone is not enough to recover damages when the underlying proof is shaky. Patentees often survive summary judgment motions if they have a plausible expert opinion, but this ruling suggests that at the JMOL stage, that plausibility must be crystallized into clear, non-contradictory testimony. The more an expert has to pivot during cross-examination to salvage their logic, the higher the risk that appellate court will find no substantial evidence supported the verdict. More broadly, this might prompt experts to be more cautious about ambiguous claims during their testimony, and it might prompt patentees to refine the claim drafting process in the prosecution stage to ensure that the "invention" is more clearly defined before the facts of infringement are litigated.

## Conclusion

Ultimately, *Finesse v. AT&T* should be viewed as a cautionary tale against the seduction of expert testimony that relies on implicit connections. The decision signals that when an expert asks a jury to draw conclusions that they themselves did not articulate, and where that conclusion doesn't hold up to fundamental engineering or mathematical logic, the appellate court will step in. In the world of IP, where the line between valid protection and overreach is thin, this decision provides a clear boundary: the evidence must stand on its own merits, not on its refusal to admit faults during a trial. For the industry, the '134 patent's fate serves as a reminder that clarity is not just preferred—it is mandatory.
