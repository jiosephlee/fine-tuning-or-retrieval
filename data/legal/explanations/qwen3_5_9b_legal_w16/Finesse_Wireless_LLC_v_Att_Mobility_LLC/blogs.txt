Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# The Wall of Expert Testimony: Why Finesse v. AT&T Shows the Risk of Ambiguous Mapping

Patent litigation often entrails abstract legal questions to high-stakes engineering realities. When *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* reached the Federal Circuit in September 2025, it came on the heels of a district court in the Eastern District of Texas awarding over $166 million in damages. For Finesse and the implementer AT&T, the outcome turned not on the validity of the technology regarding intermodulation products, but on whether the evidence added up to a "substantial evidence" standard for jury infringement. The Federal Circuit's decision to reverse the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) provides a stark lesson for patentees and their expert witnesses: a claim construction that is supported by a technically confused explanation will not survive appellate scrutiny.

The case highlights the Federal Circuit’s commitment to ensuring that infringement verdicts are supported by more than just a "fig leaf" of testimony that falls apart under cross-examination. By focusing so heavily on the internal logic of the expert’s mapping of technology to claim terms, the court effectively lowereds the playing field for plaintiffs who rely on constructive ambiguity to bridge the gap between their patents and the accused products.

## The '134 Patent and the "Sampling" Contradiction

At the heart of this reversal lies the asserted '134 patent, which covers a method for mitigating interference by isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals." The crucial limitation at issue required the accused radio to sample a passband that included both categories of signals simultaneously. In the legal arena, Finesse struggled to prove that the physical architecture of AT&T’s Nokia radios actually performed this action.

The core problem emerged during the testimony of Finesse's expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. Wells initially mapped the claimed "interference generating signals" to a "modeled PIM path" depicted in a technical document. However, when cross-examined, the physical impossibility of the theory surfaced. As the expert noted, the radio’s Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) samples signals *before* they are generated downstream; therefore, the ADC could not sample a path that physically occurs later in the circuit. Dr. Wells attempted to pivot, declaring that the inputs "x1 and x2" represented the signals of interest and interference, respectively. The problem is that he never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to those terms. Instead, he continued to maintain that the "modeled PIM path" was the interference signal, a position that contradicted his own explanation of the sampling mechanism.

The Federal Circuit was unswayed by the jury's leap of logic. Applying the Fifth Circuit's standard—which requires evidence to point so strongly in favor of one party that no reasonable jurors could conclude otherwise—the court found the expert's testimony insufficient. The court noted that Piecing together testimony from ninety pages apart to create a map that was never explicitly drawn in the evidence was not enough to satisfy the burden of proof. This decision underscores that when an expert rests their entire case on a shifting explanation that contradicts basic circuit theory, the jury has no basis to find infringement.

## The '775 Patent and Missing Mathematical Links

The decision regarding the '775 patent is less about physics and more about mathematics. This patent claimed a process where a receiver generates cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals ($S_1, S_2, S_3$) in seven specific multiplications. While the court allowed the '775 patent's infringement verdict regarding the "number of signals" to stand—finding no disavowal of scope regarding "three signals"—it revoked the finding on the "seven multiplications."

Finesse's infringement expert relied on a Nokia document showing three distinct multiplications versus the seven required by the claim. The expert did not provide evidence of how those three multiplications mapped to the seven necessary for infringement. Notably, the document involved operations like complex conjugates and modulus, which are not identical to the simple digital multiplications claimed. Finesse attempted to argue that these operations represented a larger pool of ten multiplications, but failed to identify which subset satisfied the claim.

The Federal Circuit emphasized that this evidentiary gap prevents a reasonable jury from finding infringement. The court noted that while drill-in amendments can sometimes be clear and unambiguous, the mathematical gap between what the Nokia document actually disclosed and what the claims required was not bridged by inference. It is worth noting that the court distinguished this from the '134 patent regarding the "two signals" argument, where Finesse did not surrender scope. This distinction highlights the court's precision in separating what constitutes a voluntary waiver of patent scope versus what constitutes a mere necessity to make a legitimate product work within the bounds of law.

## The Precedent of "Substantial Evidence" and Downstream Effects

The significance of *Finesse* extends beyond the specific electrical signals involved. The decision reaffirms that the Federal Circuit applies the intense scrutiny of the Fifth Circuit regarding the "substantial evidence" standard. This is notable in the Federal Circuit's national appellate forum, where the regional circuit often sets the tone for the regional standard of review. In patent cases, where the Federal Circuit is accustomed to its own strict evidentiary standards, adopting the "strongly and overwhelmingly" clarity test signals a move toward greater skepticism of patentee theories that require needle-in-a-haystack logic.

For litigation strategies, this ruling suggests that the "verdict sheet" alone is not enough to recover damages when the underlying proof is shaky. Patentees often survive summary judgment motions if they have a plausible expert opinion, but this ruling suggests that at the JMOL stage, that plausibility must be crystallized into clear, non-contradictory testimony. The more an expert has to pivot during cross-examination to salvage their logic, the higher the risk that appellate court will find no substantial evidence supported the verdict. More broadly, this might prompt experts to be more cautious about ambiguous claims during their testimony, and it might prompt patentees to refine the claim drafting process in the prosecution stage to ensure that the "invention" is more clearly defined before the facts of infringement are litigated.

## Conclusion

Ultimately, *Finesse v. AT&T* should be viewed as a cautionary tale against the seduction of expert testimony that relies on implicit connections. The decision signals that when an expert asks a jury to draw conclusions that they themselves did not articulate, and where that conclusion doesn't hold up to fundamental engineering or mathematical logic, the appellate court will step in. In the world of IP, where the line between valid protection and overreach is thin, this decision provides a clear boundary: the evidence must stand on its own merits, not on its refusal to admit faults during a trial. For the industry, the '134 patent's fate serves as a reminder that clarity is not just preferred—it is mandatory.



## Prosecution History and the Perils of Narrowing: Was Finesse's 'Three-Signal' Claim a Trap?

**By [Your Name/Legal Avatar]**

The $166 million verdict in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* lay discarded like an expensive but defective telegraph key by the Federal Circuit on September 24, 2025. For the defendant, AT&T and Nokia, the outcome was a win on principle, but a strategic victory on a massive scale. The court reversed the district court's denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, effectively tossing the jury's finding that AT&T infringed Finesse's patents. While the vacuum in our attention to this case might suggest the details are dry and routine, the fight for key claim construction and the interpretation of prosecution history makes *Finesse* a critical data point for patent practitioners. Specifically, the court's analysis of the '775 patent steps deep into the treacherous waters of disavowing claim scope. It illustrates a nuanced reality: smart prosecution history maneuvers can protect scope, but they cannot shield you from the rigorous demands of technical drafting and expert testimony.

The heart of the blog's focus is the '775 patent, which covers methods for interfering intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation in radios. During prosecution, Finesse amended the claim language to recite "generating impulse product (IMP) cancellation signals... using copies of transmitter signals... digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2 and S3." This points to a conflict with earlier claims that mentioned "two or three signals." AT&T argued that by amending to "three," Finesse surrendered claim scope to cover only two signals, thereby barring infringement of the Nokia radio they alleged used only two inputs. If the surrender argument held, "two or three" would be interpreted as "three," and the phone would escape infringement. The Federal Circuit rejected the surrender argument, holding that "clear and unambiguous disavowal" was not required to show scope over two unique input signals.

The legal standard governing this issue comes from *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019), which requires patentees to engage in a clear and unambiguous disavowal of known claim scope to be bound by prosecution history. In *Finesse*, the court looked at the context of the rejection. The Patent Office rejected the "two or three" claims as obvious over a reference called *Filipovic*. However, the examiner admitted that *Filipovic* taught "active" IMP mitigation, whereas Finesse's patent addressed "passive" IMP mitigation. When Finesse amended their claims to "three signals," the court found that they did not unambiguously disavow a product that used two signals. Why? Because the focus of the amendment was likely to overcome a rejection based on the *type* of interference (active vs. passive) rather than the absolute number of signal inputs. This is a crucial doctrinal distinction for practitioners. It means that narrowing claims to overcome a rejection doesn't always function as a surrender for the purpose of estopping coverage, provided the examiner's reasoning focuses on a specific distinguishing feature unrelated to the narrowed language.

However, let's call out the irony here. Despite successfully maneuvering around the "clear and unambiguous disavowal" standard, Finesse still lost the infringement claim related to the '775 patent. The court did not reverse the denial of JMOL because the "three signals" limitation was met; rather, they vacated the damages because Finesse failed to prove the "seven multiplications" limitation. Dr. Wells, Finesse's own expert, relied on a Nokia document that only showed three distinct multiplications and failed to map those to the seven claimed. The court noted that Finesse's expert never testified how the three distinct multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications, and asked the jury to draw inferences that were "confusing and unclear."

This leads to the real takeaway: prosecution history doesn't guarantee a patentee survives the "fine print" of their own invention. While the argument that Finesse couldn't surrender to "two signals" when the licensee only used two was rejected by the Federal Circuit, the underlying limit on the *math* of the invention was fatal. The '775 patent required seven multiplications, but the evidence only supported three distinct ones. By fixing the claim language (or failing to clear the bar of disavowal), the patentee might assume they had secured a fortress. But if the underlying technical capability of the accused device doesn't match the specific numeric requirement, the strongest construct is still hollow.

The implications for downstream litigation are significant. For patentees, this suggests that narrowing claims during prosecution is a tactical necessity in the face of obviousness rejections, but it shouldn't be used as a crutch in litigation to assume the scope is broader than the intrinsic evidence supports. The "trap" isn't necessarily that the narrowing forces the claim to exclude the infringer; in *Finesse*, the court explicitly said it didn't. The trap is that you can't confuse claim construction victories with factual wins. You can argue that "three signals" didn't mean "exclusive of two," but you still had to prove the accused device did the seven multiplications that three inputs seemed capable of producing.

For the defense, the decision reinforces that expert testimony must be explicit. Finesse's expert was dragged on cross-examination for the time he insisted one signal path was the interference generating one, and then pivoted to "two signals" without clarifying the mapping. The opinion is stern in rejecting idea that an expert can "piece together" testimony from pages apart to force an infringement finding. The fact that the Federal Circuit looked at the competence of the expert testimony alongside the prosecution history is a reminder that infringement is a matter of fact, not just construction.

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* serves as a reminder of the asymmetry between drafting history and courtroom performance. Prosecution history is a shield against estoppel, but the sword of infringement is held in the hands of the evidence. Even if the court says your narrowed claim still covers the defendant, if you cannot prove the defendant actualized the math described in the patent, the shield offers no protection. For intellectual property counsel, the lesson is clear: don't narrow your claims so aggressively that you risk estoppel, but don't count on prosecution history carrying the weight of a jury verdict where the underlying technical evidence is thin. In this balance, *Finesse* found a moment of clarity before a loud crash.



# Damages and Procedure: How a JMOL Reversal Erases the $166M Verdict

## Why This Matter Matters

The sheer scale of the damages awarded in a patent infringement case is often the news hook that captures market attention. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit addressed a verdict that included a lump-sum damages award of $166 million across the remaining life of two patents. Yet, the outcome of this appeal wasn't just a math adjustment; it was a procedural demolition. The Federal Circuit did not coddle the evidence it found. Instead, in a reversal presided over by Chief Judge Moore, the Court effectively tuned the standards for sufficiency of evidence in the Eastern District of Texas back to a higher level of scrutiny. The result is a stark reminder for patent owners that the rigidity of jury verdicts—particularly lump-sum damages—depends entirely on the integrity of the underlying infringement finding. This case serves as a cautionary tale for those viewing regional venue motion districts: even as trial courts in Texas frequently handle high-volume patent disputes, the appellate perspective remains skeptical of expert-led inferences born of imprecise testimony.

## The Eastern District Of Texas Procedural Pipeline

A critical takeaway from the opinion lies in the procedural framework the Federal Circuit applied. Because the district court for the Eastern District of Texas denied the motion for judgment as a matter of law, the Court of Appeals was required to defer to the regional standard of review. Citing *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, the Federal Circuit adopted the Fifth Circuit's de novo review for JMOL, which demands a showing that evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not arrive at a contrary conclusion. This standard differs from the substantial evidence test applied to factual findings in patent infringement. The opinion reveals that the district court's reliance on piecemeal testimony to bridge gaps in an expert's timeline was insufficient to meet the high bar required for noninfringement. While Eastern District judges often face criticism for volume, the Federal Circuit is reinforcing that the burden of proof remains with the plaintiff, and legal arguments about the sufficiency of that burden are not lightly waived at the Rule 50 stage.

## Battle for The Wire: The '134 Patent Failure

The first pillar of the appellate upset involved the ’134 patent, where the core dispute centered on the physical reality of signal sampling. The patent claimed a method where the radio samples a passband of signals that includes “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” For infringement to be found, the radio must sample these signals *before* the interference generating signals are created. The district court relied on Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Wells, to map these signals to the defendant’s Nokia radios. The trouble was that Wells' testimony contained a paradoxical loop that no reasonable jury could have reasonably justified.

During cross-examination, appellate counsel pointed out that the radio could not sample modeled intermodulation products generated downstream after the analog-to-digital converter had already sampled data. Dr. Wells initially mapped the modeled PIM path to the claimed interference signals but later retreated to vague references to inputs x1 and x2 without explicitly tying them to the claimed terms. The Federal Circuit found the expert's pivot confusing and self-contradictory. If the modeled PIM path were the interference generating signal, limitation one of the claim could not be met because that signal was not in the passband before sampling. However, the district court held that because Dr. Wells had testimony on x1 and x2, there was sufficient evidence. The appellate Court found no reasonable jury could have bridged that gap between a confused expert witness and the clear physics of the alleged device.

## Multiplying The Doubt: The '775 Patent Failure

The second patent, the ’775, presented a mathematical failure that was arguably more discrete. This patent required the generation of intermodulation product cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals in seven specific multiplications. The evidence relied upon heavily relied on a specific Nokia technical document to show that the accused radios performed these multiplications. On its face, the technical document did not show seven multiplications but rather three distinct ones: `x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|`, `x1⋅x1⋅x2'`, and `x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|`. While Finesse argued on appeal that the radio performed ten multiplications shown in a different document, there was no record evidence presented to the jury mapping those ten multiplications to the seven claimed ones.

The court noted that while the plaintiff had amended claims to add a “three signals” limitation, that did not preclude the radio from using two unique input signals and copies. However, the math did not add up for the seven distinct multiplications required by the claim. Dr. Wells testified about the three distinct multiplications shown in the document but offered no explanation for how those map onto the seven claimed multiplications. Because the mapping evidence was never presented to the jury, the Federal Circuit ruled that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications. The inability of the expert to connect the technical document to the specific claim language created a evidentiary hole that was too wide to stand.

## The Mechanics Of Destroying A Jury Award

The final procedural point in this opinion is the automatic vacatur of the damages award because the underlying noninfringement verdict was reversed. The district court had found infringement and awarded $166 million in lump-sum damages based on the remaining life of the patents. While the Fifth Circuit allows discretion on the grant of a new trial on damages, the Federal Circuit is clear that the procedural domino effect of a JMOL reversal commands the vacation of the damages. This stands in contrast to cases where infringement is found but damages methodology is disputed; here, the grant of the license was illegal from the start. The result is that regardless of the complexities of calculating patent life or reasonable royalties, the total absence of liability means the full award cannot exist. This mechanical link means that any damages award in lump-sum form is inherently parasitic to the merits of infringement.

## Downstream Effects And Litigation Strategy

The implications for patent litigation in the Eastern District Of Texas are significant. Practitioners in this venue often rely on maintaining the benefits of local familiarity and the imperitability of jury benefits. However, *Finesse Wireless* suggests that the Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of the interference of evidence will tightly constrain the claims that rely heavily on expert inference where the roadmap is unclear. This opinion reinforces the Federal Circuit’s stance that expert testimony cannot be substitutive for the technical reality of the asserted device. Prospectively, this means that patent owners looking to seek lump-sum damages must ensure their expert's testimony reconstructs the accused device with absolute precision, making every claimed limitation a verifiable fact rather than a best guess. The opinion reminds us that procedural correctness on the merits is the single strongest defense against high-value verdicts, and that the damages verdict is merely a mirror of the infringement decision, reflecting its light or fancy.
