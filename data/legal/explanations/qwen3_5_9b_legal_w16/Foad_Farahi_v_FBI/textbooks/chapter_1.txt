Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## Foad Farahi's Immigration and FBI Profile
Understanding the context of Foad Farahi’s involvement with the government is essential to appreciating the stakes of the freedom of information request. Farahi, an Iranian national, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings following a visa overstay. His immigration case was complex, involving applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. During these proceedings, the Department of Homeland Security relied on documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who documented Farahi’s alleged connections to high-level terrorists. This interaction established a specific factual relationship between Farahi and the FBI, which served as the foundation for the government’s claim that his records were compiled for law enforcement purposes.

## The Nature of the FOIA Request (Volume and Scope)
Foad Farahi initiated the litigation by filing a Freedom of Information Act request for his FBI file. In response, the agency attempted to locate large volumes of potentially responsive records. The record indicates that the FBI located approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing information responsive to the request. After the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding, Farahi filed suit. Over the course of several years, the agency conducted a review, disclosed some information, and withheld the majority of the requested materials. This voluminous record created a complex factual picture regarding the scope of the withholding decisions, necessitating extensive judicial review of whether the records fell within statutory exemptions.

## Litigation Timeline and District Court Deadlines
The procedural history spans several years as the parties navigated the district court's requirements. After Farahi sued in 2018, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019. In support, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section. The Hardy declaration asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement proceedings and could interfere with pending enforcement proceedings. Importantly, the FBI moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. The district court granted this motion in October 2020, recognizing that providing certain details required protections to preserve the necessity of the secrecy claim.

## Initial Summary Judgment Motion by the FBI
When the district court adjudicated the initial summary judgment motion in November 2022, the court concluded that the FBI likely met its burden to show the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes. The court noted that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden to show the records could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. However, the court identified gaps in the briefing process. Specifically, due to a significant passage of time, the court required the FBI to update its declarations to assess whether the enforcement proceedings remained relevant. Furthermore, the court determined it needed additional information regarding segregability to decide whether the non-exempt portions of records could be disclosed. Consequently, the district court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

## First Amendment/Adjudication (Substitutionary) Order and the Update Requirement
The procedural mechanism employed by the district court to address the FBI's burden of proof is central to the litigation's development. In its initial ruling, the judge ordered the FBI to update its assessment regarding the pendency of enforcement proceedings. This step was critical because the court needed to ensure that the enforcement proceedings referenced in the Hardy declaration remained "pending or reasonably anticipated" at the time of the ruling. The court also determined that more was needed to address segregability. This judicial intervention effectively forced the agency to refresh its legal basis for withholding, acknowledging that the duration of potential investigations required dynamic justification rather than static claims.

## Renewed Motion and Final District Court Judgment
Following the district court's order, the FBI resubmitted declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This submission included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender explained that RIDS had consulted the agent overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. Bender’s ex parte declaration expanded on this confirmation and provided additional details addressing the court's earlier concerns regarding segregability. The district court then granted the renewed motion. The court credited the FBI's confirmation that relevant proceedings were still ongoing and found, based on the ex parte submission, that the FBI had adequately explained why exempt material was not reasonably segregable. The agency successfully retained its withholding decisions on appeal.

## Grounds for Appeal on Foad Farahi's Part
Farahi’s appeal focuses primarily on the sufficiency of the FBI’s post-update justifications. A primary contention is that the passage of time since the initial 2019 declarations casts doubt on the pendency of the enforcement proceedings. Farahi argues that invoking Exemption 7(A) requires more than a vague assertion that an investigation remains ongoing. He challenges the Bender declaration as being surprisingly terse in isolation, suggesting it does not sufficiently substantiate the claim of ongoing investigations. Farahi also objects to the definitions of document categories used for withholding, arguing they were overbroad. Finally, he challenges the district court's reliance on ex parte declarations regarding segregability, claiming that no meaningful information should have been deemed non-segregable given the massive volume of records involved.
