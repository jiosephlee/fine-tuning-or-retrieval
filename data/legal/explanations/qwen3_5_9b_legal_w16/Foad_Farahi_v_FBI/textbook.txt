Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## Foad Farahi's Immigration and FBI Profile
Understanding the context of Foad Farahi’s involvement with the government is essential to appreciating the stakes of the freedom of information request. Farahi, an Iranian national, has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings following a visa overstay. His immigration case was complex, involving applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. During these proceedings, the Department of Homeland Security relied on documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who documented Farahi’s alleged connections to high-level terrorists. This interaction established a specific factual relationship between Farahi and the FBI, which served as the foundation for the government’s claim that his records were compiled for law enforcement purposes.

## The Nature of the FOIA Request (Volume and Scope)
Foad Farahi initiated the litigation by filing a Freedom of Information Act request for his FBI file. In response, the agency attempted to locate large volumes of potentially responsive records. The record indicates that the FBI located approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs containing information responsive to the request. After the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding, Farahi filed suit. Over the course of several years, the agency conducted a review, disclosed some information, and withheld the majority of the requested materials. This voluminous record created a complex factual picture regarding the scope of the withholding decisions, necessitating extensive judicial review of whether the records fell within statutory exemptions.

## Litigation Timeline and District Court Deadlines
The procedural history spans several years as the parties navigated the district court's requirements. After Farahi sued in 2018, the FBI filed a motion for summary judgment in October 2019. In support, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section. The Hardy declaration asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement proceedings and could interfere with pending enforcement proceedings. Importantly, the FBI moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. The district court granted this motion in October 2020, recognizing that providing certain details required protections to preserve the necessity of the secrecy claim.

## Initial Summary Judgment Motion by the FBI
When the district court adjudicated the initial summary judgment motion in November 2022, the court concluded that the FBI likely met its burden to show the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes. The court noted that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden to show the records could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. However, the court identified gaps in the briefing process. Specifically, due to a significant passage of time, the court required the FBI to update its declarations to assess whether the enforcement proceedings remained relevant. Furthermore, the court determined it needed additional information regarding segregability to decide whether the non-exempt portions of records could be disclosed. Consequently, the district court denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice.

## First Amendment/Adjudication (Substitutionary) Order and the Update Requirement
The procedural mechanism employed by the district court to address the FBI's burden of proof is central to the litigation's development. In its initial ruling, the judge ordered the FBI to update its assessment regarding the pendency of enforcement proceedings. This step was critical because the court needed to ensure that the enforcement proceedings referenced in the Hardy declaration remained "pending or reasonably anticipated" at the time of the ruling. The court also determined that more was needed to address segregability. This judicial intervention effectively forced the agency to refresh its legal basis for withholding, acknowledging that the duration of potential investigations required dynamic justification rather than static claims.

## Renewed Motion and Final District Court Judgment
Following the district court's order, the FBI resubmitted declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This submission included both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender explained that RIDS had consulted the agent overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. Bender’s ex parte declaration expanded on this confirmation and provided additional details addressing the court's earlier concerns regarding segregability. The district court then granted the renewed motion. The court credited the FBI's confirmation that relevant proceedings were still ongoing and found, based on the ex parte submission, that the FBI had adequately explained why exempt material was not reasonably segregable. The agency successfully retained its withholding decisions on appeal.

## Grounds for Appeal on Foad Farahi's Part
Farahi’s appeal focuses primarily on the sufficiency of the FBI’s post-update justifications. A primary contention is that the passage of time since the initial 2019 declarations casts doubt on the pendency of the enforcement proceedings. Farahi argues that invoking Exemption 7(A) requires more than a vague assertion that an investigation remains ongoing. He challenges the Bender declaration as being surprisingly terse in isolation, suggesting it does not sufficiently substantiate the claim of ongoing investigations. Farahi also objects to the definitions of document categories used for withholding, arguing they were overbroad. Finally, he challenges the district court's reliance on ex parte declarations regarding segregability, claiming that no meaningful information should have been deemed non-segregable given the massive volume of records involved.

# Legislative and Judicial Framework for FOIA Exemption 7(A)

## The Freedom of Information Act's General Mandate
The foundational principle of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is that federal agency records are presumptively available to the public upon request. Under the Act, agencies must release relevant records unless a specific statutory exemption applies. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit recalled this basic structure when explaining why the FBI could withhold Farahi's file. The statute codifies a right to access, creating a statutory scheme where disclosure is the default and withholding is the exception. The petitioner, Farahi, initiated a suit after the FBI withheld the majority of records in response to his request. The Court noted that because the FBI is a federal agency, it is obligated to comply with the statutory and regulatory requirements set forth in the FOIA. The default position is transparency, meaning the burden of justification rests on the agency to articulate valid reasons for any non-disclosure. This statutory design ensures that the executive branch remains open to public scrutiny, except where Congress has explicitly authorized withholding.

## The Specifics of Exemption 7(A) Statutory Language
To validly withhold records under the FOIA, an agency must fall within an enumerated exemption. In this case, the points of contention hinged on Exemption 7(A), which protects records specifically compiled for law enforcement purposes if disclosure is expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. The statute requires two distinct prongs to be met: the records must be linked to law enforcement, and their release must pose a specific interference risk. The D.C. Circuit clarified in *Farahi* that these proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." The Court referenced its precedent, including *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ*, to establish that the mere conception of an enforcement action does not qualify; there must be a concrete, actionable enforcement trajectory. Furthermore, the statute implies that "reasonably severable" portions of documents must be released while exempt portions remain redacted. The Court in *Farahi* emphasized that Exemption 7(A) is not a blanket immunity for law enforcement records; rather, it is a conditional privilege that triggers only when the specific statutory predicates regarding the nature of the file and the potential harm of its release are satisfied.

## Burden of Proof on the Government
Under the FOIA framework, the agency bears the ultimate burden of proving that an exemption applies to the records being withheld. This dynamic is crucial because the requesting party often does not possess the information needed to rebut an agency's assertion. In *Farahi*, the Court explicitly stated that the government must demonstrate that the withheld records fall within an exemption and that the specific legal requirements of that exemption have been met. The opinion clarified that this burden applies to the initial justification of the exemption as well as the final determination of segregability. When an agency uses an exemption to withhold a record, it must provide a factual basis for its determination, such as affidavits or declarations. While the Court in *Farahi* scrutinized the FBI's submissions, it underscored that absent a burden-shifting rule, it is the agency's responsibility to raise the color of a defense by objectifying its reason for withholding.

## Standard of Review for Summary Judgment (De Novo)
Appellate courts generally review the legal and factual conclusions reached by district courts during the summary judgment process. In *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit stated that FOIA cases are reviewed *de novo* regarding the grant of summary judgment. This means the appellate court reviews the district court's application of the law to the evidence without deference to the lower court's reasoning. The *de novo* standard is particularly relevant because it requires the appellate court to independently assess whether the government has carried its burden of proof. The Court in *Farahi* examined whether the FBI's declarations met the statutory requirements, rather than simply deferring to the district court's initial assessment of the affidavits. By applying a *de novo* standard, the Court ensures that the statutory balancing of public disclosure against law enforcement interests is scrutinized carefully at the appellate level, particularly given the importance of the right to access government information.

## Deference to Agency Determinations in National Security/Enforcement Cases
Although the standard of review is *de novo*, the legal weight of agency determinations varies based on the subject matter. The Court in *Farahi* noted that the FBI, as a specialized law enforcement agency, is entitled to a "fair measure of deference" when asserting Exemption 7(A). This deference is "particularly appropriate for records implicating national security," where the executive branch possesses specialized expertise that courts may not match. In the context of counterterrorism investigations, as seen in *Farahi*, the Court has consistently reiterated that deference to the executive is warranted when national security concerns are implicated. However, the opinion specifies that deference is not absolute; the agency's declaration does not need to be pére imperious or beyond reproach, but must establish a "rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties." The Court accepted the FBI's declaration as meeting this high threshold because it detailed the investigation into national security violations, thereby balancing judicial oversight with executive competence in sensitive areas.

## Requirement for Reasonable Segregation of Nonexempt Information
Even when a record falls within an exemption, the FOIA mandates that agencies segregate and release nonexempt information. The statute requires that any reasonably segregable portion of a record be provided after deletion of exempt portions. In *Farahi*, the district court and the Court of Appeals focused heavily on whether the non-exempt portions could be physically separated from the withheld material. The Court reiterated that the "focus of the FOIA is information," not words written down by the Government, meaning that information too entangled to be separated should be withheld in its entirety if non-exempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt material. A critical procedural element addressed in *Farahi* involved the use of ex parte declarations to justify non-segregability. The appellate court affirmed the district court's reliance on these sealed declarations for that specific analysis, noting that while ex parte declarations generally handicap FOIA plaintiffs, they are inherent in cases where the materials sought are precisely what would be disclosed by a public record. Thus, the Court held that the FBI had provided a detailed justification for why no meaningful information was reasonably segregable from the law enforcement records.

# Substantive Application of Exemption 7(A) Elements

## Establishing a 'Law Enforcement Purpose' Nexus
To prevail on a claim under Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) Exemption 7(A), the government must demonstrate that the records withheld were compiled for law enforcement purposes. Exemption 7(A) protectively covers records maintained for the purpose of law enforcement if their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. Under *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the court applied this standard by examining the specific agency and the nature of the investigation. Because the FBI is inherently a law enforcement agency, the district court in *Farahi* granted deference to the agency’s determination that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes. However, this deference assumes the agency’s declarations establish a rational “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” as well as a connection between the individual and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. In *Farahi*, the court credited the FBI’s assertions that its files were compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism. The court noted that while the agency asserted this purpose, it could not rely solely on rote assertions; the declarations had to provide a rational basis connecting the file to these statutory functions. The appellate court found the FBI's burden satisfied because the agency’s declaration linked the records to pending law enforcement proceedings regarding the subject’s association with individuals involved in terrorism.

## Determining 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated' Proceedings
Beyond the purpose of compilation, Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure of the records could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated. Expleteness of these proceedings is a crucial statutory threshold derived from the FOIA’s text. The court in *Farahi* interpreted this requirement to include ongoing criminal investigations, stating that so long as an investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, the exemption can apply if that case would be jeopardized by premature release. In *Farahi*, the district court initially granted summary judgment but vacated the decision to order the FBI to reevaluate whether enforcement proceedings remained pending. This procedural step underscored the court’s requirement for a factual update rather than a static assumption of pendency. The appellate court affirmed the district court’s renewed decision, emphasizing that the relevant investigation remained ongoing as confirmed by supplemental agency declarations. This holding illustrates that the burden rests upon the government to affirmatively show that the clandestine proceedings exist at the time of the litigation, and the court assesses whether the specific records sought could interfere with the progress of those specific proceedings.

## The 'Tipping Off' Theory of Interference
When a government agency seeks to withhold information under Exemption 7(A), it must articulate how release would interfere with enforcement proceedings. The most common justification recognized by the judiciary for such interference is the feared risk of “tipping off” the subjects of the investigation. *Farahi* describes this reasoning by reiterating that disclosure of information regarding an ongoing investigation could reasonably be expected to interfere by informing the targets about the scope of the investigation, thereby impairing law enforcement efforts. This rationale is rooted in the understanding that law enforcement requires the element of surprise to effectively gather evidence and maintain operational security. The court in *Farahi* accepted the government's assertion that releasing the records would expose the scope of the investigation, a standard that aligns with prior D.C. Circuit precedent regarding Exemption 7(A). The court reasoned that the classic justification for withholding information is the preservation of key evidence by preventing the targets from altering their behavior, which directly supports the interference prong of the exemption.

## Rethinking the Passage of Time (Staleness Argument)
Time often poses a challemge for the government in FOIA litigation, particularly in terrorism investigations where statutes or investigations may span years. In *Farahi*, because the investigation began years before the litigation, Farahi argued the claim of interference was stale, asserting that the agency’s declaration regarding the pendency of proceedings was no longer credible due to the passage of time. The district court initially ordered a supplemental declaration to address this concern. The appellate court agreed that the earlier declaration alone was insufficient to demonstrate current pendency because of the significant time that had elapsed. However, the court affirmed the final grant of summary judgment because the agency provided an updated declaration that confirmed the investigation remained ongoing. This analysis highlights that an older declaration can still be valid if supported by regular updates that confirm the status of the investigation, rather than being treated as superseded by the time that passes. The key takeaway is that the court does not categorically reject the Exemption 7(A) due to time alone, provided the agency can substantiate the current status of the proceedings through updated evidence.

## Sufficiency of Government Affidavits (Hardy and Bender Declarations)
To satisfy Exemption 7(A), the agency must provide affidavits or declarations that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated or why prosecution would be impeded. In *Farahi*, the government relied on declarations from agency officials David Hardy and Joseph Bender to satisfy the burden of proof. The court emphasized that a plaintiff must object to these declarations to challenge their adequacy on appeal, and since Farahi did not formally oppose the motions to submit ex parte declarations, the appellate court deferred to the district court's granular review. The court found that the public declaration from Hardy, while brief, provided the necessary attestation of existence, and the ex parte declaration supplied necessary detail. The distinction between public and ex parte declarations here is significant; while public declarations must state the general grounds for withholding, the ex parte submissions allow for a detailed justification to strangers where national security or privacy is at stake, provided the record of the ex parte nature is kept confidential or subject to court approval. The court affirmed that the combined effect of the Hardy and Bender declarations was sufficient to withstand the burden.

## Contextual Evidence: Terrorist Connections and Ongoing Investigations
While agency affidavits are the primary evidence of interference, the underlying context of the investigation provides the macro justification for the exemption. In *Farahi*, the court looked beyond the abstract assertions to the specific nature of the targets of the investigation to determine if the government’s claim of interference was plausible. The court noted that the investigation targeted an individual associated with the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood and connections to high-level terrorists such as Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and Jose Padilla, as well as support for charities that financed terrorism. This factual background reinforced the reasonableness of the government’s belief that disclosure would interfere with enforcement proceedings. The court reasoned that the broader context of the terrorist investigation confirmed the pendency of the proceedings and the plausibility of the “tipping off” risk. Thus, when reviewing Exemption 7(A) claims involving national security, the court considers the nature of the underlying case file to ensure the agency’s claim of pending enforcement is not merely a generic label, but a representation supported by the specific factual circumstances of the investigation.

# Segregation Duties and Review of Ex Parte Materials

## The Obligation to Release 'Reasonably Segregable' Material

In the context of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation, even when an agency invokes an exemption to withhold records, a fundamental statutory duty remains: the release of any information that is not covered by that exemption. The court notes in Section I and again in Section V that the Freedom of Information Act requires agencies to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." This obligation is codified in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii), which instructs the agency to disclose "any reasonably segregable portion of a record after deletion of the portions which are exempt." Consequently, an agency cannot use a FOIA exemption as a blanket justification to withhold an entire document if non-exempt portions can be unidentified and released. In *Farahi*, the agency sought to withhold vast volumes of records under Exemption 7(A), but the district court still applied this segregation requirement. The court concluded that the FBI had satisfied its burden by providing a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable, thereby meeting their statutory obligation to release what they could.

## The 'Inextricably Intertwined' Standard for Full Withholding

While the segregation duty is general, it is not absolute. The court explains that an agency may withhold records in their entirety only when non-exempt portions are "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." This standard, drawn from *Juarez v. DOJ* and *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force*, creates a narrow exception to the requirement of releasing public information. In *Farahi*, the agency argued that the non-exempt portions could not be separated without releasing information that the government claimed would interfere with law enforcement proceedings. The court affirmed the district court's conclusion, which relied on the agency's assertion that the exemption applied to the whole file because the material could not be further segregated. The analysis focuses on whether physical separation is possible without compromising the rationale for the exemption. Under this standard, if documents are heavily redacted or if the non-exempt information is merged with the exempt information in a way that prevents release, full withholding is permissible.

## Line-by-Line vs. Meaningful Information Segregation

A critical limitation on the segregation duty is the definition of what constitutes a "meaningful" portion of a record eligible for release. The court references *Mead Data Cent.* and *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs.* to explain that the "focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." This distinction is vital for students reading the opinion because it clarifies that the requirement is not to release even random words or isolated phrases that might be exempted from context. The court rejected Farahi’s argument that there must be some segregable, releasable information in the massive volume of records. Instead, the court found that non-exempt material was "randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," rendering it not "reasonably segregable" in a practical sense. Thus, the law does not require agencies to surgically remove irrelevant public words from a document where such extraction is functionally impossible.

## Procedural Acceptance of Ex Parte Declarations

Courts must also address how they make their fact-finding determinations when sensitive information is involved. In *Farahi*, the district court relied on ex parte declarations to make its decision on the segregability analysis. Specifically, the court credited information from the Acting Section Chief of RIDS that was submitted under seal and not available to the public record. Farahi argued that the court relied too much on these ex parte materials, but the appellate court affirmed the procedural propriety of this action. Citing *Perioperative Servs.*, 57 F.4th at 1067, the court reasoned that this problem is "inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." The court emphasized that nothing requires a court to put its analysis of an ex parte declaration on the public record if doing so would defeat the very purpose of keeping records confidential. Furthermore, the court noted that Farahi had not opposed the district court's acceptance of these declarations, which further legitimized the reliance on the sealed submissions for the record.

## Forfeiture of Segregability Challenges (Farahi's Silence)

Procedural history can sometimes limit the substantive arguments a party can raise on appeal. In this opinion, a specific issue regarding the categories of documents the FBI used to withhold records is relevant to the segregation discussion. In Section IV, the court addresses Farahi's objection that the government did not adequately justify the categories identified by the FBI, noting that Farahi did not explain why the categories were overbroad. Because the plaintiff failed to specify why the categories used by the agency were improper, the court concluded that Farahi "forfeited his arguments on this score." The opinion cites *Khine v. DHS*, explaining that plaintiffs should not complain about category definitions if they have not detailed why the agency’s specific categorization was flawed. This establishes a rule of confinement: when an appellant fails to reach across the arguments to explain why a segregation strategy (or lack thereof) is flawed, that argument may be considered waived for appellate purposes.

## Final Disposition and Affirmance of the District Court

The ultimate disposition in *Farahi* rests on the appellate court's de novo review of the summary judgment, where the district court correctly applied Exemption 7(A) and fulfilled the segregation duties. The court found that the FBI's burden was met regarding the compilation of records for law enforcement purposes and the expectation that release would interfere with ongoing investigations. The court also accepted the FBI's justification that the records were not reasonably segregable and upheld the reliance on ex parte declarations as procedurally acceptable. By affirming the district court, the D.C. Circuit validated the FBI's withholding of the records, signaling that when an agency provides a specific, detailed rationale for secrecy, supported by ex parte declarations when necessary, the district court's reliance on that evidence is sufficient to satisfy FOIA requirements. The dismissal of Farahi's appeal confirms that the protections of Exemption 7(A) and the procedural accommodations for ex parte evidence work together to safeguard law enforcement investigations.
