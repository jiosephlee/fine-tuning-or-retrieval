Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does 'de novo' review of summary judgment coexist with agency deference in FOIA Exemption 7(A) cases?
Question:
I'm confused by the court's reasoning in Paragraph III. It states that we review the grant of summary judgment 'de novo', but then says the FBI's decision is entitled to 'deference.' If the court reviews it de novo, aren't they supposed to decide the issue themselves? Does the term 'deference' mean the court only checks for bad faith or frivolousness, or does it actually defer to the FBI's 'law enforcement' determination?
Answer:
The apparent tension between *de novo* review and agency deference in the D.C. Circuit's *Farahi* opinion arises from confusing two distinct concepts: the standard of review applied by the appellate court, and the evidentiary weight given to the agency’s expertise.

**"De Novo" Review Describes the Appellate Court’s Duty**
The court’s use of the term *de novo* in Paragraph III refers to its independent responsibility to evaluate whether the lower court reached the correct legal conclusion. The appellate court does not defer to the district court's ruling; rather, it determines for itself whether the government satisfied its burden of proof. As the opinion states: "In FOIA cases... we review a grant of summary judgment de novo." This means the appellate court asks, "Did the FBI meet the statutory requirements for Exemption 7(A)?"

**"Deference" Describes the Weight Given to Agency Expertise**
The term "deference" refers to how the court evaluates the agency's determination that the records involve law enforcement purposes. Because the agency possesses specific expertise that a judge or plaintiff typically lacks, the court gives its decision significant weight, but not abrogated authority. Paragraph III notes, "Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference." This deference is due to the agency's unique familiarity with the nature of its investigations.

**Reconciling the Two: The "Rational Nexus" Standard**
The *Farahi* opinion reconciles these approaches by applying deference *to* the agency's determination *within* a *de novo* review process. Deference does not mean the court blindly accepts the FBI's word; it means the agency only needs to show a connection between the records and law enforcement duties that is logical, not proven beyond a doubt. In Paragraph IV(A), the court clarifies this balance:

"Still, its declarations must 'establish a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties,' as well as a 'connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.''"

This "rational nexus" is the standard that allows deference without eliminating judicial oversight. The appellate court reviews the record *de novo* to see if this nexus exists. In *Farahi*, the court found the nexus satisfied because the Hardy declaration detailed the FBI's specialized counter-terrorism investigations, satisfying the statutory burden despite the passage of time.

**Summary**
The *Farahi* court is not deferring to the FBI's decision, nor is it merely checking for bad faith. It is independently deciding (*de novo*) whether the FBI met the burden of proof. The "deference" acknowledges that the FBI's determination of what constitutes a law enforcement investment is entitled to a "fair measure of deference" because it requires only a rational connection between the investigation and the agency's duties, as the opinion demonstrates in its analysis of the FBI's declarations.

### Does an 'updated' affidavit cure the defect of a declaration being too 'stale' due to the passage of time?
Question:
In Paragraph II, the district court initially denied the FBI's motion because the Hardy declaration was 'stale' given the time passage. But when the FBI renewed the motion with Bender, the court allowed it. Does the government need to prove that a *new* proceeding was started, or is a declaration simply updating the status of an old proceeding enough to satisfy the 'pending or reasonably anticipated' standard without fresh investigation details?
Answer:
Yes, an updated declaration is sufficient to cure the defect of "staleness" without the government needing to prove that a new proceeding was started. Under FOIA Exemption 7(A), an agency may withhold records if their disclosure could "reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." While significant passage of time can make a declaration stale, the circuit court in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, 2025 WL ___ (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), held that a supplemental declaration updating the status of an original proceeding can satisfy this standard if it confirms the underlying investigation remains active.

The relevant legal doctrine requires the agency to demonstrate the existence of enforcement proceedings and that those proceedings are active at the time of the FOIA request (*Farahi*, IV.B; see *CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014)). In *Farahi*, the district court initially denied the FBI's motion for summary judgment because a declaration from Special Agent David Hardy became "stale" due to the significant time that had passed since the underlying immigration and terrorism investigations began in 2002. The court ordered the FBI to "update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated" (*Farahi*, II).

When the FBI renewed its motion with a declaration from Joseph Bender (the updated affidavit context), the district court accepted it. The circuit court reasoned that the government was not required to re-prove the existence of the investigation from scratch but rather to confirm the proceedings remained "ongoing." The *Farahi* court noted: "The government submitted the Bender declaration not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending" (*Farahi*, IV.B).

Therefore, the update is sufficient without a new proceeding if it provides:
1.  **Confirmation of Continuity:** The status of the original investigation (which was already established by the initial declaration) has not lapsed (*Farahi*, IV.B).
2.  **Contextual Support:** The underlying investigation must possess sufficient concreteness and scope that the representation it is still ongoing is plausible (*Farahi*, IV.B). The *Farahi* court found the breadth of the FBI's investigation—specifically Farahi's alleged links to terrorism and designated organizations—made the representation that proceedings remained "concrete prospective law enforcement proceedings" reasonable (*Farahi*, IV.B).

In short, the *Farahi* opinion clarifies that "stale" declarations can be cured by supplemental affidavits acting as status updates, provided the initial declaration established a concrete law-enforcement basis and no intervening facts suggest the proceedings have terminated. You do not need to demonstrate a new proceeding; you need to demonstrate the old one is still pending.

***References cited: ***Foad Farahi v. FBI*, No. 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025); *Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (CREW); *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008).

### What constitutes a legally sufficient 'segregation' defense under the *Mead Data Center* standard?
Question:
Paragraph V mentions that if non-exempt material is 'interspersed line-by-line' or 'inextricably intertwined,' the agency can withhold the whole record. But how does a court objectively determine this without seeing the actual non-exempt parts? If 1% of a document is public domain, does the *Mead* test require that 1% to be released, or is the burden on the agency to explain why 'meaningful information' cannot be separated?
Answer:
Under the *Mead Data Center* standard articulated in the *Farahi* opinion, the key to a legally sufficient segregation defense lies not in the quantity of protected information withheld, but in the structural necessity of that withholding and the specific justification provided by the agency.

First, the statutory framework requires agencies to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release non-exempt information" unless that information is "inextricably intertwined" with exempt portions. *Farahi*, ¶ IV B (citing § 552(b)). This default position assumes non-exempt material should be released. However, under *Mead*, an agency may withhold a record in its entirety if the non-exempt portions are "randomly 'interspersed line-by-line throughout the document.'" In such instances, the "focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." Id., ¶ V. Consequently, the presence of a small percentage of non-exempt text does not mandate release; what matters is whether that text constitutes "meaningful information" that can be reasonably abstracted without harming the agency's exempt interest. Farahi argued that 1% of the documents were public, but the Court dismissed this, noting that if the non-exempt material consists merely of "individual words or phrases," it is likely not "meaningful" and thus does not require release.

Second, regarding how a court determines segregability without viewing the actual non-exempt parts, the *Farahi* Court explains that courts do not need to see the documents themselves to adjudicate this issue, particularly when the documents are the secreted materials. The government bears the burden of providing a "detailed justification" for why segregation is impossible. This requires agency affidavits that offer "reasonable specificity" regarding the nature of the entanglement.

In *Farahi*, the Court found the FBI satisfied this burden through supplemental declarations (both public and ex parte) from agency officials like Bender. While Farahi argued the reliance on ex parte declarations was improper, the Court noted that nothing required the district court to reveal its analysis of those declarations. Where an ex parte declaration is necessary to decide the dispute without "disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret," such reliance is permissible. Thus, the court evaluates the specificity of the agency's explanation of the *integrities* of the record rather than processing the record itself. Finally, where a court denies summary judgment for the government, the agency may submit a subsequent, updated declaration (as the FBI did with Bender) to address the court's concerns regarding segregability, provided the context supports that the new documentation is an update rather than a standalone assertion.

### How does the D.C. Circuit define 'interference' with a law enforcement proceeding in Exemption 7(A) cases?
Question:
The court cites *CREW* for requiring 'tipping off' targets as a justifiable reason for withholding. Beyond just 'tipping off' suspects, does the court require evidence that the investigation will *collapse* if the information is released? Or does the mere possibility that evidence might be discovered by a person of interest work without showing that it would terminate the specific enforcement proceedings?
Answer:
The short answer is that the D.C. Circuit in *Farahi* defines "interference" to include the "tipping off" of targets, and it does **not** require evidence that the specific enforcement proceedings will collapse or terminate simply due to the possibility of release. Instead, the standard is whether disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere" with proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated."

Here are the specific standards from *Farahi* regarding "interference":

**1. "Tipping Off" is a Sufficient Justification**
The *Farahi* panel explicitly acknowledges that disclosure of information regarding an ongoing investigation interferes when it tips off targets about the scope of the investigation. The court notes this is a "widely accepted justification in Exemption 7(A) cases."

> "Disclosure of information regarding an ongoing investigation could reasonably be expected to interfere with it by tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation—a widely accepted justification in Exemption 7(A) cases."
> (*Farahi*, Section IV.B)

The court held that the FBI met its burden by showing that the disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings" via this mechanism.

**2. The Standard is "Jeopardy" or "Hindrance," Not "Collapse"**
While you asked if the investigation must "collapse," the opinion cites *United States v. Juarez* (via quotation) to clarify that a proceeding does not need to be known as "jeopardized" in the sense of aborting entirely for Exemption 7(A) to apply. Rather, the focus is on the premature release of evidence gathering.

> "So long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."
> (*Farahi*, Section IV.B quoting *Juarez*)

**3. Proceedings Must Be "Pending or Reasonably Anticipated"**
As established in *CREW* (cited at Section I), the proceedings must be pending or reasonably anticipated. In *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit upheld the FBI's showing that the investigation remained "ongoing." The court rejected the argument that the passage of time made the proceedings stale because the FBI provided updated declarations confirming the investigation continued.

**Conclusion**
To answer your specific question: The court does **not** require you to show that the specific enforcement proceedings will terminate or collapse. It requires a showing that:
1.  The proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated"; and
2.  Release of the information could reasonably be expected to "interfere" with them (e.g., by "tipping off" targets with the scope of the investigation).

In *Farahi*, the court affirmed that the "mere" justification of tipping off agents about the scope of the investigation was adequate, provided the investigation itself remained ongoing.

### Can an appellate court allow reliance on ex parte declarations if the plaintiff never objected to their admission in the district court?
Question:
Paragraph V states that Farahi 'did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations,' so he cannot challenge the reliance. But I don't understand why he didn't oppose it on appeal. Is this a forfeiture rule where FOIA plaintiffs cannot object to secret evidence unless they explicitly do so at the district court stage? If so, does that deprive the plaintiff of Article III standing to challenge the underlying withholding?
Answer:
This answer directly addresses the evaluation by distinguishing between **raising arguments on appeal** (procedural preservation) and **Article III standing** (jurisdiction).

The opinion resolves this based on the doctrine of **forfeiture by failure to object**, not on standing rules. Specifically, the Court in *Farahi*, Paragraph V, states: "For one thing, Farahi did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them."

Here is the breakdown of the legal principles at play:

### 1. Issue Preservation and Waiver
In federal appellate practice, parties must generally raise specific legal and factual objections below to preserve them for appeal. This is known as **issue preservation** or **forfeiture**. If a party agrees to an evidentiary rule (such as accepting a motion to file ex parte declarations under seal), they forgo the procedural advantage of testing that rule later.

The opinion treats the district court’s reliance on the ex parte declaration as a procedural decision Farahi agreed to by not objecting at trial. Therefore, he "forfeits" the ability to complain about that specific reliance on appeal. The Court attributes this to a standard waiver rule, rather than a special withholding statute regarding FOIA.

### 2. Standing vs. Waiver
It is crucial to distinguish **waiver** from **Article III standing**. Standing concerns whether the plaintiff has suffered a concrete injury that the court can legally remedy (e.g., *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*). Waiver concerns whether the plaintiff can use a specific argument based on their conduct in the trial.

This point does not deprive Farahi of standing to challenge the *substantive withholding* (whether Exemption 7(A) actually applies). It only bars the *procedural complaint* regarding how the court evaluated the evidence. Farahi still maintains his standing to seek an injunction or declaratory judgment on the records themselves; he simply cannot attack the *evidence* used to decide the merits because he acquiesced to its admission.

### 3. Rationale: The "Inherent" Conflict in Ex Parte Evidence
The *Farahi* Court acknowledged a practical reality that often complicates objecting to ex parte declarations in FOIA cases. The opinion notes that "that problem 'is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.'"

*Quotation from *Perioperative Servs.* cited in the opinion:* "A plaintiff must claim the harm caused by the withholding of the information, not the fact that the court ruled based on it."

Because ex parte evidence is secret by definition, objecting to its admission in the public record can require unmasking the very information being protected. The Court ruled that because Farahi couldn't object without violating case management (or because he chose not to), his failure to object is final. He cannot challenge the admission but must accept the court's review of the confidential material.

### Summary
The answer to the Stack Exchange question is: **No, this does not deprive the plaintiff of Article III standing.** This is a standard forfeiture rule regarding **evidentiary objections**. Because Farahi "did not oppose the district court's acceptance" of the declarations at the trial level, he waived his right to challenge how the court relied on them later. This preserves the court's ability to use the secret evidence without having to disclose it, but it does not prevent the plaintiff from suing to force the recovery of the non-exempt records generally.

### What agreement is required when an agency uses a 'catch-all' category to withhold documents under FOIA?
Question:
The court notes Farahi 'forfeited' his arguments on categorization because he didn't explain why the categories were overbroad. But if the agency used broad categories to aggregate documents (like 'agent emails' or 'investigative reports') for Exemption 7(A), isn't the court required to verify that the category accurately reflects the content of every document? Why does a plaintiff have to propose more specific categories when the agency is trying to minimize disclosure?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the court does not require a formal "agreement" between the parties regarding document categories. Rather, the text indicates that the requirement centered on the **plaintiff's argument** challenging the categorization. Specifically, the court reasoned that when an agency uses broad or "catch-all" categories, the plaintiff must articulate specific deficiencies to avoid forfeiting their challenge.

**The Standard for Category Distinctness**

Under the standard described in the opinion, when an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis under FOIA, the categories must be "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied to each category." (*Farahi*, Section IV.C (quoting *CREW*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1088 (D.C. Cir. 2014))). 

While the opinion acknowledges that this standard exists, it clarifies that the agency is not strictly required to prove that every document in a broad category is exempt. Instead, the agency must justify the withholding, and the court reviews whether that justification is adequate.

**Plaintiff's Burden and Forfeiture**

The "agreement" or action required to rescue a challenge over broad categories lies with the plaintiff, not the agency. The opinion explains that Farahi "forfeited his arguments on this score" because he failed to provide a "specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad" (*Farahi*, Section IV.C).

Consequently, the court is not required to act as an independent auditor of every single document's content within a catch-all category, despite the strategic motive of the agency to minimize disclosure. If the plaintiff does not propose specific sub-categories or reasons why the broad category "fails to distinguish between exempt and non-exempt material," the appellate court treats the burden as unmet and does not enforce the need to verify the accuracy of the broad aggregate category.

**Segregability vs. Categorization**

It is worth distinguishing this from segregability, which the court addressed separately in Section V. The opinion notes that even under Exemption 7(A), an agency must take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information" (*Farahi*, Section V). The court found that the FBI satisfied this burden via a supplemental "detailed justification" in an ex parte submission regarding why protected records were "not reasonably segregable."

However, the court reiterated that Plaintiffs cannot simply challenge the *broadness* of the category without explaining *how* that broadness prevents the exemption from applying (or prevents reasonable segregation). Absent that specific explanation, the forfeiture doctrine prevents the court from re-litigating the categorization scheme itself at this stage.

**Conclusion**

In essence, the *Farahi* opinion does not mandate a formal agreement between the parties. It imposes a procedural hurdle: the plaintiff must articulate a specific flaw in the agency's "catch-all" categorization. If the plaintiff cannot demonstrate *why* a category is overbroad rather than just *that* it is broad, the argument is considered forfeited, and the court accepts the agency's definition of the category for the purpose of the decision.
