Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The Exemptive Timeframe: How 'Pending' Proceedings Survive Two Decades of FOIA Litigation

***

When a plaintiff in a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) case requests their own government file, the standard presumption is that the information is subject to review, provided it does not violate specific exemptions. But what happens when those exemptions pivot on the "stale" issue of timing? In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided in the D.C. Circuit in September 2025, the court addressed a procedural puzzle that has bedeviled national security litigants for years: How does an agency prove an investigation is still "pending" when the underlying event occurred twenty years prior? The opinion affirms the district court’s dismissal of Farahi’s FOIA request, upholding Exemption 7(A) for 10,750 pages of FBI records. While ostensibly a narrow case about record segregation and affidavit updates, the ruling offers a blueprint for how courts will handle the "pending or reasonably anticipated" requirement in long-tail counterterrorism investigations, reinforcing a high deference to the executive branch over the passage of time.

**## The "Pending" Standard and the Risk of Stale Certainty**

The crux of Exemption 7(A) is that documents compiled for law enforcement can be withheld if their release risks interfering with "enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated." The D.C. Circuit has long interpreted this to mean the government must show an active investigation, not just an abstract future one. This makes sense where proceedings are imminent. It is far less clear when an investigation, formally tied to a 2002 visa overstay case, claims to be ongoing in the 2020s.

Farahi, an Iranian national who has lived in the US since 1993, filed a FOIA request for his file. The FBI withheld records citing active counterterrorism investigations linking him to figures like Jose Padilla and the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood. By the time Farahi mandated the court to force updated declarations in 2022, nearly twenty years had passed since the initial DHS removal proceedings began. The district court originally denied summary judgment because the initial affidavit from David Hardy, a Section Chief, was deemed too stale given the passage of time. The Court of Appeals, however, agreed with the district court on one level but disagreed on the standard for updating that trust. The appellate court emphasized that Farahi had handed the agency ample time to prove a *continuity* of the investigation since the 2000s, not just the 2010s.

The key to the Court's reasoning lies in its view of the petitioner's burden regarding that continuity. The opinion notes that while the initial declaration confirmed proceedings, Farahi did not successfully challenge the Baxter-Bender updates that followed. The court distanced itself from the strict "vague nature" line established in* CREW v. DOJ. It relied on the notion that the updated declarations from Joseph Bender were not isolated assertions but were "reasonably understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document." This pragmatic approach suggests that as long as the government establishes a "rational nexus" initially, a subsequent affidavit confirming the investigation is merely a confirmation of the *prior* working conditions rather than a fresh re-invention of the case.

**## The Danger of Isolated Evidence and the "Context" Defense**

The most analytically significant passage in the opinion is where the court rejects Farahi’s insistence on reading agency declarations in a vacuum. Farahi argued that Bender’s declaration, which stated the investigation remained "ongoing," was too terse. The court countered that the declaration must be viewed *in context*. This legal move protects agencies from a repetitive game of "raise the bar" litigation. If a lawyer sues an agency, claiming the agency’s "ongoing investigation" claim is too vague without reading it at a specific depth, the agency can claim a procedural win: "We are an FBI unit, not a law journal; we must show the investigation was ongoing, not how the FBI would describe its ongoing investigation to a public."

This reliance on the context of the original investigation is vindicated by the underlying nature of the threats involved. The opinion details the serious allegations initially made public: ties to internacional terrorism and foreign terrorist organizations. The court reasoned that the "breadth of the FBI’s underlying investigation" makes plausible its representation that the investigation remains ongoing. By tethering the sufficiency of the updated declaration to the specificity of the original allegations (KSM, Al Qaeda, etc.), the court effectively allowed the initial burden to satisfy the duration requirement, provided the agency came back and checked the box once in an intermediate step. This creates a high-fidelity alignment with the agency’s structure, ensuring that the "pending" prong does not become a 240-requirement that the investigation *restarted* by the examiner to ensure the user was up to date on the "policy."

**## The Question of Segregation When Information Is Inextricable**

The bulk of the dispute also involved the 10,750 pages of redacted material, which Farahi demanded be unredacted and released. The D.C. Circuit adhered to the doctrine that where material is "inextricably intertwined," it need not be segregated. The opinion highlights that while Farahi might find individual bits of value, they are "interspersed line-by-line" with other sensitive information regarding ongoing discussions and counterfeit procedures.

Here, the opinion relies heavily on the concept that FOIA is about information, not words. The court notes that if the focus is on specific records of specific observations made by witnesses regarding the subject, those records must be protected if the public is likely to be tipped off. This finding hampers the ability of an appellant to argue that a small amount of material—say, a single memo about a meeting—should be redacted from the rest. This is particularly salient in this context because the nature of the case is regarding the investigation. If the investigation is the investigation, then the context of the investigation is the investigation. Consequently, the result is that the plaintiff must accept the entire file as withheld rather than picking pieces of it. The opinion supports the district court's reliance on the *ex parte* submissions regarding segregability. Farahi did not oppose the acceptance of these declarations, so he could not claim he suffered from them. This confirms a reciprocal arrangement where the government can use private declarations to justify public secrecy, provided the plaintiff does not object to the procedure itself.

**## Theocene of Precedent and Future Implications**

The implications of *Foad Farahi* extend beyond the specific facts of Farahi’s inclusion in the file. The court’s willingness to accept "contextual" updates for stale affidavits signals a shift in how the D.C. Circuit handles Exemption 7(A) assertions over long time periods. In cases where agencies argue that terrorism investigations span decades, the plaintiff may face a burden not just to prove refutation of the initial investigation's validity, but to prove the failure of the investigation's modern continuation.

Furthermore, the mandate that "context counsels against isolation" is a powerful litigation strategy for the executive branch. It means that the sufficiency of an affidavit can be gauged by the difficulty of the investigation, not just the clarity of the text. This creates a significant hurdle for *future* plaintiffs, as it raises the standard for challenging the sufficiency of the government’s justification. For instance, a plaintiff in a similar case might argue that the agency should have provided a statement from a different witness in 2024. The *Farahi* court’s approach suggests that the initial statement and the subsequent brevity of the update are sufficient if they contextually link back to the original. The result is a system that, while seemingly protective of transparency, prioritizes the secrecy of long-standing investigations. The opinion does not change the law on "inextricably intertwined" material significantly but sharpens the application to the context of national security investigations.

Ultimately, the Court’s decision reaffirms that the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard does not mean a fresh start for the agency. It means a continuous line. By upholding the agency's deference to its own determination of what constitutes a pending proceeding, the court protects the FBI’s ability to lock files away for the duration of the investigation, regardless of how long that duration extends. While this ensures the privacy of ongoing national security inquiries, it significantly diminishes the plaintiff’s ability to challenge the very nature of those inquiries once the initial timeline begins to fade.



# Ex Parte Evidence in the D.C. Circuit: The Trust Required to Seal the Public Record

The promise of transparency under the Freedom of Information Act rests on a simple premise: the government holds the public’s rights to know, and agencies must release records unless a specific legal exemption applies. However, recent litigation involving counterterrorism investigations reveals the fragility of that promise when the records in question are themselves the subject of a national security exemption. In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, decided by the D.C. Circuit last month, the court grappled with a specific doctrinal knot: whether and how an Appellate Court can determine that sensitive law enforcement documentation must be kept in the shadows while making a public ruling relying on the very evidence being concealed. The decision’s reliance on ex parte declarations to resolve both the pendency of proceedings and the segregability of documents highlights a deepening tension between the judiciary’s duty to explain itself and the executive branch’s insistence on confidentiality in counterterrorism investigations.

## The Procedural Tug-of-War

The friction between transparency and secrecy in *Farahi* arose from Farahi’s request for 10,750 pages of FBI records spanning decades of immigration and terrorism investigations. When the agency invoked Exemption 7(A), claiming disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings, the district court initially ruled in Farahi’s favor sufficiently to require the FBI to update its stale declarations regarding the status of the cases. This ruling suggested that a passage of time required fresh proof of investigation, but the appellate court ultimately sided with the agency. The crux of the dispute leaned heavily on the procedural mechanism used to verify the exemption. In its affirmance, Katsas, the D.C. Circuit, explicitly relied on sealed ex parte declarations submitted by FBI officials to confirm that the investigations remained ongoing and to justify why certain documents could not be redacted or severed.

This reliance presents a notable departure from the typical administrative litigation model. Usually, courts resolve disputes on the public record to preserve the integrity of judicial proceedings. Here, the D.C. Circuit addressed the sufficiency of the FBI’s burden without a full public airing of the evidence used to tip the scales. The agency submitted public declarations from record keepers confirming ongoing investigations while simultaneously filing ex parte declarations detailing the specific scope of the file and the reasons for non-segregability. The appellate court affirmed the district court’s granting of summary judgment based largely on these sealed submissions, finding that the FBI met its burden to show that the records were compiled for law enforcement and that their release would interfere with proceedings. This procedural posture reinforces the Executive’s control over the parameters of the disclosure itself.

## The Justification for Secrecy

To understand why the court accepted such a heavy reliance on ex parte submissions, one must look at the specific constraints laid out in the opinion. The court identified a logical bind inherent in FOIA litigation involving classified or highly sensitive law enforcement matters. Katsas wrote that an ex parte declaration is often the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret. This reasoning acknowledges that a judge cannot fully apply Exemption 7(A) without investigating the content of the file to determine if parts are separable, yet many plaintiffs argue they can use independent research to guess at the nature of the document. In Farahi’s case, the volume of the records—over 10,000 pages—made it difficult for the plaintiff to challenge specific redactions without pointing him toward the very material that was hidden.

The court also grappled with Farahi’s challenge regarding the time lapse between the initial withholding and the updated declarations. Farahi argued that the certificate of pendency had become so vague over the years that it could not survive judicial scrutiny. While the court noted that the initial Bender declaration appeared terse, it contextualized the reliance on ex parte sealed submissions as a mechanism to update the agency’s self-attestation. The court did not require the FBI to publicly disclose the details of the investigation to prove it was current. This approach underscores a judicial preference for trusting agency representations in the context of law enforcement, provided that those representations are backed by the reasonable specificity that sealed affidavits can offer.

## Doctrinal Implications and Future Transparency

The reliance on ex parte declarations in *Farahi* sets a significant precedent for future FOIA challenges in the D.C. Circuit, particularly where national security is implicated. By effectively endorsing the agency’s right to withhold the reasoning behind certain segregability decisions behind a veil of secrecy, the court may be streamlining the path for agencies to maintain broad withholding decisions. The decision implicitly lowers the bar for plaintiffs on the issue of "reasonably segregable portions." While the plaintiff had the burden to show why documents were overbroad, Farahi’s failure to do so in the provided record meant the court could accept the agency’s sealed assertions that no meaningful information was separable.

This procedural dynamic raises concerns for the balance of power in administrative litigation. If courts routinely use ex parte evidence to evaluate the very documents withheld from the public, the check-and-balance system of FOIA is significantly diminished. The administrative process becomes less about verifying the government’s claim of necessity and more about accepting the government’s bureaucratic procedure at a high level of deference. This does not necessarily mean the government can ignore the "active" nature of the investigation requirement laid out in statutory interpretations of Exemption 7(A); however, the D.C. Circuit is signaling that a formal confirmation of an active investigation, even if sealed, is sufficient to maintain the exemption.

Ultimately, *Farahi v. FBI* serves as a reminder that in the context of counterterrorism, the judicial system often acts as a gatekeeper for information rather than a mirror of the truth. The court navigates the immediate case by protecting the underlying law enforcement efforts through procedural mechanisms that prioritize agency security advocates over plaintiff access. This tightening of transparency protocols suggests that in future cases, the D.C. Circuit may continue to justify sealed evidence as a necessary "trust" required to seal the public record when that record is deemed too sensitive for public scrutiny.



# Information vs. Ink: Applying the 'Mead Data' 'Reasonable Segregation' Standard to Massive Investigative Files

When John Doe asks for the FBI file on John Smith, the government often returns a stack of paper. Sometimes, that stack is the size of a small apartment building. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Information*, the D.C. Circuit had to decide what to do with a request for a 10,750-page FBI file and 80 CDs. The government’s answer was a grand withholding, relying entirely on Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act. This isn't just about a man named Foad. It is a rare front-row seat to the tension between the public’s right to know and the administrative ability to say, "We can’t give you anything intelligible without doing the impossible." Judge Katsas’s opinion in affirming the denial of release offers a sobering look at how the "reasonable segregation" standard functions when the information at stake is counterterrorism in nature.

In a typical FOIA case, the government is required to segregate redactions so that any non-exempt portions are released. If I write a memo criticizing a policy but also include a phone number I can't share, I must release the memo and delete the phone number. However, the *Mead Data* standard, codified in D.C. Circuit jurisprudence, creates a backstop that allows agencies to withhold an entire file if the exempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with the non-exempt ones. In *Farahi*, the district court found that while Farahi was the subject of a counterterrorism investigation, the protective information covering his potential connections to Al Qaeda and the Muslim Brotherhood was so uniformly interwoven with his general file that releasing any "random" word would violate the core purpose of the **Exemption 7(A)**. This is not a case where the government hid one paragraph in a three-hundred-page document. It is a case where the document itself was considered a single unit of intelligence.

The opinion meticulously walks through the statutory requirements for Exemption 7(A) before arriving at the segmentation question. To withhold under the exemption, the agency must show the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that release would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. Farahi argued that years passed between the initial investigation and the FOIA request, casting doubt on whether proceedings were still "pending." The deviation here is standard but significant. The court required the FBI to update its declarations from 2019 to 2022. This update is where the court found support for the exemption. The declaration stated the investigation remained ongoing. Judge Katsas allowed standing ex parte declarations to affirm the investigation’s status, noting that the plaintiff did not object to the agency filing supplemental ex parte declarations. This procedural posture is critical; the court explicitly states that while ex parte declarations can handicap FOIA plaintiffs, that handicap is inherent to cases where disclosure relies on that very secrecy.

The real doctrinal meat of the opinion lies in the segregability analysis. *Juarez v. Department of Justice* and *Mead Data* teach that FOIA is about information, not stopping words from appearing. The standard asks if non-exempt material is "reasonably segregable." If non-exempt words are "randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," the court held they are not separable. In *Farahi*, the court agreed with the second district court order that the protected records were not reasonably segregable. This is a high bar for a plaintiff who has suffered a judgment losing the FOIA. It means that even in a file this large, if the file records a continuous timeline of covert operations, one cannot select out the "administrative housekeeping" bits without revealing the dirty work. For practitioners, this signals a shift away from viewing FOIA as a discovery tool and toward viewing it as a query for information that can be cleanly isolated. The opinion reinforces that the focus of the law is on "information, not simply words which the Government has written down."

The downstream implications for counterterrorism FOIA requests are substantial. In cases where the agency asserts that a file is compiled in furtherance of an investigation into a "foreign terrorist organization," aggrieved individuals will find the courts consistently interpreting the inextricability of information narrowly. The reliance on the *Mead Data* standard suggests that the judiciary views massive databases as structurally risky for partial release. This protects the agency's workflow, at least in the sense that they do not have to create massive, redacted versions of deeply interne documents. However, it also deprives the public of context. If 10,750 pages are withheld because every line contains, or touches on, sensitive intel, the public gets zero insight into which specific documents might have been cleared. This creates a "damned if you do" dynamic for the courts: order the release, and national security is compromised; deny the release, and FOIA is rendered a stack of paper. Katsas’s opinion does nothing to resolve this structural tension, but it solidifies the executive branch's ability to demand that the scale of the redaction outweighs the utility of segregation.

Ultimately, *Farahi* serves as a cautionary tale for those who believe FOIA grants them the right to read a government file and selectively copy the safe parts. In the eyes of the D.C. Circuit, the *Mead Data* standard is not merely a clearinghouse for redactions; it is a threshold test for the very existence of the public record. When the government provides a declaration that the investigation is ongoing and that the threat involves high-level terrorism, the court is willing to accept that the entire file is a single unit of sensitive intelligence. It is a robust application of administrative law, prioritizing practical enforcement efficacy over granular access, but it leaves plaintiffs wondering if there is any "meaningful information" available to them at all, or simply words trapped in ink.
