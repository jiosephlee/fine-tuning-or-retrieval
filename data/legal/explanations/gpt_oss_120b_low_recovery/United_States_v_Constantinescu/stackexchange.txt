Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Does a securities‑fraud indictment require a traditional property interest, or can “money or property” be satisfied by inducing a purchase?
Question:
In United States v. Constantinescu the Fifth Circuit reversed a dismissal, saying the indictment alleged a scheme to defraud followers of “money or property” by fraudulently inducing them to buy securities. How does the court interpret the phrase “money or property” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348? Is the induced purchase of a security sufficient to satisfy the property‑interest element, even though the victim ultimately receives a market‑value security?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Fifth Circuit holds that “money or property” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 is satisfied whenever the fraud scheme causes a victim to part with cash or an asset‑like interest – even if the victim receives a security of roughly equal market value in return.  The statute does **not** require the victim to lose a “traditional” property right such as cash alone, nor does it require the victim to be left poorer after the transaction.  Because the indictment alleges that the defendants **fraudulently induced** their followers to purchase securities, the “money or property” element is met.

---

### Why the phrase “money or property” is satisfied by an induced purchase

1. **Statutory language and the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory**  
   § 1348 criminalizes a scheme “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses … any **money or property** in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.”  The Fifth Circuit reads this in line with the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in *Kousisis v. United States* and *Ciminelli v. United States*.  *Kousisis* expressly recognizes a **fraudulent‑inducement theory**: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property,” and the crime is complete when the defendant obtains that money or property, **regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value**.  The Court in *Constantinescu* applies that same reasoning.

2. **Distinguishing the “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in *Ciminelli***  
   *Ciminelli* held that a fraud conviction cannot rest on the deprivation of merely “valuable economic information” – the “right‑to‑control” theory.  The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the *Constantinescu* indictment does **not** rely on that theory; it alleges that the defendants caused followers to **hand over cash** (or an equivalent property interest) by convincing them to buy a stock.  The victims’ “property” is the money they spent; the security they receive is *not* the protected interest the statute requires the government to prove.

3. **The indictment’s allegations meet the element**  
   - ¶ 13 of the superseding indictment states that defendants “posted false … information … so that other investors were **induced to purchase the security**.”  
   - The purchase requires the followers to surrender **money** (or other property) in exchange for the security.  
   - The government therefore alleges that the defendants obtained **money or property** from the victims, satisfying the statutory element.

4. **No requirement that the victim suffer a net loss**  
   The Fifth Circuit cites *Kousisis* for the proposition that the fraud statutes do **not** demand a “mirror‑image” loss.  A defendant can be convicted even if the victim ends up with an asset of market value, because the crime is completed when the defendant obtains the victim’s money or property through deception.  The same logic underlies the Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the indictment is sufficient.

---

### How the decision should be read for future cases

- **“Money or property”** is a broad term that includes any **exchange of value** caused by a fraudulent misrepresentation, not merely the loss of cash or a tangible asset.  
- An **induced purchase** of a security is enough because the victim’s **money** (or other property) is transferred to the defendant (or a related party) in exchange for the security.  
- The fact that the victim receives a security of comparable market value **does not negate** the “money or property” element; the Supreme Court has held that the fraud statute protects the **act of obtaining** that money/property, not the net economic outcome.  
- The prosecution does **not** need to allege that the victim is left poorer; it only needs to allege that the defendant **intended to obtain** the victim’s money or property through deception.

---

**Bottom line:** In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit interprets “money or property” under § 1348 to include the victim’s money that is handed over in a fraud‑induced securities purchase.  The receipt of a market‑value security by the victim does not defeat the element; the indictment’s allegations of fraudulent inducement satisfy the statutory requirement.

### What is the difference between the “right‑to‑control” theory and the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory in federal fraud statutes?
Question:
The opinion contrasts Ciminelli’s rejected right‑to‑control theory with the Supreme Court’s acceptance of the fraudulent‑inducement theory in Kousisis. Could someone explain the doctrinal distinction between these two theories and why one is permissible for a securities‑fraud charge while the other is not?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu* (Oct. 2 2025) draws a sharp line between two doctrinal “theories” that Congress has used to prosecute fraud:  

|                     | **Right‑to‑control theory** | **Fraudulent‑inducement theory** |
|---------------------|----------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **What the victim “loses”** | The victim’s *right to control* its own property – i.e., the ability to make a discretionary economic decision based on information that is later withheld. | The victim’s *money or other property* that it actually parts with after being induced by a material misrepresentation. |
| **Property interest protected by the statutes** | “Traditional” property interests only.  The Supreme Court held in **Ciminelli v. United States**, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023), that a mere deprivation of valuable economic information is **not** a protected property interest. (See *Constantinescu*, ¶ A.) | Money or other property that the victim actually surrenders; this is a classic “property” interest that falls squarely within the scope of the wire‑, mail‑, and securities‑fraud statutes. (See *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S.Ct. 1382, 1388‑90 (2025); *Constantinescu*, ¶ A.) |
| **Core element of the fraud** | The government must show the defendant “schemed to deprive” the victim of the *right to control* its assets. | The government must show the defendant “used a material misstatement to trick the victim into a contract that required handing over money or property.” (Id. at 1388.) |
| **Why it was rejected in *Ciminelli*** | The Court said the “right‑to‑control” label merely repackages the ordinary “information‑only” loss, which the statutes do **not** punish because no traditional property is taken. (¶ A.) | Not at issue in *Ciminelli*; the Court expressly distinguished the “fraudulent‑inducement” model as targeting the acquisition of the victim’s money or property, which is permissible. |
| **Why it is permissible in *Kousisis* and *Constantinescu*** | N/A – the theory is barred. | The Court confirmed that a conviction can rest on the fact that the defendant obtained the victim’s money or property, even if the victim also received something of value or suffered no additional loss. (¶ B.; *Kousisis*, 1392.) |

### How the distinction matters for a securities‑fraud charge

1. **Statutory language** – 18 U.S.C. § 1348 criminalizes a “scheme to… obtain… any money or property… in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” The phrase *money or property* signals a **property‑based** injury, not a loss of information.  

2. **Ciminelli’s rejection** – In *Ciminelli* the government tried to treat the victim’s loss of a “right to control” as a property loss. The Supreme Court held that the statutes protect only “traditional property interests” (¶ A.) and therefore the right‑to‑control theory cannot support a fraud conviction.  

3. **Kousisis’s acceptance** – *Kousisis* clarified that the **fraudulent‑inducement theory** is distinct because it focuses on the victim’s **actual transfer of money or property** induced by a misrepresentation, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value or suffers a net loss. The Court emphasized that the fraud statutes do **not** require the government to prove the victim was left “worse off” (¶ B., 1392).  

4. **Application to *Constantinescu*** – The indictment alleges that the defendants posted false statements that *induced* followers to **purchase** securities, thereby causing the followers to part with money. That allegation satisfies the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a misrepresentation was used to obtain the victims’ money, a protected property interest. The district court’s dismissal was therefore contrary to *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*; the Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded.  

### Bottom line

- **Right‑to‑control theory** → treats the loss of the ability to make an informed decision as “property.” The Supreme Court has ruled that such a loss is **not** covered by federal fraud statutes.  
- **Fraudulent‑inducement theory** → requires proof that the defendant’s false statement caused the victim to *actually part with* money or other property. This fits squarely within the statutory language and is therefore a **valid basis** for a securities‑fraud prosecution.

Consequently, a securities‑fraud charge grounded in fraudulent inducement (as in *Constantinescu*) is permissible, whereas one grounded solely in a right‑to‑control claim (as in *Ciminelli*) is not.

### Is actual economic loss required to prove the “intent to defraud” element under 18 U.S.C. § 1348?
Question:
Defendants in Constantinescu argue the indictment fails to allege intent to harm because they only sought profit. The Fifth Circuit cites Kousisis, which says victim loss isn’t required. How does “intent to defraud” get satisfied if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss? Does the government need any evidence of the victim’s harm at all?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No.  Under the fraud statutes, including 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the government does **not** have to show that a victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.  The “intent to defraud” element is satisfied when the defendant *intends to obtain the victim’s money or property* through a material misrepresentation, even if the defendant’s scheme provides the victim with something of equal value or leaves the victim’s overall wealth unchanged.  The Fifth Circuit’s decision in **United States v. Constantinescu**, 2025 WL … (5th Cir. Oct. 2 2025), relies directly on the Supreme Court’s holdings in **Kousisis v. United States**, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), to reach this conclusion.

---

### Why the victim’s loss is irrelevant

1. **Statutory text and the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory**  
   § 1348 criminalizes a scheme “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.”  The Fifth Circuit treats this language the same way the Supreme Court treats the analogous wire‑fraud provision.  The statute therefore protects *the victim’s money or property* as the object of the fraud, not the victim’s *financial condition* after the transaction.

2. **Supreme Court precedent**  
   In **Kousisis**, the Court held that a defendant can be convicted under the fraudulent‑inducement theory “*regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss*” (145 S. Ct. at 1392).  The Court explained that the offense is complete when the defendant “*schemes to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off*” (id. at 1392).  The Court expressly rejected the requirement that the government prove the defendant intended to “*hurt the victim’s bottom line*” (id. at 1391).

3. **Application in Constantinescu**  
   Judge Engelhardt adopts Kousisis verbatim.  The opinion states:

   > “Because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, defendants’ followers’ receipt of market‑value securities does not obviate the fraud charges” (Constantinescu, ¶ B).  
   > “A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—*regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss*” (Constantinescu, ¶ B, citing Kousisis at 1394 n.5).

   The indictment therefore alleges that the defendants **intended to obtain money** (the profit of $114 million) by **fraudulently inducing** followers to hand over their own money to purchase the securities.  That allegation satisfies the “intent to defraud” element even though the followers received securities that, at the time of purchase, had market value.

4. **No separate “victim‑harm” element**  
   The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the fraud statutes do **not** impose a “mirror‑image” requirement—that the defendant’s gain must equal the victim’s loss.  See **United States v. Baker**, 923 F.3d at 403‑05 (5th Cir. 2019).  Consequently, the government need not produce independent evidence of a victim’s net loss; it only needs to show that the defendant intended to acquire the victim’s money or property through deception.

---

### What the government must prove

To satisfy the “intent to defraud” element under § 1348, the prosecution must allege—and ultimately prove—two things:

1. **Deception** – a material misrepresentation intended to deceive the victim (e.g., false statements about the defendants’ trading positions and the security’s price).  
2. **Objective to obtain money or property** – the defendant’s purpose was to get the victim’s money or property, not merely to withhold information.  The defendant’s own profit, as alleged in the indictment, evidences this purpose.

The presence or absence of a measurable loss to the victim is immaterial to the element.  As the Fifth Circuit notes, “*the indictment’s allegation of defendants’ fraudulently inducing their followers to purchase securities is sufficient to allege an injury*” (Constantinescu, ¶ B).

---

### Bottom line for a law student

- **Actual loss = not required.**  
- The statutory focus is on the *intent to obtain the victim’s money or property* through deceit.  
- Supreme Court authority (Kousisis) and the Fifth Circuit’s application (Constantinescu) make clear that a conviction can proceed even if the victim ultimately receives something of equal value or suffers no net financial detriment.

Thus, in a § 1348 case the government does **not** need to prove that the victim’s net wealth decreased; it only needs to show that the defendant intended to extract the victim’s money or property by fraudulent misrepresentation.

### What standard of review does the Fifth Circuit apply to a motion to dismiss for insufficiency of an indictment?
Question:
The opinion states the court reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo, citing United States v. Rafoi. How does that de novo standard differ from a more deferential review (e.g., for facial sufficiency), and what specific elements must the court look for when evaluating a fraud‐related indictment?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit reviews a motion to dismiss an indictment for “insufficiency of the indictment” **de novo**.  That means the appellate court does **not** give any deference to the district court’s factual findings; it simply takes the allegations in the indictment as true and asks whether, on their face, the statutory elements of the charged offense are stated.  United States v. **Rafoi**, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023) (cited in *Constantinescu*).  

### How de novo review differs from a more deferential standard  

A deferential (facial‑sufficiency) standard—often phrased as “review for facial sufficiency” or “review under the **Rule 12(b)(3)** standard—asks only whether the indictment **fails to allege a crime at all**.  Under that approach a court may look to the *overall* adequacy of the charge and may give weight to the district court’s interpretation of the indictment’s language.  The focus is narrow: does the pleading omit an essential element such that the charge is legally insufficient?

By contrast, the Fifth Circuit’s de novo rule requires the appellate court to:

1. **Take the indictment’s factual allegations as true.**  
2. **Compare those allegations, element by element, with the statutory requirements** of the offense.  
3. **Conclude whether each material element is alleged**; if any element is missing, the indictment is insufficient and must be dismissed.

Thus, the de novo review is a *plain‑language* test of whether the charge, as written, states an offense, without regard to how the district court interpreted the pleadings.

### Elements the Fifth Circuit looks for in a securities‑fraud indictment  

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the court spelled out the substantive elements that must appear in the indictment for a violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348/1349 (securities fraud and conspiracy to commit securities fraud).  The opinion treats the securities‑fraud statute as borrowing the classic “scheme‑to‑defraud” language of the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes and therefore requires the same two‑part analysis:

| Element | What the indictment must allege (per *Constantinescu*) |
|---------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| **(1) Scheme to defraud** | A *material misrepresentation* or *false pretenses* intended to deceive another party **and** an intent to obtain something of value (money or property) from the victim. *Id.* at 339 (quoting *Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12). |
| **(2) Intent to defraud** | The defendant **intended** (a) to deceive the victim and (b) to cause some harm—or at least to obtain the victim’s money or property—by that deceit. *Id.* at 339‑340 (quoting *Evans*, 892 F.3d at 712). |

The opinion emphasizes two doctrinal points drawn from recent Supreme Court cases:

1. **The “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is sufficient.**  *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) held that a fraud conviction is proper when the defendant uses a material false statement to induce a victim to **hand over money or property**, even if the victim ultimately receives something of equal value.  The indictment therefore must allege that the defendants **induced followers to purchase securities**, i.e., to part with their money.

2. **Actual economic loss is not required.**  The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* rejected the notion that a victim must suffer a pecuniary loss; it is enough that the defendant **sought to obtain** the victim’s money or property.  Accordingly, the Fifth Circuit looks for allegations that the defendants intended to **profit** (the “dump” portion) rather than merely to deprive victims of information.

Applying those principles, the Fifth Circuit found that the superseding indictment in *Constantinescu*:

* Alleged that defendants **purchased** a security and then **posted false, misleading information** to cause others to buy it (fulfilling the “material misrepresentation” requirement).  
* Alleged that the scheme was designed to **inflate the price** so the defendants could **sell** at a profit (showing intent to obtain money/property).  
* Cited the profit figure ($114 million) to demonstrate the **object of the scheme** was monetary gain.

Because each of the two statutory elements is alleged, the indictment satisfies the de novo sufficiency test, and the appellate court reversed the district court’s dismissal.

### Take‑away for a motion to dismiss

When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment on insufficiency grounds in the Fifth Circuit, the court will:

1. **Read the indictment’s allegations as true.**  
2. **Identify whether the indictment states each of the statutory elements** (scheme to defraud + intent to defraud).  
3. **Ignore any argument that the district court’s factual analysis was correct**; the appellate court does not defer to that analysis.  

If the indictment contains factual allegations that a defendant **misrepresented** material facts and **intended to obtain money or property** from the victim—whether or not the victim ultimately suffered a net loss—the Fifth Circuit will deem the indictment sufficient.

### How does the Fifth Circuit treat the securities‑fraud statute’s “scheme to defraud” element when the alleged scheme is a pump‑and‑dump via social‑media posts?
Question:
The opinion says a “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation intended to obtain money or property. In a pump‑and‑dump where the defendants post false statements on social media, what constitutes the “scheme” for purposes of § 1348? Does each false post count as a separate act, or is the overall campaign treated as one scheme?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit treats the “scheme to defraud” element in a pump‑and‑dump case as a *single, continuous fraudulent plan*, not as a collection of discrete schemes each tied to an individual social‑media post.

### Why the opinion reaches that conclusion  

1. **Statutory language and the court’s definition** – § 1348 criminalizes a “scheme to defraud… by means of false…pretenses…to obtain…money or property.”  The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the term *scheme* is borrowed from the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes and therefore is interpreted in the same way those courts have used it (see *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th at 339).  

2. **What constitutes a “scheme”** – In *Constantinescu*, the court explains that a scheme is “the overall plan or course of conduct designed to deceive victims and obtain their money or property.”  The opinion does **not** require the indictment to enumerate each individual false statement; it merely needs to allege that the defendants “engaged in a…‘pump‑and‑dump’ scheme” in which they **purchased** a security, **‘pumped’** it by posting false information, and then **‘dumped’** it for profit.  ¶ 13‑14 of the indictment satisfies this requirement.  

3. **Analogy to other fraud cases** – The Fifth Circuit repeatedly cites *Kousisis* and *Evans* for the proposition that a fraud “is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” and that the fraud statutes do not require a *mirror‑image* of loss.  The same logic applies here: the truth‑or‑falsehood of each post is merely a **means** of carrying out the broader deceptive plan.  

4. **Indictment sufficiency does not depend on “per‑post” pleading** – The court’s reversal of the district court rests on the view that the indictment “sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud.”  The court never says the government must plead *each* post as a separate scheme.  Instead, it says the indictment “properly alleges defendants’ scheme to defraud their followers of money” by describing the **overall pump‑and‑dump campaign**.  

### Practical take‑away

- **One scheme per campaign**: For purposes of § 1348, a coordinated series of false statements posted on social media that are intended to inflate a stock’s price and then allow the defendants to sell at the higher price is treated as **one scheme**.  

- **Individual posts are not separate schemes**: While each false statement can be evidence of the scheme’s *execution* (i.e., the *act* component), they do not each have to be pleaded as a distinct “scheme to defraud.”  

- **What the indictment must show**: It must allege (a) a *plan* to induce others to purchase securities, (b) that the plan is carried out using false or misleading statements, and (c) that the defendants’ objective was to obtain money or property (the profit from the dump).  *Constantinescu* demonstrates that satisfying those three ingredients is enough; the court does not require a “per‑post” tally.

### How to apply this in future cases

If you are drafting or reviewing an indictment for a modern “pump‑and‑dump” that uses Twitter, TikTok, or other platforms, focus on describing the **overall fraudulent enterprise**—the purchase, the false promotional communications, and the subsequent sale—rather than enumerating every individual post.  The Fifth Circuit will consider the campaign as a single “scheme to defraud” so long as the indictment alleges the requisite elements of deception and the intent to obtain money or property.

### Can a defendant be charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under § 1349 if the underlying fraud theory is the fraudulent‑inducement theory?
Question:
The indictment includes conspiracy to commit securities fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1349) alongside substantive fraud counts. Does the Supreme Court’s approval of the fraudulent‑inducement theory automatically extend to the conspiracy charge, or must the indictment separately allege agreement to pursue that specific theory?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No.  Under the Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the conspiracy charge under § 1349 does **not** require the government to allege a separate agreement to pursue the fraudulent‑inducement theory.  It is enough that the indictment alleges a scheme that satisfies the elements of securities fraud – a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” – because a conspiracy to commit the substantive offense is satisfied by an agreement to join that scheme, regardless of which fraud theory the government relies on for the underlying counts.

**Why the opinion reaches that conclusion**

1. **Elements of a § 1349 conspiracy**  
   A § 1349 charge requires only (i) an agreement among two or more persons to commit a crime and (ii) an overt act in furtherance of that agreement.  The underlying crime is securities fraud under § 1348, which the Fifth Circuit defines as requiring (a) a *scheme to defraud* and (b) an *intent to defraud* (see *Constantinescu*, §§ III‑A‑B).  The conspirators need not agree to a particular doctrinal “theory” of fraud; they must agree to engage in the *scheme* that satisfies those elements.

2. **The indictment’s sufficiency**  
   The opinion holds that the superseding indictment “sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud” because it **states** that defendants posted false and misleading information to *induce* followers to purchase the securities and then *dump* the securities for profit (Indictment ¶ 13‑14).  This allegation satisfies the *scheme to defraud* element (material misrepresentation intended to obtain money or property) and the *intent to defraud* element (intent to deceive and obtain money).  The Court expressly rejects the district court’s view that the indictment fails because the alleged injury is “deprivation of valuable economic information” rather than a traditional property interest.

3. **No need to plead the fraudulent‑inducement theory separately**  
   The Fifth Circuit treats the “fraudulent‑inducement theory” as a legitimate way to satisfy the *scheme* and *intent* elements (see *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1388‑89).  Because the indictment already alleges that defendants *fraudulently induced* investors to buy securities, the conspiratorial agreement to pursue that conduct is already captured.  The Court does not require the indictment to *label* the theory; it only requires that the factual allegations show a conspiratorial agreement to execute the fraud‑scheme.  Thus, the conspiracy charge is satisfied when the indictment alleges the same factual conduct that supports the substantive fraud counts.

4. **Precedent applied**  
   The opinion draws on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* to clarify that the fraud statutes protect “money or property” obtained through deception, not merely the loss of “valuable economic information.”  By aligning the indictment’s allegations with the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the Court confirms that the underlying fraud theory is already embedded in the charging document.  Consequently, a separate allegation that the conspirators agreed specifically to use the fraudulent‑inducement theory is unnecessary.

**Practical implication for future indictments**

- When drafting a § 1349 conspiracy count, prosecutors need only describe the *scheme* (e.g., posting false statements to induce purchases) and the *intent* (e.g., intent to obtain money/property).  
- They may repeat the factual allegations supporting the substantive fraud counts, but they do **not** need to state that the conspirators agreed to employ a particular doctrinal theory of fraud.  
- If the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme that, under *Constantinescu*, satisfies the elements of securities fraud, the conspiracy charge will survive a facial sufficiency challenge.

**Bottom line**

The Supreme Court’s endorsement of the fraudulent‑inducement theory in *Kousisis* does **not** create an additional pleading requirement for a § 1349 conspiracy.  As *Constantinescu* makes clear, an indictment that alleges a scheme to induce investors to part with money or property—regardless of how the government later characterizes the theory—is enough to support a conspiracy charge.  No separate “agreement to pursue the fraudulent‑inducement theory” needs to be pleaded.

### What is the “double jeopardy” purpose of an indictment, and how does the Fifth Circuit ensure it is met in fraud cases?
Question:
The opinion cites United States v. Gaytan, noting an indictment must provide a “double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” In the context of securities‑fraud prosecutions, how does the court verify that the indictment’s allegations are sufficiently particular to protect the defendants from later re‑prosecution on the same conduct?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit treats the “double‑jeopardy purpose” of an indictment as one of three statutory requisites for a sufficient charging document.  As Judge Engelhardt restates, an indictment must:

1. **Allege each element of the offense;**  
2. **Fairly inform the defendant of the charge;** and  
3. **Provide the defendant with a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions**  
(United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996)).

The double‑jeopardy function is satisfied only when the **theory of the crime and the factual allegations are sufficiently particular to bind the government to the same “scheme” in any later proceeding**.  If the indictment is vague enough that the government could later bring a charge on a different set of facts or a different legal theory, the defendant could be subject to multiple prosecutions for the same conduct, violating the Fifth Amendment.

In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit explained how it checks that the indictment meets this requirement in securities‑fraud cases:

1. **Take the indictment’s allegations as true.**  
   The court reviews the charging document de novo, assuming every factual claim is correct, and asks whether the alleged conduct *already* satisfies all elements of the offense.  (See *Rafoi*, 60 F.4th at 993.)

2. **Require a complete “scheme‑to‑defraud” allegation.**  
   The indictment must describe a **coherent scheme**—not just isolated misstatements—but a plan that “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived” (see *Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12).  In *Constantinescu* the superseding indictment alleged that defendants **“posted false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security … and then secretly sold the security for a profit.”**  This allegation ties the deceptive conduct directly to the obtaining of money or property, satisfying the scheme element.

3. **Require an “intent to defraud” allegation.**  
   The charging document must show that the defendants **intended both to deceive and to obtain money or property**.  The opinion points out that the indictment’s statement that defendants “profited $114 million” from the pump‑and‑dump scheme is sufficient to demonstrate the required intent (see *Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct. at 1388‑89).

4. **Link the alleged conduct to the victims’ “money or property.”**  
   Because the indictment alleges that followers were induced to **purchase securities**—a transaction that requires the victims to part with money or other property—the government is bound to that particular mode of fraud.  The court notes that, under the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory approved in *Kousisis*, the fraud statutes protect the victim’s money or property even if the victim later receives securities of market value.  (See *Constantinescu*, ¶ 13.)

5. **Resulting limitation on future prosecutions.**  
   By demanding that the indictment specify the **misrepresentations, the induced purchases, and the defendants’ profit motive**, the Fifth Circuit ensures that any subsequent prosecution must be grounded in the same factual “scheme.”  The government cannot later pivot to a “right‑to‑control” theory (rejected in *Ciminelli*) or to a different set of misrepresentations without running afoul of the double‑jeopardy bar.

**In short**, the Fifth Circuit satisfies the double‑jeopardy purpose by requiring that an indictment in a securities‑fraud case **clearly outline a single, continuous scheme that involves the deception of victims into parting with money or property, and that the defendants intended to obtain that money or property.**  When the charging document meets these particularity requirements, the defendant is protected from any later prosecution that attempts to relitigate the same conduct under a different legal theory.

### Why did the Fifth Circuit rely on mail‑ and wire‑fraud precedent to interpret the securities‑fraud statute in Constantinescu?
Question:
The opinion mentions that § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and that the court often relies on those cases. What are the key parallels, and are there any limits to using mail‑/wire‑fraud jurisprudence to resolve securities‑fraud elements?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit treats § 1348 (the securities‑fraud statute) as a “cousin” of the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes.  The opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu* explains that the statute “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and therefore the court “often relies on mail‑ and wire‑fraud cases in analyzing securities‑fraud charges.”  The reliance is grounded in three doctrinal parallels:

| Element of § 1348 | Parallel in mail/wire fraud | How the Fifth Circuit applies it |
|-------------------|----------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Scheme to defraud** | A *scheme* is a “plan or artifice” that involves a *material misrepresentation* intended to deceive.  (18 U.S.C. § 1341/§ 1343) | The court reads § 1348’s “scheme to defraud” the same way it reads the mail‑/wire‑fraud element, borrowing the definition from *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12.  It therefore requires a “material misrepresentation … intended to deceive … to obtain something of value, such as money, from the victim.” |
| **Intent to defraud** | The fraud statutes require an *intent* to deceive and to cause *some harm* (the “intent to defraud” element). | The Fifth Circuit imports the two‑prong formulation from *Evans* (and the Supreme Court’s articulation in *Kousisis*) – (1) intent to deceive, and (2) intent that the deception cause the victim to part with money or property.  The opinion stresses that “the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss” (citing *Kousisis*). |
| **Property‑theft requirement** | Both sets of statutes protect *traditional property interests*; they do not punish the deprivation of mere information. | The court follows the Supreme Court’s *Ciminelli* rule that “the right‑to‑control theory” (i.e., deprivation of valuable information) is insufficient.  Instead, it looks for the “fraudulent‑inducement theory,” which protects money or property that the victim actually parts with, even if the victim receives something of equal value.  This mirrors the analysis used in mail‑/wire‑fraud cases such as *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th at 339 n.6. |

### Why the Fifth Circuit leans on that precedent

1. **Statutory language is almost identical** – § 1348’s text (“scheme to defraud … obtain … money or property …”) is a verb‑by‑verb copy of the mail‑ and wire‑fraud provisions.  The opinion notes the “scant caselaw” directly construing § 1348, so the court looks to the well‑developed mail‑/wire‑fraud jurisprudence for guidance.

2. **Supreme Court guidance** – The Supreme Court’s recent decisions (*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*) were rendered in the context of the wire‑fraud statute but hinged on the same “scheme‑to‑defraud” and “intent‑to‑defraud” language.  The Fifth Circuit therefore treats those holdings as controlling for § 1348 as well.

3. **Policy consistency** – Applying the same “fraudulent‑inducement” theory to securities fraud prevents a loophole whereby a defendant could escape liability simply because the fraud involved a securities transaction rather than a traditional mail‑ or wire‑communication scheme.

### Limits to the borrowed approach

The opinion also implicitly flags the boundaries of this analogy:

* **No “right‑to‑control” liability** – *Ciminelli* is expressly distinguished.  The Fifth Circuit will not allow a securities‑fraud conviction on the theory that the victim merely lost a chance to use information; the prosecution must allege that the victim actually transferred money or property.  This mirrors the wire‑fraud limit that “the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a fraud conviction.”

* **Requirement of a *material* misrepresentation** – As in mail‑/wire‑fraud cases, the misstatement must be material.  The court will not extend § 1348 to any untrue statement; it must be one that a reasonable investor would consider important to the purchase decision.

* **Presence of a “victim” who can be deceived** – The borrowed analysis presupposes a traditional victim‑defrauded relationship.  The Fifth Circuit will not treat a purely abstract market manipulation (e.g., affecting price indices without a specific purchaser) as satisfying the “scheme to defraud” element unless a specific class of investors is identified, just as the mail‑/wire‑fraud doctrine requires a definable victim class.

* **No “mirror‑image” requirement** – While the court adopts the *Kousisis* rule that the victim need not suffer a net loss, it still requires that the defendant’s objective be to obtain the victim’s money or property.  A claim that the defendant merely “shared” securities of equal market value would not satisfy the element, consistent with the mail‑/wire‑fraud limitation that the fraud must be for gain, not merely exchange.

### Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit leans on mail‑ and wire‑fraud precedent because § 1348’s language and the Supreme Court’s recent holdings treat the securities‑fraud statute as a direct extension of the older fraud statutes.  The key parallels are the definitions of “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud,” and the requirement that the fraud target a traditional property interest (money or property), not just information.  The limits are the same as in mail‑/wire‑fraud cases: the prosecution must allege a material misrepresentation that actually induces victims to part with money or property, and the “right‑to‑control” theory is unavailable.  Within those bounds, the Fifth Circuit can reliably apply mail‑/wire‑fraud jurisprudence to resolve the elements of securities‑fraud charges, as *Constantinescu* demonstrates.
