Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Can a defendant be charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under § 1349 if the underlying fraud theory is the fraudulent‑inducement theory?
Question:
The indictment includes conspiracy to commit securities fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1349) alongside substantive fraud counts. Does the Supreme Court’s approval of the fraudulent‑inducement theory automatically extend to the conspiracy charge, or must the indictment separately allege agreement to pursue that specific theory?
Answer:
**Short answer**

No.  Under the Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the conspiracy charge under § 1349 does **not** require the government to allege a separate agreement to pursue the fraudulent‑inducement theory.  It is enough that the indictment alleges a scheme that satisfies the elements of securities fraud – a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” – because a conspiracy to commit the substantive offense is satisfied by an agreement to join that scheme, regardless of which fraud theory the government relies on for the underlying counts.

**Why the opinion reaches that conclusion**

1. **Elements of a § 1349 conspiracy**  
   A § 1349 charge requires only (i) an agreement among two or more persons to commit a crime and (ii) an overt act in furtherance of that agreement.  The underlying crime is securities fraud under § 1348, which the Fifth Circuit defines as requiring (a) a *scheme to defraud* and (b) an *intent to defraud* (see *Constantinescu*, §§ III‑A‑B).  The conspirators need not agree to a particular doctrinal “theory” of fraud; they must agree to engage in the *scheme* that satisfies those elements.

2. **The indictment’s sufficiency**  
   The opinion holds that the superseding indictment “sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud” because it **states** that defendants posted false and misleading information to *induce* followers to purchase the securities and then *dump* the securities for profit (Indictment ¶ 13‑14).  This allegation satisfies the *scheme to defraud* element (material misrepresentation intended to obtain money or property) and the *intent to defraud* element (intent to deceive and obtain money).  The Court expressly rejects the district court’s view that the indictment fails because the alleged injury is “deprivation of valuable economic information” rather than a traditional property interest.

3. **No need to plead the fraudulent‑inducement theory separately**  
   The Fifth Circuit treats the “fraudulent‑inducement theory” as a legitimate way to satisfy the *scheme* and *intent* elements (see *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1388‑89).  Because the indictment already alleges that defendants *fraudulently induced* investors to buy securities, the conspiratorial agreement to pursue that conduct is already captured.  The Court does not require the indictment to *label* the theory; it only requires that the factual allegations show a conspiratorial agreement to execute the fraud‑scheme.  Thus, the conspiracy charge is satisfied when the indictment alleges the same factual conduct that supports the substantive fraud counts.

4. **Precedent applied**  
   The opinion draws on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* to clarify that the fraud statutes protect “money or property” obtained through deception, not merely the loss of “valuable economic information.”  By aligning the indictment’s allegations with the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the Court confirms that the underlying fraud theory is already embedded in the charging document.  Consequently, a separate allegation that the conspirators agreed specifically to use the fraudulent‑inducement theory is unnecessary.

**Practical implication for future indictments**

- When drafting a § 1349 conspiracy count, prosecutors need only describe the *scheme* (e.g., posting false statements to induce purchases) and the *intent* (e.g., intent to obtain money/property).  
- They may repeat the factual allegations supporting the substantive fraud counts, but they do **not** need to state that the conspirators agreed to employ a particular doctrinal theory of fraud.  
- If the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme that, under *Constantinescu*, satisfies the elements of securities fraud, the conspiracy charge will survive a facial sufficiency challenge.

**Bottom line**

The Supreme Court’s endorsement of the fraudulent‑inducement theory in *Kousisis* does **not** create an additional pleading requirement for a § 1349 conspiracy.  As *Constantinescu* makes clear, an indictment that alleges a scheme to induce investors to part with money or property—regardless of how the government later characterizes the theory—is enough to support a conspiracy charge.  No separate “agreement to pursue the fraudulent‑inducement theory” needs to be pleaded.
