Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Why did the Fifth Circuit rely on mail‑ and wire‑fraud precedent to interpret the securities‑fraud statute in Constantinescu?
Question:
The opinion mentions that § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and that the court often relies on those cases. What are the key parallels, and are there any limits to using mail‑/wire‑fraud jurisprudence to resolve securities‑fraud elements?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit treats § 1348 (the securities‑fraud statute) as a “cousin” of the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes.  The opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu* explains that the statute “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes” and therefore the court “often relies on mail‑ and wire‑fraud cases in analyzing securities‑fraud charges.”  The reliance is grounded in three doctrinal parallels:

| Element of § 1348 | Parallel in mail/wire fraud | How the Fifth Circuit applies it |
|-------------------|----------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Scheme to defraud** | A *scheme* is a “plan or artifice” that involves a *material misrepresentation* intended to deceive.  (18 U.S.C. § 1341/§ 1343) | The court reads § 1348’s “scheme to defraud” the same way it reads the mail‑/wire‑fraud element, borrowing the definition from *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12.  It therefore requires a “material misrepresentation … intended to deceive … to obtain something of value, such as money, from the victim.” |
| **Intent to defraud** | The fraud statutes require an *intent* to deceive and to cause *some harm* (the “intent to defraud” element). | The Fifth Circuit imports the two‑prong formulation from *Evans* (and the Supreme Court’s articulation in *Kousisis*) – (1) intent to deceive, and (2) intent that the deception cause the victim to part with money or property.  The opinion stresses that “the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss” (citing *Kousisis*). |
| **Property‑theft requirement** | Both sets of statutes protect *traditional property interests*; they do not punish the deprivation of mere information. | The court follows the Supreme Court’s *Ciminelli* rule that “the right‑to‑control theory” (i.e., deprivation of valuable information) is insufficient.  Instead, it looks for the “fraudulent‑inducement theory,” which protects money or property that the victim actually parts with, even if the victim receives something of equal value.  This mirrors the analysis used in mail‑/wire‑fraud cases such as *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th at 339 n.6. |

### Why the Fifth Circuit leans on that precedent

1. **Statutory language is almost identical** – § 1348’s text (“scheme to defraud … obtain … money or property …”) is a verb‑by‑verb copy of the mail‑ and wire‑fraud provisions.  The opinion notes the “scant caselaw” directly construing § 1348, so the court looks to the well‑developed mail‑/wire‑fraud jurisprudence for guidance.

2. **Supreme Court guidance** – The Supreme Court’s recent decisions (*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*) were rendered in the context of the wire‑fraud statute but hinged on the same “scheme‑to‑defraud” and “intent‑to‑defraud” language.  The Fifth Circuit therefore treats those holdings as controlling for § 1348 as well.

3. **Policy consistency** – Applying the same “fraudulent‑inducement” theory to securities fraud prevents a loophole whereby a defendant could escape liability simply because the fraud involved a securities transaction rather than a traditional mail‑ or wire‑communication scheme.

### Limits to the borrowed approach

The opinion also implicitly flags the boundaries of this analogy:

* **No “right‑to‑control” liability** – *Ciminelli* is expressly distinguished.  The Fifth Circuit will not allow a securities‑fraud conviction on the theory that the victim merely lost a chance to use information; the prosecution must allege that the victim actually transferred money or property.  This mirrors the wire‑fraud limit that “the right‑to‑control theory cannot form the basis for a fraud conviction.”

* **Requirement of a *material* misrepresentation** – As in mail‑/wire‑fraud cases, the misstatement must be material.  The court will not extend § 1348 to any untrue statement; it must be one that a reasonable investor would consider important to the purchase decision.

* **Presence of a “victim” who can be deceived** – The borrowed analysis presupposes a traditional victim‑defrauded relationship.  The Fifth Circuit will not treat a purely abstract market manipulation (e.g., affecting price indices without a specific purchaser) as satisfying the “scheme to defraud” element unless a specific class of investors is identified, just as the mail‑/wire‑fraud doctrine requires a definable victim class.

* **No “mirror‑image” requirement** – While the court adopts the *Kousisis* rule that the victim need not suffer a net loss, it still requires that the defendant’s objective be to obtain the victim’s money or property.  A claim that the defendant merely “shared” securities of equal market value would not satisfy the element, consistent with the mail‑/wire‑fraud limitation that the fraud must be for gain, not merely exchange.

### Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit leans on mail‑ and wire‑fraud precedent because § 1348’s language and the Supreme Court’s recent holdings treat the securities‑fraud statute as a direct extension of the older fraud statutes.  The key parallels are the definitions of “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud,” and the requirement that the fraud target a traditional property interest (money or property), not just information.  The limits are the same as in mail‑/wire‑fraud cases: the prosecution must allege a material misrepresentation that actually induces victims to part with money or property, and the “right‑to‑control” theory is unavailable.  Within those bounds, the Fifth Circuit can reliably apply mail‑/wire‑fraud jurisprudence to resolve the elements of securities‑fraud charges, as *Constantinescu* demonstrates.
