Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 4: # Supreme Court Guidance: Ciminelli and Kousisis  

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court’s dismissal in **United States v. Constantinescu** rests heavily on two recent United States Supreme Court decisions: *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*.  Both cases interpret the federal fraud statutes—wire fraud, mail fraud, and the securities‑fraud provisions of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343, 1348—by clarifying what “property” means under the statutes and by confirming that a defendant’s intent to obtain money or other property is sufficient for liability even when the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss.  Understanding these holdings is essential for analyzing whether an indictment sufficiently alleges a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite “intent to defraud” in the Constantinescu case.

## Ciminelli: Rejection of the Right‑to‑Control Theory and Protection of Traditional Property Interests  

### The Court’s Holding  

In *Ciminelli v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), the Supreme Court considered a wire‑fraud prosecution that rested on the “right‑to‑control” theory.  The government alleged that the defendant, a construction firm, obtained a lobbyist’s services to secure state‑funded contracts and that the fraud consisted of depriving the state of the “right to control” its own assets by withholding valuable economic information.  The Court held that the federal fraud statutes protect **traditional property interests**—interests that the law historically treats as property, such as money, tangible assets, or legal claims to money—but **do not protect a mere “right to valuable economic information.”**  The Court therefore rejected the government’s reliance on the right‑to‑control theory and dismissed the indictment.  

### Traditional Property Interest vs. Valuable Economic Information  

*Traditional property interest* is a term the Court uses to encompass “property” in the ordinary sense of the word—money, securities, real or personal property, and legal rights that are recognized as property under statutes and case law.  The key features are:

1. **Legally cognizable ownership or entitlement** – The interest can be bought, sold, or transferred.  
2. **Protected by property law** – The interest is safeguarded by the usual remedies for conversion, theft, or trespass.  

In contrast, *valuable economic information*—for example, insider tips, market forecasts, or data that enables better investment decisions—does not, by itself, constitute a property right.  While it may be **economically valuable**, the law does not treat the information itself as property that can be “taken” or “stolen.”  Consequently, a scheme that merely withholds such information, without depriving a victim of money or a legally protected asset, cannot satisfy the “scheme to defraud” element of the fraud statutes.

### Relevance to Constantinescu  

Defendants in *Constantinescu* attempted to argue that the district court’s dismissal was proper because the indictment only alleged that the defendants deprived followers of “potentially valuable economic information” rather than a property interest.  The Fifth Circuit, following *Ciminelli*, rejected that argument: the indictment does **not** rely on a right‑to‑control theory.  Instead, it alleges that defendants **induced followers to part with their money** by purchasing securities under false pretenses.  Because the alleged misrepresentations were aimed at obtaining **money or securities** from the victims, the conduct falls squarely within the “traditional property” category that *Ciminelli* preserves for fraud prosecutions.

## Kousisis: affirmation of the fraudulent‑inducement theory and the irrelevance of victim’s net loss  

### The Court’s Holding  

In *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1388 (2025), the Court addressed a fraud prosecution based on the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory.  The defendant obtained state contracts by misrepresenting that his company would meet a statutory subcontracting requirement.  The Court held that a conviction is proper when the defendant **uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires the victim to hand over money or property**, regardless of whether the defendant’s conduct actually causes the victim a net economic loss.  The Court emphasized that the fraud statutes are satisfied by the **objective acquisition of the victim’s money or property**, not by a subjective desire to harm the victim’s bottom line.

### Why Victim’s Loss Is Not an Element  

The fraudulent‑inducement theory distinguishes itself from the right‑to‑control theory by focusing on the **transactional element** of the fraud: the victim is induced to **part with money or property** in exchange for something of purported value (e.g., a contract, a security, a service).  The Court in *Kousisis* clarified two points:

1. **Intent to Obtain Money or Property Is Sufficient** – The defendant’s purpose must be to obtain the victim’s money or property; the defendant does not need to **intentionally cause a loss** beyond the mere exchange.  
2. **Actual Loss May Be Absent** – Even if the victim later receives something of equal or greater market value, the fraud is complete once the false statement causes the victim to part with money or property.  The statute does not require proof of a “mirror‑image” loss.

### How Kousisis Shapes the Analysis in Constantinescu  

The indictment in *Constantinescu* alleges that the defendants posted false statements to **induce followers to purchase securities**—a classic example of fraudulent inducement.  The defendants’ profit of $114 million demonstrates an **intent to obtain money** from the victims.  Under *Kousisis*, the government need not prove that each follower suffered a net loss; it is enough that the followers were persuaded to spend money on securities based on deceit.  Consequently, the Fifth Circuit’s determination that the indictment sufficiently alleges both a **scheme to defraud** (the coordinated “pump‑and‑dump” plan) and an **intent to defraud** (the purposeful misrepresentations designed to obtain followers’ money) follows directly from *Kousisis*.

## How These Decisions Shape the Analysis of Scheme and Intent in Constantinescu  

1. **Scheme to Defraud** – The *Ciminelli* decision tells us that a scheme must target a **traditional property interest**.  The indictment’s allegations that defendants caused followers to **hand over money** to buy securities satisfy this requirement.  The “pump‑and‑dump” plan is presented as a coordinated effort to **create a market distortion** that compels victims to part with money; therefore, the scheme element is met.

2. **Intent to Defraud** – *Kousisis* confirms that the government need only show the defendants **intended to obtain money or property** through deception.  The indictment’s description of the defendants’ profit motive—purchasing securities, inflating their price, then selling at a profit—demonstrates the requisite intent, even if the defendants claim that they did not intend to “hurt” their followers.  The Supreme Court’s language that a fraud is complete when the defendant **“schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss”** directly supports the Fifth Circuit’s conclusion.

3. **Impact on the Sufficiency Review** – Because the Supreme Court has drawn a clear line between **invalid right‑to‑control theories** and **valid fraudulent‑inducement theories**, the Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of the indictment focuses on whether the complaint’s facts invoke the latter.  The *Constantinescu* indictment does so; therefore, the district court’s dismissal—predicated on a mischaracterization of the alleged injury—fails under the guidance of *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*.

In sum, *Ciminelli* bars reliance on a theory that treats the deprivation of information as “property,” while *Kousisis* opens the door for convictions where the defendant’s deception causes victims to part with money or other traditional property, irrespective of the victim’s ultimate economic outcome.  These holdings together provide the doctrinal foundation for the Fifth Circuit’s reversal: the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu* adequately alleges a **scheme to defraud** and an **intent to defraud** within the parameters set by the Supreme Court.
