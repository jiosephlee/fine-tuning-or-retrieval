Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 1: # Background and Procedural History  

The United States v. Constantinescu case provides a contemporary illustration of how federal securities‑fraud statutes are applied to modern, internet‑based “pump‑and‑dump” schemes. Understanding the factual backdrop, the charges asserted, and the procedural journey from the district court to the Fifth Circuit is essential for reading the opinion’s analysis of indictment sufficiency.

## The Alleged Pump‑and‑Dump Scheme on Social‑Media Platforms  

The government’s complaint focuses on a coordinated effort by eight defendants—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight—to manipulate the market price of publicly traded securities through false statements on social‑media sites. Each defendant maintained a sizable following on platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and Twitter, where they cultivated an image of successful, “insider” traders.  

According to the superseding indictment, the scheme unfolded in three steps:

1. **Acquisition** – The defendants purchased a target security, establishing a genuine ownership interest that permitted them to profit from a price rise.  
2. **Pump** – While holding the security, they posted or streamed material that misrepresented their actual trading position, the security’s intrinsic value, and the likelihood of future price appreciation. The posts were crafted to appear credible and to induce followers to buy the same security, thereby creating artificial demand.  
3. **Dump** – After the price was inflated by the influx of follower purchases, the defendants sold their holdings at the elevated price, realizing a profit. The indictment alleges that the defendants collectively earned roughly **$114 million** from these transactions.

The government characterizes the conduct as a classic “pump‑and‑dump” fraud, adapted to the digital age: the false statements constituted the “pump,” and the subsequent sale of the security constituted the “dump.” By using their social‑media influence to induce others to part with money, the defendants allegedly obtained money or property of the victims through fraudulent pretenses, the very conduct prohibited by the securities‑fraud provisions of the criminal code.

## Count of Conspiracy and Securities‑Fraud Charges (18 U.S.C. §§ 1349, 1348)  

The indictment charges the defendants with two distinct statutory offenses:

1. **Conspiracy to Commit Securities Fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1349)** – This count alleges an agreement among the eight defendants to engage in a scheme that violates §§ 1348 (securities fraud) and §§ 1342 (wire fraud), and that each defendant knowingly participated in that agreement. Conspiracy requires proof of an overt act in furtherance of the illegal agreement, which the government points to the repeated false postings and the coordinated timing of the securities purchases and sales.

2. **Securities Fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1348)** – The indictment contains multiple counts under this provision. Section 1348 criminalizes any scheme “to defraud any person…in connection with any security” and any scheme “to obtain…money or property…in connection with the purchase or sale of any security” by means of false or fraudulent pretenses. The core elements the government must plead are:  
   * a **scheme to defraud**,  
   * the **use of false or misleading statements**,  
   * the **intent to deceive**, and  
   * **obtaining money or property** (or the object of obtaining such) from the victims.  

The indictment’s allegations—false social‑media statements, induced purchases by followers, and the defendants’ secret profit from the ensuing price rise—are framed to satisfy each of these statutory components.

## Outcome in District Court: Dismissal for Failure to Allege Deprivation of Property  

Defendant Daniel Knight entered a guilty plea, but the remaining seven defendants moved to dismiss the superseding indictment. The district court granted the motion, holding that the indictment “failed to state an offense by merely alleging that defendants sought to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information instead of a traditional property interest.” The court’s reasoning hinged on the Supreme Court’s decision in **Ciminelli v. United States**, which held that fraud statutes do not protect a “right‑to‑control” theory of ownership—that is, a claim that the victim’s valuable economic information, rather than money or property, was the target of the fraud.

The district court concluded that the indictment did not allege that the defendants intended to obtain the followers’ money or property; rather, it portrayed the alleged harm as the deprivation of information. Consequently, the court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it did not articulate a scheme to appropriate a protected property interest.

## The Government’s Timely Appeal and the Standard of Review for Indictment Sufficiency  

Following the dismissal, the United States filed an appeal. Under the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, an appeal from a district‑court order dismissing an indictment is timely if filed within 30 days after service of the order; the government met this deadline, making the appeal procedurally proper.

The Fifth Circuit’s review of an indictment’s sufficiency is **de novo**. As the opinion repeats, *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023) states that the appellate court must “take the allegations of the indictment as true and determine whether an offense has been stated.” This standard obliges the reviewing court to focus exclusively on the factual allegations set forth in the charging document, without weighing the sufficiency of the evidence or the credibility of witnesses. The court asks whether the indictment **(1)** enumerates each prima facie element of the charged offense, **(2)** fairly informs the defendant of the charges, and **(3)** supplies a basis for a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. (*United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996)).

Thus, the appellate task is to determine whether, **assuming the truth of the indictment’s factual statements**, the government has alleged every element of §§ 1348 and 1349. The Fifth Circuit’s analysis therefore centers on the indictment’s language describing (a) the defendants’ false statements, (b) the induced purchases by followers, (c) the defendants’ secret sales, and (d) the resulting profit, and whether these allegations collectively satisfy the statutory requirements of a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” that obtains victims’ money or property.  

In the following sections of the opinion, the Fifth Circuit evaluates those allegations against the Supreme Court’s recent interpretations of the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory (as distinguished from the rejected “right‑to‑control” theory) and ultimately reverses the district court’s dismissal, holding that the indictment does, in fact, sufficiently allege a scheme and intent to defraud.  

---  

By appreciating the factual underpinnings of the alleged pump‑and‑dump operation, the specific statutory charges, the district court’s rationale for dismissal, and the de novo standard applied on appeal, a law student can follow the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning and understand how modern securities‑fraud prosecutions are analyzed.
