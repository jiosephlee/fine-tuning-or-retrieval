Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 3: # Statutory Framework: Securities Fraud and Its Relationship to Wire/Fraud Statutes  

In United States v. Constantinescu the Fifth Circuit confronted a fundamental question: does the federal securities‑fraud provision, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, require the same “scheme‑to‑defraud” and “intent‑to‑defraud” elements that Congress embedded in the older mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes?  The court’s answer—yes—rests on a close reading of the text of § 1348, an understanding that the provision borrows its key definitions from §§ 1341‑1343, and a body of circuit precedent that applies those definitions to the “material‑misrepresentation” and “fraudulent‑inducement” theories of liability.  This chapter breaks down each of those components so that you can see how the opinion fits the statutory scheme and why the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment was sufficient.

## The Text of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and Its Core Elements  

Section 1348 criminalizes two distinct types of conduct in connection with any security:

1. **A scheme or artifice to defraud any person** in connection with a security, **and**  
2. **A scheme to obtain money or property** by false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises **in connection with the purchase or sale of any security**.  

Both subsections require, at minimum, **(a) a “scheme to defraud”** and **(b) an “intent to defraud.”**  The statute does not enumerate additional elements such as loss to the victim; the focus is on the defendants’ deceptive conduct and their purpose to obtain something of value.  In Constantinescu the court stressed that the indictment must allege each of these material elements in order to survive a motion to dismiss.

## Borrowed Definitions from the Mail‑ and Wire‑Fraud Statutes ( §§ 1341‑1343 )  

Congress drafted §§ 1341 (mail fraud) and 1343 (wire fraud) earlier, providing detailed definitions of “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud.”  Because § 1348 “borrows key concepts” from those statutes, courts treat the language as interchangeable unless the securities statute offers a different meaning.

- **Scheme to Defraud** (see § 1341(4)): a “plan or design which, if carried out, would result in the deprivation of money or property.”  The Fifth Circuit reads this as requiring a **material misrepresentation or omission** that is intended to deceive a victim into parting with money or property.

- **Intent to Defraud** (see § 1341(4)): the **purpose to deceive** and **cause a victim to suffer a deprivation of money or property**.  The intent element is satisfied when the defendant **knows** the falsity of the statement and **purposefully** uses it to obtain the victim’s money or property.

Because § 1348 does not redefine these terms, the Fifth Circuit applies the mail‑ and wire‑fraud definitions directly.  This doctrinal shortcut is reflected in the opinion’s citation to Greenlaw, which notes that “courts have given the terms similar treatment” when interpreting securities‑fraud charges.

## Material‑Misrepresentation and Fraudulent‑Inducement Theories  

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis in Constantinescu turns on two recognized fraud theories that satisfy the “scheme” and “intent” elements.

### 1. Material‑Misrepresentation Theory  

Under this theory, a defendant makes **a false statement of fact that is material**—i.e., a fact that a reasonable investor would consider important in deciding whether to buy or sell a security.  The misrepresentation must be **intended to deceive** the victim, and the defendant must **seek to obtain money or property** from the victim as a result.

- *Key case*: **United States v. Evans**, 892 F.3d 692 (5th Cir. 2018).  Evans defined the scheme element as “a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the entity to be deceived.”  The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu adopts this language verbatim, showing that the indictment’s allegation that defendants posted “false and misleading information” about a security satisfies the material‑misrepresentation prong.

### 2. Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory  

Here the focus is not on the falsity of a statement about the security itself, but on the **use of a material false statement to induce a victim to enter into a contract or transaction** that requires the victim to hand over money or property.  The victim’s loss is not a prerequisite; the crime is complete when the defendant successfully induces the victim to part with money or property under false pretenses.

- *Key case*: **Kousisis v. United States**, 145 S.Ct. 1382 (2025).  The Supreme Court clarified that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is **not a repackaging of the “right‑to‑control” theory** rejected in Ciminelli.  Instead, it protects “money and property” and does not require proof of the victim’s pecuniary loss.  The Constantinescu opinion leans heavily on Kousisis, emphasizing that the indictment’s allegation that defendants “induced” followers to purchase securities fits squarely within this theory.

Both theories satisfy the statutory “scheme to defraud” requirement because they each involve a **deceptive plan that, if carried out, deprives victims of money or property**.  They also meet the “intent to defraud” element because the defendants are alleged to have **purposefully used false statements to obtain the victims’ funds**.

## Circuit Precedent Interpreting the Elements  

The Fifth Circuit has a modest but growing body of cases that apply the mail‑ and wire‑fraud definitions to securities‑fraud prosecutions.  Two decisions are especially instructive for Constantinescu.

### *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th 325 (5th Cir. 2023)  

Greenlaw treats § 1348 as a “straightforward borrowing” of the fraud‑statute language.  The court notes that where the statute is silent, courts may look to §§ 1341‑1343 for guidance on what constitutes a “scheme” and an “intent.”  This precedent gives the Constantinescu court authority to import the well‑developed definitions from mail‑ and wire‑fraud jurisprudence.

### *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d 692 (5th Cir. 2018)  

Evans is the leading Fifth Circuit exposition of the “material misrepresentation” component of a fraud scheme.  The court emphasized that **the misrepresentation must be material and the defendant must intend to obtain money or property**.  Constantinescu’s opinion directly cites Evans for the proposition that the indictment’s alleged false social‑media posts satisfy the material‑misrepresentation requirement.

Together, Greenlaw and Evans provide the analytical scaffolding the Fifth Circuit uses to conclude that the Constantinescu indictment alleged both required elements.  The court does not need to invent new categories; it simply applies the established definitions to the facts alleged in the superseding indictment.

## How the Framework Operates in Constantinescu  

Applying the statutory framework:

1. **Scheme to Defraud** – The indictment alleges that defendants posted false statements (“pump” posts) to induce followers to buy securities.  This is a **material misrepresentation** (the price and trading position were false) intended to cause followers to part with money.  Alternatively, under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the false posts are the “means” by which the defendants induced the purchase contract.

2. **Intent to Defraud** – The indictment further alleges that defendants “secretly sold” the securities for profit, showing a **purpose to obtain money or property**.  The Supreme Court’s Kousisis decision confirms that a defendant’s **profit motive** alone satisfies the intent element; proving an intent to *harm* the victim is unnecessary.

Because the indictment contains factual allegations that meet both elements, the Fifth Circuit held that the complaint was legally sufficient, reversing the district court’s dismissal.

---

**Take‑away for the student:** When reading a securities‑fraud indictment, always ask whether the complaint alleges (a) a **material misrepresentation** or a **fraudulent inducement** that would cause a victim to give up money or property, and (b) the defendant’s **purpose** to obtain that money or property.  The Fifth Circuit (and the Supreme Court) treat § 1348 as a direct import of the mail‑ and wire‑fraud definitions, so the same analytical roadmap applies.  If both prongs are pleaded with enough factual detail, the indictment survives a motion to dismiss, as it did in United States v. Constantinescu.
