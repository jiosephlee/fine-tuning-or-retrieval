Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Factual and Procedural Background  

The purpose of this chapter is to lay out, in a clear, step‑by‑step fashion, the factual matrix and procedural history that gave rise to the appeal in *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, 7th Cir. No. 24‑3051 (Oct. 7 2025). Understanding the timeline, the dialogue captured on the officers’ body‑camera recordings, and the lower‑court rulings is essential before the court’s Fourth‑Amendment analysis can be examined.

---

## Timeline of the 911 Call and Officer Arrival  

1. **April 20, 2023, approx. 4:00 a.m.** – A 911 caller reports that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, has threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and is still inside the residence.  
2. **Officer Jamie Kizer** of the Marshfield, Wisconsin Police Department is dispatched and arrives at the Coleman home a few minutes later.  
3. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer **knocks, announces himself**, and waits for a response. The initial exchange is recorded by the officer’s body‑camera system, which becomes the primary factual source for the district court and the appellate panel.  

---

## Key Dialogue Between Officer Kizer and Lisa Coleman  

The video shows a series of questions and answers that illustrate both the police officer’s concern for the children’s safety and Lisa Coleman’s fluctuating willingness to allow entry. The most significant exchanges are reproduced below (minor filler words omitted for readability).

| **Officer Kizer** | **Lisa Coleman** |
|-------------------|------------------|
| *“Are the kids okay?”* | *“I… I… I don’t… They’re… They’re okay right this second…”* (unintelligible) |
| *“I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.”* | **“Okay.”** |
| *“Where are the kids?”* | *“They’re in my bedroom.”* |
| *“Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in?”* | *“You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.”* |
| *“Where do you think Coleman is right now?”* | *“I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.”* |

A few additional points from the surrounding context are important:

* **Initial refusal** – When Officer Kizer first asked to enter, Lisa responded, “I’d prefer you not.” This demonstrates that she possessed the ability to deny entry.  
* **Subsequent request to retrieve shoes** – Lisa later told the officer she needed to go inside to get her shoes; the officer interpreted this as an opportunity to re‑ask for permission.  
* **Final consent** – After the officer’s repeated, but non‑coercive, request (“I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside…”) Lisa answered “Okay” and subsequently **opened the door**, brushed the dogs aside, and led the officers to the back bedroom where the children were located.  

These exchanges are the factual foundation for the district court’s determination that consent was voluntarily given.

---

## Entry into the Home and Subsequent Discovery of the Firearms  

1. **Entry** – Following Lisa’s affirmative “Okay,” Officer Kizer and two other officers entered the residence. Lisa physically opened the door and guided the officers past the dogs.  
2. **Protective sweep** – Inside, the officers conducted a brief “sweep” to locate all occupants and assess immediate danger. They found the children in a bedroom and, after questioning, learned that the stepfather (Coleman) had been “loud, aggressive, and mean,” and had previously pointed a gun at Lisa.  
3. **Locating Coleman** – The sweep continued until the officers found Coleman in a separate bedroom. He **consented to a protective pat‑down**, during which no firearm was discovered.  
4. **Obtaining a warrant** – Based on the information gathered (the children’s testimony about prior gun threats and the ongoing domestic disturbance), the officers secured a **search warrant** for the Coleman home.  
5. **Search and seizure** – The warrant was executed, uncovering **several firearms** (three guns) that later formed the basis of the federal charge under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) – unlawful possession of a firearm by a convicted felon.  

---

## District Court Motion to Suppress and Its Denial  

After the warrant‑supported search, Coleman moved to suppress the firearms on the ground that the **initial entry** into the home was unlawful because his wife, Lisa, had not validly consented.  

* **Magistrate judge’s report** – After reviewing the body‑camera footage, the magistrate concluded that Lisa **did** consent to the officers’ entry. The report recommended denying the motion to suppress.  
* **District court’s ruling** – The district court adopted the magistrate’s finding, holding that: (1) Lisa’s consent was voluntary, and (2) the officers’ subsequent sweep remained within the scope of that consent. Accordingly, the motion to suppress was denied.  

The district court therefore **admitted the firearms as evidence**, leading to Coleman’s indictment and trial.

---

## Coleman’s Guilty Plea, Reservation of Error, and Appellate Jurisdiction  

Faced with the admitted firearms, Coleman entered a **plea of guilty** to the firearm possession charge. However, he **reserved his right to appeal** the district court’s suppression ruling, preserving the issue for appellate review.  

* **Reservation of error** – By pleading guilty while expressly reserving the right to challenge the denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman created a **judicially manageable error** that the appellate court could review.  
* **Appeal filed** – Coleman appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, arguing that Lisa’s consent was not voluntarily given and that, even if consent existed, the officers exceeded its scope.  
* **Appellate jurisdiction** – The Seventh Circuit has jurisdiction to review **“clear error”** findings of fact regarding consent and scope, as articulated in *United States v. Jones*, 22 F.4th 667, 675 (7th Cir. 2022). The appellate panel therefore examined the district court’s factual determinations in light of the statutory and constitutional standards governing consent searches.  

---

### Summary  

The factual and procedural backdrop to *United States v. Coleman* is anchored in a domestic‑violence emergency, a body‑camera‑recorded dialogue that shows an initial refusal followed by a later, uncoerced “Okay,” and a subsequent entry that the district court deemed within the scope of consent. The denial of the motion to suppress led to the seizure of firearms, Coleman’s guilty plea, and the preservation of an appeal on the consent issue. The next chapter will explore the legal standards governing consent under the Fourth Amendment and how the Seventh Circuit applied those standards to the facts outlined here.
