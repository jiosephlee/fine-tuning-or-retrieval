Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Beyond Consent: Why Exigent Circumstances and Inevinate Discovery Were Unnecessary (and Unexplored) in United States v. Coleman  

## Why the case matters  

United States v. Jaison Coleman isn’t just another Fourth‑Amendment consent case from the Seventh Circuit; it is a window into how appellate courts are increasingly treating consent as a “catch‑all” justification that can eclipse the traditional exigent‑circumstances and inevitable‑discovery doctrines. The appellate panel affirmed the district court’s finding that Lisa Coleman’s verbal acquiescence to police entry was sufficient—without even addressing whether an emergency or inevitable‑discovery analysis might have been available. That omission matters because it leaves unanswered a fundamental question: when a police officer’s request is framed as urgent but not coercive, does consent alone satisfy the Fourth Amendment, or must courts still weigh the classic exceptions? The answer shapes the perimeter of lawful police conduct in domestic‑violence calls, a setting that is already fraught with tension between protecting victims and preserving privacy.

## The court’s reasoning on consent  

Judge Scudder’s opinion leans heavily on the well‑settled *Matlock*/*Bustamante* line that a third party may consent to a search of a shared dwelling, provided the consent is “freely and voluntarily” given. The Seventh Circuit applied the “totality of the circumstances” test, enumerating the usual factors: age, intelligence, presence of coercion, and whether the person was in custody. The panel found no error in the district court’s determination that Lisa, a competent adult who was not detained, gave voluntary consent after a brief, non‑coercive exchange.  

The opinion emphasizes two specific observations: (1) Officer Kizer’s tone was “concerned, not coercive,” and (2) Lisa demonstrated an ability to refuse entry—she initially said “I’d prefer you not.” The panel concluded that the subsequent “Okay” after the officer said “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside” was a genuine, unpressured assent. It therefore rejected Coleman’s argument that the word “need” rendered the request overly persuasive.  

## What the opinion left untouched  

### Exigent circumstances  

The subpoena‑driven urgency of a domestic‑violence call often triggers the exigent‑circumstances exception, which permits entry without a warrant when “the police have reason to believe that a delay in obtaining a warrant would jeopardize public safety, the preservation of evidence, or the escape of a suspect” (*Brigham City v. Garrett*, 532 U.S. 915, 923 (2001)). In Coleman, officers arrived amid a report that a potentially armed father had threatened to kill his wife. The facts—an alleged gun threat, children present, and an unknown location of the suspect—clearly satisfied the *Garrett* test for an emergency entry. Yet the Seventh Circuit’s opinion never engaged that analysis, treating consent as the sole foundation for the entry.

By not addressing exigent circumstances, the court missed an opportunity to delineate when consent can serve as a “backup” to an emergency justification versus when the emergency itself suffices. Some appellate courts (e.g., the Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Karpinski*, 742 F.3d 1068 (5th Cir. 2014)) have held that when a valid emergency exists, consent is superfluous; the police may act even if consent is later denied. Clarifying whether that principle holds in domestic‑violence situations would give officers clearer guidance about whether they need to secure consent at all when the facts point to imminent danger.

### Inevitable discovery  

The “inevitable discovery” doctrine, born from *Nix v. Williams* (467 U.S. 431 (1984)), allows the admission of evidence that would have been found inevitably through lawful means. In Coleman, the officers’ sweep of the home produced a probable‑cause basis for a warrant, which then yielded the firearms. The panel noted that the government’s alternative contention—that exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery could have justified the entry—was unnecessary because consent was found. This is a classic “unexplored alternative” scenario: the court sidestepped a doctrinal analysis that could have clarified how inevitable discovery interacts with voluntary consent.  

If the officers had been denied consent, the inevitable‑discovery argument would have been the next logical line of defense. By never testing that path, the Seventh Circuit left lower courts without a precedent indicating whether an initial, consensual entry that later yields a warrant can salvage the evidence if consent is later deemed invalid. Some circuits have treated the two doctrines as mutually exclusive—once consent is found, the other analyses are irrelevant. Others, such as the Ninth Circuit in *United States v. Miller*, 915 F.3d 831 (9th Cir. 2019), have held that even a valid consent does not preclude an independent exigent‑circumstances review if the officer’s conduct transcends the consent’s scope. Coleman would have been an ideal vehicle to address that tension.

## Doctrinal implications  

The Seventh Circuit’s narrow focus on consent may encourage law‑enforcement agencies to prioritize obtaining a “yes” at the expense of articulating the emergency rationale. This could lead to two practical problems:

1. **Reliance on fragile consent** – In volatile domestic‑violence scenarios, victims may be under duress, confused, or fearful of retaliation. A “yes” given in the heat of a crisis may later be challenged as involuntary, jeopardizing the admissibility of critical evidence. If courts treat consent as the sole gateway, officers may feel pressured to secure it even when an exigent‑circumstances justification is clearly present, creating a risky reliance on a potentially vulnerable factual determination.

2. **Stunted development of the exigent‑circumstances doctrine** – By consistently treating consent as a substitute, appellate courts may fail to refine the standards for emergency entry in the home. The Supreme Court’s *Garrett* analysis remains fact‑intensive, and additional circuit jurisprudence is needed to apply it to the nuances of domestic‑violence calls—particularly the question of whether the threat to a third party (the children) alone satisfies the “public safety” prong.

Moreover, the decision leaves the inevitable‑discovery doctrine in a gray area. Future defendants could argue that even a brief, consensual sweep exceeded the scope of consent, and that the evidence should be excluded unless the government can independently demonstrate inevitability. Without precedent, district courts will be left to grapple with that argument on a case‑by‑case basis, potentially leading to inconsistent outcomes.

## Potential downstream effects  

Law schools and training programs will likely cite *Coleman* as a textbook example of “plain‑vanilla consent,” but practitioners should beware of the hidden assumptions. Police departments may start to embed consent‑seeking scripts into their domestic‑violence protocols, emphasizing “Do you consent to us entering?” even when the circumstances satisfy *Garrett*. While such scripts can protect officers from liability, they also risk creating a false sense of security that the consent will survive any future suppression challenge.

Litigators, on the other hand, will have a new angle: attack the voluntariness of consent in domestic‑violence calls by highlighting the inherent power imbalance and the pressure of “need” language. If successful, they can force courts to revisit the exigent‑circumstances analysis, potentially leading to a split among circuits. The Ninth Circuit, for instance, might pick up the mantle and require an independent emergency assessment whenever the police’s request hinges on child safety—a factor that, in *Coleman*, was central but never formally examined.

Finally, the decision could influence how federal prosecutors pursue firearm‑possession charges under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). If the “consent‑only” logic remains unchallenged, prosecutors may be more confident in charging felons based on evidence obtained after a consent‑based entry, even in cases where the consent may later be deemed involuntary. That could raise concerns about the balance between public‑safety goals and Fourth‑Amendment protections.

## Bottom line  

*United States v. Coleman* resolves the dispute on the narrow issue of whether Lisa Coleman consented, but it does so by deliberately sidestepping the broader Fourth‑Amendment landscape of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. The decision therefore leaves a doctrinal vacuum: courts now have an affirmative consent ruling, but no clarification on how that ruling interacts with the classic exceptions that have long guarded emergency police conduct.  

Future appellate panels will inevitably be confronted with cases where consent is ambiguous, the emergency is palpable, and the evidence could have been discovered anyway. How those courts marshal (or ignore) the exigent‑circumstances and inevitable‑discovery doctrines will shape the contours of lawful police entry into homes—not just in Wisconsin, but across the nation. Until then, practitioners should treat *Coleman* as a reminder that consent, while powerful, is not a blanket justification, and that the “need” language used by officers may still invite a deeper constitutional inquiry.
