Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Chapter 1: Factual and Procedural Background  

In order to understand the Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, a law student must first grasp who the parties are, what marks are at issue, and how the dispute moved through the trademark registration system to the appellate court. This chapter walks through those foundational facts and procedural steps, laying the groundwork for the later analysis of the Board’s likelihood‑of‑confusion determination.

## 1.1. Parties and Their Business Activities – CC Serve’s Credit‑Card Services and Apex Bank’s Banking Operations  

**CC Serve Corp.** is a company that “offers credit card services to customers.”  The record describes CC Serve’s business model as a partnership with banks: a bank issues a credit card bearing the **ASPIRE** brand, and CC Serve (or its affiliates) administers the account, handles billing, provides credit‑card counseling, and performs related transactional functions.  Because CC Serve does not itself issue banking licenses or hold deposits, its activities are limited to the **credit‑card side** of the financial relationship.  

**Apex Bank** is a Tennessee‑chartered retail bank with 18 physical branches.  Its portfolio includes personal and business checking accounts, savings accounts, mortgages, and consumer and business loans.  Notably, Apex does **not** offer any credit‑card products at the time of the dispute.  In 2019 Apex announced plans to launch an “internet bank” under a new brand, intending to market “banking and financing services” under the **ASPIRE BANK** word and design marks.  The distinction is important: Apex’s proposed services are broader (banking and financing) than CC Serve’s credit‑card services, yet both fall within the general financial services market.

Understanding these business distinctions is crucial for the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis.  The courts must assess whether ordinary consumers are likely to believe that the two sets of services—credit‑card administration versus full‑service banking—could come from a single source.

## 1.2. The ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK Marks – Registration History and Filing Dates  

- **ASPIRE (standard character mark)** – CC Serve is the owner of Registration No. 2126948 for the word “ASPIRE” in connection with credit‑card services.  The registration was issued in 1998 and carries an **effective priority date of October 17, 1996**.  This early priority gives CC Serve a strong presumptive right to the mark for the goods listed in its registration.  

- **ASPIRE BANK (word and design marks)** – In August 2019 Apex filed **intent‑to‑use (ITU) applications** with the USPTO to register both a **word mark** (“ASPIRE BANK”) and a **design mark** incorporating that wording for “banking and financing services.”  An ITU filing indicates that the applicant has a bona‑fide intention to use the mark in commerce but has not yet begun actual use.  

During examination, CC Serve filed a **letter of protest** asserting that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to cause confusion with its earlier “ASPIRE” registration.  Despite the protest, the USPTO examiner approved the marks for publication, and they appeared in the Official Gazette on **December 17, 2019**.  Publication initiates a 30‑day window during which any party may file an **opposition** to the registration.

## 1.3. Trademark Prosecution Timeline – Filing, Protest, Publication, and Opposition  

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| **Oct 17, 1996** | CC Serve obtains priority date for “ASPIRE” (credit‑card services). |
| **1998** | CC Serve’s “ASPIRE” registration issues. |
| **Aug 2019** | Apex files ITU applications for “ASPIRE BANK” (word and design). |
| **Aug 2019 (post‑filing)** | CC Serve submits a **letter of protest** to the USPTO examiner. |
| **Dec 17, 2019** | “ASPIRE BANK” marks publish in the Official Gazette. |
| **Feb 2020** | CC Serve files a **formal opposition** under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act, alleging likelihood of confusion. |
| **2020‑2021** | TTAB (Trademark Trial and Appeal Board) conducts the opposition proceedings; after briefing and evidence, the Board **sustains** the opposition and refuses registration of “ASPIRE BANK.” |
| **2022‑2023** | Apex files a **notice of appeal** to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. |

The opposition is the key procedural mechanism for a senior mark holder to challenge a junior applicant’s registration.  Under **§ 2(d) of the Lanham Act**, the TTAB must refuse registration when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.”  The Board’s decision to sustain the opposition set the stage for appellate review.

## 1.4. Oppositions Under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act – Grounds and Procedural Steps  

A § 2(d) opposition focuses exclusively on **likelihood of confusion** between the senior (prior) mark and the junior (applicant) mark.  The statutory elements require the Board to consider the **DuPont factors**, a thirteen‑factor framework developed in *In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.* (476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973)).  While the Board may consider **all** thirteen factors, it is not obligated to address those that are “not relevant or not of record.”  

Key procedural steps in a § 2(d) opposition:  

1. **Notice of Opposition** – Filed within the 30‑day opposition period after publication.  The opposer (CC Serve) must assert a likelihood‑of‑confusion claim.  
2. **Answer and Counter‑Statement** – The applicant (Apex) may file an answer denying the claim and may submit evidence (e.g., third‑party registrations).  
3. **Discovery and Evidence** – Both sides may exchange declarations, exhibit lists, and may request discovery under the TTAB’s rules.  
4. **Briefing** – Each party submits a legal brief addressing the DuPont factors and supporting evidence.  
5. **Oral Argument (optional)** – The Board may hold oral argument, though it is not required.  
6. **Decision** – The Board issues a written decision, stating which factors weigh for or against confusion and whether the opposition is sustained or dismissed.  

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve*, the Board concluded that the **second**, **sixth**, and **first** DuPont factors weighed in favor of the senior user, leading to a sustained opposition.  Apex’s appeal focuses on alleged errors in the Board’s legal analysis of those three factors.

## 1.5. Appeal to the Federal Circuit – Jurisdiction and Standard of Review  

Under **28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)**, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit has **exclusive jurisdiction** to review final decisions of the TTAB when the dispute arises under § 2(d).  This statutory grant allows a party dissatisfied with a TTAB decision to seek appellate review in a single, specialized appellate court rather than in a regional circuit.  

The Federal Circuit’s **standard of review** distinguishes between the Board’s **legal conclusions** and its **findings of fact**:  

- **Legal conclusions** (e.g., the proper application of a DuPont factor) are reviewed **de novo**.  The appellate court does not defer to the Board’s reasoning; it applies the law anew.  
- **Findings of fact** (e.g., the factual similarity of the services) are reviewed for **substantial evidence**.  The court asks whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence presented as adequate to support the Board’s conclusion.  

These standards are rooted in **M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Communications, Inc.**, 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006), which the Court cites in its opinion.  Consequently, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s factual determination that the parties’ services are highly similar (second DuPont factor) because it was supported by substantial evidence, but it vacated the Board’s analyses of the sixth and first factors, finding legal error in the way the Board scoped the relevant public and evaluated the marks’ commercial impression.

Understanding this jurisdictional framework and the bifurcated standard of review is essential for appreciating why the appellate court affirmed some portions of the TTAB decision while remanding others for further fact‑finding.

# Chapter 2: Governing Law – Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Framework and Review Standards  

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit examined the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis under the Lanham Act.  The court’s discussion provides a clear illustration of the legal concepts that govern trademark opposition disputes, the structure of the DuPont factor test, and the standards of review the appellate court applies when assessing the Board’s conclusions. This chapter unpacks those concepts for the student reader, showing how they operate in practice and why the court reached the specific procedural holdings in this case.

## 2.1. Likelihood of Confusion as a Question of Law Grounded in Factual Findings  

The Lanham Act makes clear that a refusal to register a mark under §2(d) “requires registration refusal when **confusion is likely** because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.”  *Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d at 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973).  The Supreme Court and the Federal Circuit have consistently treated the **likelihood of confusion** as a **question of law** that must be resolved by weighing a set of underlying **facts**.  See *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Communications, Inc.*, 450 F.3d at 1381 (likelihood of confusion “a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts”).  

In practice, this means that the appellate court must accept the Board’s factual determinations if they are supported by **substantial evidence**, but it must review the Board’s legal conclusions—such as the weighting of each factor—**de novo** (i.e., without deference).  The *Apex Bank* opinion explicitly restates this dual‑track approach:  

> “We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382.  

Thus, the likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry is a hybrid: the court looks first to the factual record (e.g., the services offered, the similarity of the marks, the marketplace conditions) and then applies the law (the DuPont factors and how they are weighed) to reach a legal conclusion.  

## 2.2. The Thirteen DuPont Factors – Purpose and Typical Application  

The Federal Circuit derives its analytical framework from the **DuPont factors**, a thirteen‑factor test first articulated in *In re E. I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d at 1359 (C.C.P.A. 1973).  The factors are **not a checklist**; rather, they are **considerations** that the Board (or the court) may evaluate, each of which asks a factual question.  The factors are:

1. **Similarity of the marks** (appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression).  
2. **Similarity of the goods/services**.  
3. **Strength of the senior mark**.  
4. **Proximity of the parties’ actual and intended markets**.  
5. **Evidence of actual confusion**.  
6. **Number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods**.  
7. **Marketing channels used**.  
8. **Likelihood of expansion of the product lines**.  
9. **Degree of care exercised by the purchasing public**.  
10. **Consumer sophistication**.  
11. **Presence or absence of a “classic” complexion** (no direct counterpart in modern cases).  
12. **Defendant’s intent** in adopting the mark.  
13. **Likelihood of dilution** (in newer jurisprudence, often treated separately).  

The purpose of the test is to gauge whether ordinary consumers, exercising ordinary care, are likely to **mistake the source** of the goods or services.  Because the test is fact‑driven, the Board must develop a **record** that addresses each factor it elects to consider.  An absent factor is not fatal; the Board may waive a factor if it is **irrelevant** or **unsupported by the record**.  See *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382 (the Board need not consider every factor, only those “that are relevant and of record”).

In *Apex Bank*, the Board focused on factors **2, 6, and 1**.  The court affirmed the Board’s finding on factor 2 (similarity of services), but vacated its conclusions on factors 6 and 1, finding procedural error in how the Board defined the relevant market for those factors.

## 2.3. When a TTAB Must Consider Each Factor – Relevance and Record‑Keeping Requirements  

Although the DuPont factors collectively form the analytical scaffold, the TTAB is **not required** to weigh every factor in every case.  The Board must first determine **whether a factor is “relevant”** to the particular dispute.  Relevance is judged by the **nature of the marks and the goods/services**.  For example, “likelihood of expansion” (factor 8) may be irrelevant if the parties’ offerings are static and clearly delineated.

Once a factor is deemed relevant, the Board must **build a factual record** sufficient to support its weighting.  The Federal Circuit requires **substantial evidence** of the underlying facts for each factor that the Board relies upon.  In *Apex Bank*, the court found that the Board’s factual findings on factor 2 were supported by substantial evidence (the Board’s detailed definition of “credit card services” and its reliance on dictionary definitions of “banking” and “finance”).  By contrast, the Board’s handling of factor 6 was deemed defective because it **restricted the scope of “similar goods”** to a subset (credit‑card services only) despite an earlier factual finding that the services were “highly similar” overall.  The court held that the Board’s narrower definition was **legally erroneous**, not merely a failure to produce evidence.

Thus, the TTAB’s duty is two‑fold:

1. **Identify** which factors are relevant to the particular opposition.  
2. **Create a record** that demonstrates, with substantial evidence, how each considered factor weighs for or against confusion.

If the Board either **omits a relevant factor** or **fails to support its weighting with adequate evidence**, an appellate court may vacate that portion of the decision, as occurred with factors 6 and 1 in this case.

## 2.4. Appellate Standards: De Novo Review of Legal Conclusions; Substantial Evidence for Fact Findings  

When a trademark opposition is appealed, the Federal Circuit applies a **mixed standard of review**:

* **Legal conclusions**—the Board’s interpretation of the Lanham Act, the weighting of the DuPont factors, the determination of “similarity” in the legal sense—are reviewed **de novo**.  The appellate court conducts its own independent analysis, free from deference.  See *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382 (“We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo”).  

* **Findings of fact**—such as the description of the parties’ services, the existence of third‑party marks, or evidence of actual confusion—are reviewed for **substantial evidence**.  Substantial evidence is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.”  *Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. at 229.  

In *Apex Bank*, the court applied this framework as follows:

1. **Factor 2 (Similarity of Services)** – The Board’s factual findings (the services’ definitions) were upheld because they were supported by substantial evidence.  The legal conclusion that the similarity “weighed heavily” in favor of confusion was reviewed de novo, and the court agreed.  

2. **Factor 6 (Number and Nature of Similar Marks)** – The Board’s legal conclusion that the relevant public was limited to “credit‑card consumers” was deemed a **legal error** because it misapplied the factual finding from factor 2.  Consequently, the court vacated the Board’s conclusion on factor 6 and remanded for a proper analysis.  

3. **Factor 1 (Similarity of the Marks)** – Because factor 6’s error could affect the overall commercial impression of the marks, the court also vacated the Board’s analysis of factor 1.  This demonstrates the **interconnectedness** of the factors: a mistake on one can ripple through the others, warranting a fresh review.  

The mixed standard ensures that the Board’s **factual expertise** is respected while preserving **judicial control** over the legal application of trademark law.

## 2.5. Prior Federal Circuit Precedent on the Interplay of the Second, Sixth, and First Factors  

The *Apex Bank* decision is not an isolated statement on factor interplay; it builds on a line of Federal Circuit precedent that treats the DuPont factors as a **coherent, interdependent set**.

* **Second Factor (Similarity of Goods/Services)** – The court has long recognized that similarity of the underlying offerings is a **gate‑keeping** consideration.  When the goods are deemed “highly similar,” the Board must consider the possibility of a single source, and this finding influences the analysis of other factors.  *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1381 (likelihood of confusion “a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors”).

* **Sixth Factor (Similar Marks on Similar Goods)** – The Sixth factor looks to the **crowdedness of the marketplace**.  In *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc.*, 961 F.2d at 204, the court explained that a “large number of similar marks on similar goods” shows that the senior mark is **weak** and therefore entitled to a **narrower scope** of protection.  However, the factor **does not require identical goods**; similarity is sufficient.  The *Apex Bank* court emphasized this point, holding that the Board’s limitation to “credit‑card services” was an impermissibly narrow view of similarity, given the earlier finding that the parties’ services were “partially legally identical.”

* **First Factor (Overall Commercial Impression of the Marks)** – The First factor is often the **culminating factor** because it integrates the other factors into a judgment about the **overall likelihood of confusion**.  In *In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC*, 866 F.3d at 1323, the court stressed that the commercial impression “informs” the overall similarity analysis.  Because the Sixth factor can affect the **strength or weakness** of the senior mark, an error there can alter the commercial impression, necessitating a new First‑factor analysis.  The *Apex Bank* court applied this logic, vacating the First‑factor findings on the basis that the Sixth‑factor error could change the perception of the senior mark’s strength.

Collectively, these precedents illustrate a **principle of consistency**: once the Board makes a factual finding on the similarity of the goods (factor 2), that same factual regime should inform the analysis of the marketplace’s crowdedness (factor 6) and, consequently, the overall commercial impression (factor 1).  A failure to maintain this consistency—such as narrowing the scope of “similar goods” for factor 6 while having already held the services are “highly similar” for factor 2—constitutes a legal error warranting vacatur and remand.

---

### Take‑aways for the Student  

1. **Likelihood of confusion** is a legal conclusion that rests on factual foundations; appellate courts respect factual findings if supported by substantial evidence but re‑evaluate the legal reasoning.  
2. The **DuPont factors** are a flexible, fact‑driven toolbox; the Board must consider only those factors that are **relevant and supported by the record**.  
3. **Consistency** across factors is essential. A factual determination made under one factor (e.g., high similarity of services under factor 2) must be applied uniformly when the Board examines other factors that depend on the same factual premise (e.g., the marketplace analysis under factor 6).  
4. **De novo review** applies to the Board’s legal judgments—how it weighs the factors—while **substantial‑evidence review** protects the Board’s factual findings from being overturned absent a clear evidentiary deficiency.  
5. Prior Federal Circuit cases provide a roadmap for understanding how the Second, Sixth, and First factors interrelate; *Apex Bank* reinforces that misapplying the scope of similarity in one factor can cascade into errors in the others, justifying vacatur and remand.

By mastering these principles, a law student will be equipped to read future trademark opinions with a clear sense of **what the appellate court is reviewing, how it applies the DuPont framework, and why certain findings survive while others are sent back to the TTAB**.

# Chapter 3: The Court’s Analysis of the Disputed DuPont Factors  

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit reviewed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (the “Board”) likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis and found that the Board’s treatment of three of the thirteen DuPont factors was inconsistent with controlling law. This chapter walks you through the court’s step‑by‑step reasoning on each of those factors—why the court affirmed the Board’s conclusion on the second factor, why it vacated the Board’s conclusions on the sixth and first factors, and what the court instructed the Board to do on remand. Understanding this portion of the opinion requires a grasp of (1) the evidentiary standard the court applies to the Board’s factual findings, (2) the legal content of each factor, and (3) the way the factors are interrelated in a likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis.

---  

## 3.1. Factor Two – Similarity of Goods/Services  

### 3.1.1. Board’s factual findings and definition of “credit‑card services”  

The Board began its analysis by asking whether the goods and services offered under the contested marks were “similar” within the meaning of the Lanham Act. Because “credit‑card services” no longer appears as a discrete entry in the Trademark ID Manual, the Board first reconstructed the scope of that term. Relying on industry practice and internal USPTO explanations, the Board defined “credit‑card services” to include (1) issuance of credit cards, (2) counseling about credit‑card debt, (3) processing of credit‑card transactions, (4) authorization and monitoring, (5) account management, and (6) provision of credit scores. This definition appears at **J.A. 25** of the opinion.

### 3.1.2. Legal standard for similarity of services under the Lanham Act  

Similarity does not require identity. The Federal Circuit has held that the “services need not be identical—the evidence need only establish that the respective products are related in some manner … or that the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source” (*Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, 668 F.3d at 1369). The key question is whether the average consumer, viewing the marks in the relevant commercial context, would think the same company provides the services.

### 3.1.3. Court’s affirmation: substantial‑evidence support  

The court examined the Board’s factual record. The Board had identified that “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” are defined in dictionaries to encompass extending credit, a function that overlaps with the issuance of credit cards. Moreover, the Board cited third‑party registrations that covered both credit‑card and banking services, showing that the USPTO itself treats those services as potentially co‑offered under a single mark. The appellate court concluded that this record provided **substantial evidence**—the standard defined by *Consol. Edison* as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Because the Board’s factual findings were supported by expert testimony and documentary evidence, the court held that the Board’s conclusion that the services are “highly similar” satisfies the de novo legal standard for factor two. Consequently, the court **affirmed** the Board’s finding on this factor.  

---  

## 3.2. Factor Six – Number and Nature of Similar Marks on Similar Goods  

### 3.2.1. Board’s limited scope to credit‑card marks only  

For the sixth factor, the Board counted only those third‑party marks that used “Aspire” (or a variant) **in connection with credit‑card services**. The Board identified nine such marks and held that this number was insufficient to render the mark “weak” or to show that consumers were accustomed to distinguishing among similar marks. The Board deliberately excluded the remaining 42 registrations that paired “Aspire” with other financial services—such as banking, loans, or financing—labeling them “essentially irrelevant” because they were not tied to “credit‑card services.” This limitation is discussed at **J.A. 47‑51**.  

### 3.2.2. The requirement to consider all marks used on *similar* goods, not just identical ones  

The Federal Circuit’s precedent makes clear that the sixth factor looks at the **breadth of use of similar marks on similar goods or services**. The court has repeatedly emphasized that “similar” does not equal “identical.” See *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc.*, 961 F.2d at 204 (recognizing that the factor applies where the goods are merely related). Moreover, the factor is intended to gauge the **commercial strength** of the senior mark; a crowded field of similar marks can signal weakness, whereas a sparsely populated field may suggest stronger protection.  

In *Apex Bank*, the appellate court observed that the Board had already concluded, under factor two, that the parties’ services are “highly similar” and even “partially legally identical.” Given that finding, the same scope of similarity must carry over to the sixth factor. By restricting the analysis to credit‑card services alone, the Board applied a narrower definition of similarity than it did in its own earlier analysis—a procedural inconsistency that the court deemed a legal error.  

### 3.2.3. Court’s finding of legal error and the need for a broader analysis  

The court held that the Board’s failure to consider the 42 additional “Aspire” marks—used for banking and financing services—contravened the requirement to evaluate **all similar marks on similar goods**. Because the services are already deemed similar, the relevant public is not limited to “credit‑card consumers” but includes the broader group of consumers who purchase banking and financing services. The court therefore **vacated** the Board’s sixth‑factor finding and **remanded** for a new analysis that (1) adopts the broader scope of services identified in factor two, and (2) assesses whether the totality of “Aspire” marks on those services creates a “crowded field” sufficient to show weakness of the senior mark.  

---  

## 3.3. Factor One – Overall Commercial Impression of the Marks  

### 3.3.1. Interdependence of factor six and factor one via commercial strength/weakness  

Factor one evaluates the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” While this factor is primarily a visual‑and‑aural comparison, the court has recognized that a mark’s **commercial strength or weakness**—often derived from factor six—feeds into the overall impression. A weak mark is more likely to be deemed “generic” or “descriptive,” which can diminish its commercial impression and increase the probability of confusion. In *In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC*, 866 F.3d at 1323, the court explained that the commercial impression analysis must consider the mark’s overall market presence.  

### 3.3.2. Why vacating factor six necessitates vacating factor one  

Because the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis was legally flawed, the commercial strength determination that underlies the first‑factor analysis is unreliable. The court reasoned that any reassessment of the number and nature of similar marks could shift the perception of the senior mark’s strength, thereby altering the “commercial impression” of both marks. As a result, the court could not safely leave the first‑factor finding untouched. Accordingly, the opinion states: “Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here” (**J.A. 7**).  

### 3.3.3. Directions for remand – reconsideration in light of a revised sixth‑factor analysis  

The court instructed the Board to redo its first‑factor analysis after completing a proper sixth‑factor evaluation. The Board must now (1) re‑examine the appearance, sound, and connotation of “ASPIRE” versus “ASPIRE BANK,” (2) consider whether the broader field of “Aspire” marks on banking and financing services influences the overall commercial impression, and (3) integrate any revised finding on commercial strength into its assessment of likelihood of confusion. The remand instructions are succinct: “We thus vacate the Board’s findings with respect to DuPont factors six and one and remand for the Board to consider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods and the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks in light of its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.”  

---  

### Summary of the Court’s Step‑by‑Step Evaluation  

1. **Factor Two (Similarity of Services)** – The Board’s factual findings were well‑supported; the court applied the de novo legal standard and affirmed the Board’s conclusion.  
2. **Factor Six (Number/Nature of Similar Marks)** – The Board’s analysis was limited to a subset of marks inconsistent with its own earlier finding of service similarity. The court identified a legal error, vacated the finding, and remanded for a broader, correct analysis.  
3. **Factor One (Overall Commercial Impression)** – Because the commercial‑strength assessment depends on factor six, the court vacated the first‑factor finding and required the Board to reconsider it after the sixth‑factor analysis is corrected.  

By structuring its review in this way, the Federal Circuit preserved the Board’s well‑founded conclusions where the evidentiary record was adequate, while ensuring that the remaining factors were evaluated in a coherent and legally consistent manner. This approach reflects the appellate court’s dual mandate: to respect the Board’s expertise when supported by substantial evidence, and to correct procedural or legal misapplications that could distort the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion determination.

# Chapter 4: Result, Remand Instructions, and Broader Implications  

The Federal Circuit’s opinion in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.** ends the appellate review with a mixed disposition, directs the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) to revisit key portions of its likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis, and offers guidance that will shape how future parties structure oppositions and appeals. This chapter walks a law‑student through each component of the court’s final order, explains the procedural effect of the instructions, and highlights the policy rationales that underlie the decision.

## 4.1. Final Holding – affirmation in part, vacatur in part, and remand  

The court’s ultimate judgment is a classic “affirm‑in‑part, vacate‑in‑part, and remand” order.  

1. **Affirmed** – The court upheld the TTAB’s determination that the **second DuPont factor** (similarity of the parties’ goods and services) weighed in favor of a likelihood of confusion. The appellate panel found that the Board’s factual findings—supported by substantial evidence—correctly concluded that credit‑card services and “banking and financing services” are highly similar, if not partially legally identical.  

2. **Vacated** – The court struck down the Board’s conclusions on the **sixth** and **first** DuPont factors. The sixth factor (number and nature of similar marks on similar goods) was vacated because the Board erroneously limited its analysis to marks used only in the “credit‑card services” niche, ignoring the broader pool of Aspire‑related marks that exist in the wider banking and financing market. The first factor (overall similarity of the marks) was vacated as a logical corollary: a change in the sixth‑factor analysis could alter the assessment of the mark’s commercial strength, which in turn affects the overall commercial impression examined in the first factor.  

3. **Remand** – The opinion directs the TTAB to redo its analysis of the sixth and first factors in light of the affirmed second‑factor finding. The Board must consider all third‑party Aspire‑formative marks that are used on **any goods or services that are similar** to the parties’ offerings, not just those confined to credit‑card services.  

The court’s holding therefore leaves the opposition **still in effect** (the TTAB’s overall refusal to register Apex’s marks remains), but it requires a more comprehensive factual inquiry before a final determination of likelihood of confusion can be made.

## 4.2. Specific Instructions to the TTAB – broadened third‑party mark pool, unified similarity scope, and renewed first‑factor assessment  

The remand instructions are the practical core of the opinion. They oblige the TTAB to undertake three distinct, interrelated tasks:

### 4.2.1. Expand the universe of “similar marks” examined under the sixth factor  

* **What the court demands** – The Board must collect and evaluate **all** third‑party registrations that employ the term “Aspire” (or a derivative) in connection with **any** financial‑service category that is *similar* to the services identified in the second factor (i.e., credit‑card services, banking, financing, and related financial products). The court rejected the Board’s earlier limitation to marks used solely for credit‑card services.  

* **Why this matters** – The sixth factor measures the “strength” of a mark by looking at market crowding. When a mark co‑exists with many similar marks across a broad sector, the mark is viewed as weaker and its scope of protection narrows. By widening the pool, the TTAB may find that Aspire is a relatively weak mark, which could tip the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis in Apex’s favor.  

* **How to implement** – The Board should:  
  1. Search USPTO databases for all “Aspire” or “Aspire‑*” registrations in the International Class 36 (financial services) and any related classes.  
  2. Filter for marks that are used on **goods or services that the court has already deemed highly similar** (see the second‑factor analysis).  
  3. Compile the number of distinct marks, their dates of first use, and evidence of market presence (e.g., advertising, consumer surveys).  

### 4.2.2. Apply a **unified scope of similarity** across the second, sixth, and first factors  

* **What the court demands** – The Board must not adopt a narrower definition of “similar goods/services” for the sixth factor than it used for the second factor. The appellate panel emphasized that the factual finding of “high similarity” in the second factor is controlling and should guide the similarity inquiry for all other factors.  

* **Why this matters** – Consistency prevents the Board from “double‑counting” similarity (once in the second factor, then again by artificially narrowing the field for the sixth factor). A uniform scope ensures that the same market context underlies each factor, producing a coherent overall analysis.  

* **How to implement** – When evaluating third‑party marks for the sixth factor, the Board should base its similarity assessment on the same criteria it used in the second factor—namely, that credit‑card services and banking/financing services are related and can be offered by a single source. Any mark used in a context that falls within this broader definition qualifies for inclusion.

### 4.2.3. Re‑evaluate the first factor in light of the revised sixth‑factor findings  

* **What the court demands** – After the sixth factor is reconsidered, the Board must return to the **first factor** (overall similarity of the marks). The first factor looks at appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. Because the sixth factor feeds into the mark’s perceived strength, a change in the sixth‑factor outcome could alter the “commercial impression” component of the first factor.  

* **Why this matters** – If the sixth factor demonstrates that Aspire is a weak mark surrounded by many similar marks, the commercial impression of “ASPIRE” may be less distinctive, reducing the likelihood that consumers will associate Apex’s “ASPIRE BANK” with CC Serve’s “ASPIRE.” Conversely, if the sixth factor confirms a strong, unique mark, the first factor may weigh more heavily toward confusion.  

* **How to implement** – The Board should:  
  1. Re‑assess visual, phonetic, and conceptual similarity between “ASPIRE BANK” and the pre‑existing “ASPIRE” standard character mark.  
  2. Incorporate any new evidence on consumer perception that arises from the broader third‑party mark analysis (e.g., survey data showing consumers treat “Aspire” as a generic financial descriptor).  
  3. Balance the revised commercial‑strength analysis against the other DuPont factors to reach a final likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion.

## 4.3. Cost award and procedural consequences  

The opinion awards **costs to the appellant** (Apex Bank). In the context of a TTAB appeal, “costs” typically cover filing fees, transcript fees, and any reasonable expenses incurred in preparing the appeal. The practical effect is twofold:

1. **Financial incentive** – Apex recoups part of the expense of litigating the appeal, which may encourage parties to bring appeals when they have viable arguments about procedural or evidentiary errors.  

2. **Procedural posture** – The cost award does not affect the substantive remand. The TTAB must still issue a new decision after completing the revised factor analyses. If the Board ultimately sustains the opposition, the costs award stands; if it reverses, the parties may be entitled to a refund of the costs.

## 4.4. Policy considerations – balancing mark strength with market crowding  

Beyond the mechanical instructions, the court’s reasoning reflects broader trademark policy goals:

* **Protecting consumer confusion** – The core purpose of the Lanham Act’s likelihood‑of‑confusion test is to prevent consumers from being misled about the source of goods or services. By insisting that the Board apply a consistent similarity scope across all factors, the court reinforces a consumer‑centric analysis.  

* **Recognizing weak marks in crowded fields** – The opinion underscores that a mark’s **commercial strength** is not static; it can be diluted when many similar marks coexist in the same market. This aligns with precedent (e.g., *Jack Wolfskin*, *Juice Generation*) that courts will narrow the protective scope of a mark that is “weak” because of market saturation.  

* **Encouraging thorough factual development** – The vacatur of the sixth factor signals that the TTAB must conduct a robust evidentiary record, not a perfunctory check of a narrow subset of marks. This promotes diligence on both the opposing party and the Board, reducing the likelihood of reversible errors.  

* **Balancing federal‑circuit deference with de‑novo review** – Although the court reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, it still respects the Board’s factual findings where supported by substantial evidence. The opinion threads the needle between deference to the TTAB’s expertise and the appellate court’s duty to ensure correct application of the law.

## 4.5. Practical takeaways for practitioners handling oppositions and appeals  

1. **Document the entire market landscape early.** When filing an opposition or a protest, compile a comprehensive list of third‑party marks that could be relevant under any DuPont factor. This pre‑emptive research can later shield you from claims that the Board “failed to consider” certain marks.  

2. **Tailor arguments to each factor’s evidentiary requirements.**  
   * For the **second factor**, focus on the functional relationship between the services (e.g., both involve extending credit).  
   * For the **sixth factor**, supply quantitative data on the number of similar marks and qualitative evidence of consumer awareness.  
   * For the **first factor**, prepare side‑by‑side visual and phonetic comparisons, and consider consumer surveys that probe commercial impression.  

3. **Anticipate the Board’s “scope of similarity” analysis.** The *Apex Bank* decision makes clear that once the Board defines the services as “highly similar,” that definition must flow through the entire likelihood‑of‑confusion matrix. Argue for a broad definition early, and be prepared to defend it if the Board attempts to narrow it on a later factor.  

4. **Be ready to address “crowded field” arguments.** If you are the senior mark holder, collect evidence of distinctiveness (e.g., advertising spend, consumer recognition studies) to counter a claim that your mark is weak due to market saturation.  

5. **Understand the cost implications.** A successful appeal can yield a costs award, but the award is limited to “reasonable expenses.” Keep meticulous records of filing fees, transcript requests, and expert fees to maximize recoverable costs.  

6. **Monitor TTAB remand orders closely.** The TTAB’s new decision will be bound by the appellate court’s clarified legal framework. If the Board again errs—especially on the sixth factor—further appellate review may be available, but each subsequent appeal will be scrutinized more tightly for procedural propriety.  

By internalizing these lessons, practitioners can craft more persuasive oppositions, anticipate appellate pitfalls, and better navigate the interplay between the TTAB’s fact‑finding role and the Federal Circuit’s de novo legal review. The *Apex Bank* opinion thus serves as a roadmap for both the analytical rigor required in likelihood‑of‑confusion cases and the strategic considerations that shape successful trademark litigation.
