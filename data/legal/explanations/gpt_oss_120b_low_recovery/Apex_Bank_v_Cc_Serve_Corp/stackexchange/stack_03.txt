Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How does the removal of “credit card services” from the USPTO ID Manual affect the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis?
Question:
The Board noted that the ID Manual no longer lists “credit card services” and therefore defined the term itself. Does the absence of a term from the manual make a difference in interpreting a filing’s description of services, or is the Board free to adopt its own definition? How binding is the ID Manual in this context?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit makes clear that the Trademark ID Manual is **not a binding source of law** for the Board’s likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis.  It is a “useful, but not controlling, reference” that the Board may consult when it must give meaning to a description of goods or services.  When a term that once appeared in the Manual has been deleted, the Board is free to **define the term itself** so long as the definition is supported by the record (e.g., dictionary definitions, the applicant’s own wording, or other evidence).  The opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, 2025 WL … (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25 2025) illustrates exactly how this works.

### What the court says about the ID Manual

1. **The Manual’s status** – The opinion notes that the Board “first determined the meaning of the identified services” after observing that the entry for “credit‑card services” had been removed from the ID Manual (¶ III.A).  The court does **not** treat the Manual’s omission as a dispositive ruling on the scope of the services; instead, it treats the Board’s own definition as a factual finding subject to the usual “substantial‑evidence” standard.

2. **Board’s authority to define** – The court affirms the Board’s approach on the *second* DuPont factor (goods similarity).  It states that the Board “carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services” and that “substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.”  This shows that the Board’s independent definition of “credit‑card services” is permissible so long as it is grounded in the record.

3. **Binding effect** – Because the court reviews the Board’s legal conclusions **de novo** but its factual findings **for substantial evidence**, the Manual’s absence does not itself bind the Board.  The Manual is a secondary source; the controlling standard is the Lanham Act and the DuPont factors, not the Manual’s listings.

### How this affects the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis

- **Second DuPont factor (goods/services similarity):** The Board may define “credit‑card services” in a way that captures the activities it believes are comparable to “banking and financing services.”  The Federal Circuit upheld that definition, emphasizing that “the services need not be identical” and that the Board’s factual finding was supported by the record.  The Manual’s silence on the term did **not** limit the Board.

- **Sixth DuPont factor (number and nature of similar marks):** The court vacated the Board’s finding because the Board **unduly narrowed** the relevant market to “credit‑card services” only, ignoring other marks used for broader banking/financing services.  This error was unrelated to the Manual’s omission; it stemmed from an inconsistent application of the similarity analysis already performed under the second factor.  The opinion therefore underscores that the Board must keep its definition of the services **consistent** across all factors.

- **First DuPont factor (overall commercial impression):** Because the sixth factor was flawed, the court also remanded the first‑factor analysis.  Again, the issue was not the Manual but the Board’s failure to apply a uniform definition of the services when evaluating the marks’ commercial impression.

### Practical take‑away for practitioners

1. **Do not rely on the ID Manual as a “hard rule.”**  Its listings are helpful for drafting accurate descriptions, but if a term is removed, the applicant (or the Board) must supply a definition backed by evidence.

2. **Ensure consistency.**  Once the Board adopts a definition of the goods or services for one DuPont factor, that same definition must be used for the other factors, unless a new factual finding justifies a different scope.

3. **Be prepared to support the definition.**  Provide dictionary citations, industry usage, or the applicant’s own description in the record.  The court will look for “substantial evidence” that the definition is appropriate.

In short, the removal of “credit‑card services” from the Trademark ID Manual does **not** limit the Board’s ability to define the term for its analysis, and the Manual is not binding on the Board.  The controlling requirement is that any definition the Board adopts be supported by the record and applied consistently across the relevant DuPont factors.
