Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why does the Federal Circuit review the TTAB’s legal conclusions de novo but factual findings for substantial evidence?
Question:
The opinion says “We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” I’m confused about the distinction. When does an appellate court apply de novo review to a TTAB decision, and what exactly qualifies as a “finding of fact” that gets the substantial‑evidence standard? Also, how does this affect the weight given to the Board’s analysis of the DuPont factors?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit’s review scheme for TTAB decisions is set out in the opinion you quoted:

> “We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.”  *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc.*, 450 F.3d at 1382.

The opinion in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.** applies that scheme to the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis, which is organized around the thirteen *DuPont* factors. The Court treats each factor differently depending on whether the Board’s determination is legal or factual.

---

### 1. What gets **de novo** review?

A “legal conclusion” is a determination that the law, when applied to a set of facts, requires a particular result. The Court repeatedly calls the **overall likelihood‑of‑confusion test** a question of law:

> “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.”  *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1381.

Thus, once the underlying facts are fixed, the Board’s **legal judgment** about whether those facts satisfy the “likelihood‑of‑confusion” threshold is reviewed **de novo**. In the Apex case the Court does exactly that when it says it will **affirm** the Board’s legal analysis of the second DuPont factor because the Board “did not err in its legal analysis” (¶ III.A).

**Take‑away:** Whenever the TTAB is deciding *how* the law should be applied—e.g., whether a particular factor “weighs heavily” in favor of confusion, or whether a mark is “inherently distinctive”—the appellate court does not give deference; it re‑examines the issue from scratch.

---

### 2. What is a “finding of fact” that receives the **substantial‑evidence** standard?

A “finding of fact” is a concrete determination about what the record shows. The Court defines the standard:

> “Substantial evidence is ‘such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.’”  *Consol. Edison*, 305 U.S. at 229.

In the TTAB context, the **DuPont factors that are purely factual** are those that require the Board to *ascertain* empirical matters—e.g., the similarity of the goods, the actual number of third‑party marks, the channels of trade, etc. The opinion explicitly calls each DuPont factor a “question of fact” for the purpose of the substantial‑evidence test:

> “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.”  *Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods.*, 293 F.3d at 1370.

Consequently:

| DuPont factor | Nature of the Board’s finding | Review standard |
|---------------|------------------------------|-----------------|
| **2 (Similarity of goods/services)** | Determination that “credit‑card services” and “banking and financing services” are “legally identical, in part.” | **Substantial evidence** (the Court affirmed because the Board’s factual support was adequate). |
| **6 (Number and nature of similar marks)** | Determination of how many “Aspire‑formative” marks exist in the relevant field and whether that number shows weakness. | **Substantial evidence** (the Court vacated because the Board’s factual record was too narrow). |
| **1 (Similarity of the marks themselves)** | Assessment of appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression. | **Substantial evidence** (vacated and remanded for reconsideration). |

The Court treats the *second* factor as a factual finding (similarity of services) and therefore applies the “substantial‑evidence” test. The *first* and *sixth* factors, while they involve legal weighting, are rooted in factual determinations (the commercial impression of the marks, and the count of third‑party uses). Those factual underpinnings are examined under the substantial‑evidence standard.

---

### 3. How does this affect the Board’s analysis of the DuPont factors?

Because **legal conclusions are reviewed de novo**, the appellate court can freely re‑weight the factors or reach a different overall conclusion about likelihood of confusion if it believes the law requires it. However, **factual findings are insulated unless the record fails to contain substantial evidence**.

In Apex:

* The Court **affirmed** the Board’s factual finding on factor 2 because the record (dictionary definitions, third‑party registrations) met the substantial‑evidence threshold.
* The Court **vacated** the Board’s factual finding on factor 6 because the Board’s factual record was limited to “credit‑card” marks and ignored other banking‑related “Aspire” marks, a limitation the Court found unsupported by the record.
* The Court also **vacated** the Board’s factor 1 analysis because the commercial impression (a legal conclusion) depends on the factual context of factor 6; changing the factual finding on factor 6 may alter the legal assessment of the mark’s strength.

Thus, the de novo review of the **legal conclusion** (the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion test) allows the Court to say, “Even if the Board’s factual findings were correct, we would still weigh the factors differently.” But the Court can only overturn a factual finding when the **record does not contain substantial evidence**—as it did for factor 6.

---

### 4. Practical rule for future appeals

1. **Identify whether the Board’s determination is legal or factual.**  
   *Legal*: how the law is applied to the facts (e.g., “the factor weighs heavily in favor of confusion”). → **de novo**.  
   *Factual*: what the record shows (e.g., “there are nine similar marks in the credit‑card market”). → **substantial‑evidence**.

2. **Check the record.** If the record contains enough evidence that a reasonable mind could accept, the factual finding stands; otherwise, the appellate court may vacate and remand.

3. **Remember the interaction.** Even a factual finding that survives the substantial‑evidence test can be re‑interpreted in the de novo legal step, because the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion determination is a legal conclusion.

---

**Bottom line:** The Federal Circuit reviews TTAB **legal conclusions (including the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion) de novo**, but it requires **substantial evidence** to uphold the Board’s **factual findings**. In *Apex Bank*, the Court applied that distinction to each DuPont factor, affirming the factual similarity of the services (factor 2) while vacating the factual underpinnings of the sixth and first factors because the Board’s record did not meet the substantial‑evidence threshold. This framework determines how much weight an appellate court gives to the Board’s analysis on any given factor.

### What is the proper scope of the sixth DuPont factor when the parties’ services are found to be highly similar?
Question:
The court vacated the Board’s analysis of the sixth factor because it limited the “relevant public” to credit‑card consumers and ignored other Aspire marks used for banking/financing services. Should the sixth factor always consider all similar marks on any similar goods, even if the goods aren’t identical? How does the earlier finding on the second factor (goods similarity) constrain the analysis of the sixth factor?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Sixth DuPont factor‑‑*the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods*‑‑must be evaluated with the same “similar‑goods” scope that the Board used for the Second factor. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit held that once the Board has found, on the record, that the parties’ services are “highly similar” (indeed, “partially legally identical”) under the Second factor, it cannot later shrink that definition when it comes to the Sixth factor. The Sixth factor does **not** require identical goods; it only requires that the third‑party marks be used on *goods or services that are similar* to the goods or services at issue. (See § II‑B of the opinion.)

The opinion explains the error succinctly:

> “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar … the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the Sixth DuPont factor.”  (Fed. Cir. 2025).

Consequently, the Board’s restriction of the “relevant public” to *only* credit‑card consumers – and its exclusion of the 42 other “Aspire” marks that appear on banking and financing services – was improper. Because the Second factor analysis concluded that “credit card services” and “banking and financing services” overlap enough to be considered the same class of services, the Sixth factor must consider **all** third‑party “Aspire” marks that are used on *any* financial‑services marks that are *similar* to the parties’ services, not merely those limited to credit‑card services.

In practice, this means:

1. **Identify the factual similarity of the parties’ services** (as the Board already did: highly similar/partially identical).  
2. **Apply that similarity definition to the Sixth factor**: any third‑party “Aspire” mark used on *banking, financing, credit‑card, loan, or other financial‑service* offerings is relevant.  
3. **Count and evaluate the nature of those marks** (e.g., strength, ubiquity, consumer awareness) to determine whether the “Aspire” mark is weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection.

Thus, the Sixth factor does *not* demand identical goods, but it does require that the goods/services be within the same *similar* category established by the Second factor. The Court’s decision vacates the Board’s narrow analysis and remands for a reconsideration that includes the full universe of similar‑service marks.

### How does the removal of “credit card services” from the USPTO ID Manual affect the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis?
Question:
The Board noted that the ID Manual no longer lists “credit card services” and therefore defined the term itself. Does the absence of a term from the manual make a difference in interpreting a filing’s description of services, or is the Board free to adopt its own definition? How binding is the ID Manual in this context?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit makes clear that the Trademark ID Manual is **not a binding source of law** for the Board’s likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis.  It is a “useful, but not controlling, reference” that the Board may consult when it must give meaning to a description of goods or services.  When a term that once appeared in the Manual has been deleted, the Board is free to **define the term itself** so long as the definition is supported by the record (e.g., dictionary definitions, the applicant’s own wording, or other evidence).  The opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, 2025 WL … (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25 2025) illustrates exactly how this works.

### What the court says about the ID Manual

1. **The Manual’s status** – The opinion notes that the Board “first determined the meaning of the identified services” after observing that the entry for “credit‑card services” had been removed from the ID Manual (¶ III.A).  The court does **not** treat the Manual’s omission as a dispositive ruling on the scope of the services; instead, it treats the Board’s own definition as a factual finding subject to the usual “substantial‑evidence” standard.

2. **Board’s authority to define** – The court affirms the Board’s approach on the *second* DuPont factor (goods similarity).  It states that the Board “carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services” and that “substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.”  This shows that the Board’s independent definition of “credit‑card services” is permissible so long as it is grounded in the record.

3. **Binding effect** – Because the court reviews the Board’s legal conclusions **de novo** but its factual findings **for substantial evidence**, the Manual’s absence does not itself bind the Board.  The Manual is a secondary source; the controlling standard is the Lanham Act and the DuPont factors, not the Manual’s listings.

### How this affects the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis

- **Second DuPont factor (goods/services similarity):** The Board may define “credit‑card services” in a way that captures the activities it believes are comparable to “banking and financing services.”  The Federal Circuit upheld that definition, emphasizing that “the services need not be identical” and that the Board’s factual finding was supported by the record.  The Manual’s silence on the term did **not** limit the Board.

- **Sixth DuPont factor (number and nature of similar marks):** The court vacated the Board’s finding because the Board **unduly narrowed** the relevant market to “credit‑card services” only, ignoring other marks used for broader banking/financing services.  This error was unrelated to the Manual’s omission; it stemmed from an inconsistent application of the similarity analysis already performed under the second factor.  The opinion therefore underscores that the Board must keep its definition of the services **consistent** across all factors.

- **First DuPont factor (overall commercial impression):** Because the sixth factor was flawed, the court also remanded the first‑factor analysis.  Again, the issue was not the Manual but the Board’s failure to apply a uniform definition of the services when evaluating the marks’ commercial impression.

### Practical take‑away for practitioners

1. **Do not rely on the ID Manual as a “hard rule.”**  Its listings are helpful for drafting accurate descriptions, but if a term is removed, the applicant (or the Board) must supply a definition backed by evidence.

2. **Ensure consistency.**  Once the Board adopts a definition of the goods or services for one DuPont factor, that same definition must be used for the other factors, unless a new factual finding justifies a different scope.

3. **Be prepared to support the definition.**  Provide dictionary citations, industry usage, or the applicant’s own description in the record.  The court will look for “substantial evidence” that the definition is appropriate.

In short, the removal of “credit‑card services” from the Trademark ID Manual does **not** limit the Board’s ability to define the term for its analysis, and the Manual is not binding on the Board.  The controlling requirement is that any definition the Board adopts be supported by the record and applied consistently across the relevant DuPont factors.

### When can the TTAB ignore third‑party marks that are not on the identical goods in the sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
In this case, the Board treated only Aspire marks used for credit‑card services as relevant and dismissed the other 42 marks used for broader financial services. Is there precedent allowing the Board to narrow the universe of comparable marks, or must it consider any similar mark on any similar service once the underlying goods are deemed “highly similar”?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, 2025 WL 512, 2025 Fed. Cir. ## (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25 2025) makes clear that the TTAB may **not** “narrow” the pool of third‑party marks it looks at under the sixth DuPont factor simply because those marks are used on goods or services that are not the exact same *label* as the plaintiff’s.  

### What the opinion says

1. **Sixth factor definition** – The court restates the classic formulation: “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” 476 F.2d at 1361.  The key phrase is *similar* goods, not *identical* goods.

2. **Error in the Board’s analysis** – The Board first found, under the **second** DuPont factor, that CC Serve’s “credit‑card services” and Apex’s “banking and financing services” are “highly similar” and even “partially legally identical.”  J.A. 27‑28.  Yet when it turned to the sixth factor it limited its comparison to only those third‑party “Aspire” marks that were also used for credit‑card services, calling marks used for other banking and financing services “essentially irrelevant.”  J.A. 47.

3. **Court’s holding** – The Federal Circuit held that this limitation was a legal error.  It emphasizes that once the Board has **factually determined** that the parties’ services are “highly similar,” the same factual footing must be applied to *all* other DuPont factors.  The Court says:

   > “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar… the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.”  J.A. 7‑8.

4. **Result** – The sixth‑factor finding is vacated and the matter remanded for the Board to consider **all** third‑party “Aspire” marks that are used on *any* goods or services that are similar to the “highly similar” services identified in the second factor (i.e., not just credit‑card services).  The opinion also vacates the first‑factor analysis because the scope of the sixth factor can affect the commercial impression of the marks.

### Practical takeaway

- **Scope of “similar goods”**: The TTAB must look at third‑party marks used on any goods or services that are *similarly* related to the plaintiff’s goods, once similarity has been established. The services need not be identical; they only need to be in the same “field” as defined by the second factor.

- **No “narrowing” rule**: There is no precedent authorizing the Board to narrow the universe of comparable third‑party marks to a subset of goods (e.g., only credit‑card services) when the factual record already shows broader similarity. The *Apex* decision expressly rejects such a narrowing.

- **What to do on remand**: The Board must recalculate the sixth factor by counting **all** Aspire‑formative marks that appear on *any* financial‑services marks that are comparable to the “highly similar” services (credit‑card, banking, financing, etc.). Whether that number reaches the “considerable/ubiquitous” threshold will determine whether the mark is deemed weak and therefore entitled to a narrower scope of protection.

Thus, the TTAB cannot ignore third‑party marks merely because those marks are used on a broader category of financial services; once the second factor finds the services “highly similar,” the sixth factor must consider *all* similar‑service marks. The *Apex Bank* opinion is binding authority on this point.

### Why did the court vacate the first DuPont factor analysis along with the sixth factor?
Question:
The opinion says the first factor must be reconsidered because the sixth factor’s remand could change the commercial impression of the marks. How does the analysis of the number and nature of similar marks (sixth factor) influence the overall commercial impression (first factor)? Is this a common practice to vacate multiple factors together?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit vacated the Trademark Board’s rulings on both the sixth and the first DuPont factors because the two analyses are not independent – the “commercial impression” that the first factor measures is partly derived from the evidence the Board considered under the sixth factor.  

### 1.  What the sixth factor does

The sixth factor asks whether *the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods* suggest that the senior mark is weak or strong.  If many “Aspire”‑type marks already exist in the same market, a consumer has been “educated” to distinguish among them, and the senior mark is deemed weaker and entitled to a narrower scope of protection.  The court explains this in **DuPont**, 476 F.2d at 1361, and again in **Palm Bay Imps.**, 396 F.3d at 1369 and **Juice Generation**, 794 F.3d at 1338.

In this case the Board limited its search to “Aspire” marks that were used **only** for credit‑card services, even though the Board had already found (under the second factor) that Apex’s “banking and financing” services are *highly similar*—indeed “partially legally identical”—to CC Serve’s credit‑card services.  Because the second‑factor finding makes the two categories of services interchangeable for the purposes of confusion analysis, the same breadth must apply when counting third‑party “Aspire” marks under the sixth factor.  The court therefore held that the Board’s restriction was an error and vacated the sixth‑factor finding so that the Board can consider **all** Aspire‑formative marks used on *any* similar financial‑services goods, not just those limited to credit cards.

### 2.  Why the first factor must be revisited

The first DuPont factor looks at the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and **commercial impression**.”  See **DuPont**, 476 F.2d at 1361; **In re I.AM.Symbolic**, 866 F.3d at 1323.  The “commercial impression” is not a static characteristic of the mark; it is shaped by the *strength* of the mark in the relevant market.  A mark that is shown to be weak (because many similar marks exist) will create a weaker commercial impression, and a consumer will be less likely to assume a common source.

Because the sixth‑factor inquiry determines whether the senior mark is strong or weak, any change in that inquiry can change the commercial impression that the first factor requires the Board to assess.  The court states this directly:

> “Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here **because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression**.”  (**Apex Bank v. CC Serve**, 2025‑FCA‑2143 at § III.C).

In other words, if the Board on remand concludes that there are many “Aspire” marks on similar financial services, it may find the CC Serve mark to be weaker than the Board currently thinks.  That weakened commercial strength would diminish the “overall commercial impression” of the mark, which in turn could tip the balance of the first‑factor analysis toward Apex.  The court therefore vacated the first‑factor finding as a *by‑product* of the sixth‑factor error.

### 3.  Is it common to vacate multiple factors together?

Yes.  The Federal Circuit has repeatedly held that when a mistake on one DuPont factor *necessarily* impacts the factual underpinnings of another factor, the board’s rulings on both must be vacated.  The decision cites **M2 Software**, 450 F.3d at 1381 (the Board’s legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, but its factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence).  If a factual finding—here, the scope of “similar goods” used to count third‑party marks—is erroneous, any subsequent factor that depends on that factual foundation (the commercial impression under the first factor) is likewise tainted.  The court’s approach is therefore consistent with precedent: it vacates the erroneous factor **and** any other factor whose analysis is based on the same faulty factual premise.

### 4.  Bottom line for the parties

* **Sixth factor:** The Board must redo its tally of third‑party “Aspire” marks, counting all marks used on goods and services that are *similar* to the parties’ services (i.e., the broader “banking and financing” category), not just those limited to credit‑card services.

* **First factor:** After the sixth‑factor remand, the Board must reassess the overall commercial impression of the “ASPIRE” mark in light of whatever strength or weakness the new sixth‑factor analysis reveals.

Thus, the vacatur of both factors is not a procedural shortcut; it is required because the sixth‑factor error could change the commercial‑strength analysis that drives the first‑factor conclusion.

### What standard does the court apply to determine whether services are “legally identical, in part” under the second DuPont factor?
Question:
The Board concluded that banking/finance services “legally” include issuing credit cards, and the appellate court affirmed that finding. What legal authority or test governs this “legal identity” analysis, and how does it differ from a purely factual similarity assessment?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit holds that the “legal‑identity” inquiry in the second DuPont factor is a **question of law**, not a factual comparison of how the services are marketed or who actually performs them.  

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the court explains that the Board’s determination that credit‑card services and “banking and financing services” are *“legally identical, in part”* rests on **dictionary definitions and the express language of the Lanham Act’s service classifications**, not on a comparison of the parties’ business practices.  The opinion states:

> “The Board determined that ‘credit card services’ … includes … issuing credit cards … The Board determined that the dictionary definitions for ‘banking,’ ‘bank,’ and ‘finance’ **encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards**, Apex and CC Serve’s services are ‘legally identical, in part.’”  (¶ 27‑28)

Because the similarity analysis under the second DuPont factor asks whether “the respective products are related in some manner and/or … could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source,” the court treats the **legal categorization of the services** as the controlling standard.  The court further clarifies that this legal conclusion is reviewed **de novo**:

> “We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.”  (¶ II)

Thus, the governing test is:

1. **Identify the statutory or dictionary meaning of each service term** (e.g., “credit‑card services,” “banking,” “finance”).  
2. **Determine whether those meanings fall within the same legal category** under the Lanham Act’s International Classification of Goods and Services (or other controlling legal definitions).  
3. If they do, the services are deemed “legally identical, in part,” satisfying the similarity requirement of the second DuPont factor.

### How this differs from a purely factual similarity assessment

A factual similarity analysis would look at **how the services are actually performed**, market overlap, consumer perception, and competitive relationship.  The *Apex* opinion expressly rejects that approach for the legal‑identity prong, noting that the Board’s conclusion does **not** require the services to be identical in practice; it only requires that the **law recognizes them as falling under the same category**.

The court distinguishes this from the sixth DuPont factor, where “similarity” is evaluated **factually**—the number and nature of third‑party marks on similar goods must be considered in the context of the factual similarity already found under the second factor.  The court remands the sixth factor because the Board improperly narrowed the factual scope, but it leaves the **legal‑identity** determination untouched.

**In short:**  
- The standard for “legally identical, in part” is a **legal‑category test** based on statutory/dictionary definitions, reviewed de novo.  
- It is **not** a factual comparison of business operations or market competition; those facts come into play for the other DuPont factors, but the legal identity question is decided on the law alone.

### How does the Federal Circuit treat a TTAB’s failure to consider a “considerable” number of similar marks under the sixth factor?
Question:
The opinion references cases like Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation on what counts as “considerable” evidence of third‑party use. What quantitative or qualitative threshold must be met for the Board’s analysis to be deemed sufficient, and what happens if the Board’s sample is deemed too limited?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Federal Circuit has not set a bright‑line numeric threshold for “considerable” use of third‑party marks in the sixth DuPont factor.  Instead, it looks to the *qualitative* weight of the evidence in the context of the particular market.  In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Court explained that a Board’s finding that nine “Aspire‑formative” marks in the credit‑card niche are **not** “considerable” is an error because the Board **artificially narrowed** the relevant goods.  The Court held that once the Board has found the parties’ goods to be “highly similar” (the second factor), the same breadth must be used for the sixth factor; the Board may not limit itself to a tiny subset of the market and then claim that the number of similar marks is insufficient.  If the Board’s sample is too limited, the Federal Circuit will vacate the sixth‑factor finding (and any other factor that depends on it) and remand for a new analysis that includes **all** third‑party marks used on goods that are *similarly* related, not just those that are identical.

**Why the opinion reaches that conclusion**

1. **The sixth factor’s purpose** – The Court reiterated the well‑settled principle that the sixth factor measures the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” (citing *DuPont* and *Palm Bay Imps.*).  The factor is meant to show whether a mark is weak (many similar marks → narrow scope) or strong (few similar marks → broader scope).

2. **No quantitative rule** – The opinion cites *Jack Wolfskin* (14 “notable” third‑party registrations) and *Juice Generation* (26 third‑party marks) as examples of what courts have called “considerable” evidence, but it does **not** say that 14 or 26 is the fixed line.  The Court stresses that “considerable” is a factual determination, not a mathematical one.

3. **The Court’s qualitative test** –  
   * The Board must look at all marks that are **similar** and used on **similar** goods.  
   * The similarity analysis should be consistent with that used for the second factor. In *Apex Bank*, the Board had already held that credit‑card services and banking‑and‑financing services were “highly similar” (indeed, “legally identical, in part”).  
   * Because the services are highly similar, the pool of relevant third‑party marks is broad – it includes not only marks used on credit‑card services but also those used on other financial‑services marks that overlap with the parties’ offerings.

4. **Effect of an overly narrow sample** – The Court found the Board’s restriction to only the nine “Aspire” marks that were used on credit‑card services “too narrow” and therefore “legally flawed.”  The decision therefore:
   * **Vacated** the Board’s sixth‑factor finding (and, by logical extension, the first‑factor finding that depends on the mark’s perceived strength).  
   * **Remanded** for the Board to reconsider the sixth factor using the proper, broader set of third‑party marks.

**Practical take‑away for practitioners**

| Situation | What the Federal Circuit expects | Consequence of missing it |
|-----------|----------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Quantitative “considerable” evidence** | No fixed number; look at the *totality* of marks that are similar and used on similar goods.  The more marks you have, the stronger the inference of weakness, but a small number may still be “considerable” if the market is crowded. | Relying on a low count (e.g., only 5 marks) without explaining why the market is limited may lead to vacatur. |
| **Scope of “similar goods”** | Use the same scope as the second factor.  If the Board already found the goods “highly similar,” the sixth factor must consider **all** marks on any goods that fall within that same similarity range. | Limiting the analysis to a subset (e.g., only “credit‑card services”) when the goods are broader (e.g., “banking and financing”) is an error. |
| **When the Board’s sample is too limited** | The Court will vacate the sixth‑factor finding and remand for a new analysis that includes the broader set of marks.  Any downstream factor that depends on the mark’s strength (first factor, overall confusion conclusion) is also vacated. | The opposition/opposition can be reopened; the USPTO may have to re‑evaluate the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis from scratch. |

**In sum**, *Apex Bank* makes clear that “considerable” is a fact‑specific determination, and the Federal Circuit will not accept a Board’s conclusion that a handful of marks is insufficient where the Board has already found the parties’ services to be broadly similar.  A proper sixth‑factor analysis must consider **all** third‑party marks used on **any** goods that are similar to the parties’ goods, and the presence of a “considerable” number of such marks is judged qualitatively, not by a strict numerical cut‑off.

### What procedural steps must the TTAB take on remand after a Federal Circuit vacates its findings on the first and sixth DuPont factors?
Question:
The court vacated the Board’s findings and remanded for reconsideration. Does the TTAB have to redo the entire likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis from scratch, or can it rely on its prior factual findings (e.g., the second factor) and only re‑evaluate the vacated factors? What is the standard of review on this second round of appeal?
Answer:
**Answer**

When the Federal Circuit vacates only certain DuPont‑factor findings and remands, the TTAB must conduct a new *likelihood‑of‑confusion* analysis that incorporates those vacated factors, but it need not redo every factual finding that was left intact. The opinion in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 WL … (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025)** spells out exactly how the Board should proceed.

---

### 1. Which parts of the TTAB’s decision survive the remand?

The Court expressly **affirms** the Board’s finding on the **second DuPont factor** (the similarity of the parties’ goods and services).  
> “We affirm the Board’s analysis as to the second DuPont factor because … substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (Section III.A)

All other findings that the Court did **not** vacate remain in force. The Court only **vacated** the findings on the **first** and **sixth** factors and **remanded** for reconsideration of those two.

Thus, on remand the Board may rely on the second‑factor determination (and on any other undisputed factual findings that the Court has not struck) and need not repeat that analysis.

---

### 2. What must the TTAB redo?

* **First factor** – similarity of the marks (appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression).  
* **Sixth factor** – number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods.

The Court requires the Board to reevaluate these factors **in light of the affirmed finding that the parties’ services are “highly similar.”** (Section III.B). The Board must therefore:

1. **Expand the universe of third‑party “Aspire” marks** it considers for the sixth factor to include all marks used on *any* goods or services that are similar to the parties’ services, not just those limited to credit‑card services.  
2. **Reassess the commercial impression of the marks** (first factor) after any change in the analysis of the sixth factor, because the commercial strength of the senior mark may shift once a broader set of competing marks is taken into account. (Section III.C)

The Board does **not** need to recreate the factual record for the second factor; it can simply adopt the Court’s affirmation of that finding.

---

### 3. Standard of review on the second appeal

The standard of review is the same as on the first appeal:

* **Legal conclusions** (the application of each DuPont factor) are reviewed **de novo**.  
* **Factual findings** (the underlying evidence supporting each factor) are reviewed for **substantial evidence**.  

The opinion cites the controlling precedent:

> “We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc.*, 450 F.3d at 1382.  

Therefore, on remand the TTAB must:

* **Apply the law** to the facts concerning the first and sixth factors anew (de novo).  
* **Support any new factual determinations** with **substantial evidence**—i.e., evidence that a reasonable mind could accept as adequate.  

If the Board’s new analysis of the first or sixth factor is supported by such evidence, the Federal Circuit will again defer to those factual findings on any further appeal.

---

### 4. Practical take‑away for the TTAB

* Keep the **second‑factor finding** as is.  
* Conduct a **fresh, de‑novo** analysis of the **first and sixth factors**, using the broader “similar goods/services” scope dictated by the affirmed second‑factor finding.  
* Ensure that the factual record for those two factors meets the **substantial‑evidence** threshold, because that is the standard the appellate court will apply on any subsequent review.

In short, the TTAB need not redo the entire likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis; it must only revisit the vacated factors, applying the same de novo/substantial‑evidence hybrid standard that governs all TTAB decisions on appeal.
