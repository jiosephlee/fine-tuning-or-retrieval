## When “Similar Services” Means the Same Thing: The Court’s Take on DuPont Factor Two  

**By [Your Name],* Lawfare*  

The Federal Circuit’s opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* (Fed. Cir. 2025) may seem like a narrow trademark win for a veteran credit‑card processor, but the decision actually signals a broader shift in how courts treat “similar services” under the second DuPont factor. By endorsing the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) conclusion that credit‑card services and banking/financing services are “highly similar,” the court nudged the standard toward a more functional, market‑focused analysis—one that pays little heed to the quirks of the USPTO’s ID Manual. Below we unpack the reasoning, locate it within the doctrinal history of factor‑two jurisprudence, and consider what it means for startups eyeing the crowded financial‑services space.  

### Why This Case Matters  

At first glance the dispute is straightforward: CC Serve holds a 1998 registration for the word mark **ASPIRE** covering “credit‑card services,” while Apex Bank seeks registration of **ASPIRE BANK** for “banking and financing services.” The TTAB refused Apex’s marks, finding a likelihood of confusion, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that refusal—*but only after* remanding the board’s analysis of two other DuPont factors. The court’s affirmation of the second factor is the part that will echo in future USPTO examinations and litigation.  

If the appellate court had declined to endorse the board’s “highly similar” finding, applicants could carve out a narrower path—arguing that credit‑card services, even when offered in partnership with banks, remain distinct from pure banking activities. Instead, the court cemented a view that, for trademark purposes, the functional overlap of extending credit and managing payments binds these services together. That functional lens will likely be invoked in future disputes involving fintech platforms, neobanks, and any service that blurs the line between “banking” and “card‑issuing.”  

### The Court’s Reasoning on Factor Two  

The second DuPont factor asks whether the goods or services offered under the contested marks are “similar.” The Federal Circuit reiterated the well‑settled principle that exact identity is unnecessary; the test is whether the services are “related in some manner” and whether their marketing context could create a mistaken belief of common source. (*Coach Servs.* v. *Triumph Learning*, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012)).  

The board’s analysis began with a hurdle: the USPTO’s Trademark ID Manual no longer listed “credit‑card services” as a discrete entry, prompting the board to reconstruct the term through dictionary definitions and an industry survey. It concluded that “credit‑card services” include issuance, processing, monitoring, and even credit‑score access. The board then turned to definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” noting that these concepts “encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards.” Relying on third‑party registrations that paired both concepts, the board found “a high degree of similarity” and weighted factor two heavily toward confusion.  

Apex argued that CC Serve never issues cards itself—it merely partners with banks—so its services are not “banking” per se. The Federal Circuit rejected this functional distinction, emphasizing that the board had already engaged in a fact‑finding exercise supported by “substantial evidence.” The appellate judges emphasized that the court’s role is to review the board’s legal conclusions de novo, but its factual findings survive if they are backed by a reasonable evidentiary record. The court therefore affirmed the board’s factor‑two finding, noting that the overlapping credit‑extension functions of the two services are sufficient to deem them “highly similar.”  

### Doctrinal Context: From *Brookfield* to *Goya*  

The second factor has evolved from the early *DuPont* cases, where the emphasis was on literal product similarity, to a more nuanced approach that looks at the consumer’s “mind” and the commercial realities of the marketplace.  

- **Brookfield Communications, Inc. v. West Coast Entertainment Corp.**, 174 F.3d 1036 (9th Cir. 1999), introduced the “related‐goods” analysis, allowing confusion findings even when the services were not identical.  
- **Kellogg Co. v. National Biscuit Co.**, 472 U.S. 325 (1985), stressed the “overall commercial impression” that may arise from related but distinct goods.  
- More recently, *In re* *Neiman Marcus* (Fed. Cir. 2021) underscored that functional overlap—such as prepaid‑card issuance—can bridge seemingly disparate categories.  

*Apex* fits squarely within this trajectory, reinforcing that courts and the TTAB will look beyond the categorical labels in the ID Manual and focus on the substance of the services.  

### The ID Manual’s Role—and Its Limits  

The TTAB’s reliance on the Trademark ID Manual is a double‑edged sword. On the one hand, the manual offers a convenient shorthand for examiners and applicants alike. On the other, it is a “living document” that can change without notice, as happened when “credit‑card services” was excised.  

In *Apex*, the board’s decision to reconstitute the term via dictionaries and industry practice sidestepped the manual’s omission, but the Federal Circuit’s endorsement of that approach effectively tells trademark practitioners that the manual is not determinative. If a service description disappears from the manual, applicants can still rely on an expansive, function‑based definition, provided they produce a robust evidentiary record.  

### Implications for New Entrants in Overlapping Financial Markets  

Fintech startups are now launching everything from “bank‑as‑a‑service” platforms to embedded credit‑card APIs. The *Apex* decision suggests that the USPTO and the courts will be willing to consider those products “highly similar” to traditional banking services for trademark purposes, even when the startup does not hold a banking charter.  

Two practical takeaways arise:  

1. **Broader Clearance Searches** – Companies should screen not only for identical marks in the same registration class but also for marks in adjacent financial categories. A “credit‑card” search may surface “banking” registrations that could later be deemed confusing.  

2. **Strategic Claim Drafting** – When filing an intent‑to‑use application, applicants should be prepared to justify a narrower description of services if they wish to avoid the “highly similar” trap. Conversely, if they want robust protection, they may benefit from emphasizing the functional overlap to capture a wider market.  

### The Court’s Partial Remand: A Reminder of the DuPont Interplay  

While the opinion affirms factor two, it vacates the board’s findings on factors one and six, noting that a more accurate assessment of the “number and nature of similar marks” (factor six) may affect the overall commercial impression (factor one). This illustrates a fundamental truth of DuPont analysis: the factors are not isolated silos but a holistic puzzle.  

The vacatur signals that a different weighting of factor six—perhaps recognizing a larger pool of “Aspire” marks in broader financial services—could tip the balance toward a finding of weakness, thereby narrowing the scope of protection for CC Serve’s mark. For Apex, that could mean a chance to secure **ASPIRE BANK** on remand.  

### Looking Ahead  

*Apex Bank v. CC Serve* clarifies that the functional reality of extending credit can bind credit‑card and banking services together for trademark purposes. The decision nudges the USPTO toward a more market‑focused, less categorical approach to the second DuPont factor, especially when the ID Manual’s taxonomy changes.  

For practitioners, the take‑away is clear: when drafting applications or mounting oppositions in the fin‑tech arena, expect the Board and the courts to ask whether the services *actually* overlap in the eyes of the consumer, not whether they sit neatly in a static manual. As the lines between banking, payments, and credit continue to blur, the trademark landscape will be forced to adapt—*Apex* is a early, telling sign of that evolution.



# The Sixth DuPont Factor Re‑examined: Why Scope Matters in Third‑Party Mark Surveys  

## Why This Decision Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s opinion in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.** may look like a routine “vacate and remand” on a likelihood‑of‑confusion dispute, but it actually delivers a sharp reminder to trademark practitioners about the proper reach of the **sixth DuPont factor**—the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”  By striking the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) decision to restrict its third‑party mark survey to only “credit‑card” marks, the court forces the Board (and, by extension, future litigants) to adopt a broader, more context‑sensitive view of similarity.  In an era where financial‑services marks are proliferating across a dizzying array of sub‑sectors—credit cards, peer‑to‑peer lending, digital wallets, “bank‑as‑a‑service,” and so on—this ruling reshapes how parties must compile and evaluate evidence of “crowdedness” and mark strength.  

## The Court’s Reasoning: A Two‑Step Recalibration  

### 1. The DuPont Factors Are Not Isolated Islands  

The Federal Circuit reiterated a well‑settled principle: **likelihood of confusion is a legal question built on factual findings** under the thirteen DuPont factors. Each factor may involve factual determinations, but the legal conclusion depends on a holistic balancing. The court emphasized that once the Board makes a factual finding that two parties’ services are “highly similar” (as it did under factor two), that same factual backdrop must inform the analysis of the other factors.  

### 2. The Sixth Factor Requires a Consistent Definition of “Similar Goods”  

The Board’s error, according to Judge Hughes, was a **semantic narrowing** of the term “similar goods” when it examined factor six. The Board limited its third‑party mark survey to marks used in *credit‑card services* only, even though it had previously concluded that *banking and financing services* and *credit‑card services* are “legally identical, in part.” The court held that **such a split‑screen approach is impermissible**.  

The Sixth factor asks whether a “large number of similar marks on similar goods” weakens a senior mark’s scope of protection. The Federal Circuit pointed to precedents (e.g., *Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation*) that recognize “similar” rather than “identical” goods, and to the rationale that a crowded marketplace educates consumers to distinguish among marks. By cutting off the broader set of Aspire‑related marks in banking, financing, and related financial‑services categories, the Board artificially narrowed the evidence pool and effectively insulated the senior mark from the “crowded‑field” analysis it should have considered.  

### 3. Vacatur and Remand Are the Proper Remedy  

Because the Sixth factor’s analysis can influence the First factor’s assessment of commercial impression and overall strength, the court **vacated both factor six and factor one findings** and remanded for reconsideration. This ensures that any recalibration of the Sixth factor can flow through the entire confusion analysis, rather than being isolated.  

## What This Means for Trademark Practice  

### A. Broader “Similar Goods” Surveys Are Now Mandatory  

Practitioners must now **map the full contour of the relevant market**, not just the narrow slice defined by the immediate services at issue. In financial‑services contexts, that means looking at marks used for credit cards, debit cards, online banking platforms, mobile payment apps, and even ancillary services like credit‑score monitoring—all of which could be deemed “similar” under the *DuPont* framework.  

A practical tip: when assembling a third‑party mark docket, start with the **International Nice Classification** headings that cover the senior mark’s goods, then expand to adjacent classes that the USPTO’s ID Manual or case law treats as “related.” The Apex decision warns against a **post‑hoc narrowing** that conflicts with a prior similarity finding.  

### B. The “Crowded‑Field” Argument Gains Strength  

If a senior mark operates in a market saturated with similar marks, the Sixth factor can tip the scales toward a **narrower scope of protection**. The Federal Circuit reaffirmed that “voluminous evidence” of similar marks (as illustrated in *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*) can overcome a prima facie perception of distinctiveness. After Apex, an opponent can more readily argue that a senior mark is *relatively weak* if the marketplace contains **dozens** of Aspire‑type marks across the broader financial‑services spectrum, not just credit cards.  

### C. Evidentiary Burdens May Shift  

Previously, senior users could sometimes rely on the “innocent use” of a narrowly defined market to sidestep a crowded‑field analysis. Post‑Apex, the burden to **show commercial strength** may be higher, because the Board (or a court) will likely consider a more expansive set of third‑party marks. Conversely, junior applicants must be prepared to **produce comprehensive surveys** that demonstrate the extent of the crowd, and articulate why the senior mark’s “normal scope” should be narrowed.  

### D. Impact on the First DuPont Factor  

Because the court also vacated the First factor analysis, we can anticipate that **commercial impression** will now be evaluated in light of the Sixth factor’s findings. If a senior mark is deemed weak due to crowding, its overall commercial impression may shift from “strong, inherently distinctive” to “weak, descriptive,” which can further tilt the confusion balance.  

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects  

1. **Uniformity Across DuPont Factors** – Apex reinforces the principle that the factual matrix established for one factor permeates the others. This discourages “factor‑shopping,” where a Board might apply a stricter standard for one factor while using a more lenient analysis for another.  

2. **Potential for More Frequent Remands** – The decision highlights the tightrope the TTAB walks when it conducts independent factual findings but must stay consistent across the thirteen factors. Boards that gloss over the interdependence may face more appellate vacaturs, leading to longer resolution times.  

3. **Strategic Use of Third‑Party Mark Surveys** – Counsel will likely invest more heavily in **comprehensive “crowded‑field” analyses**, perhaps employing specialized databases (e.g., Corsearch, Markify) to capture marks across related classes and service descriptions. The cost of such surveys may rise, but the payoff—avoiding a Sixth‑factor misstep—could be decisive.  

4. **Implications for Financial‑Services Branding** – The financial sector, already a hotbed of overlapping terminology (“bank,” “credit,” “wallet,” “pay”), may see **greater scrutiny of new brand launches**. Companies seeking to add “bank” or “credit” to a pre‑existing brand name will need to anticipate a broader third‑party landscape than previously assumed.  

5. **Judicial Guidance for Future Courts** – Though the Federal Circuit’s decision is limited to the facts of Apex, its reasoning provides a **template for later cases** involving other industries with naturally overlapping services, such as telecommunications, health‑tech, or e‑commerce platforms.  

## Bottom Line  

The Apex Bank opinion does more than correct a TTAB slip; it clarifies the **scope requirement** embedded in the Sixth DuPont factor and binds that scope to the factual findings of other factors. For trademark practitioners, the takeaway is clear: **don’t let your third‑party mark survey be the narrowest part of your likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis**. Embrace a holistic view of “similar goods,” anticipate the cascading effects on the First factor, and be prepared for the possibility that a crowded field will shrink the protection afforded to senior marks. In the crowded, ever‑evolving arena of financial‑services branding, the court’s admonition is a timely reminder that breadth matters—both in the marketplace and in the evidence we bring before the Board.



## First Factor Fallout: How Commercial Strength and Impression Pivot on the Sixth Factor  

The Federal Circuit’s opinion in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.**, 2025 WL 3742152 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25 2025), may look at first glance like a routine “vacate‑and‑remand” on two of the DuPont factors. Yet the decision does something more subtle—and far more consequential for trademark practitioners: it ties the *first* factor’s commercial‑impression analysis directly to the *sixth* factor’s inquiry into the number and nature of similar marks. In effect, the court tells us that a finding (or lack thereof) on mark strength under factor 6 can reshape the entire “overall impression” analysis under factor 1. Below we unpack why the court took this path, how it fits within the broader DuPont framework, and what it means for the way we marshal evidence of a mark’s strength or weakness in future oppositions and cancellations.

### Why the case matters  

The DuPont factors are the backbone of every likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis in the United States. While courts have long treated each factor as a distinct inquiry, the Federal Circuit has, in recent years, emphasized their *interconnected* nature. In **Apex**, the court explicitly linked the sixth factor—“the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”—to the first factor’s “similarity of the marks … as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression.” By doing so, the panel signals that the *scope* of the “relevant public” and the *strength* of the senior mark cannot be isolated from each other.

Practically, this means that any error in the sixth‑factor analysis—especially an error that narrows the universe of comparable marks—requires a fresh look at the commercial impression. The decision thus sets a new procedural checkpoint: if a board’s factor‑6 analysis is flawed, the board (or a district court) must also revisit factor 1, even if the parties have not raised new evidence on the latter.

### The court’s reasoning, step by step  

1. **Second factor affirmed** – The panel agreed with the TTAB that “credit‑card services” and “banking and financing services” are “highly similar.” The Board’s factual findings were supported by substantial evidence, so the factor 2 determination stood.  

2. **Sixth factor mis‑applied** – The Board limited its analysis of “similar marks” to those used *solely* in connection with credit‑card services, despite its own earlier finding that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services overlap with broader banking and financing activities. The Federal Circuit held that once the Board has concluded the services are “highly similar,” it must apply the same breadth when assessing the marketplace saturation of similar marks. To restrict the universe of marks only to credit‑card‑specific uses was “legally flawed” and created an artificial niche that understates the prevalence of “Aspire”‑type marks in the wider financial services arena.  

3. **First factor follows from sixth** – The first factor asks whether the *overall commercial impression* of the marks would likely cause confusion. Commercial impression is not a static, purely visual test; it incorporates the *strength* of the senior mark. If a mark is weak—because it co‑exists with a multitude of similar marks on related goods—its overall impression is narrowed, making confusion less likely. Conversely, a strong mark that enjoys a “scarce” presence in the marketplace commands a broader impression.  

   Because the Board’s mis‑step on factor 6 likely mis‑characterized the senior mark’s strength, the panel concluded that the first‑factor analysis was tainted. The court therefore vacated the Board’s factor 1 finding and remanded for a fresh commercial‑impression inquiry that reflects the corrected view of the mark’s market saturation.

4. **No new legal standard, but a procedural reminder** – The opinion does not rewrite the “strength” analysis per se; rather, it reinforces that the *same factual backdrop* must be used across related factors. The panel quoted **Olde Tyme Foods** and **Juice Generation** to emphasize that the “similar goods” requirement for factor 6 is not stricter than that for other factors. The decision is a reminder that the Board (and lower courts) cannot cherry‑pick a narrower definition of similarity for one factor while using a broader one for another.

### Doctrinal implications  

#### 1. **Integrated factor analysis is now a procedural requirement**  
While prior cases (e.g., *M2 Software* and *Bose*) already noted that each DuPont factor is a question of law grounded in factual findings, the Apex opinion pushes the integration a step further. Practitioners must be prepared to demonstrate that the factual matrix supporting *all* relevant factors is consistent. A successful challenge to one factor’s factual foundation will likely ripple to any other factor that leans on the same facts.  

#### 2. **Strength evidence must be comprehensive**  
Evidence of “crowding”—the number of similar marks—must now address *all* goods and services that the senior mark covers, not just the narrow slice the opposition focuses on. In practice, this means that a party asserting weakness should submit **cross‑industry** registrations and use data, not merely those limited to the specific class in dispute.  

#### 3. **Re‑framing the “relevant public”**  
The panel’s decision underscores that the relevant public is defined by the *totality* of the services at issue, not by the narrower subset a Board may select for convenience. This broadens the field for consumer perception surveys and consumer testimony, which must now reflect a more heterogeneous buying public (e.g., both credit‑card users and general banking customers).  

#### 4. **Potential for increased remands**  
Because a factor‑6 error now triggers a mandatory factor‑1 revisit, we may see a rise in appellate vacatur‑and‑remand orders where the TTAB’s “crowding” analysis is deemed overly narrow. Parties can leverage this procedural link to press for a more favorable first‑factor result without having to re‑file a separate motion regarding commercial impression.  

### Practical takeaways for practitioners  

1. **Gather *expanded* third‑party mark inventories** – When opposing a registration (or defending one), compile all marks that share the senior mark’s wording or design, regardless of the exact NICE classification. Include evidence of use in related financial services (e.g., loans, prepaid cards, digital wallets).  

2. **Prepare a unified strength narrative** – Your brief should present a single “strength/weakness” story that integrates factor 2 (goods similarity), factor 6 (crowding), and factor 1 (commercial impression). Explicitly cross‑reference the evidence: “Because the senior mark co‑exists with 52 Aspire‑type registrations across credit, debit, and financing services, its commercial impression is necessarily limited, making confusion unlikely.”  

3. **Tailor consumer surveys to the broader public** – If you rely on survey evidence, ensure the sample includes respondents who have interacted with any of the “similar” services, not just credit‑card holders. This aligns the survey’s scope with the court’s broader view of the relevant public.  

4. **Anticipate a second‑factor review on remand** – Even though factor 2 was affirmed, the remand may prompt the Board to revisit it in light of a revised factor‑6 analysis. Be ready to reinforce the similarity argument with additional market data or updated USPTO class definitions.  

5. **Leverage the procedural link in motions** – In district‑court infringement or cancellation suits, a party can move to strike a first‑factor finding by pointing to a defect in the sixth factor, citing *Apex* as authority that the two are inseparable for purposes of “commercial strength.”  

### Looking ahead  

The Apex decision does not overturn any deep‑seated doctrine; rather, it sharpens the procedural lens through which the DuPont factors are applied. By insisting on consistency across factors, the Federal Circuit encourages a more holistic, fact‑driven approach that better reflects how ordinary consumers perceive marks in a crowded marketplace.  

For trademark counsel, the takeaway is clear: *the battle over “strength” is fought on multiple fronts simultaneously.* Evidence that bolsters a weakness claim under factor 6 will now have a direct, beneficial impact on the commercial‑impression analysis under factor 1. Conversely, a strong showing of distinctiveness on factor 1 must be buttressed by a convincing argument that the mark does not suffer from pervasive crowding on related goods.  

In short, the first factor is no longer a standalone visual‑or‑phonetic test; it is a dynamic assessment that pivots on the market realities illuminated by the sixth factor. The next wave of trademark disputes will likely see more comprehensive “crowding” dossiers, broader consumer surveys, and a heightened focus on the interconnected nature of the DuPont factors—precisely the landscape Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. forces us to navigate.



## Procedural Lessons from Apex Bank v. CC Serve: De Novo Review, Substantial Evidence, and the Limits of Board Discretion  

When a trademark opposition reaches the Federal Circuit, the stakes are not only the fate of the marks at issue but also the procedural roadmap that litigants must follow to survive appellate scrutiny.  Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 Fed. Cir. ____, offers a clear illustration of how the court applies the two‑pronged standard of review—de novo for legal conclusions, substantial‑evidence for factual findings—and how it draws the line between a vacatur that clears the way for a fresh analysis and an affirmation that leaves the Board’s reasoning untouched.  Below, we walk through the opinion’s procedural anatomy, flag the take‑aways for trademark practitioners, and consider how this decision might shape future appellate practice before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB).

### Why the case matters  

The Federal Circuit’s holding does not create a new substantive test for likelihood of confusion; rather, it clarifies *how* the appellate court will police the Board’s application of the DuPont factors.  By affirming the Board’s finding on the second factor (the similarity of goods and services) while vacating its conclusions on the first and sixth factors, the court sends a precise message: **legal conclusions are yours to get right on the record, but factual findings must be backed by “substantial evidence” and cannot be reshaped by an overly narrow interpretation of the same fact set.**  For practitioners, the decision is a reminder that a well‑crafted record on the merits of similarity will anchor the Board’s analysis across multiple factors, and that an appeal that merely attacks the Board’s *conclusions* without pointing to a record deficiency will likely be struck.

### De novo review of legal conclusions  

The opinion opens with a quotation from *M2 Software*, reminding readers that the Federal Circuit “review[s] the Board’s legal conclusions de novo.”  This is the standard that applies to each of the DuPont factors themselves, which are *questions of law*—the weight to be given to a particular factor, the correct legal definition of “similar goods,” or the proper approach to “commercial impression.”  

In Apex, the court treated the Board’s analysis of the second factor (goods similarity) as a legal determination.  The Board had already defined “credit card services” and, relying on dictionary definitions, concluded that those services overlap with “banking and financing” because both involve extending credit.  The court found the Board’s legal reasoning sound and affirmed, emphasizing that the Board’s interpretation of the statutory language was *reasonable* and supported by the record.  

**Take‑away:** When you argue a legal point—say, that “credit card services” are not “banking services” for purposes of the Lanham Act—you must do more than point to the statutory text; you need to embed that argument in a factual foundation that survives the “substantial evidence” filter.  A purely doctrinal argument unsupported by the record will likely be dismissed on the merits of de novo review.

### Substantial‑evidence review of factual findings  

The other side of the appellate scalpel is the “substantial evidence” standard for factual findings.  The court reiterated that “substantial evidence” is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.”  This low‑threshold test is not a rubber‑stamp, but it does require the Board to have a factual scaffold that can’t be easily toppled.  

**Factor six** illustrates the point.  The Board’s factual finding—that the “relevant public” was limited to “consumers of credit card services”—was unsupported by the broader record the parties had supplied.  Apex showed the Board 42 additional registrations that used “Aspire” in the broader financial‑services arena, yet the Board excluded them on a *legal* ground that the marks were “essentially irrelevant.”  The court called that approach a “legal error” that contaminated the factual analysis, vacated the factor, and remanded for a reconsideration that respects the factual finding of high similarity already made under factor two.  

In other words, once the Board determines that the services are “highly similar,” it cannot later *shrink* the universe of comparable marks to a narrower subset without a factual justification.  The court requires consistency across the factors; the factual premise that the services are similar must inform the assessment of third‑party mark density.

**Take‑away:** Make sure the factual record you lay at the TTAB is *comprehensive* and *coherent* across all the DuPont factors.  If you can show that the public for whom the marks are relevant is broader than the Board assumes, you force the Board to consider a larger set of comparable marks—potentially weakening the senior mark’s claim to a “normal scope” of protection.

### Vacatur versus affirmation: strategic implications  

The decision’s split outcome—affirming factor two, vacating factors one and six—highlights the strategic nuance of appellate relief.  A vacatur erases the Board’s finding, sending the matter back for fresh analysis; an affirmation leaves the Board’s reasoning intact.  Importantly, the court did **not** vacate the entire opposition.  By preserving the Board’s determination that the services are “highly similar,” the Federal Circuit kept the *core* of the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis alive while giving the Board a chance to revisit the peripheral but still critical factors.

For litigants, this underscores the importance of *targeted* appeals.  If you can show that the Board erred on a specific factor—perhaps because you have additional evidence of market segmentation or a plethora of third‑party uses—you should focus your brief on that factor, anticipating that the court may only vacate the offending portion.  Conversely, a blanket challenge that the Board “got it all wrong” is less likely to succeed, especially where the record supports at least one factor.

### Doctrinal ripple effects  

While the case does not overturn any precedent on the DuPont test, it does reinforce two doctrinal trends:

1. **Uniform application of factual findings across factors.**  The court’s insistence that the Board’s similarity finding must permeate the analysis of the sixth factor signals a move toward *holistic* factor weighting, discouraging Boards from compartmentalizing facts for each factor.

2. **Broadening the “similar goods” inquiry.**  By criticizing the Board’s narrow definition of the “relevant public” and its exclusion of marks outside the credit‑card niche, the opinion aligns with earlier Federal Circuit cases that have emphasized a flexible, market‑based view of “similar goods.”  Practitioners should be prepared to argue that a mark’s reach extends beyond a single subcategory when the services overlap substantially.

### Practical lessons for trademark practitioners  

1. **Build a record that speaks to every factor you anticipate.**  In the Apex docket, Apex prepared a substantial exhibit list of third‑party “Aspire” marks.  The Board’s failure to consider them was a reversible error because the factual foundation existed.  Bring every piece of evidence you think may matter for *any* DuPont factor into the TTAB record; the appellate court will not allow you to cherry‑pick later.

2. **Frame similarity arguments in both legal and factual terms.**  The Board’s successful legal analysis of factor two relied on dictionary definitions and industry practice.  Replicating that approach—citing authoritative sources that define the services as overlapping—will fortify your legal conclusions against de novo review.

3. **Anticipate the “relevant public” analysis.**  The appellate court reminded us that the public’s perception cannot be limited mechanically.  If your mark operates in a broader financial context, submit market surveys, consumer testimony, or advertising examples that demonstrate the wider audience.

4. **Separate “legal error” from “insufficient evidence.”**  The court vacated the sixth factor because the Board’s *legal* restriction of the relevant public conflicted with the factual findings, not because the evidence was lacking.  When drafting appellate briefs, make clear whether you are arguing that the Board erred in law, misapplied the factual record, or both.

### Looking ahead  

Apex Bank v. CC Serve will likely be cited in future TTAB appeals as a benchmark for the interplay between de novo legal review and substantial‑evidence factual review.  Its emphasis on consistent factual premises across the DuPont factors may prompt TTAB examiners and members to adopt a more integrated approach when weighing the thirteen factors, reducing the chances that a party can “win” on a narrow slice of the analysis while the rest of the record remains unchallenged.

For trademark counsel, the case is a procedural primer: **craft a robust, cross‑factor record; anchor your legal arguments in that record; and be prepared to argue that the Board’s discretion is bounded not only by the law but by the very facts it has already found.**  In the end, the appellate court’s toolbox is simple—de novo for law, substantial evidence for fact—but wielding it effectively requires the same meticulous preparation that the TTAB demands at the trial level.
