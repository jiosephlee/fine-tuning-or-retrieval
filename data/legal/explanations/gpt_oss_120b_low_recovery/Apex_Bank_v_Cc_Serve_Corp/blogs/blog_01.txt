Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## When “Similar Services” Means the Same Thing: The Court’s Take on DuPont Factor Two  

**By [Your Name],* Lawfare*  

The Federal Circuit’s opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* (Fed. Cir. 2025) may seem like a narrow trademark win for a veteran credit‑card processor, but the decision actually signals a broader shift in how courts treat “similar services” under the second DuPont factor. By endorsing the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) conclusion that credit‑card services and banking/financing services are “highly similar,” the court nudged the standard toward a more functional, market‑focused analysis—one that pays little heed to the quirks of the USPTO’s ID Manual. Below we unpack the reasoning, locate it within the doctrinal history of factor‑two jurisprudence, and consider what it means for startups eyeing the crowded financial‑services space.  

### Why This Case Matters  

At first glance the dispute is straightforward: CC Serve holds a 1998 registration for the word mark **ASPIRE** covering “credit‑card services,” while Apex Bank seeks registration of **ASPIRE BANK** for “banking and financing services.” The TTAB refused Apex’s marks, finding a likelihood of confusion, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that refusal—*but only after* remanding the board’s analysis of two other DuPont factors. The court’s affirmation of the second factor is the part that will echo in future USPTO examinations and litigation.  

If the appellate court had declined to endorse the board’s “highly similar” finding, applicants could carve out a narrower path—arguing that credit‑card services, even when offered in partnership with banks, remain distinct from pure banking activities. Instead, the court cemented a view that, for trademark purposes, the functional overlap of extending credit and managing payments binds these services together. That functional lens will likely be invoked in future disputes involving fintech platforms, neobanks, and any service that blurs the line between “banking” and “card‑issuing.”  

### The Court’s Reasoning on Factor Two  

The second DuPont factor asks whether the goods or services offered under the contested marks are “similar.” The Federal Circuit reiterated the well‑settled principle that exact identity is unnecessary; the test is whether the services are “related in some manner” and whether their marketing context could create a mistaken belief of common source. (*Coach Servs.* v. *Triumph Learning*, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012)).  

The board’s analysis began with a hurdle: the USPTO’s Trademark ID Manual no longer listed “credit‑card services” as a discrete entry, prompting the board to reconstruct the term through dictionary definitions and an industry survey. It concluded that “credit‑card services” include issuance, processing, monitoring, and even credit‑score access. The board then turned to definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” noting that these concepts “encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards.” Relying on third‑party registrations that paired both concepts, the board found “a high degree of similarity” and weighted factor two heavily toward confusion.  

Apex argued that CC Serve never issues cards itself—it merely partners with banks—so its services are not “banking” per se. The Federal Circuit rejected this functional distinction, emphasizing that the board had already engaged in a fact‑finding exercise supported by “substantial evidence.” The appellate judges emphasized that the court’s role is to review the board’s legal conclusions de novo, but its factual findings survive if they are backed by a reasonable evidentiary record. The court therefore affirmed the board’s factor‑two finding, noting that the overlapping credit‑extension functions of the two services are sufficient to deem them “highly similar.”  

### Doctrinal Context: From *Brookfield* to *Goya*  

The second factor has evolved from the early *DuPont* cases, where the emphasis was on literal product similarity, to a more nuanced approach that looks at the consumer’s “mind” and the commercial realities of the marketplace.  

- **Brookfield Communications, Inc. v. West Coast Entertainment Corp.**, 174 F.3d 1036 (9th Cir. 1999), introduced the “related‐goods” analysis, allowing confusion findings even when the services were not identical.  
- **Kellogg Co. v. National Biscuit Co.**, 472 U.S. 325 (1985), stressed the “overall commercial impression” that may arise from related but distinct goods.  
- More recently, *In re* *Neiman Marcus* (Fed. Cir. 2021) underscored that functional overlap—such as prepaid‑card issuance—can bridge seemingly disparate categories.  

*Apex* fits squarely within this trajectory, reinforcing that courts and the TTAB will look beyond the categorical labels in the ID Manual and focus on the substance of the services.  

### The ID Manual’s Role—and Its Limits  

The TTAB’s reliance on the Trademark ID Manual is a double‑edged sword. On the one hand, the manual offers a convenient shorthand for examiners and applicants alike. On the other, it is a “living document” that can change without notice, as happened when “credit‑card services” was excised.  

In *Apex*, the board’s decision to reconstitute the term via dictionaries and industry practice sidestepped the manual’s omission, but the Federal Circuit’s endorsement of that approach effectively tells trademark practitioners that the manual is not determinative. If a service description disappears from the manual, applicants can still rely on an expansive, function‑based definition, provided they produce a robust evidentiary record.  

### Implications for New Entrants in Overlapping Financial Markets  

Fintech startups are now launching everything from “bank‑as‑a‑service” platforms to embedded credit‑card APIs. The *Apex* decision suggests that the USPTO and the courts will be willing to consider those products “highly similar” to traditional banking services for trademark purposes, even when the startup does not hold a banking charter.  

Two practical takeaways arise:  

1. **Broader Clearance Searches** – Companies should screen not only for identical marks in the same registration class but also for marks in adjacent financial categories. A “credit‑card” search may surface “banking” registrations that could later be deemed confusing.  

2. **Strategic Claim Drafting** – When filing an intent‑to‑use application, applicants should be prepared to justify a narrower description of services if they wish to avoid the “highly similar” trap. Conversely, if they want robust protection, they may benefit from emphasizing the functional overlap to capture a wider market.  

### The Court’s Partial Remand: A Reminder of the DuPont Interplay  

While the opinion affirms factor two, it vacates the board’s findings on factors one and six, noting that a more accurate assessment of the “number and nature of similar marks” (factor six) may affect the overall commercial impression (factor one). This illustrates a fundamental truth of DuPont analysis: the factors are not isolated silos but a holistic puzzle.  

The vacatur signals that a different weighting of factor six—perhaps recognizing a larger pool of “Aspire” marks in broader financial services—could tip the balance toward a finding of weakness, thereby narrowing the scope of protection for CC Serve’s mark. For Apex, that could mean a chance to secure **ASPIRE BANK** on remand.  

### Looking Ahead  

*Apex Bank v. CC Serve* clarifies that the functional reality of extending credit can bind credit‑card and banking services together for trademark purposes. The decision nudges the USPTO toward a more market‑focused, less categorical approach to the second DuPont factor, especially when the ID Manual’s taxonomy changes.  

For practitioners, the take‑away is clear: when drafting applications or mounting oppositions in the fin‑tech arena, expect the Board and the courts to ask whether the services *actually* overlap in the eyes of the consumer, not whether they sit neatly in a static manual. As the lines between banking, payments, and credit continue to blur, the trademark landscape will be forced to adapt—*Apex* is a early, telling sign of that evolution.
