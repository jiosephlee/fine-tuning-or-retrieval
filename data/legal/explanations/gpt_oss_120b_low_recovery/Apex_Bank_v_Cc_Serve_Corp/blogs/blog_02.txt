Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# The Sixth DuPont Factor Re‑examined: Why Scope Matters in Third‑Party Mark Surveys  

## Why This Decision Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s opinion in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.** may look like a routine “vacate and remand” on a likelihood‑of‑confusion dispute, but it actually delivers a sharp reminder to trademark practitioners about the proper reach of the **sixth DuPont factor**—the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”  By striking the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) decision to restrict its third‑party mark survey to only “credit‑card” marks, the court forces the Board (and, by extension, future litigants) to adopt a broader, more context‑sensitive view of similarity.  In an era where financial‑services marks are proliferating across a dizzying array of sub‑sectors—credit cards, peer‑to‑peer lending, digital wallets, “bank‑as‑a‑service,” and so on—this ruling reshapes how parties must compile and evaluate evidence of “crowdedness” and mark strength.  

## The Court’s Reasoning: A Two‑Step Recalibration  

### 1. The DuPont Factors Are Not Isolated Islands  

The Federal Circuit reiterated a well‑settled principle: **likelihood of confusion is a legal question built on factual findings** under the thirteen DuPont factors. Each factor may involve factual determinations, but the legal conclusion depends on a holistic balancing. The court emphasized that once the Board makes a factual finding that two parties’ services are “highly similar” (as it did under factor two), that same factual backdrop must inform the analysis of the other factors.  

### 2. The Sixth Factor Requires a Consistent Definition of “Similar Goods”  

The Board’s error, according to Judge Hughes, was a **semantic narrowing** of the term “similar goods” when it examined factor six. The Board limited its third‑party mark survey to marks used in *credit‑card services* only, even though it had previously concluded that *banking and financing services* and *credit‑card services* are “legally identical, in part.” The court held that **such a split‑screen approach is impermissible**.  

The Sixth factor asks whether a “large number of similar marks on similar goods” weakens a senior mark’s scope of protection. The Federal Circuit pointed to precedents (e.g., *Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation*) that recognize “similar” rather than “identical” goods, and to the rationale that a crowded marketplace educates consumers to distinguish among marks. By cutting off the broader set of Aspire‑related marks in banking, financing, and related financial‑services categories, the Board artificially narrowed the evidence pool and effectively insulated the senior mark from the “crowded‑field” analysis it should have considered.  

### 3. Vacatur and Remand Are the Proper Remedy  

Because the Sixth factor’s analysis can influence the First factor’s assessment of commercial impression and overall strength, the court **vacated both factor six and factor one findings** and remanded for reconsideration. This ensures that any recalibration of the Sixth factor can flow through the entire confusion analysis, rather than being isolated.  

## What This Means for Trademark Practice  

### A. Broader “Similar Goods” Surveys Are Now Mandatory  

Practitioners must now **map the full contour of the relevant market**, not just the narrow slice defined by the immediate services at issue. In financial‑services contexts, that means looking at marks used for credit cards, debit cards, online banking platforms, mobile payment apps, and even ancillary services like credit‑score monitoring—all of which could be deemed “similar” under the *DuPont* framework.  

A practical tip: when assembling a third‑party mark docket, start with the **International Nice Classification** headings that cover the senior mark’s goods, then expand to adjacent classes that the USPTO’s ID Manual or case law treats as “related.” The Apex decision warns against a **post‑hoc narrowing** that conflicts with a prior similarity finding.  

### B. The “Crowded‑Field” Argument Gains Strength  

If a senior mark operates in a market saturated with similar marks, the Sixth factor can tip the scales toward a **narrower scope of protection**. The Federal Circuit reaffirmed that “voluminous evidence” of similar marks (as illustrated in *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*) can overcome a prima facie perception of distinctiveness. After Apex, an opponent can more readily argue that a senior mark is *relatively weak* if the marketplace contains **dozens** of Aspire‑type marks across the broader financial‑services spectrum, not just credit cards.  

### C. Evidentiary Burdens May Shift  

Previously, senior users could sometimes rely on the “innocent use” of a narrowly defined market to sidestep a crowded‑field analysis. Post‑Apex, the burden to **show commercial strength** may be higher, because the Board (or a court) will likely consider a more expansive set of third‑party marks. Conversely, junior applicants must be prepared to **produce comprehensive surveys** that demonstrate the extent of the crowd, and articulate why the senior mark’s “normal scope” should be narrowed.  

### D. Impact on the First DuPont Factor  

Because the court also vacated the First factor analysis, we can anticipate that **commercial impression** will now be evaluated in light of the Sixth factor’s findings. If a senior mark is deemed weak due to crowding, its overall commercial impression may shift from “strong, inherently distinctive” to “weak, descriptive,” which can further tilt the confusion balance.  

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects  

1. **Uniformity Across DuPont Factors** – Apex reinforces the principle that the factual matrix established for one factor permeates the others. This discourages “factor‑shopping,” where a Board might apply a stricter standard for one factor while using a more lenient analysis for another.  

2. **Potential for More Frequent Remands** – The decision highlights the tightrope the TTAB walks when it conducts independent factual findings but must stay consistent across the thirteen factors. Boards that gloss over the interdependence may face more appellate vacaturs, leading to longer resolution times.  

3. **Strategic Use of Third‑Party Mark Surveys** – Counsel will likely invest more heavily in **comprehensive “crowded‑field” analyses**, perhaps employing specialized databases (e.g., Corsearch, Markify) to capture marks across related classes and service descriptions. The cost of such surveys may rise, but the payoff—avoiding a Sixth‑factor misstep—could be decisive.  

4. **Implications for Financial‑Services Branding** – The financial sector, already a hotbed of overlapping terminology (“bank,” “credit,” “wallet,” “pay”), may see **greater scrutiny of new brand launches**. Companies seeking to add “bank” or “credit” to a pre‑existing brand name will need to anticipate a broader third‑party landscape than previously assumed.  

5. **Judicial Guidance for Future Courts** – Though the Federal Circuit’s decision is limited to the facts of Apex, its reasoning provides a **template for later cases** involving other industries with naturally overlapping services, such as telecommunications, health‑tech, or e‑commerce platforms.  

## Bottom Line  

The Apex Bank opinion does more than correct a TTAB slip; it clarifies the **scope requirement** embedded in the Sixth DuPont factor and binds that scope to the factual findings of other factors. For trademark practitioners, the takeaway is clear: **don’t let your third‑party mark survey be the narrowest part of your likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis**. Embrace a holistic view of “similar goods,” anticipate the cascading effects on the First factor, and be prepared for the possibility that a crowded field will shrink the protection afforded to senior marks. In the crowded, ever‑evolving arena of financial‑services branding, the court’s admonition is a timely reminder that breadth matters—both in the marketplace and in the evidence we bring before the Board.
