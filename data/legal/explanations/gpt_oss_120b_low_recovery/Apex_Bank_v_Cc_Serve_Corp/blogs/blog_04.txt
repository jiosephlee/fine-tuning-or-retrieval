Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## Procedural Lessons from Apex Bank v. CC Serve: De Novo Review, Substantial Evidence, and the Limits of Board Discretion  

When a trademark opposition reaches the Federal Circuit, the stakes are not only the fate of the marks at issue but also the procedural roadmap that litigants must follow to survive appellate scrutiny.  Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 Fed. Cir. ____, offers a clear illustration of how the court applies the two‑pronged standard of review—de novo for legal conclusions, substantial‑evidence for factual findings—and how it draws the line between a vacatur that clears the way for a fresh analysis and an affirmation that leaves the Board’s reasoning untouched.  Below, we walk through the opinion’s procedural anatomy, flag the take‑aways for trademark practitioners, and consider how this decision might shape future appellate practice before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB).

### Why the case matters  

The Federal Circuit’s holding does not create a new substantive test for likelihood of confusion; rather, it clarifies *how* the appellate court will police the Board’s application of the DuPont factors.  By affirming the Board’s finding on the second factor (the similarity of goods and services) while vacating its conclusions on the first and sixth factors, the court sends a precise message: **legal conclusions are yours to get right on the record, but factual findings must be backed by “substantial evidence” and cannot be reshaped by an overly narrow interpretation of the same fact set.**  For practitioners, the decision is a reminder that a well‑crafted record on the merits of similarity will anchor the Board’s analysis across multiple factors, and that an appeal that merely attacks the Board’s *conclusions* without pointing to a record deficiency will likely be struck.

### De novo review of legal conclusions  

The opinion opens with a quotation from *M2 Software*, reminding readers that the Federal Circuit “review[s] the Board’s legal conclusions de novo.”  This is the standard that applies to each of the DuPont factors themselves, which are *questions of law*—the weight to be given to a particular factor, the correct legal definition of “similar goods,” or the proper approach to “commercial impression.”  

In Apex, the court treated the Board’s analysis of the second factor (goods similarity) as a legal determination.  The Board had already defined “credit card services” and, relying on dictionary definitions, concluded that those services overlap with “banking and financing” because both involve extending credit.  The court found the Board’s legal reasoning sound and affirmed, emphasizing that the Board’s interpretation of the statutory language was *reasonable* and supported by the record.  

**Take‑away:** When you argue a legal point—say, that “credit card services” are not “banking services” for purposes of the Lanham Act—you must do more than point to the statutory text; you need to embed that argument in a factual foundation that survives the “substantial evidence” filter.  A purely doctrinal argument unsupported by the record will likely be dismissed on the merits of de novo review.

### Substantial‑evidence review of factual findings  

The other side of the appellate scalpel is the “substantial evidence” standard for factual findings.  The court reiterated that “substantial evidence” is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.”  This low‑threshold test is not a rubber‑stamp, but it does require the Board to have a factual scaffold that can’t be easily toppled.  

**Factor six** illustrates the point.  The Board’s factual finding—that the “relevant public” was limited to “consumers of credit card services”—was unsupported by the broader record the parties had supplied.  Apex showed the Board 42 additional registrations that used “Aspire” in the broader financial‑services arena, yet the Board excluded them on a *legal* ground that the marks were “essentially irrelevant.”  The court called that approach a “legal error” that contaminated the factual analysis, vacated the factor, and remanded for a reconsideration that respects the factual finding of high similarity already made under factor two.  

In other words, once the Board determines that the services are “highly similar,” it cannot later *shrink* the universe of comparable marks to a narrower subset without a factual justification.  The court requires consistency across the factors; the factual premise that the services are similar must inform the assessment of third‑party mark density.

**Take‑away:** Make sure the factual record you lay at the TTAB is *comprehensive* and *coherent* across all the DuPont factors.  If you can show that the public for whom the marks are relevant is broader than the Board assumes, you force the Board to consider a larger set of comparable marks—potentially weakening the senior mark’s claim to a “normal scope” of protection.

### Vacatur versus affirmation: strategic implications  

The decision’s split outcome—affirming factor two, vacating factors one and six—highlights the strategic nuance of appellate relief.  A vacatur erases the Board’s finding, sending the matter back for fresh analysis; an affirmation leaves the Board’s reasoning intact.  Importantly, the court did **not** vacate the entire opposition.  By preserving the Board’s determination that the services are “highly similar,” the Federal Circuit kept the *core* of the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis alive while giving the Board a chance to revisit the peripheral but still critical factors.

For litigants, this underscores the importance of *targeted* appeals.  If you can show that the Board erred on a specific factor—perhaps because you have additional evidence of market segmentation or a plethora of third‑party uses—you should focus your brief on that factor, anticipating that the court may only vacate the offending portion.  Conversely, a blanket challenge that the Board “got it all wrong” is less likely to succeed, especially where the record supports at least one factor.

### Doctrinal ripple effects  

While the case does not overturn any precedent on the DuPont test, it does reinforce two doctrinal trends:

1. **Uniform application of factual findings across factors.**  The court’s insistence that the Board’s similarity finding must permeate the analysis of the sixth factor signals a move toward *holistic* factor weighting, discouraging Boards from compartmentalizing facts for each factor.

2. **Broadening the “similar goods” inquiry.**  By criticizing the Board’s narrow definition of the “relevant public” and its exclusion of marks outside the credit‑card niche, the opinion aligns with earlier Federal Circuit cases that have emphasized a flexible, market‑based view of “similar goods.”  Practitioners should be prepared to argue that a mark’s reach extends beyond a single subcategory when the services overlap substantially.

### Practical lessons for trademark practitioners  

1. **Build a record that speaks to every factor you anticipate.**  In the Apex docket, Apex prepared a substantial exhibit list of third‑party “Aspire” marks.  The Board’s failure to consider them was a reversible error because the factual foundation existed.  Bring every piece of evidence you think may matter for *any* DuPont factor into the TTAB record; the appellate court will not allow you to cherry‑pick later.

2. **Frame similarity arguments in both legal and factual terms.**  The Board’s successful legal analysis of factor two relied on dictionary definitions and industry practice.  Replicating that approach—citing authoritative sources that define the services as overlapping—will fortify your legal conclusions against de novo review.

3. **Anticipate the “relevant public” analysis.**  The appellate court reminded us that the public’s perception cannot be limited mechanically.  If your mark operates in a broader financial context, submit market surveys, consumer testimony, or advertising examples that demonstrate the wider audience.

4. **Separate “legal error” from “insufficient evidence.”**  The court vacated the sixth factor because the Board’s *legal* restriction of the relevant public conflicted with the factual findings, not because the evidence was lacking.  When drafting appellate briefs, make clear whether you are arguing that the Board erred in law, misapplied the factual record, or both.

### Looking ahead  

Apex Bank v. CC Serve will likely be cited in future TTAB appeals as a benchmark for the interplay between de novo legal review and substantial‑evidence factual review.  Its emphasis on consistent factual premises across the DuPont factors may prompt TTAB examiners and members to adopt a more integrated approach when weighing the thirteen factors, reducing the chances that a party can “win” on a narrow slice of the analysis while the rest of the record remains unchallenged.

For trademark counsel, the case is a procedural primer: **craft a robust, cross‑factor record; anchor your legal arguments in that record; and be prepared to argue that the Board’s discretion is bounded not only by the law but by the very facts it has already found.**  In the end, the appellate court’s toolbox is simple—de novo for law, substantial evidence for fact—but wielding it effectively requires the same meticulous preparation that the TTAB demands at the trial level.
