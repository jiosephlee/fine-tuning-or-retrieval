Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Governing Law – Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Framework and Review Standards  

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit examined the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (TTAB) likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis under the Lanham Act.  The court’s discussion provides a clear illustration of the legal concepts that govern trademark opposition disputes, the structure of the DuPont factor test, and the standards of review the appellate court applies when assessing the Board’s conclusions. This chapter unpacks those concepts for the student reader, showing how they operate in practice and why the court reached the specific procedural holdings in this case.

## 2.1. Likelihood of Confusion as a Question of Law Grounded in Factual Findings  

The Lanham Act makes clear that a refusal to register a mark under §2(d) “requires registration refusal when **confusion is likely** because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.”  *Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d at 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973).  The Supreme Court and the Federal Circuit have consistently treated the **likelihood of confusion** as a **question of law** that must be resolved by weighing a set of underlying **facts**.  See *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Communications, Inc.*, 450 F.3d at 1381 (likelihood of confusion “a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts”).  

In practice, this means that the appellate court must accept the Board’s factual determinations if they are supported by **substantial evidence**, but it must review the Board’s legal conclusions—such as the weighting of each factor—**de novo** (i.e., without deference).  The *Apex Bank* opinion explicitly restates this dual‑track approach:  

> “We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382.  

Thus, the likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry is a hybrid: the court looks first to the factual record (e.g., the services offered, the similarity of the marks, the marketplace conditions) and then applies the law (the DuPont factors and how they are weighed) to reach a legal conclusion.  

## 2.2. The Thirteen DuPont Factors – Purpose and Typical Application  

The Federal Circuit derives its analytical framework from the **DuPont factors**, a thirteen‑factor test first articulated in *In re E. I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d at 1359 (C.C.P.A. 1973).  The factors are **not a checklist**; rather, they are **considerations** that the Board (or the court) may evaluate, each of which asks a factual question.  The factors are:

1. **Similarity of the marks** (appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression).  
2. **Similarity of the goods/services**.  
3. **Strength of the senior mark**.  
4. **Proximity of the parties’ actual and intended markets**.  
5. **Evidence of actual confusion**.  
6. **Number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods**.  
7. **Marketing channels used**.  
8. **Likelihood of expansion of the product lines**.  
9. **Degree of care exercised by the purchasing public**.  
10. **Consumer sophistication**.  
11. **Presence or absence of a “classic” complexion** (no direct counterpart in modern cases).  
12. **Defendant’s intent** in adopting the mark.  
13. **Likelihood of dilution** (in newer jurisprudence, often treated separately).  

The purpose of the test is to gauge whether ordinary consumers, exercising ordinary care, are likely to **mistake the source** of the goods or services.  Because the test is fact‑driven, the Board must develop a **record** that addresses each factor it elects to consider.  An absent factor is not fatal; the Board may waive a factor if it is **irrelevant** or **unsupported by the record**.  See *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382 (the Board need not consider every factor, only those “that are relevant and of record”).

In *Apex Bank*, the Board focused on factors **2, 6, and 1**.  The court affirmed the Board’s finding on factor 2 (similarity of services), but vacated its conclusions on factors 6 and 1, finding procedural error in how the Board defined the relevant market for those factors.

## 2.3. When a TTAB Must Consider Each Factor – Relevance and Record‑Keeping Requirements  

Although the DuPont factors collectively form the analytical scaffold, the TTAB is **not required** to weigh every factor in every case.  The Board must first determine **whether a factor is “relevant”** to the particular dispute.  Relevance is judged by the **nature of the marks and the goods/services**.  For example, “likelihood of expansion” (factor 8) may be irrelevant if the parties’ offerings are static and clearly delineated.

Once a factor is deemed relevant, the Board must **build a factual record** sufficient to support its weighting.  The Federal Circuit requires **substantial evidence** of the underlying facts for each factor that the Board relies upon.  In *Apex Bank*, the court found that the Board’s factual findings on factor 2 were supported by substantial evidence (the Board’s detailed definition of “credit card services” and its reliance on dictionary definitions of “banking” and “finance”).  By contrast, the Board’s handling of factor 6 was deemed defective because it **restricted the scope of “similar goods”** to a subset (credit‑card services only) despite an earlier factual finding that the services were “highly similar” overall.  The court held that the Board’s narrower definition was **legally erroneous**, not merely a failure to produce evidence.

Thus, the TTAB’s duty is two‑fold:

1. **Identify** which factors are relevant to the particular opposition.  
2. **Create a record** that demonstrates, with substantial evidence, how each considered factor weighs for or against confusion.

If the Board either **omits a relevant factor** or **fails to support its weighting with adequate evidence**, an appellate court may vacate that portion of the decision, as occurred with factors 6 and 1 in this case.

## 2.4. Appellate Standards: De Novo Review of Legal Conclusions; Substantial Evidence for Fact Findings  

When a trademark opposition is appealed, the Federal Circuit applies a **mixed standard of review**:

* **Legal conclusions**—the Board’s interpretation of the Lanham Act, the weighting of the DuPont factors, the determination of “similarity” in the legal sense—are reviewed **de novo**.  The appellate court conducts its own independent analysis, free from deference.  See *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382 (“We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo”).  

* **Findings of fact**—such as the description of the parties’ services, the existence of third‑party marks, or evidence of actual confusion—are reviewed for **substantial evidence**.  Substantial evidence is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.”  *Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. at 229.  

In *Apex Bank*, the court applied this framework as follows:

1. **Factor 2 (Similarity of Services)** – The Board’s factual findings (the services’ definitions) were upheld because they were supported by substantial evidence.  The legal conclusion that the similarity “weighed heavily” in favor of confusion was reviewed de novo, and the court agreed.  

2. **Factor 6 (Number and Nature of Similar Marks)** – The Board’s legal conclusion that the relevant public was limited to “credit‑card consumers” was deemed a **legal error** because it misapplied the factual finding from factor 2.  Consequently, the court vacated the Board’s conclusion on factor 6 and remanded for a proper analysis.  

3. **Factor 1 (Similarity of the Marks)** – Because factor 6’s error could affect the overall commercial impression of the marks, the court also vacated the Board’s analysis of factor 1.  This demonstrates the **interconnectedness** of the factors: a mistake on one can ripple through the others, warranting a fresh review.  

The mixed standard ensures that the Board’s **factual expertise** is respected while preserving **judicial control** over the legal application of trademark law.

## 2.5. Prior Federal Circuit Precedent on the Interplay of the Second, Sixth, and First Factors  

The *Apex Bank* decision is not an isolated statement on factor interplay; it builds on a line of Federal Circuit precedent that treats the DuPont factors as a **coherent, interdependent set**.

* **Second Factor (Similarity of Goods/Services)** – The court has long recognized that similarity of the underlying offerings is a **gate‑keeping** consideration.  When the goods are deemed “highly similar,” the Board must consider the possibility of a single source, and this finding influences the analysis of other factors.  *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1381 (likelihood of confusion “a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors”).

* **Sixth Factor (Similar Marks on Similar Goods)** – The Sixth factor looks to the **crowdedness of the marketplace**.  In *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc.*, 961 F.2d at 204, the court explained that a “large number of similar marks on similar goods” shows that the senior mark is **weak** and therefore entitled to a **narrower scope** of protection.  However, the factor **does not require identical goods**; similarity is sufficient.  The *Apex Bank* court emphasized this point, holding that the Board’s limitation to “credit‑card services” was an impermissibly narrow view of similarity, given the earlier finding that the parties’ services were “partially legally identical.”

* **First Factor (Overall Commercial Impression of the Marks)** – The First factor is often the **culminating factor** because it integrates the other factors into a judgment about the **overall likelihood of confusion**.  In *In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC*, 866 F.3d at 1323, the court stressed that the commercial impression “informs” the overall similarity analysis.  Because the Sixth factor can affect the **strength or weakness** of the senior mark, an error there can alter the commercial impression, necessitating a new First‑factor analysis.  The *Apex Bank* court applied this logic, vacating the First‑factor findings on the basis that the Sixth‑factor error could change the perception of the senior mark’s strength.

Collectively, these precedents illustrate a **principle of consistency**: once the Board makes a factual finding on the similarity of the goods (factor 2), that same factual regime should inform the analysis of the marketplace’s crowdedness (factor 6) and, consequently, the overall commercial impression (factor 1).  A failure to maintain this consistency—such as narrowing the scope of “similar goods” for factor 6 while having already held the services are “highly similar” for factor 2—constitutes a legal error warranting vacatur and remand.

---

### Take‑aways for the Student  

1. **Likelihood of confusion** is a legal conclusion that rests on factual foundations; appellate courts respect factual findings if supported by substantial evidence but re‑evaluate the legal reasoning.  
2. The **DuPont factors** are a flexible, fact‑driven toolbox; the Board must consider only those factors that are **relevant and supported by the record**.  
3. **Consistency** across factors is essential. A factual determination made under one factor (e.g., high similarity of services under factor 2) must be applied uniformly when the Board examines other factors that depend on the same factual premise (e.g., the marketplace analysis under factor 6).  
4. **De novo review** applies to the Board’s legal judgments—how it weighs the factors—while **substantial‑evidence review** protects the Board’s factual findings from being overturned absent a clear evidentiary deficiency.  
5. Prior Federal Circuit cases provide a roadmap for understanding how the Second, Sixth, and First factors interrelate; *Apex Bank* reinforces that misapplying the scope of similarity in one factor can cascade into errors in the others, justifying vacatur and remand.

By mastering these principles, a law student will be equipped to read future trademark opinions with a clear sense of **what the appellate court is reviewing, how it applies the DuPont framework, and why certain findings survive while others are sent back to the TTAB**.
