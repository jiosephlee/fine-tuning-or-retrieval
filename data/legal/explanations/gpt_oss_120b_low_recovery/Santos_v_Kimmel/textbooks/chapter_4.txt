Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 4: # Legal Reasoning Framework: From Pleadings to Disposition  

The Second Circuit’s opinion in **Santos v. Kimmel** illustrates the disciplined, step‑by‑step analysis a federal appellate court uses when it reviews a district court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal.  The opinion walks the reader through the standards of review, the sources of factual material, the application of the “reasonable observer” test to the fair‑use analysis, the balancing of the four statutory factors at the pleading stage, the treatment of preemption and third‑party‑beneficiary defenses, and finally the disposition of the case.  Understanding each of these steps helps a law student see how appellate courts move from the bare allegations in a complaint to a final affirmation (or reversal) of a dismissal.

## Standard of Review for Rule 12(b)(6) Motions – De Novo, Sufficiency of the Complaint  

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion challenges the legal sufficiency of a complaint.  The appellate court reviews the district court’s decision **de novo**—that is, it considers the matter anew, without deference to the trial court’s conclusions.  The Supreme Court articulated the modern standard in *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*: the complaint must contain “plausible” factual allegations that, when taken as true, entitle the plaintiff to relief.  The Second Circuit follows this guidance, as noted in *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d at 110, emphasizing that the court must look at “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached … and documents incorporated by reference.”  The appellate judge therefore asks two questions:

1. **Did the plaintiff plead facts sufficient to state a claim?**  
2. **If the pleadings are taken as true, does the law support a claim?**  

If the answer to either question is no, the complaint may be dismissed outright, even before discovery.

## Use of the Complaint, Exhibits, and Incorporated Documents as the Factual Foundation  

Because the court cannot conduct discovery at the pleading stage, it must rely on the plaintiff’s own allegations and any accompanying documents.  In *Santos*, the panel expressly adopted the “complaint‑centric” approach: the factual record is limited to the narrative in the pleading, the exhibits (the videos posted on Cameo, the copyright registrations), and any documents the plaintiff incorporated by reference (e.g., the Cameo Terms of Service).  The court may also take **judicial notice** of publicly available registrations in the Copyright Office’s record, as the Second Circuit has done in *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 413 F.3d at 261.  No extrinsic evidence is permitted; the analysis must be confined to what the plaintiff has already put forward.

## Application of the “Reasonable Observer” Test to the Purpose‑and‑Character Factor  

The first fair‑use factor asks whether the secondary use is “transformative.”  The Second Circuit has clarified that transformation is measured by the perspective of a **reasonable observer**, not by the subjective intent of either the copyright holder or the secondary user.  This principle appears in *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. at 544.  In *Santos*, the court rejected the plaintiff’s argument that Kimmel’s parody “served the same purpose” as the original videos because the plaintiff himself had intended the videos to demonstrate “hope… and perseverance.”  The court held that a reasonable viewer of the broadcast segment would perceive it primarily as **satirical commentary** on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—different from the inspirational tone of the original works.  Consequently, the purpose‑and‑character factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use.”

### Why Subjective Intent Is Irrelevant  

Even though Kimmel allegedly acted in “bad faith” by misrepresenting his identity on Cameo, the Supreme Court’s *Harper & Row* language that “fair use presupposes good faith” has been narrowed in subsequent cases.  The Second Circuit noted that later decisions, such as *Swatch Group Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, treat good‑faith considerations as peripheral when the core “transformative” inquiry is satisfied.  Thus, the court focused on the **objective effect** of the use on the audience, not on Kimmel’s internal motivations.

## Balancing the Four Fair‑Use Factors at the Pleading Stage  

Although the statutory fair‑use analysis is traditionally a “four‑factor” test, the Second Circuit recognizes that at the pleading stage the court may decide the issue based solely on the plaintiff’s allegations.  The opinion cites *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, which permits dismissal when the fair‑use defense is “clearly established” by the complaint.  The panel applied each factor as follows:

1. **Purpose and Character** – Transformative parody; strong support for fair use.  
2. **Nature of the Copyrighted Work** – The original videos are creative, which ordinarily leans against fair use, but this factor is weighted less when the secondary use is highly transformative.  
3. **Amount and Substantiality** – Kimmel used the entire video, but the court noted that parody may appropriate the whole work if necessary to convey its critique, following *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc.*.  
4. **Effect on the Market** – The complaint showed no evidence that the parody served as a market substitute for the original inspirational videos.  The panel emphasized that potential licensing markets for parody are “nonexistent,” a point reinforced by *Campbell* and *Authors Guild v. Google*.

Because the first factor alone tipped the balance heavily in favor of fair use, and the remaining three did not counteract that effect, the court concluded that the fair‑use defense was “clearly established” and therefore the copyright claims could be dismissed on a Rule 12(b)(6) basis.

## Assessment of Preemption and Third‑Party‑Beneficiary Defenses as Matters of Law  

The plaintiff also asserted state‑law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.  The Second Circuit treated the preemption analysis as **purely legal**: if a state claim is “virtually identical” to a copyright claim, the Copyright Act preempts it under 17 U.S.C. § 301.  However, the court declared that it need not resolve the preemption question because the plaintiff’s contract claims **failed to state a claim** under state law.

### Third‑Party‑Beneficiary Analysis  

Santos relied on the Cameo Terms of Service, arguing that the defendants breached obligations owed to him as a third‑party beneficiary.  The court applied two jurisdiction‑specific principles:

* **Illinois law** (governing the Terms of Service) creates a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties,” requiring an “express declaration” that the contract was intended to benefit the plaintiff.  ( *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.* ).  
* **New York law** (governing the implied‑contract claim) demands that the plaintiff allege the essential terms of the contract and show a “meeting of the minds.”  The complaint’s allegations were conclusory and omitted the requisite specificity ( *Caniglia*; *DG & A Mgmt. Servs.* ).

Because the plaintiff did not satisfy either jurisdiction’s threshold for establishing a third‑party beneficiary relationship, the court dismissed the contract claims as a matter of law, irrespective of any preemption analysis.

## Disposition: Affirmation of Dismissal and Implications for Future Litigation  

Having found that (1) the copyright claims were barred by the fair‑use defense at the pleading stage, and (2) the state‑law claims either were preempted or failed to state a claim, the Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s **final judgment of dismissal**.  The affirmation is a **summary judgment** on the merits of the claims, not merely a procedural dismissal; the appellate court’s analysis resolves the substantive issues.

### Practical Implications  

1. **Pleading Adequacy** – Plaintiffs must articulate why a use is *not* transformative and must identify a concrete market harm.  Vague assertions that “the purpose was the same” are insufficient.  
2. **Document Reliance** – At the pleading stage, a plaintiff may rely only on the complaint and attached documents.  Incorporating external facts requires either a pleading allegation or a properly attached exhibit.  
3. **Preemption Defense** – Even if a plaintiff frames a claim in state‑law terminology, the court will strip away any “virtually identical” elements to the copyright claim and may preempt the claim outright.  
4. **Third‑Party Beneficiary Claims** – To succeed, a plaintiff must point to an express third‑party beneficiary clause or, under New York law, allege the essential contract terms and a clear meeting of the minds.  General assertions that a contract “applies” to non‑signatories will not survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.  

In sum, **Santos v. Kimmel** offers a clear template: the appellate court begins with a de novo review of the pleading’s sufficiency, constructs the factual record from the complaint and its attachments, applies the reasonable‑observer standard to the fair‑use analysis, balances the statutory factors without venturing beyond the pleadings, treats preemption and third‑party‑beneficiary issues as pure questions of law, and then either affirms or reverses the district court’s dismissal.  Mastery of this logical framework equips a law student to navigate Rule 12(b)(6) practice and to anticipate the evidentiary and legal hurdles that lie ahead in copyright and contract litigation.
