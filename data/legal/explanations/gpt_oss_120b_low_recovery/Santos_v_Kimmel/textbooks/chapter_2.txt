Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # Copyright Infringement Claim and the Fair‑Use Analysis  

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of George Santos’s copyright‑infringement claims on the ground that the defendants’ use of the videos he created for the Came Cameo platform was protected by the fair‑use defense.  The opinion walks the reader through each of the four statutory factors of 17 U.S.C. § 107, shows how the court applied them, and explains why a fair‑use defense can be decisive even at the pleading stage.  The discussion below breaks down the court’s analysis and the legal principles that underlie it, keeping the focus on the facts and reasoning in *Santos*.

## The Fair‑Use Defense under 17 U.S.C. § 107  

Section 107 codifies a flexible, equitable defense that permits “otherwise infringing” uses when they serve socially valuable purposes such as criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, or parody.  The statute enumerates four non‑exclusive factors that a court must balance:

1. **Purpose and character of the use** (including whether the use is transformative).  
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work**.  
3. **Amount and substantiality of the portion used**.  
4. **Effect of the use on the market for the original work**.  

The Second Circuit repeatedly stresses that the factors are not a checklist; they are considered holistically, and the outcome can hinge on the “totality of the circumstances” (see *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024)).  Moreover, the court may determine that fair use “is so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright‑infringement claim” at the pleading stage (see *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 178 (2d Cir. 2016)).  This de novo standard means the appellate court reviews the district court’s 12(b)(6) dismissal without deference to the lower court’s factual findings, focusing instead on the pleadings, attached exhibits, and any incorporated documents.

## Purpose and Character of the Use – Transformation, Parody, and Commentary  

The first factor asks whether the second work merely supersedes the original or adds something new—i.e., whether it is **transformative**.  Transformation is assessed by asking what a reasonable observer would perceive, not by looking at the subjective intent of either party (see *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023)).  

In *Santos*, the defendants’ videos were aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a recurring segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The segment repeatedly showed Santos fulfilling absurd requests (e.g., shouting a nonsensical phrase for money) and then mocked him on‑air.  The district court—and ultimately the circuit—found that the purpose of the defendants’ use was **commentary and criticism** of Santos’s willingness to say “absurd things for money.”  The court rejected Santos’s argument that the same purpose—promoting “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity”—was also his original intent.  The Second Circuit emphasized that the transformation inquiry looks to the **reasonable observer**: a viewer of the *JKL* segment would see a satirical, critical take on Santos rather than a straightforward promotional message.

Parody is a subset of commentary that often satisfies the transformation requirement because it invokes the original work for the purpose of ridicule.  The court cited *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994), noting that a parody’s commentary on the original “removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”  Although the *Santos* videos were not a parody of a specific copyrighted work (they were original recordings made for Cameo), the *JKL* segments functioned as a **parodic commentary** on the content of those recordings.  Consequently, the first factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use.”

The court also addressed Santos’s allegation that Kimmel’s “false representations” demonstrated bad‑faith intent, which Santos claimed should defeat the fair‑use defense.  While *Harper & Row* once suggested that fair use “presupposes good faith,” later Second Circuit precedent has moved away from treating good‑faith as a decisive element (see *Swatch Group Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014)).  The court concluded that the alleged bad faith did not undermine the transformative, critical nature of the use.

## Nature of the Copyrighted Work  

The second factor considers whether the work is **published** or **unpublished**, and whether it is highly creative versus factual.  Works that are unpublished or highly creative receive stronger protection.  In *Santos*, the videos were created expressly for the Cameo platform and were **published** on the same day they were recorded, as shown by the registration exhibits (Appendix 160‑169).  Because the works were already in the public domain (in the sense that they were disseminated to customers), the factor weighed in favor of fair use.  Moreover, the videos were largely **creative**—they contained Santos’s performance—but the fact of prior publication reduces the weight of this factor, consistent with the Second Circuit’s approach in *TCA Television* (the court stressed that publication “lessens” the protection afforded by this factor).

## Amount and Substantiality of the Portion Used  

The third factor asks how much of the original work was taken and whether the portion used was the “heart” of the work.  The pleadings in *Santos* identified that the *JKL* segments incorporated the **entire** Cameo video into each broadcast.  At first glance, copying the whole work might weigh against fair use.  However, the court noted that in parody and commentary cases, taking the whole work can be justified when it is necessary to “convey its expressive qualities” (see *Campbell*, 510 U.S. at 592).  

Because the *JKL* segments sought to mock the very content of the videos, the complete duplication was deemed **necessary** to achieve the critical purpose.  The Second Circuit therefore found that the amount and substantiality factor did **not** outweigh the transformation found in the first factor, echoing the reasoning in *Cariou v. Prince*, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013), which held that the “necessity” of copying for a critical purpose can tip this factor in favor of fair use.

## Effect on the Market – Market Substitution Test and Parody Exception  

The fourth factor focuses on whether the secondary use usurps the market for the original work or harms its potential value.  The court distinguished two sub‑analyses:

1. **Direct market substitution** – does the secondary work serve as a competing product?  
2. **Potential licensing market** – would the copyright holder have been willing to license the secondary use for commentary or parody?

In *Santos*, the *JKL* broadcast did not offer a **substitute** for the Cameo videos; rather, it presented a satirical critique that likely **decreased** the market value of the original videos for a prospective buyer.  The court echoed *Campbell*’s observation that “creators of imaginative works will not license critical reviews or lampoons,” thereby signaling that there is **no viable licensing market** for the defendants’ use.  Accordingly, any alleged market harm was minimal and, if anything, the use could be viewed as a *market‑creating* activity for the defendants’ own show.

The court also pointed out that the plaintiff had **no evidence** of actual market damage or of a market for a non‑parodic, non‑critical version of the videos.  The absence of a demonstrable market for a “competing substitute” meant the fourth factor “does not bar” the fair‑use defense.  This aligns with the Second Circuit’s approach in *TCA Television* and *Cariou*, where the court found that speculative market harm is insufficient at the pleading stage.

## Reliance on Precedent and De Novo Review Standards  

*Sant*os* relied heavily on a line of Second Circuit cases that have shaped fair‑use analysis:

* **Warhol** – stressed that the *objective* view of a reasonable observer controls the transformation inquiry.  
* **Campbell** – articulated the parody exception and the “no licensing market” rationale.  
* **Cariou** – clarified that a court need only compare the *original* works with the *secondary* works, not conduct a full factual inquiry, at the pleading stage.  
* **TCA Television** – confirmed that fair use can be “clearly established” by the complaint, justifying a 12(b)(6) dismissal.  

Because the appellate court reviews a 12(b)(6) motion **de novo**, it looks at the pleadings, attached evidence, and any incorporated documents, applying the law afresh.  The Second Circuit’s standard requires that the plaintiff’s complaint contain **sufficient factual allegations** to make the claim plausible; if the asserted fair‑use factors, when taken together, point unmistakably toward a protected use, the claim may be dismissed without further fact‑finding.  In *Santos*, the court concluded that the plaintiff’s own allegations—particularly the commentary motive and the lack of market substitution—already established that fair use was “clearly” applicable.

## Why Fair Use Can Defeat a Claim at the Pleading Stage  

A plaintiff’s 12(b)(6) motion challenges the **legal sufficiency** of the complaint, not the merits of the case.  The fair‑use defense is a **complete defense**: if it is shown to apply, the plaintiff cannot recover even if infringement occurred.  The Second Circuit recognizes that the fair‑use inquiry is **often dispositive** on the pleadings because the plaintiff must allege facts that overcome the defense’s presumptive strengths.  When the plaintiff’s allegations, read in the light most favorable to the defendant, satisfy the statutory factors in the defendant’s favor, the court may conclude that the complaint “fails to state a claim” (see *TCA Television*, 839 F.3d at 178).

In *Santos*, the combination of (1) a transformative, critical purpose, (2) a published work, (3) a necessary full‑copy for parody, and (4) an absence of market substitution, collectively painted a picture that a **reasonable court** would view as classic fair use.  The court therefore held that even assuming the facts alleged were true, the fair‑use defense would likely defeat the copyright claim.  Under the *TCA* standard, that suffices to grant summary dismissal.

---  

By dissecting each statutory factor as applied in *Santos v. Kimmel*, this chapter illustrates how the Second Circuit balances the interests of copyright owners against the public’s interest in free expression, and why a strong fair‑use defense can extinguish a copyright claim before any trial‑level fact‑finding occurs.
