Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # State‑Law Claims: Contractual and Fraud Grounds  

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of all of former Congressman George Santos’s state‑law claims.  Although the appeal was framed as a challenge to the district court’s rulings, the appellate court’s analysis provides a clear illustration of three doctrinal areas that law students must master when evaluating state‑law causes of action that arise in the context of copyrighted works: (1) the preemption doctrine, (2) the third‑party‑beneficiary and implied‑in‑fact contract analyses, and (3) the pleading standards required to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion on a fraudulent‑inducement claim.  This chapter breaks down the court’s reasoning, explains the legal standards it applied, and shows how the cited precedent fits into each step of the analysis.  

---

## 1. Preemption of State Contract Claims by the Copyright Act  

### 1.1 The Preemption Rule  

Section 301 of the Copyright Act provides that *“all rights… that are equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright”* are preempted when they are covered by federal law.  The Supreme Court, in *Petrofina Corp. v. United States*, explained that preemption is triggered when a state claim “covers the same subject matter as a copyright claim and is “equivalently” regulated by the Act.”  The Second Circuit follows this approach, looking first to whether the state claim is “a claim that could be litigated in a federal copyright action.”  When the answer is yes, the state claim is barred, even if the plaintiff frames the dispute in contract terms.

### 1.2 Application to Santos’s Claims  

Santos asserted two contract theories arising from the Cameo platform’s Terms of Service (TOS):  

1. **Breach of contract** – alleging that Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney violated provisions of the TOS that govern how a creator may use the videos he supplied.  
2. **Breach of implied contract** – contending that, although not a signatory, he was entitled to contractual protections because the parties had “an intention” to treat him as a beneficiary of the TOS.  

The district court dismissed both claims on the ground of preemption.  The Second Circuit declined to revisit that ground because, even assuming the claims were not preempted, the pleadings failed to state a viable claim (see below).  Nonetheless, the court noted that the *California*‑style “contract‑based” claim would, in effect, be a claim that *“the defendants used the plaintiff’s copyrighted videos without permission”*—the very subject of a copyright infringement action.  Because the Copyright Act already governs the exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, and publicly display the videos, the state‑law contract claims were “functional equivalents” of a copyright claim and therefore preempted.  

The court’s brief discussion of preemption reflects the well‑established principle that a claimant cannot sidestep the exclusive federal scheme by couching the dispute in contract language.  See *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.*, 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (contract claim “wholly subsumed” by copyright).  In *Santos*, the appellate panel treated the preemption analysis as a threshold issue that, even if decided otherwise, would not rescue the state claims because of additional procedural deficiencies.

## 2. Third‑Party‑Beneficiary Analysis Under Illinois Law  

### 2.1 The General Presumption  

Illinois courts apply a strong presumption against the creation of enforceable rights for non‑signatories of a contract.  The presumption can be overcome only when the contract contains “express language” or “clear, unequivocal intent” that the parties intended to confer benefits on a third party.  *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016).  The presumption reflects the policy that contracts should bind only those who have bargained for the obligations.

### 2.2 How the Circuit Applied the Presumption  

Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claim rested on the Cameo TOS, which Kimmel agreed to when he created his “Will Santos Say It?” videos.  Santos argued that, because he was the creator of the underlying videos, the TOS’s provisions that protect “creators” should also protect him, even though he never signed the agreement.  

The Second Circuit rejected that inference.  First, the opinion emphasized that the TOS contains explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language for certain provisions, illustrating that the parties **did** use clear language when they intended to create enforceable rights for outsiders.  The absence of comparable language for the provisions Santos relied upon means the parties **did not** intend to extend those benefits to him.  Second, the court pointed to *Barry* and noted that a pleading must allege “an express declaration” that the contract applies to the non‑signatory.  Santos’s complaint merely alleged a vague “relationship” to the TOS, without any factual foundation that the parties intended to create enforceable rights for him.  

Because the presumption was not rebutted, the claim could not proceed under Illinois law.  The court therefore affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the breach‑of‑contract claim on the basis that Santos lacked standing to enforce the TOS as a third‑party beneficiary.

## 3. Implied‑in‑Fact Contracts Under New York Law  

### 3.1 Elements of an Implied‑In‑Fact Contract  

New York recognizes implied‑in‑fact contracts when the parties’ conduct demonstrates a “meeting of the minds” and the essential terms of the agreement can be inferred.  The standard requires:  

1. **Mutual assent** (a meeting of the minds).  
2. **Essential terms** that are sufficiently definite (e.g., price, subject matter, performance obligations).  
3. **A basis for liability**—the plaintiff must show that the defendant’s conduct was “reasonable to infer” the existence of a contract.  

*Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Grp., Inc.*, 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) sets out this framework.  Moreover, pleading rules demand that a complaint **allege the essential terms in non‑conclusory language**; speculative or conclusory statements are insufficient.  *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.*, 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994) emphasizes the need for detailed factual allegations supporting each element of the contract claim.  

### 3.2 The Court’s Reasoning on Santos’s Implied‑Contract Claim  

Santos argued that, although he never signed the Cameo TOS, the parties’ interaction—Kimmel requesting a video, Santos delivering it, and Kimmel subsequently broadcasting it—created an implied contract obligating Kimmel to compensate Santos or to refrain from using the video beyond the original purpose.  

The Second Circuit found the pleading deficient on three fronts:  

1. **Lack of a meeting of the minds.**  The complaint did not allege any specific communications indicating that Kimmel promised to respect Santos’s rights beyond the initial transaction.  The court noted that an implied contract cannot be inferred from a simple purchase‑like exchange; there must be an indication that the parties contemplated additional obligations.  

2. **Failure to state essential terms.**  Santos’s complaint merely alleged that “defendants breached the contract” without identifying the price, the scope of the license, duration, or any other term that would define the parties’ obligations.  As *Caniglia* teaches, “the complaint must set out the essential terms of the alleged contract in non‑conclusory language”—something the Santos pleading omitted.  

3. **Absence of factual support for liability.**  The pleading did not explain why a reasonable observer would infer that Kimmel intended to be bound by an implied agreement to limit the use of the videos.  The Second Circuit stressed that “the implication of a contract must be grounded in the parties’ conduct,” and the conduct at issue (Kimmel’s request and use of the videos) was insufficient to establish such an implication.  

Because the complaint failed to satisfy the pleading requirements for an implied‑in‑fact contract, the district court’s dismissal was affirmed.  The appellate court stressed that even if some factual basis existed, Santos would still need to amend the complaint to plausibly allege the essential terms and a clear meeting of the minds, which he did not provide.

## 4. Fraudulent Inducement – New York Elements and the Out‑of‑Pocket Loss Requirement  

### 4.1 Elements of Fraudulent Inducement in New York  

Under New York law, a plaintiff asserting fraudulent inducement must plead, with particularity, the following elements:  

1. **A false representation of a material fact** (or omission of a material fact).  
2. **Knowledge of its falsity** or reckless disregard for the truth.  
3. **Intent to induce reliance** by the plaintiff.  
4. **Justifiable reliance** by the plaintiff on the misrepresentation.  
5. **Actual damages**—specifically, **out‑of‑pocket loss** directly caused by the reliance.  

*Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996).  The damages element is not a speculative “harm to reputation” or “emotional distress”; the plaintiff must demonstrate a concrete monetary loss that resulted from the fraud.  *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142‑43 (2017) clarified that a plaintiff’s claim fails if it does not allege any actual out‑of‑pocket loss.

### 4.2 How the Court Evaluated Santos’s Allegations  

Santos claimed that Kimmel, through “false representations” about the purpose of the videos, fraudulently induced him to create content that he would not otherwise have produced.  The district court dismissed the claim for lack of a pleading on out‑of‑pocket loss; the Second Circuit affirmed.  

The appellate panel pointed out that Santos’s complaint did **not** allege any specific monetary loss:

* He did not assert that he incurred expenses (e.g., production costs) that he would not have otherwise incurred.  
* He failed to show that the broadcast of the videos caused a loss of future revenue, licensing opportunities, or any other identifiable financial detriment.  

Because the complaint omitted a concrete description of damages, it did not satisfy the “out‑of‑pocket loss” requirement.  The court therefore applied the *Lama* and *Connaughton* standards and concluded that the fraudulent‑inducement claim could not survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

## 5. Synthesis: Why Each State Claim Failed  

Putting the four analyses together, the Second Circuit’s reasoning can be summarized as follows:

| Claim | Key Legal Standard | How the Standard Was Applied |
|------|--------------------|------------------------------|
| **Breach of contract (Cameo TOS)** | Illinois third‑party‑beneficiary presumption; requires express language extending benefits to non‑signatory. | No express declaration; TOS expressly limited benefits to signatories; presumption remained intact. |
| **Breach of implied contract** | New York implied‑in‑fact contract: meeting of the minds, essential terms, factual basis; pleading must allege terms in non‑conclusive language. | No alleged meeting of the minds; no essential terms (price, scope, duration) pleaded; lack of factual support. |
| **Fraudulent inducement** | New York fraud: false statement, knowledge, intent, reliance, **out‑of‑pocket loss**. | No allegation of concrete monetary loss; damages element absent. |
| **Preemption** (underlying bar to both contract claims) | Copyright Act § 301 preempts state claims that are “equivalently” regulated by federal law. | Claims essentially sought to prohibit an unauthorized copy/derivative use, which is precisely the subject of copyright law. |

Because each claim either ran afoul of a substantive doctrinal bar (preemption) or failed to meet the pleading standards required for a viable state‑law cause of action, the district court’s dismissal was upheld.

## 7. Take‑aways for Students  

1. **Preemption is a substantive bar**: Even if a plaintiff can articulate a state‑law claim, the court will first ask whether the Copyright Act occupies the field.  When the claim is “functionally equivalent” to a copyright infringement action, it is preempted.  
2. **Third‑party‑beneficiary rights are narrow**: Under Illinois law, the default rule is that non‑signatories cannot enforce a contract absent clear, express language.  Plaintiffs must point to that language; vague references to “benefit” are insufficient.  
3. **Implied contracts demand precise pleading**: A New York implied‑in‑fact contract claim must identify the parties’ meeting of the minds and the essential terms—price, scope, duration, etc.—in a non‑conclusory manner.  Assertions of “an agreement” without factual detail will be struck.  
4. **Fraud claims require concrete loss**: New York courts will not allow a fraudulent‑inducement claim to proceed if the plaintiff cannot plausibly allege an out‑of‑pocket monetary loss caused by reliance.  Mere reputational or intangible harms do not satisfy the damages element.  
5. **Rule 12(b)(6) is a high hurdle at the pleading stage**: The court’s de novo review of the complaint focuses on whether the allegations, taken as true, state a claim that is plausible on its face.  If any of the doctrinal elements—preemption, third‑party status, essential terms, or loss—are missing, the claim is dismissed without reaching the merits.  

By dissecting *Santos v. Kimmel* through these lenses, law students can see how appellate courts blend substantive doctrine with procedural pleading rules to prune untenable state‑law claims that arise alongside federal copyright disputes.
