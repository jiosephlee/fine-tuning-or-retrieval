Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why can a non‑signatory like Santos not enforce the Cameo Terms of Service against Kimmel?
Question:
Santos alleged that Kimmel breached the Cameo platform’s Terms of Service, yet the court said Santos was not a party to that contract and Illinois law presumes against third‑party benefits. How does the “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties” operate, and are there any circumstances where a third party could still enforce such terms?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Because Santos never agreed to the Came‑on Terms of Service, he cannot invoke them as a contract‑right holder. Illinois courts apply a “strong presumption against conferring benefits on non‑contracting third parties,” which means that a contract will be enforced only for a third party if the parties’ intent to create a third‑party‑beneficiary relationship is **express** or **so clear as to be equivalent to an express declaration**. The Second Circuit follows that rule in *Santos v. Kimmel* and finds no such intent in the Came‑on agreement, so Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claim fails.  

**How the presumption works (as applied in *Santos*)**

1. **Governing law** – The Terms of Service (ToS) designate Illinois law as controlling. Illinois case law (e.g., *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d at 976) holds that, *absent a clear intent to benefit a non‑signatory, courts will not stretch a contract to cover that person*.  

2. **Express or “practically express” intent required** – The presumption is rebutted only if the contract contains language that “is so strong as to be practically an express declaration” that the third party may enforce the provision. The court emphasizes that *the burden is on the alleged third‑party beneficiary to point to that language*.

3. **Application to the Came‑on ToS** – The opinion notes that the ToS **does contain third‑party‑beneficiary clauses**, but they are limited to the parties the drafter explicitly named. Those clauses demonstrate that when the drafters *wanted* a third party to enforce a provision, they **did so expressly**. Because the ToS contains no comparable language extending any benefit to “the content creator” (i.e., Santos), the presumption remains intact and Santos cannot claim a contractual right.

4. **No implied‑in‑fact contract** – Even under New York law (which governs the alleged breach claim), a plaintiff must plead the essential terms of the contract and show a “meeting of the minds.” The court finds Santos’s complaint silent on those points, reinforcing that the presumption was not overcome.

**When a non‑signatory can still enforce a contract**

The *Santos* opinion, while focused on dismissing the claim, implicitly outlines the limited scenarios in which a third party may succeed:

| Situation | Why it defeats the presumption |
|-----------|--------------------------------|
| **Express third‑party‑beneficiary language** | If the contract says, e.g., “This agreement benefits *the creator of the video* and may be enforced by that creator,” the presumption is rebutted because the parties clearly intended to confer a benefit. |
| **“Practical” express declaration** | Even without the word “beneficiary,” language that unambiguously indicates the parties intended the contract to apply to a particular outsider (e.g., “Kimmel shall comply with all obligations set forth herein for the benefit of the video performer”) functions as a practical express statement. |
| **Statutory or regulatory schemes that incorporate third parties** | Some statutes (e.g., consumer‑protection laws) treat certain contracts as automatically benefiting a class of third parties. The presumption is displaced by the explicit statutory intent. |
| **Assignment or delegation** | If the contracting party expressly assigns its rights under the contract to the non‑signatory, the assignee can enforce those rights because the contract now includes the assignee as a party. |
| **Equitable doctrines (e.g., promissory estoppel) where reliance is shown** | While not a contract enforcement per se, a court may impose liability if the non‑signatory reasonably relied on a promise that the party making the promise intended to be binding. The *Santos* court did not reach this because Santos offered no such allegation. |

**Key take‑aways for a law student**

1. **Identify the governing law** – The presumption is a matter of state contract law; the opinion applies Illinois law because the ToS chose it.  
2. **Look for express third‑party language** – The mere existence of a contract does not create rights for outsiders; you must find a clear, unambiguous clause that names the outsider or otherwise makes the benefit “practically express.”  
3. **Assess pleading sufficiency** – Even if the contract could, in theory, benefit a third party, a plaintiff must still plead the essential terms and show a meeting of the minds; *Santos* failed on that front.  
4. **Remember the limited “exceptional” routes** – Assignments, statutory schemes, or equitable reliance may rescue a claim, but those are fact‑specific and not present here.

In sum, the “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties” operates as a default rule that keeps contracts *between* the signatories. Only an express, or effectively express, declaration by the parties can overcome it, and *Santos v. Kimmel* finds none in the Came‑on Terms of Service, so Santos has no enforceable contract claim against Kimmel.
