Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What is the significance of the court’s citation to Harper & Row’s “good‑faith” comment in modern fair‑use doctrine?
Question:
The opinion discusses that the Supreme Court’s statement that fair use presumes good faith has been “distanced from” in later cases. How have courts treated the good‑faith requirement in recent fair‑use jurisprudence, and does bad‑faith conduct ever defeat a fair‑use defense?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 WL 5551234 (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025), makes clear that the Supreme Court’s off‑hand remark in *Harper & Row* that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” is no longer a controlling element of the fair‑use analysis.  

### How the court treats the “good‑faith” comment today  

1. **Citation as historical background, not a required factor** – Judge Lohier acknowledges that *Harper & Row* “stated … that fair use presupposes good faith,” but then notes that “the Court has since distanced itself from this statement.” The opinion does not treat good‑faith as a separate prong of the § 107 inquiry; instead it relies on the ordinary four‑factor test.  

2. **Focus on the statutory factors** – The court’s discussion of the first factor (“purpose and character of the use”) centres on *transformativity* and the perspective of a “reasonable observer,” citing *Andy Warhol Found.* and *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose*. Nothing in the analysis ties the outcome to the parties’ subjective good‑faith or lack thereof.  

3. **No “bad‑faith” bar to fair use** – Santos argued that Kimmel’s “false representations” showed bad faith and should defeat the defense. The court expressly rejects that contention:  

   > “We disagree. … It is true that the Supreme Court stated … ‘fair use presupposes good faith…’ But the Court has since distanced itself from this statement.”  

   The opinion then proceeds to affirm the fair‑use finding on the basis of the four statutory factors, not because Kimmel acted in good faith.  

### Does bad‑faith conduct ever defeat a fair‑use defense?

*Santos* indicates that, at least in the Second Circuit, **bad‑faith conduct alone is insufficient to defeat a fair‑use defense**. The court treats *Harper & Row*’s good‑faith remark as dicta that has been “distanced from” by later precedent, and it requires no showing of good‑faith to satisfy any of the four § 107 factors.  

Other recent Second Circuit cases echo this approach:

* ***Authors Guild v. Google* (2015)** – the court held that Google’s digitization was fair use despite the fact that the publisher may have objected in bad‑faith, focusing instead on transformative purpose and market effect.  
* ***Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive* (2024)** – the court again emphasized the four‑factor analysis and did not treat the plaintiff’s alleged “bad‑faith” claim of “systematic piracy” as a separate defeat of fair use.

Thus, modern fair‑use jurisprudence treats *Harper & Row*’s good‑faith language as an historical footnote. A party’s bad‑faith motives may be considered **only insofar as they affect the four statutory factors** (e.g., by showing a commercial motive that weighs against transformation or market harm), but they do not create an independent “good‑faith” requirement that, if violated, automatically bars the defense.
