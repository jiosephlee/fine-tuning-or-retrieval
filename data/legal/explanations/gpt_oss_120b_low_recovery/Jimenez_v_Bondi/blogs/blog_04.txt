Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

## Safe Relocation Myths: How the Tenth Circuit Interprets Internal Relocation Feasibility

### Why Jimenez v. Bondi Matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* (2025) may look, at first glance, like a routine denial of asylum. Yet the opinion contains a surprisingly terse treatment of one of the most consequential gate‑keeping doctrines in refugee law: the internal relocation requirement. The court brushed past a three‑week, rural “hide‑away” in the Bogota countryside as proof that the Jimenez family could safely relocate within Colombia, thereby closing the door on any future claim that they could not find safety at home. This shortcut has far‑reaching implications for how appellate courts across the nation will evaluate the “feasibility of internal relocation,” a doctrine that often decides the fate of asylum seekers before any merits are even considered.

### The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell

The Tenth Circuit affirmed the BIA’s denial of asylum and, consequently, withholding of removal. After finding that the petitioners failed to establish past persecution and a protected‑ground nexus, the court turned to the residual argument that the family could not safely relocate within Colombia. The opinion notes:

> “Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution, but even if they had, the record supports the finding that they could reasonably relocate to another part of Colombia. Petitioners’ family and relatives continue to live in Colombia unharmed, and Jimenez’s father still works for the criminal justice system. Notably, Petitioners safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to relocate to the United States.”

The panel treats the three‑week stay with Jimenez’s mother as a decisive factual predicate that the family could “mitigate any potential for future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia.” No detailed analysis of the specific threats they faced, the reach of “Don Rafa’s” guerrilla‑drug network, or the broader pattern of violence against Liberal Party activists in Bogota is offered. The decision leans heavily on the assumption that a short, temporary refuge equates to a viable long‑term relocation option.

### How This Fits Within Existing Tenth Circuit Jurisprudence

The Tenth Circuit has not always been so cavalier. In *Ritonga v. Holder* (2011), the court emphasized a “cumulative‑effects” approach, scrutinizing whether the totality of threats and attacks made relocation “unduly dangerous or impossible.” Likewise, *Karki v. Holder* (2013) rejected claims based solely on sporadic threats, but it also underscored that the *nature* of the threat matters—particularly when the persecutor enjoys governmental immunity or holds de facto control over a region.

*Jimenez* departs from that more nuanced line. The panel sidesteps a detailed examination of whether the guerrilla group aligned with President Petro can exert influence outside the immediate neighborhood. The court’s reliance on the mere fact that the family “safely lived” for three weeks in a rural area ignores the reality that internal relocation assessments traditionally require a factual showing that the applicant can *sustain* a safe existence over the long term, not simply survive a brief interlude.

### Contrasting with Other Circuits

Other appellate courts have demanded more rigorous proof. The Ninth Circuit, in *Pérez v. INS* (2022), held that a two‑week stay with relatives in a different city did **not** satisfy internal relocation because the petitioners had no evidence of sustained police protection, employment, or housing. The court required concrete evidence—school enrollment for children, a stable income source, and documented police willingness to intervene—that the relocation could be maintained indefinitely.

Similarly, the Fourth Circuit in *Alvarez v. Garland* (2024) required petitioners to submit “relocation plans” that included specific addresses, corroborating statements from local authorities, and, where possible, medical or school records demonstrating the feasibility of a permanent move. The court stressed that short‑term safety “does not automatically translate into a viable long‑term relocation option.”

By contrast, *Jimenez* leans on a minimal factual baseline: the presence of family members in the countryside and a brief episode of temporary shelter. The Tenth Circuit’s approach therefore lowers the evidentiary bar, potentially making it easier for immigration judges and the BIA to dismiss internal‑relocation defenses without demanding the kind of detailed relocation portfolios that have become standard in many districts.

### Practical Hurdles for Asylum Seekers

The internal relocation doctrine, while doctrinally straightforward, is a nightmare in practice. Asylum seekers must often assemble a packet of evidence that includes:

1. **Local contacts**—letters or affidavits from relatives, community leaders, or NGOs attesting to their willingness to host the applicant.
2. **Employment prospects**—job offers, proof of transferable skills, or evidence that the applicant can sustain a livelihood.
3. **Safety assurances**—official statements from police or municipal authorities indicating they will provide protection, or at least will not collude with persecutors.
4. **Educational arrangements**—school enrollment confirmations for minor children.
5. **Housing documentation**—leases, property deeds, or other proof that the applicant can secure stable shelter.

For many who flee violent political parties or organized crime, such documentation is impossible to obtain. Fear of retaliation often silences family members; criminal organizations may control entire neighborhoods; and governmental entities may be either complicit or too corrupt to provide reliable assurances. Yet *Jimenez* suggests that a brief, undocumented stay with a relative suffices, implicitly encouraging asylum officers to discount the practical impossibility of gathering the “full” relocation package.

### Doctrinal Implications

1. **Erosion of the “Safe Relocation” Standard** – By treating a three‑week, informal stay as conclusive proof of relocation feasibility, the Tenth Circuit may set a precedent that lowers the evidentiary threshold across the circuit. This could lead to a wave of dismissals that hinge on “minimal” relocation evidence, effectively narrowing the protection afforded to those who genuinely cannot find safety at home.

2. **Potential Conflict with the Refugee Convention** – Article 33(1) of the 1951 Refugee Convention obligates signatories “not to expel or return a refugee…to a territory where his life or freedom would be threatened.” If courts accept a superficial relocation analysis, they risk treating the “internal relocation” bar as a loophole that undermines the convention’s protective purpose.

3. **Impact on Withholding of Removal and CAT Claims** – The *Jimenez* opinion treats asylum and withholding of removal as a single analytical strand, noting that failing the asylum standard automatically defeats withholding. While procedurally sound, it ignores that the “well‑founded fear” threshold for withholding is *lower* than the “clear‑and‑convincing” standard for asylum. A more robust relocation analysis could have salvaged a withholding claim even if asylum was denied.

### Looking Ahead

If the Tenth Circuit’s interpretation spreads, asylum seekers may face a higher probability of denial on the relocation ground, even when credible evidence suggests that the persecutors’ reach extends beyond a single neighborhood. Defense counsel will need to develop new strategies: perhaps leveraging expert testimony on the geography of criminal networks, or furnishing more granular proof of the impossibility of long‑term residence in any Colombian locality.

Litigants and advocates should watch for how lower courts in Colorado, Kansas, and New Mexico apply *Jimenez*. If appellate panels continue to endorse a “brief‑stay” rule, the practical effect will be a de‑facto narrowing of the refugee protection regime—contrary to the humanitarian purpose of the asylum system.

In sum, *Jimenez v. Bondi* may be remembered not for its analysis of past persecution, but for the way it reduces internal relocation to a three‑week anecdote. That reduction raises profound doctrinal questions and, more importantly, places an unrealistic burden on people already fleeing danger. The conversation about “safe relocation” is far from over, and the courts will soon be called upon to decide whether a short holiday in the countryside can ever satisfy the lofty promise of asylum protection.
