Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 5: # Chapter 5: Government Protection, Internal Relocation, and the Final Holding  

In this chapter we walk through the appellate court’s analysis of the “unable or unwilling” prong of asylum law, the inquiry into whether petitioners could safely relocate within Colombia, and how those determinations underpin the court’s ultimate denial of both asylum and withholding of removal.  The discussion is anchored in the facts and reasoning of **Jimenez v. Bondi**, 10th Cir. (2025), and it illustrates the evidentiary burdens and legal standards that the court applied.

## 5.1. Legal Framework for Government Protection  

The “government‑protection” prong asks whether the applicant’s home‑country government is unable or unwilling to protect the individual from the persecutors identified.  The Tenth Circuit follows the statutory language in 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(A) and the regulatory guidance at 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(2).  Two recent cases are particularly instructive:

* **Singh v. Bondi**, 130 F.4th 848, 861 (10th Cir. 2025) – the court reiterates that the applicant must show, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the government either lacks the capability to stop the persecutors or is unwilling to do so.  The analysis “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution,” focusing on whether the government attempted to halt the harm or offered protective measures such as custody or police protection.  
* **Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland**, No. 23‑9547, 2024 WL 3066987 (10th Cir. June 20 2024) – the court emphasizes that the applicant’s failure to report the persecution does not automatically foreclose a showing of government unwillingness or inability, but it does make the burden heavier.  An applicant must explain why reporting would have been futile, dangerous, or otherwise ineffective.

Together, these precedents establish a two‑part test: (1) the applicant must demonstrate that the government was, or would have been, unable or unwilling to protect; and (2) the applicant must provide evidence—typically through police reports, affidavits, or corroborating testimony—that the government’s response, or lack thereof, supports the claim.

## 5.2. The Importance of Reporting to Authorities and Evidentiary Burden  

Reporting is the linchpin of the government‑protection inquiry.  The record must show that the applicant placed the government on notice of the danger and gave it a realistic opportunity to intervene.  The Tenth Circuit treats an absent or inadequate report as a substantial evidentiary gap because it deprives the court of “any way of knowing how police would respond” (Singh, 130 F.4th at 861).  

When an applicant asserts that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, the court requires a factual basis for that assertion.  The applicant must articulate a concrete risk—such as prior retaliation against complainants, systemic police collusion, or a demonstrable pattern of impunity—to render the failure to report reasonable.  Without such facts, the court typically concludes that the applicant has not satisfied the burden of showing government unwillingness or inability.

## 5.3. Evaluation of Petitioners’ Interactions with Colombian Police  

In **Jimenez v. Bondi**, the petitioners’ interactions with Colombian law‑enforcement were sparse and uncorroborated.  The record shows two isolated conversations:

1. **A police patrol officer** who, after hearing Jimenez recount an assault on his son, told the family “nothing could be done” and advised them to leave the area.  The officer’s remark was a casual admonition rather than an official investigative or protective response.  No report was filed, no protective custody offered, and no follow‑up action was documented.  

2. **A local police officer** who warned Jimenez to stay away from the property owned by “Don Rafa.”  This warning was again an informal statement without any formal filing, investigative step, or protective measure.

The court noted that these anecdotal remarks do not rise to the level of “systemic” government failure.  They are isolated statements that could reflect individual bias or limited knowledge, but they do not demonstrate a governmental policy of unwillingness or an inability to control the persecutors.  Moreover, the petitioners offered no evidence that they attempted to file a formal police report, that they sought assistance from higher‑level authorities, or that they feared retaliation for reporting.  In the absence of such evidence, the court concluded that the petitioners had not satisfied their burden under **Singh** and **Aviles‑Gonzalez**.

## 5.4. Internal Relocation Standard  

Even if the government‑protection prong had been met, an asylum applicant must also establish that internal relocation is not reasonably available.  The regulation governing this inquiry is 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), which requires the applicant to show that “relocation within the country would not be a viable option for avoiding future persecution.”  The Tenth Circuit’s leading case on this point is **Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder**, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012).  

Key elements of the relocation analysis are:

* **Geographic feasibility** – Whether there exists a region of the country where the applicant could live without heightened risk.  
* **Safety of the region** – Whether the persecutors have demonstrated the ability or intent to pursue the applicant beyond the immediate locale.  
* **Practical obstacles** – Whether the applicant would face insurmountable economic, social, or logistical barriers to moving (e.g., lack of family support, language, or livelihood).  

In **Jimenez**, the court found that petitioners’ claim of unsafe relocation was untenable.  Petitioners’ own testimony demonstrated that they spent three weeks living with Jimenez’s mother in the countryside outside Bogotá without further incident.  Their extended family, including Jimenez’s father—a government employee—remained in Colombia and were unharmed.  The threats they received were directed at “snitches” in a specific neighborhood and did not extend to the broader region.  Consequently, the court concluded that the petitioners could have reasonably relocated to another part of Colombia and avoided the specific threats they described.

## 5.5. Summary of the Court’s Holdings  

Having applied the legal framework to the factual record, the Tenth Circuit reached the following conclusions:

1. **Government Protection** – Petitioners failed to provide sufficient evidence that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.  Their limited, informal contacts with individual police officers did not demonstrate systemic governmental failure, and they offered no factual justification for why filing a formal report would have been futile or dangerous.  

2. **Internal Relocation** – The record showed that petitioners could safely relocate within Colombia.  Their brief stay in the countryside, the presence of unaffected family members, and the localized nature of the threats collectively established that internal relocation was reasonably available.  

3. **Denial of Asylum** – Because petitioners could not satisfy the nexus requirement (political opinion was not a central motive) and could not demonstrate past persecution, a well‑founded fear of future persecution, or the inability/unwillingness of the government to protect them, the asylum claim failed on every statutory element.  

4. **Denial of Withholding of Removal** – Withholding of removal requires a higher standard of proof—clear and convincing evidence of a *well‑founded* fear of persecution.  The same deficiencies that doomed the asylum claim—lack of past persecution, lack of a protected‑ground nexus, and successful internal relocation—precluded any finding of a well‑founded fear.  

5. **Affirmation of the BIA** – Under the substantial‑evidence standard of review, the court held that the Board of Immigration Appeals’ findings were supported by the record.  Accordingly, the appellate court denied the petition for review, leaving the BIA’s denial of both asylum and withholding of removal intact.  

These holdings illustrate the tightly woven relationship between the three prongs of asylum law: a credible showing of past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, and a clear inability of the home‑country government to protect the applicant.  When any prong collapses—particularly the government‑protection and internal‑relocation analyses—both asylum and withholding of removal are unavailable, as demonstrated in **Jimenez v. Bondi**.
