Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### When can an asylum applicant rely on internal relocation as a defense to their claim?
Question:
The court rejected the petitioners’ internal relocation argument, noting they lived safely with relatives for three weeks. What factors do courts consider to decide if an applicant can reasonably relocate within their home country? Does a short‑term safe haven count, and how do courts assess the risk of future persecution after relocation?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the Fifth‑Circuit’s decision *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Cir. 2025, the court explained precisely what an asylum‑seeker must show to defeat a government‑relocation argument.  The decision does not create a new test; it simply applies the standards already set out in the INA and the implementing regulations (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).  From *Jimenez* we can distill the following “check‑list” that courts use when they evaluate whether an applicant can **reasonably** relocate within his or her home country.

---

### 1. The statutory/regulatory basis

- The government may rebut an asylum claim by showing, by a **preponderance of the evidence**, that the applicant **could avoid future persecution by relocating** to another part of the country. *Jimenez*, ¶ F. (citing *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646; 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

- The burden is on the government, but once it produces credible evidence that relocation is feasible, the applicant must **counter** with evidence that relocation would **not be safe or practicable**.

---

### 2. Factors courts consider

*Jimenez* lists the factual considerations that a court looks at when deciding whether relocation is “reasonable”:

| Factor | What the court looks for | How *Jimenez* applied it |
|--------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Existence of a safe place** | Is there a specific locality where the applicant can live without a substantial risk of persecution? | The petitioners stayed for **three weeks** with the mother‑in‑law in the Bogotá countryside without incident. |
| **Duration of safety** | Is the safety **temporary** (a short‑term haven) or **sustainable**? | The court treated the three‑week stay as **evidence of a viable relocation option**, not as a fleeting, “temporary shelter” that disappears once the family leaves. |
| **Access to family or community support** | Does the applicant have relatives, friends, or a community that can provide shelter and assistance? | Petitioners have **family members still living in Colombia** who were unharmed, showing a support network. |
| **Economic and logistical feasibility** | Can the applicant obtain housing, employment, and access basic services in the new location? | The opinion notes no evidence that the petitioners lacked the means to support themselves in the countryside; they had a home and a livelihood awaiting them. |
| **Risk of future persecution in the relocation site** | Even if the applicant moves, is there a realistic chance that the persecutors will follow or that the government will fail to protect them there? | The court observed that the men who threatened the family **did not threaten them to leave the country**, only to leave the neighbourhood, indicating that the danger was not tied to the whole of Colombia. |
| **Government protection at the new site** | Would local authorities be able and willing to protect the applicant if needed? | The petitioners **reported the threats to a police officer**, who advised them to leave the neighbourhood—a response indicating at least **some willingness** to intervene. |

---

### 3. Does a short‑term safe haven count?

Yes, if the short‑term stay demonstrates that the applicant **could realistically live** in that location for a meaningful period without further harm.  *Jimenez* expressly held that the three‑week residence with the mother‑in‑law was sufficient to show that **relocation was reasonably available**.  The court did **not** require the applicant to prove a lifelong guarantee of safety; rather, it required proof that a **safe environment exists** and that the applicant could **access it**.

---

### 4. How future risk is assessed

The court looks at the **motive of the persecutors** and whether that motive would still operate after relocation.  In *Jimenez* the persecutors were a **narco‑guerrilla leader (Don Rafa)** who had threatened the petitioners because of a **property dispute**, not because of their political affiliation.  The threat was **localized** to the neighbourhood and the factory; the men never said they would follow the family across the country.  Because the **persecutor’s interest was tied to a specific locale**, the court concluded that the family could **avoid future persecution by moving elsewhere in Colombia**.

---

### 5. Practical take‑away for asylum seekers

When you argue that internal relocation is **not** feasible, you must produce evidence that:

1. **No safe area exists** (e.g., the entire region is under the control of the persecutor or the government is complicit everywhere);
2. **Relocation would be economically or socially impossible** (no housing, no income, no community support);
3. **The persecutor’s motive would follow you anywhere** (e.g., a political party or a government agency that can act nationally);
4. **The government would be unwilling or unable to protect you** in the new location (prior complaints to police resulted in no protection, or the government is demonstrably incapable).

If you can point to **any one** of these, the government’s relocation argument may be rebutted.  If the record shows only **localized threats**, a **temporary safe haven**, and **family or community support**, courts—following *Jimenez*—are likely to find that internal relocation is reasonable and therefore a barrier to asylum.
