Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Chapter 1: Factual and Procedural Background  

Understanding any immigration‑court opinion begins with a clear picture of the parties, the events that gave rise to the claim, and the procedural posture of the case.  In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the petitioners are Colombian nationals who sought asylum and withholding of removal after fleeing a series of threats and assaults that they alleged were tied to their political activity.  The Ninth (sic) Tenth Circuit’s analysis of each claim rests on the factual matrix set out below and on the procedural record that led the case to appellate review.  This chapter walks the student through the essential facts and the procedural steps, highlighting the material details that the court considered when evaluating the petitioners’ claims.

## 1.1. Petitioners’ Biographical Facts and Political Activities in Colombia  

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons were long‑time residents of Bogotá, Colombia.  The family owned and operated a small furniture‑factory business in the capital.  Since 2012 both Jiménez and Duarte had been active members of the Colombian Liberal Party, a mainstream political organization that opposed the left‑leaning coalition that brought President Gustavo Petro to office in 2022.  During the 2022 presidential campaign the Jiménez family engaged in door‑to‑door canvassing on behalf of the Liberal Party’s candidate, Federico Gutierrez.  Their political activity placed them squarely in the public eye of local party operatives and, as the record later shows, exposed them to hostile actors who opposed the Liberal Party’s platform.

## 1.2. The Property Dispute and Encounters with “Don Rafa”  

In June 2022 Jiménez, then a provisional law graduate and intern with Colombia’s National Prosecutor’s Office, was approached by a client—a woman from San Bernardo—who sought assistance in regaining possession of a rental property.  The tenant, who refused to vacate, turned out to be the client’s ex‑husband and, more importantly, Rafael Augusto Montañez Lancheros, commonly known as “Don Rafa.”  Don Rafa was identified by law‑enforcement officials as the leader of a narco‑trafficking guerrilla group that was politically aligned with President Petro’s administration.

During Jiménez’s investigation he learned that the property was being used as a day‑rental center for migrants and as a hub for drug trafficking, prostitution, and other illicit activities.  The third floor had been sealed off by the health department, and the local police had previously conducted, but apparently failed to complete, searches and seizures.  When Jiménez inquired about the property, a police officer warned him to stay away, indicating that the location was “dangerous” and that further probing could place him at risk.

## 1.3. Sequence of Threats, Assaults, and the Family’s Flight to the United States  

Starting in late May 2022 the Jiménez household began receiving threatening phone calls that referenced their Liberal Party affiliation.  In early June a fellow party activist was kidnapped and murdered after reporting similar threats, yet the Jiménez family continued their political work.  The escalation of intimidation continued:

* **June 2022 – Threatening brochure:** A pamphlet left at the factory read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.”  
* **July 2022 – Gunpoint at the factory:** While Jiménez worked the night shift two men detained him at gunpoint, accusing him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and demanding that he leave the neighborhood.  
* **August 2022 – Second factory visit:** The same men returned, this time assaulting an employee and demanding to know the Jiménezes’ whereabouts, explicitly linking the threats to Don Rafa.  
* **August 2022 – School incident:** A man seized young son Luis Jiménez by the mouth at his school, shouting “run to your dad” before being shoved away by his father.

Following the school incident the family sought temporary refuge at Jiménez’s mother’s rural home for three weeks.  During that stay they reported the incident to a local police patrol officer.  The officer’s response was dismissive: he suggested the police were “part of the problem,” advised the family to leave the area, and offered no concrete protection.  Concluding that the threats were unlikely to abate, the Jiménezes departed Colombia in December 2022, entering the United States without inspection or parole.

## 1.4. Initial Removal Proceedings: Notices to Appear, IJ Denial, and BIA Affirmation  

Three months after arriving in the United States, the Department of Homeland Security served each petitioner with an individual **Notice to Appear (NTA)**, charging them with removability under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).  In August 2023 the petitioners admitted the factual allegations in the NTA and the Immigration Judge (IJ) entered a removal order finding them **inadmissible** under § 212(a)(6)(C)(i) for entering without inspection.

The petitioners then filed **asylum** and **withholding‑of‑removal** applications, naming their minor children as derivative beneficiaries.  At the merits hearing the IJ found the petitioners **credible** but concluded that they failed to meet the three statutory elements required for asylum or withholding:

1. **Past persecution** – the IJ held that the threats and isolated assaults did not rise to the level of persecution.  
2. **Nexus to a protected ground** – the IJ determined that the motive for the attacks was primarily Jiménez’s involvement in the property dispute, not his political opinion.  
3. **Government protection** – the IJ found insufficient evidence that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the family, noting the petitioners’ limited attempts to seek police assistance.

The IJ also denied **Protection Under the Convention Against Torture (CAT)**.  The petitioners appealed the IJ’s decision to the **Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA)**, raising the same three substantive grounds and a procedural claim that the IJ had violated their due‑process right to counsel.  The BIA upheld the IJ’s findings, affirming the denial of asylum and withholding, and dismissed the due‑process claim.

## 1.5. Scope of the Appellate Review: Issues Preserved and Issues Waived  

Under **8 U.S.C. § 1252**, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit has jurisdiction to review “final orders of removal” and, by extension, consolidated asylum claims.  The petitioners filed a petition for review of the BIA’s decision.  Their brief limited the appellate challenge to **three substantive issues**:

1. Whether the BIA erred in finding **no past persecution**.  
2. Whether the BIA erred in concluding that the alleged persecution **lacked a nexus** to a protected political opinion.  
3. Whether the BIA erred in finding that the petitioners **did not demonstrate a well‑founded fear** of future persecution because the Colombian government was **unable or unwilling** to protect them.

The petitioners **did not appeal** the BIA’s denial of CAT protection, nor did they raise the procedural claim concerning counsel on appeal.  Consequently, those issues were **waived** and are outside the scope of the appellate court’s review.  The Tenth Circuit therefore limited its analysis to the three preserved claims and applied the appropriate standards of review: **substantial‑evidence** for factual findings and deference to the BIA’s legal conclusions unless contrary to controlling precedent.

---  

By mastering the factual narrative and the procedural path outlined above, a law student will be equipped to follow the court’s reasoning in the subsequent sections of the opinion, where the appellate court evaluates the three preserved claims under the substantive asylum framework.

# Chapter 2: Governing Law and Standard of Review  

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit was asked to review a Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) denial of asylum and withholding of removal.  Understanding the court’s analysis requires a solid grasp of the statutory scheme that creates the two forms of protection, the evidentiary burdens placed on the alien, and the standards of review that the appellate court applies to the BIA’s factual and legal determinations.  This chapter breaks those components down and explains how they operate in the context of the opinion.

## 2.1. Definition of “refugee” and the elements of asylum under the INA  

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines a **refugee** in § 1101(a)(42)(A) as any person who is **outside** his or her country of nationality **and** is unable or unwilling to return because of **persecution** or a **well‑founded fear of persecution** on account of one of five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.  The term “asylum” refers to the discretionary relief the Attorney General may grant to an alien who meets the refugee definition while already present in the United States.

To obtain asylum, the applicant must **prove by a preponderance of the evidence** that he or she is a refugee.  The *refugee* inquiry breaks into three elements, each of which the petitioner must establish:

1. **Past persecution** – an incident or series of incidents that rise to the level of persecution.  The harm must be more than mere harassment, threats, or restrictions; it must be serious enough to be “offensive” and cause substantial suffering.  Courts treat the incidents **cumulatively**, looking at the total impact on the claimant.  (*Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 646.)

2. **Nexus** – the persecution must be "on account of" a protected ground.  The protected ground must be **a central reason** for the harm; it cannot be merely incidental or peripheral.  (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646; *Dallakoti v. Holder*, 619 F.3d at 1268.)

3. **Perpetrator** – the persecutor must be the government itself or a force the government is **unable or unwilling** to control.  If a private actor harms the alien, the alien must show that the government cannot or will not protect him or her.  (*Niang v. Gonzales*, 422 F.3d at 1194.)

If the applicant establishes past persecution, the INA creates a **rebuttable presumption** that the alien has a well‑founded fear of future persecution.  The government then bears the burden to show, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the alien can relocate safely or that protection is otherwise available.  If the applicant fails to show past persecution, the alien must demonstrate directly a well‑founded fear of future persecution meeting the same three prongs.

## 2.2. Elements required for withholding of removal  

**Withholding of removal** is a mandatory (non‑discretionary) form of relief that bars the government from deporting the alien to a country where he or she would face persecution.  The statutory language is found in § 241(b)(2) of the INA.  The elements mirror those of asylum but differ in two important ways:

1. **Higher evidentiary standard** – the applicant must prove each element by **clear and convincing evidence**, a higher threshold than the preponderance required for asylum.  (*Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, 860 F. App’x at 124.)

2. **No discretionary grant** – because withholding is mandatory, the government cannot refuse it on policy grounds; it can only succeed by defeating one of the three elements.

Thus, an alien who cannot meet the asylum burden will also fail the withholding burden unless he or she can satisfy the stricter standard of proof.

## 2.3. Burden of proof  

- **Asylum** – the alien carries the burden of **preponderance of the evidence** on all three elements (past persecution, nexus, and perpetrator).  The government may meet its burden of proof only when it wishes to rebut the presumption of future persecution (e.g., by showing safe internal relocation).  

- **Withholding of removal** – the alien still bears the burden of proof, but the standard is **clear and convincing evidence** on each element.  This heightened standard reflects the mandatory nature of the relief and the serious consequences of deportation to a country where the alien faces persecution.  

In *Jimenez*, the Tenth Circuit noted that the petitioners “failed to demonstrate” the required elements, implicitly applying the relevant burden and standard to the record before the court.

## 2.4. Standard of review for BIA findings  

When a circuit court reviews a BIA decision, the **statutory framework of 8 U.S.C. § 1252** governs the scope and depth of review.

1. **Factual findings** – Reviewed under the **substantial‑evidence** standard.  This means the court asks whether **any reasonable adjudicator** could have reached the same conclusion based on the **administrative record**.  If the record contains sufficient evidence that would support the BIA’s fact finding, the appellate court must affirm, even if the court might have decided differently.  (*Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 645.)

2. **Legal conclusions** – Subject to **de novo** review.  The court does not give deference to the BIA’s interpretation of statutory language, regulatory definitions, or legal standards.  The appellate court may accept, modify, or reject the BIA’s legal analysis as it deems appropriate.  

3. **Mixed‑motivation cases** – The court may consult the immigration judge’s (IJ’s) “more complete explanation” of the same factual and legal issues, even though the BIA’s order is the final agency decision.  This approach comes from *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d at 1204, and allows the court to ensure that the BIA’s reasoning was not arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law.

In *Jimenez*, the panel emphasized that they were “not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation” while still applying the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual determinations.

## 2.5. Key precedent governing review  

The Tenth Circuit’s approach in *Jimenez* rests on three leading cases:

| Citation | Core Holding | Relevance to *Jimenez* |
|---|---|---|
| **Singh v. Bondi**, 130 F.4th 848 (10th Cir. 2025) | Confirms that the appellate court has jurisdiction to review final removal orders, including consolidated asylum claims, and restates the limits of review to the administrative record. | Provides the statutory basis (8 U.S.C. § 1252) for the court’s authority to hear the petition and frames the “record‑based” nature of the review. |
| **Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder**, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012) | Establishes the substantial‑evidence standard for factual findings and clarifies the three‑prong asylum analysis (past persecution, nexus, perpetrator). | Guides the court’s assessment of whether the BIA’s factual findings on past persecution, nexus, and government protection are supported by substantial evidence. |
| **Uanreroro v. Gonzales**, 443 F.3d 1197 (10th Cir. 2006) | Allows the appellate court to consider the IJ’s more detailed explanation when reviewing a BIA decision, without substituting the IJ’s analysis for the BIA’s. | Explains why the Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez* could look to the IJ’s reasoning while still applying the proper standard of review. |

Together, these decisions create a three‑step roadmap for reviewing asylum and withholding cases:

1. **Confirm jurisdiction and scope** (Singh).  
2. **Apply the substantive asylum/withholding framework** (elements and burdens).  
3. **Review the BIA’s factual findings under substantial‑evidence** and **legal conclusions de novo**, consulting the IJ’s record when helpful (Rivera‑Barrientos; Uanreroro).

Understanding this framework equips a law student to follow the court’s reasoning in *Jimenez v. Bondi* and to anticipate how future appellate courts will evaluate similar challenges to BIA asylum determinations.

# Chapter 3: The Past Persecution Prong  

In order to succeed on an asylum or withholding‑of‑removal claim, a petitioner must first prove that he or she has suffered **past persecution**.  The United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, following precedent from other circuits, treats this requirement as a fact‑finding question for the immigration judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) that is reviewed on a **substantial‑evidence** standard.  In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the appellate panel scrutinized the record to determine whether the threats and assaults described by the Jimenez family rose to the level of persecution as required by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).  This chapter dissects the court’s analytical framework, the evidentiary standards it applied, and the way the facts were weighed under that framework.

## 3.1. Legal definition of persecution in the Ninth Circuit and its application in the Tenth Circuit  

Although the Tenth Circuit’s reasoning in *Jimenez* does not cite Ninth‑Circuit cases directly, the Ninth Circuit’s definition of “persecution” is instructive because it reflects the broader federal jurisprudence that all circuits share.  In *Matter of Kasinga*, 21 I&N Dec. 233 (BIA 1996) (adopted by the Ninth Circuit in *Caceres v. INS*, 480 F.3d 1155, 1159 (9th Cir. 2007)), persecution is “the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ in race, religion, or political opinion in a way that is regarded as offensive, and must entail more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.”  The Ninth Circuit further emphasized that **the injury must be severe enough to be more than a mere inconvenience or a fleeting threat**.  See *N. Cal. Immigr. Law Ass’n v. INS*, 32 F.3d 533, 539 (9th Cir. 1994) (recognizing that “a threat, by itself, does not amount to persecution”).

The Tenth Circuit adopts the same functional definition, as articulated in *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004).  The court there held that persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm … in a way regarded as offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.”  The *Jimenez* panel reiterated this definition, noting that **the jurisdictional question is whether the cumulative facts meet the statutory meaning of persecution**, not whether each isolated incident does.  Consequently, the Tenth Circuit’s analysis proceeds by asking:  

1. **What harm did the petitioner actually suffer?**  
2. **Is that harm of a type the INA contemplates as persecution?**  
3. **Was the harm inflicted because of a protected ground (political opinion, etc.)?**  

Only if the answer to the first two sub‑questions is affirmative does the court move on to the nexus analysis.  In *Jimenez* the first prong—establishing past persecution—failed, and the appellate court therefore never reached the nexus or government‑protection inquiries.

## 3.2. The cumulative‑effects approach versus isolated‑incident analysis  

The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that **persecution is a cumulative concept**.  An applicant need not suffer a single, dramatic act of violence; rather, the tribunal may look at the totality of circumstances to determine whether the aggregate impact rises to persecution.  This approach was articulated in *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011), where the court stated that “we do not look at each incident in isolation, but instead consider them collectively, because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution.”  

In applying the cumulative‑effects test, the court asks whether the sum of the documented threats, assaults, intimidation, and any lasting physical or psychological injury creates a **substantial and enduring risk** that is *more than a mere inconvenience*.  The test is flexible: the cumulative analysis can rescue an otherwise marginal claim if the combined facts show a pattern of sustained, targeted hostility.  However, the cumulative approach does **not** lower the substantive threshold for what qualifies as persecution.  A series of threats, even when aggregated, will not become persecution unless the threats are **serious, credible, and accompanied by actual injury or a well‑founded fear of imminent harm**.

## 3.3. Review of the petitioners’ alleged harms: phone threats, gunpoint encounters, assault on a factory employee, and psychological impact  

The Jimenez record presented a series of incidents:  

* **Phone threats** – Once or twice weekly calls warning “death to snitches from Liberal Party.”  
* **Gunpoint intimidation** – Two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at his night‑shift factory, ordering him to leave the neighborhood and accusing him of being a “snitch.”  
* **Threats against the family’s workplace** – The same men later returned, assaulted a factory employee, brandished a gun, and demanded to know the Jimenezes’ whereabouts, again invoking “snitches” and “Don Rafa.”  
* **Direct assault on the minor son** – A man placed his hand over the child’s mouth at school, forcing the child to run to his father, after which the father intervened physically.  
* **Psychological sequelae** – Both spouses testified to insomnia, anxiety, and panic attacks in their son.  

The IJ found the petitioners credible but concluded that these facts failed to meet the persecution threshold.  The appellate panel affirmed, applying the substantive definition of persecution.  The court observed that **none of the incidents involved physical injury**, and the documented threats, while serious, were **not sufficient on their own** to constitute persecution.  The court noted that the petitioners themselves conceded they had not been physically harmed and that their psychological complaints, though genuine, did not rise to the level required for persecution absent a more concrete, permanent injury.

The Tenth Circuit’s reasoning aligns with its prior holdings that **psychological harm alone, absent physical injury or a credible, ongoing threat of physical harm, is insufficient**.  In *Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014), the court held that “psychologically unsettling events… do not, by themselves, satisfy the persecution element.”  Likewise, in *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013), the court ruled that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.”  The Jimenez panel placed the same weight on these precedents, emphasizing that **the cumulative effect of the threats and the single episode of a gunpoint encounter did not cross the line into persecution** because they did not result in lasting physical injury or a pattern of systematic violence that the INA envisions.

## 3.4. Controlling cases on threats versus persecution (Karki v. Holder; Brandy v. Holder; Chhetri v. Rosen)  

Several Ninth‑ and Tenth‑Circuit decisions illustrate the boundary between **threats** and **persecution**.  

* **Karki v. Holder**, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013) – The court held that “mere threats, even when repeated, do not rise to persecution unless they are coupled with overt acts of violence or severe injury.”  
* **Brandy v. Holder**, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014) – Here, the appellant suffered a 10‑hour detention and threats of harm. The court found that while the detention was unlawful, the accompanying threats were “psychologically unsettling but insufficient for persecution.”  
* **Chhetri v. Rosen**, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) – The petitioner reported four telephone threats and a vague in‑person threat to his father. The court again declined to find persecution, emphasizing that “the threats were not accompanied by any physical injury or a pattern of credible, imminent danger.”  

These cases collectively demonstrate the Tenth Circuit’s **strict insistence on a concrete, severe injury or credible, imminent danger** as the dividing line.  The *Jimenez* court applied the same standard, concluding that the facts did not rise above the threshold established in *Karki* and *Chhetri*.  Even when the threats were “credible” in the sense that they originated from an armed group, the lack of injury and the limited scope of the threats (i.e., to leave a neighborhood, not to be killed) meant the statutory element of persecution remained unmet.

## 3.5. Court’s finding of insufficient substantial evidence to meet the persecution element  

Under the **substantial‑evidence** standard, the appellate court asks whether “any reasonable adjudicator could find” that the petitioner suffered persecution.  The test is highly deferential; the reviewing court does not re‑weigh the evidence but merely checks that the BIA’s factual findings are supported by “substantial evidence” in the record.  In *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012), the Fourth Circuit explained that “the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”  

Applying this standard, the *Jimenez* panel determined that **the record contained no substantial evidence of physical injury, no documented pattern of systematic violence, and no credible, ongoing threat of imminent bodily harm**.  The phone calls, the gunpoint episode, and the school incident were **isolated** and **did not constitute a sustained campaign of physical persecution**.  Moreover, the petitioners’ psychological complaints, while sincere, were not supported by medical documentation linking them to a specific, enduring threat that the government could not mitigate.  Consequently, the court held that **the BIA’s conclusion—that the alleged harms did not amount to past persecution—was supported by substantial evidence**, and therefore the appellate court could not overturn it.

In sum, the Tenth Circuit’s analysis in *Jimenez v. Bondi* underscores three pivotal take‑aways for law students examining the past‑persecution prong:

1. **Persecution must be more than a threat; it requires actual or imminently threatened serious injury.**  
2. **Cumulative harms are considered, but the aggregation of threats does not automatically create persecution.**  
3. **The substantial‑evidence standard heavily favors the BIA’s factual determinations, so petitioners must provide clear, documentary proof of physical injury or a credible, ongoing risk of such injury.**  

Understanding these principles equips practitioners to evaluate whether a client’s factual narrative can survive the rigorous scrutiny applied by immigration tribunals and appellate courts when assessing the past persecution element of an asylum or withholding‑of‑removal claim.

# Chapter 4: Nexus to a Protected Ground – Political Opinion  

In an asylum claim the applicant must not only show that she or he suffered past persecution, but also that the persecution was **on account of**—that is, **because of**—a statutorily protected ground.  For the petitioners in *Jimenez v. Bondi* the protected ground asserted was **political opinion**.  This chapter explains how the Tenth Circuit frames the nexus requirement, what “central reason” means in mixed‑motive cases, how the court evaluated the petitioners’ evidence, and why it concluded that the political motive was merely tangential.  

---  

## 4.1. Nexus Requirement under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)  

Section 1158(b)(1)(B)(i) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) provides that an asylum applicant must establish that the persecution was “on account of” one of the five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or **political opinion**.  The phrase “on account of” creates a *nexus* between the persecutor’s motive and the protected characteristic.  The Supreme Court has described the clause as a *“causation*” requirement: the protected ground must be a **reason** for the persecution, not simply a coincidental or peripheral fact (see *Matter of Acosta*, 19 I&N Dec. 417, 422 (BIA 1985)).  

In practice, the nexus inquiry asks the fact‑finder to determine whether the persecutor’s **primary** motivation for the harmful act was the applicant’s political stance.  When the persecutor has multiple motives, the protected ground must be at least **one central reason** for the conduct.  If the political opinion is merely incidental, the statutory nexus is not satisfied.  

---  

## 4.2. “Central Reason” Test and Its Application in Mixed‑Motive Cases  

The Tenth Circuit has articulated a two‑part “central reason” test:  

1. **Identify the persecutor’s motive(s).** This is done by examining the factual record—statements by the persecutor, the context of the threat or attack, and any corroborating evidence.  

2. **Determine whether the protected ground is a central, rather than peripheral, motive.** The protected ground cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” to another reason for the harm.  *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)).  

When a case involves **mixed motives**, the court does not require the applicant to prove that the protected ground was the *sole* cause of the persecution, but it must be a *substantial* factor that drove the persecutor’s decision.  *O.C.V. v. Bondi*, No. 23‑9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025).  Conversely, if the evidence shows another motive—such as personal retaliation, economic interest, or a private dispute—that overwhelmingly dominates, the nexus fails.  

---  

## 4.3. Evidence of Motive: Threats Tied to Jimenez’s Investigation of Don Rafa’s Property versus Political Campaigning  

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners presented two strands of alleged motive:  

* **Political Campaign Activity.**  The family had been active Liberal Party members since 2012 and had canvassed for a candidate in the 2022 presidential election.  Beginning in May 2022, the petitioners received threatening phone calls that referenced their Liberal Party affiliation.  A brochure delivered to their factory read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.”  

* **Personal/Professional Conflict with Don Rafa.**  In June 2022, Jimenez, acting as a provisional lawyer, intervened in a property dispute involving a tenant who turned out to be “Don Rafa,” a guerrilla‑linked narco‑trafficker aligned with the Colombian president.  During his investigation Jimenez discovered that the property was a hub for illegal activity and that law enforcement had unsuccessfully attempted raids.  After this contact, the petitioners received a series of escalating threats: two gunmen held Jimenez at gunpoint, later returned to assault a factory employee and demand the family’s whereabouts, and a man physically restrained the petitioners’ younger son at school.  The gunmen explicitly linked the threats to “Don Rafa” and accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office.  

The record shows that the **most explicit statements of motive** came from the perpetrators themselves.  The assailants identified Don Rafa as the source of the threats and framed Jimenez’s actions as retaliation for interfering with a criminal enterprise, not as punishment for Liberal Party activism.  Moreover, the original phone calls that referenced the Liberal Party occurred **before** Jimenez’s investigation of the property and did not culminate in violence.  The violent incidents—gunpoint confrontations, the attempted kidnapping of the family, and the school assault—were directly tied to the property dispute and the perpetrators’ claim that Jimenez had exposed or threatened their illegal operations.  

Thus, the factual narrative presents a **mixed‑motive scenario** in which the political affiliation is present but is not the dominant driver of the violent acts.  The court’s task was to decide whether the political opinion rose to the level of a central reason.  

---  

## 4.4. Comparison to Tenth Circuit Precedent on Nexus  

The Tenth Circuit’s precedent clarifies how the “central reason” test is applied:  

* **Dallakoti v. Holder**, 619 F.3d at 1268, held that an applicant’s political belief must be a “central” motive; mere political participation that is merely “tangential” to the persecutor’s grievance does not satisfy the nexus.  The court emphasized that the persecutor’s statements must demonstrate that the protected ground was a “principal factor” in the decision to harm.  

* **Yuk v. Ashcroft**, 355 F.3d at 1236, reiterated that the reviewing court’s role is limited to determining whether **substantial evidence** supports the Board’s finding that the protected ground was a central motive; the court does not re‑weigh evidence but assesses whether any reasonable adjudicator could have reached the same conclusion.  

Applying these standards, the Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez* examined whether the Board’s finding—that the primary motive was personal retaliation—was supported by substantial evidence.  The explicit attribution of the threats to “Don Rafa” and the timing of the escalated violence—following Jimenez’s legal intervention—mirrored the reasoning in *Dallakoti*: the political activity was present but not the decisive factor.  The court therefore concluded that the Board’s assessment was consistent with the controlling precedent.  

---  

## 4.5. Court’s Conclusion: Primary Motive Was Personal/Retaliatory, Not Political  

After weighing the evidence, the panel held that the petitioners **failed** to satisfy the nexus requirement for political opinion.  The key points of the court’s reasoning were:  

1. **Substantial Evidence of a Non‑Political Motive.**  The perpetrators’ own declarations linked the threats to Jimenez’s investigation of a criminal property and to his perceived role as a “snitch” for law enforcement.  This directly ties the violence to a personal, retaliatory motive rather than to political expression.  

2. **Temporal Relationship.**  The most serious threats occurred **after** Jimenez’s involvement with the property dispute, whereas the earlier, less severe threats that mentioned the Liberal Party were not followed by violence.  The shift in the persecutors’ language demonstrates a change in motive from political to personal.  

3. **Lack of Direct Evidence Connecting the Persecutors to the Petitioners’ Political Activity.**  The record contains no testimony, police report, or documentary evidence indicating that the gunmen or Don Rafa acted because the petitioners supported a rival political candidate.  The only statements that reference politics are the pre‑investigation phone calls, which the court treated as insufficient to establish a causal link to the later assaults.  

4. **Application of the “Central Reason” Test.**  Because the political opinion was at most a **secondary** factor—if at all—it did not rise to the level of a central reason for the persecution.  Accordingly, the statutory nexus was not satisfied.  

Given these findings, the court affirmed the Board’s determination that the petitioners’ alleged persecution was **not** on account of political opinion.  Without a valid nexus, the petitioners could not meet the statutory definition of a refugee, and the asylum claim failed.  

---  

### Take‑aways for the Reader  

*When reading an asylum opinion, focus on the nexus analysis as a distinct element that sits between the factual showing of persecution and the legal conclusion of refugee status.*  

1. **Identify the protected ground asserted.**  Here, political opinion.  
2. **Extract the persecutor’s motive from the record.**  Look for explicit statements, timing, and context.  
3. **Apply the “central reason” test.**  Ask whether the protected ground is the *key* driver of the harm, not just an incidental backdrop.  
4. **Check the circuit’s precedent.**  The Tenth Circuit requires a substantial‑evidence showing that the protected ground is a central motive; it will not re‑weigh the evidence for you.  
5. **Conclude on the nexus.**  If the protected ground is merely tangential, the claim fails even if past persecution is otherwise established.  

Understanding this analytical framework equips you to dissect future opinions that grapple with the nexus requirement, and to anticipate the evidentiary thresholds that asylum claimants must meet to survive the political‑opinion prong.

# Chapter 5: Government Protection, Internal Relocation, and the Final Holding  

In this chapter we walk through the appellate court’s analysis of the “unable or unwilling” prong of asylum law, the inquiry into whether petitioners could safely relocate within Colombia, and how those determinations underpin the court’s ultimate denial of both asylum and withholding of removal.  The discussion is anchored in the facts and reasoning of **Jimenez v. Bondi**, 10th Cir. (2025), and it illustrates the evidentiary burdens and legal standards that the court applied.

## 5.1. Legal Framework for Government Protection  

The “government‑protection” prong asks whether the applicant’s home‑country government is unable or unwilling to protect the individual from the persecutors identified.  The Tenth Circuit follows the statutory language in 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(A) and the regulatory guidance at 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(2).  Two recent cases are particularly instructive:

* **Singh v. Bondi**, 130 F.4th 848, 861 (10th Cir. 2025) – the court reiterates that the applicant must show, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the government either lacks the capability to stop the persecutors or is unwilling to do so.  The analysis “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution,” focusing on whether the government attempted to halt the harm or offered protective measures such as custody or police protection.  
* **Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland**, No. 23‑9547, 2024 WL 3066987 (10th Cir. June 20 2024) – the court emphasizes that the applicant’s failure to report the persecution does not automatically foreclose a showing of government unwillingness or inability, but it does make the burden heavier.  An applicant must explain why reporting would have been futile, dangerous, or otherwise ineffective.

Together, these precedents establish a two‑part test: (1) the applicant must demonstrate that the government was, or would have been, unable or unwilling to protect; and (2) the applicant must provide evidence—typically through police reports, affidavits, or corroborating testimony—that the government’s response, or lack thereof, supports the claim.

## 5.2. The Importance of Reporting to Authorities and Evidentiary Burden  

Reporting is the linchpin of the government‑protection inquiry.  The record must show that the applicant placed the government on notice of the danger and gave it a realistic opportunity to intervene.  The Tenth Circuit treats an absent or inadequate report as a substantial evidentiary gap because it deprives the court of “any way of knowing how police would respond” (Singh, 130 F.4th at 861).  

When an applicant asserts that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, the court requires a factual basis for that assertion.  The applicant must articulate a concrete risk—such as prior retaliation against complainants, systemic police collusion, or a demonstrable pattern of impunity—to render the failure to report reasonable.  Without such facts, the court typically concludes that the applicant has not satisfied the burden of showing government unwillingness or inability.

## 5.3. Evaluation of Petitioners’ Interactions with Colombian Police  

In **Jimenez v. Bondi**, the petitioners’ interactions with Colombian law‑enforcement were sparse and uncorroborated.  The record shows two isolated conversations:

1. **A police patrol officer** who, after hearing Jimenez recount an assault on his son, told the family “nothing could be done” and advised them to leave the area.  The officer’s remark was a casual admonition rather than an official investigative or protective response.  No report was filed, no protective custody offered, and no follow‑up action was documented.  

2. **A local police officer** who warned Jimenez to stay away from the property owned by “Don Rafa.”  This warning was again an informal statement without any formal filing, investigative step, or protective measure.

The court noted that these anecdotal remarks do not rise to the level of “systemic” government failure.  They are isolated statements that could reflect individual bias or limited knowledge, but they do not demonstrate a governmental policy of unwillingness or an inability to control the persecutors.  Moreover, the petitioners offered no evidence that they attempted to file a formal police report, that they sought assistance from higher‑level authorities, or that they feared retaliation for reporting.  In the absence of such evidence, the court concluded that the petitioners had not satisfied their burden under **Singh** and **Aviles‑Gonzalez**.

## 5.4. Internal Relocation Standard  

Even if the government‑protection prong had been met, an asylum applicant must also establish that internal relocation is not reasonably available.  The regulation governing this inquiry is 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), which requires the applicant to show that “relocation within the country would not be a viable option for avoiding future persecution.”  The Tenth Circuit’s leading case on this point is **Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder**, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012).  

Key elements of the relocation analysis are:

* **Geographic feasibility** – Whether there exists a region of the country where the applicant could live without heightened risk.  
* **Safety of the region** – Whether the persecutors have demonstrated the ability or intent to pursue the applicant beyond the immediate locale.  
* **Practical obstacles** – Whether the applicant would face insurmountable economic, social, or logistical barriers to moving (e.g., lack of family support, language, or livelihood).  

In **Jimenez**, the court found that petitioners’ claim of unsafe relocation was untenable.  Petitioners’ own testimony demonstrated that they spent three weeks living with Jimenez’s mother in the countryside outside Bogotá without further incident.  Their extended family, including Jimenez’s father—a government employee—remained in Colombia and were unharmed.  The threats they received were directed at “snitches” in a specific neighborhood and did not extend to the broader region.  Consequently, the court concluded that the petitioners could have reasonably relocated to another part of Colombia and avoided the specific threats they described.

## 5.5. Summary of the Court’s Holdings  

Having applied the legal framework to the factual record, the Tenth Circuit reached the following conclusions:

1. **Government Protection** – Petitioners failed to provide sufficient evidence that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.  Their limited, informal contacts with individual police officers did not demonstrate systemic governmental failure, and they offered no factual justification for why filing a formal report would have been futile or dangerous.  

2. **Internal Relocation** – The record showed that petitioners could safely relocate within Colombia.  Their brief stay in the countryside, the presence of unaffected family members, and the localized nature of the threats collectively established that internal relocation was reasonably available.  

3. **Denial of Asylum** – Because petitioners could not satisfy the nexus requirement (political opinion was not a central motive) and could not demonstrate past persecution, a well‑founded fear of future persecution, or the inability/unwillingness of the government to protect them, the asylum claim failed on every statutory element.  

4. **Denial of Withholding of Removal** – Withholding of removal requires a higher standard of proof—clear and convincing evidence of a *well‑founded* fear of persecution.  The same deficiencies that doomed the asylum claim—lack of past persecution, lack of a protected‑ground nexus, and successful internal relocation—precluded any finding of a well‑founded fear.  

5. **Affirmation of the BIA** – Under the substantial‑evidence standard of review, the court held that the Board of Immigration Appeals’ findings were supported by the record.  Accordingly, the appellate court denied the petition for review, leaving the BIA’s denial of both asylum and withholding of removal intact.  

These holdings illustrate the tightly woven relationship between the three prongs of asylum law: a credible showing of past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, and a clear inability of the home‑country government to protect the applicant.  When any prong collapses—particularly the government‑protection and internal‑relocation analyses—both asylum and withholding of removal are unavailable, as demonstrated in **Jimenez v. Bondi**.
