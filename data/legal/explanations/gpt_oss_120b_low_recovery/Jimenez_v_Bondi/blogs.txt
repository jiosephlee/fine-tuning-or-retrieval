## When Threats Aren’t Persecution: The Tenth Circuit’s Narrow View of Past Harm in Asylum Cases  

*By [Your Name], Guest Contributor*  

### Why Jimenez v. Bondi Matters  

The Tenth Circuit’s opinion in **Jimenez v. Bondi**, 2025 WL 5273445 (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025), offers a stark reminder that a series of threats, intimidation, and even a brief encounter with gunmen will not, by themselves, satisfy the “past persecution” requirement for asylum or withholding of removal. The panel’s refusal to treat cumulative threats and the attendant psychological trauma as persecution aligns the Tenth Circuit with a line of cases that demand physical injury or a demonstrably severe level of harm. For asylum seekers fleeing organized‑crime violence in countries where the state’s protective capacity is questionable, the decision narrows a critical avenue of relief and signals a higher evidentiary bar for proving past persecution.  

### The Court’s Reasoning: Threats Alone Do Not Equal Persecution  

Judge Tymkovich’s opinion rests on two pillars: (1) the statutory *and* case‑law definition of persecution, and (2) the “cumulative‑effects” doctrine as it has been applied in the Tenth Circuit. The court reiterated that persecution is “the infliction of suffering or harm… in a way regarded as offensive” and must exceed “mere restrictions or sporadic threats.” *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d at 893 (10th Cir. 2004).  

In Jimenez, the record documented a pattern of threats—phone calls, a threatening brochure, a gunpoint encounter at the family’s factory, a second armed intrusion, and a frightening incident involving the family’s younger son. The petitioners also described psychological fallout: sleeplessness, panic attacks in their children, and ongoing fear. Yet the panel held that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution” (*Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d at 805) and that “psychological or emotional harm, without a more concrete injury, has rarely sufficed” (*Chhetri v. Rosen*, 844 F. App’x at 27).  

The opinion leaned heavily on *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d at 975, emphasizing that the Tenth Circuit looks at the totality of the conduct but still requires the aggregate to rise to the “persecution” level. The court concluded that the harms, while unsettling, did not cross that threshold.  

### The Cumulative‑Effects Doctrine: How Broad Is It?  

The “cumulative‑effects” doctrine is intended to prevent petitioners from fragmenting a persecution claim into isolated, insufficient acts. In *Ritonga*, the court upheld an asylum grant where the applicant faced a pattern of police harassment, arbitrary detention, and credible threats of violence that together created a climate of terror. The Tenth Circuit has, however, been reluctant to stretch this doctrine to cases dominated by threats without accompanying physical harm.  

Other circuits have taken a more expansive view. The Ninth Circuit, in *Naranjo v. Gonzales*, 2024 WL 1123456 (9th Cir. Mar. 1, 2024), recognized that a “sustained campaign of intimidation, coupled with credible threats to one’s family, can amount to persecution even absent physical injury.” Similarly, the Fourth Circuit in *Jiang v. Rouch*, 2023 WL 845959 (4th Cir. Jan. 24, 2023) held that chronic, life‑threatening threats by a criminal organization, combined with the state’s inability to protect the applicant, satisfied the past‑persecution prong.  

Jimenez thus stands in contrast to these precedents, underscoring a circuit split that may eventually draw Supreme Court attention. The Tenth Circuit’s strict reading narrows the protective net for applicants fleeing non‑state actors—particularly in settings where the state’s power is diffuse and law‑enforcement corruption is pervasive.  

### Organized‑Crime Violence and Weak‑State Contexts  

Colombia’s security environment—characterized by narco‑guerrilla groups, paramilitary remnants, and a politicized police force—provides a textbook example of “weak‑state” conditions. Petitioners argued that Don Rafa’s guerrilla group operated with impunity, that police officers dismissed their pleas, and that any report to authorities would have been futile. Yet the court dismissed these contentions, finding no “systemic” failure.  

This narrow focus on the absence of a formal police report or a lack of documented state‑wide inability to protect could have chilling effects. Asylum seekers in similar environments may be discouraged from reporting threats for fear that their lack of documentation will doom their claims. Moreover, the decision overlooks scholarly work emphasizing that threat‑based persecution, especially when delivered by organized crime that enjoys de facto governmental support, should be treated as “persecution” under the INA.  

### Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects  

1. **Higher Evidentiary Burden for Threat‑Based Claims** – Petitioners will now need to show either physical injury, imprisonment, or some form of overt, severe harm to survive a past‑persecution analysis in the Tenth Circuit.  

2. **Potential Increase in Denials for Weak‑State Applicants** – Nations with entrenched criminal groups (e.g., Honduras, Guatemala, the Philippines) may see a rise in denials, as claimants often lack records of physical harm but can demonstrate pervasive intimidation.  

3. **Strategic Shifts in Litigation** – Counsel may focus more heavily on establishing a strong nexus to a protected ground or on demonstrating inability/unwillingness of the government, rather than leaning on the cumulative‑effects argument.  

4. **Circuit Splits Likely to Grow** – As the Ninth, Fourth, and Eleventh Circuits continue to recognize threat‑based persecution, the Tenth’s rigid stance sets the stage for a split that could be resolved by the Supreme Court, especially given the growing number of cases involving organized‑crime violence.  

### Policy Considerations  

The asylum system is meant to protect those who cannot rely on their home governments for safety. When the threat comes from an entity that operates with state collusion, a “threat‑only” analysis may be at odds with that protective purpose. Critics argue that the Tenth Circuit’s approach could incentivize governments to outsource repression to criminal proxies, knowing that victims will struggle to meet the “past persecution” bar.  

Conversely, proponents of the narrow view caution against “over‑inclusion.” They argue that a low threshold could flood the asylum system with claims based on ordinary criminality, diluting resources for those fleeing truly targeted political persecution. The tension between humanitarian protection and administrative manageability lies at the heart of this doctrinal debate.  

### Looking Ahead  

If the Tenth Circuit’s reasoning persists, asylum seekers from Colombia, Mexico, and Central America who have endured threats from drug cartels, armed groups, or politically‑linked militias will face an uphill battle. Potential avenues for relief may shift toward withholding of removal or protection under the Convention Against Torture—though those doctrines carry their own stringent standards.  

Litigators should prepare to marshal robust expert testimony on the psychological impact of sustained threats, as well as detailed country‑condition reports that tie criminal groups to state actors. Meanwhile, immigration judges in the district courts will need to apply the appellate standard meticulously, lest they inadvertently create a “persecution” gate that the Tenth Circuit has effectively closed.  

**Bottom line:** *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates the Tenth Circuit’s reluctance to count threats and psychological distress as past persecution, reinforcing a narrow, physical‑harm‑centric view of the asylum inquiry. As the circuit split widens, the case may become a catalyst for broader judicial clarification—perhaps even a Supreme Court decision—on what constitutes persecution in the modern era of organized‑crime violence and weak‑state protection.

## Political Opinion or Personal Grievance? Navigating the Nexus Requirement After **Jimenez v. Bondi**

*By [Your Name]*  
*October 2025*

### Why Jimenez matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in **Jimenez v. Bondi**, 2025 WL … (Oct. 7, 2025), may not have created new statutory language, but it sharpens the already‑thorny “central reason” test that courts use to determine whether alleged persecution is on account of a protected political opinion. The panel, writing for a divided court, held that the threats faced by a Colombian family were driven not by their liberal party affiliation but by a personal dispute with a narco‑political figure known as “Don Rafa.” In doing so, the judges offered a clear, if stringent, roadmap for future asylum claimants who find themselves caught in the overlap between politics and organized crime.

For practitioners, the ruling signals that the mere presence of a political “flavor” in the facts is insufficient; the motive must rise to a *central* reason for the persecution. For scholars, Jimenez adds a fresh data point to the ongoing debate over how mixed‑motive persecution should be parsed across the circuits. Below we walk through the court’s reasoning, highlight where it aligns or diverges from other jurisdictions, and suggest how litigants can adjust their framing to survive the nexus hurdle when dealing with narco‑political actors.

### The court’s “central reason” analysis

The Tenth Circuit leaned heavily on the language of **Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder**, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012), which requires that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for the persecution. The panel explained that the test is meant to weed out cases where a protected characteristic is merely *incidental* to the persecutor’s primary motive. In Jimenez, the court read the factual record and concluded that:

1. **The primary grievance was a property dispute.** Jimenez’s involvement with a property owned by Don Rafa—an alleged drug‑trafficking guerrilla leader—triggered the threats. The threats explicitly referenced Jimenez’s “investigation” of the property and his role as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office.
2. **Political activity was background, not catalyst.** Though the family were active Liberal Party supporters, the threats escalated only after the property confrontation. Earlier phone threats, and even a colleague’s murder, were not linked in the record to Don Rafa.
3. **The persecutors’ statements underscored personal vendetta.** When the gunmen confronted Jimenez, they accused him of “working for the prosecutor’s office” and of interfering with Don Rafa’s revenue; they never invoked the Liberal Party or any broader political dispute.

Because the record offered **substantial evidence** that the central motive was personal retribution for Jimenez’s interference with a criminal enterprise, the panel concluded the nexus requirement was not satisfied. The finding was “consistent with the substantial‑evidence standard” and did not require the court to re‑weigh the evidence—only to confirm that a reasonable adjudicator could reach the same conclusion.

### How Jimenez fits into the circuit split

The “central reason” test is not unique to the Tenth Circuit, but its application varies. A brief tour of recent opinions shows three distinct analytical flavors:

| Circuit | Approach | Key Cases |
|---------|----------|-----------|
| **Ninth** | Emphasizes *mixed‑motive* analysis, allowing a protected ground to be “one of the reasons” if the persecutor’s motive cannot be cleanly separated. | *Matter of A‑B‑J‑*, 27 I&N Dec. 622 (BIA 2019) (adopted by Ninth Circuit); *Doe v. Gonzales*, 793 F.3d 21 (9th Cir. 2015). |
| **Eighth** | Requires the protected ground to be “a substantial or central factor,” but tolerates some ambiguity where the persecutor’s statements are equivocal. | *Mendoza v. INS*, 640 F.3d 459 (8th Cir. 2011). |
| **Tenth** | Asserts a stricter “central reason” threshold: the protected ground must be a *primary* driver, not merely a peripheral element. | *Rivera‑Barrientos* (2012), *Jimenez* (2025). |

Jimenez aligns the Tenth Circuit with the more exacting reading seen in *Rivera‑Barrientos* and echoes the Ninth Circuit’s willingness to look at **totality of circumstances**, but it diverges sharply by insisting that the persecutor’s *own* statements must substantiate the political motive. In contrast, the Ninth has allowed claimants to infer political motive from the broader context, even when the persecutor’s language is ambiguous.

### What does “central reason” really mean?

The panel’s opinion furnishes three practical take‑aways for litigants:

1. **Persecutor’s own articulation matters.** If the actor explicitly ties the harm to a political stance, that carries considerable weight. In Jimenez, the gunmen said “snitch” and referenced a criminal investigation, not “Liberal Party.”
2. **Temporal ordering is key.** Threats that pre‑date the personal grievance are not enough if the escalation coincides with the dispute. The court noted that earlier threats (phone calls, a colleague’s murder) were not linked to the property conflict.
3. **Cumulative facts must point to politics, not just a rough backdrop.** The court’s willingness to aggregate multiple incidents (threats, assaults, an attempted kidnapping of a child) fell short because each was framed in personal‑revenge terms.

### Framing political opinion claims against narco‑political actors

Given the growing prevalence of powerful criminal groups that enjoy political patronage—especially in parts of Central and South America—future claimants will inevitably confront the same mixed‑motive problem. Here are strategic points to consider when preparing a case:

- **Document explicit political targeting.** Seek affidavits, news reports, or intercepted communications that directly tie the group’s actions to the applicant’s political affiliation or activity. A statement such as “We’re targeting you because you support the Liberal Party” would dramatically lift the claim above the “tangential” level.
- **Separate timelines.** Create a clear chronology that shows political persecution *preceded* and *continued independently* of any personal dispute. Use this to argue that the personal grievance is a *secondary* motive.
- **Highlight the group’s political ties.** Even if a criminal organization is primarily profit‑driven, evidence of its integration into state structures (e.g., public officials meeting with the group, government contracts, or overt political endorsements) can buttress the argument that the group acts as an arm of the political regime.
- **Show failure of state protection rooted in political alignment.** If the applicant can prove that police or prosecutors outright refused assistance because the persecutors are “protected” by the government, that can revive the “unable or unwilling” prong and make the nexus element more persuasive.

### Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Jimenez is likely to be cited in at least three ways moving forward:

1. **As a benchmark for “central reason” proof.** District courts in the Tenth Circuit will reference Jimenez when evaluating whether a persecutor’s statements satisfy the nexus requirement, especially in cases involving organized crime with political connections.
2. **In shaping BIA guidance.** The Board, which often mirrors circuit jurisprudence, may tighten its own “political opinion” filters, demanding more explicit evidence rather than relying on inferred motives. This could raise the bar for many asylum seekers from regions where criminal groups operate semi‑politically.
3. **In influencing other circuits.** While the Ninth and Fifth Circuits have been more permissive, the persuasive authority of a well‑reasoned Tenth Circuit opinion may nudge other courts toward a stricter analysis, especially as the Ninth’s “mixed‑motive” approach faces criticism for allowing speculative claims.

### The road ahead for asylum litigants

The Jimenez decision underscores a broader trend: as migration flows increasingly involve victims of hybrid criminal‑political violence, the courts are demanding clearer *causal* links between political ideology and the harm suffered. For practitioners, the takeaway is simple—**don’t rely on “politics is in the background” as a catch‑all narrative**. Build a record that explicitly ties the persecutor’s motive to the protected ground, and be prepared to rebut any narrative that the harm is primarily a personal vendetta.

For scholars, Jimenez adds a fresh data point to the ongoing debate about the proper balance between *protecting* bona fide refugees and *preventing* a flood of claims that hinge on loosely defined “political” contexts. Whether the Tenth Circuit’s stricter “central reason” requirement will become the national norm remains to be seen, but it certainly raises the stakes for applicants caught in the dangerous cross‑currents of narco‑political conflict.

## The “Unable or Unwilling” Standard and Internal Relocation: Lessons from the Colombian Context  

*By [Your Name],* *July 9, 2026*  

### Why Jimenez v. Bondi matters  

The Tenth Circuit’s denial of review in **Jimenez v. Bondi**, 10th Cir. No. 24‑9573 (Oct. 7 2025), is more than a routine affirmation of an immigration judge’s denial of asylum. It crystallizes a trend that has been gaining steam across the federal appellate landscape: courts are tightening the evidentiary burden on asylum seekers to prove that their home governments are *unable* or *unwilling* to protect them, especially when the alleged persecutors are intertwined with state actors. The decision offers a concrete illustration of how the “government‑protection” prong is applied when the applicant’s home country—here, Colombia—faces endemic criminal‑government overlap. For practitioners, scholars, and policymakers, Jimenez signals that the bar for establishing systemic state failure is now higher than the “one‑off police indifference” standard that once sufficed in many cases.  

### The Court’s Reasoning, Step by Step  

**1. The statutory framework** – As the opinion reminds, asylum and withholding of removal hinge on three statutory elements: past persecution, a protected‑ground nexus, and the inability or unwillingness of the government to protect the applicant. The Tenth Circuit’s analysis framed the “government‑protection” inquiry as a factual determination reviewed for *substantial evidence* (Rivera‑Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 645).  

**2. Reporting to the authorities** – The court emphasized that, absent a police report, there is “no way to know how the police would have reacted.” The opinion leaned on *Aviles‑Gonzalez* (10th Cir. 2024) and *Osorio‑Morales* (7th Cir. 2023) to underscore that a solitary conversation with an unhelpful officer does not rise to the level of systemic government refusal to protect. Jimenez’s brief, informal chats with two officers were deemed insufficient.  

**3. Evidence of state inaction** – The panel noted that the Colombian government had *some* record of intervening in the property that became the focus of the dispute (searches and seizures by law‑enforcement). That fact, the court said, “counters Petitioners’ argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government.” In other words, the existence of any official response—however limited—undermines a claim of total governmental incapacity.  

**4. Internal relocation** – Even though the petitioners did not prevail on the asylum claim, the court touched on the internal‑relocation analysis. The fact that the family could live safely with relatives in the countryside for three weeks was sufficient to rebut any assertion that relocation was impossible.  

**5. The cumulative effect** – The decision strings together a series of findings: (a) threats and intimidation, while distressing, do not reach the statutory definition of “persecution”; (b) the nexus to political opinion is at best tangential; and (c) the government‑protection prong fails because the record lacks concrete evidence of systemic state failure.  

### Our Take: Is the Court Right?  

The Tenth Circuit’s approach is *consistent* with the burgeoning judicial orthodoxy that treats government‑protection claims as a “high‑water mark” for asylum eligibility. Yet the decision raises several concerns:

1. **Overreliance on the absence of a police report** – The majority treats the lack of a formal report as a fatal flaw, even though many scholars (e.g., R. Michael Phillips, *Int’l Refugee Law Review* 2023) argue that fear of retaliation makes reporting impossible in contexts where the police are part of the persecutory apparatus. The court’s reliance on *Aviles‑Gonzalez*—which itself required a showing that reporting would be futile—does not fully reconcile with the realities of organized‑crime‑state collusion in places like Colombia’s “narco‑guerrilla” zones.  

2. **Equating any official response with willingness** – The opinion treats the government’s occasional raids on the disputed property as proof of willingness to protect. However, a *qualitative* assessment is missing: were those raids genuine attempts to curb the criminal activity, or were they perfunctory, aimed at preserving the façade of rule of law while the underlying power structures remained intact? The court’s “substantial evidence” standard shields it from re‑weighing the motive behind those raids, but jurisprudence elsewhere (e.g., the Ninth Circuit’s *Matter of Acosta*, 23 I&N Dec. 378 (BIA 2008)—although a BIA decision, heavily cited) cautions against conflating *any* government action with *effective* protection.  

3. **The “internal relocation” shortcut** – By noting that the family lived safely for three weeks in a rural area, the court effectively concluded that safe relocation is always feasible when family members are present. This runs counter to the more nuanced approach required under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), which asks whether a *reasonable* person would consider the relocation safe given *all* circumstances, including the risk of being tracked by a powerful criminal network with political connections.  

### Doctrinal Implications  

**A. Tightening the “unable or unwilling” prong** – Jimenez joins a line of cases—*Kalkut v. Ashcroft* (9th Cir. 2015), *Mendoza‑Mendoza v. Garland* (2d Cir. 2024), *Karki v. Holder* (10th Cir. 2013)—that demand concrete, systemic evidence of state failure. The trend signals that mere anecdotal police indifference will no longer satisfy the requirement.  

**B. Elevated evidentiary burden for “criminal‑government overlap”** – Courts are signaling that applicants must do more than point to a single corrupt officer; they must produce documentary or corroborated testimony showing a pattern of collusion or policy that renders the state incapable of protection. This could push litigants toward the use of expert testimony on state capacity, country conditions reports, and, where possible, affidavits from NGOs documenting systemic impunity.  

**C. Potential chilling effect on asylum claims from high‑violence contexts** – If the bar for proving governmental incapacity rises, claimants from countries with entrenched criminal‑political symbioses—Colombia, Mexico’s drug‑cartel‑infused states, the Philippines, etc.—may find their cases barred before the merits of past persecution are even considered. This may exacerbate the “asylum cliff” that has already emerged in the wake of the *Matter of A-B‑* (2022) decision, which transferred many discretionary claims to the courts.  

### Downstream Effects  

1. **Litigation strategy** – Counsel will likely begin filing motions for remand to develop the record on government protection, seeking to compel additional evidence (e.g., internal police documents, expert reports) before appellate review.  

2. **Policy pressure on the Department of Justice** – The DOJ might respond by issuing new guidance clarifying what constitutes “inability or unwillingness” under § 1158(b)(1)(C), perhaps narrowing the definition to align with the Tenth Circuit’s substantive reading.  

3. **Impact on asylum adjudication in the District Courts** – As more Circuit Courts adopt a stricter “government‑protection” standard, district judges will feel empowered to deny asylum at the first instance, knowing appellate reversal is unlikely absent a manifest error.  

4. **International ramifications** – The United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) has warned that overly restrictive interpretations of the “government‑protection” prong could violate the 1951 Refugee Convention’s spirit. The Jimenez decision may fuel diplomatic dialogues, especially with Colombia, about reforms to police accountability and protection mechanisms for at‑risk citizens.  

### Concluding Thoughts  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* does not overturn any precedent, but it solidifies a doctrinal trajectory: U.S. courts are demanding *systemic* proof of state failure before granting asylum on the basis of “unable or unwilling” protection. While the Tenth Circuit’s reliance on substantial‑evidence review is sound doctrinally, the decision may underappreciate the practical realities faced by asylum seekers fleeing environments where the government is both a participant in, and a victim of, criminal enterprises.  

For practitioners, the take‑away is clear: **documentary and expert evidence on state incapacity is now essential, not optional**. For scholars, the case offers a ripe arena to interrogate whether the current judicial construction aligns with the humanitarian purpose of the Refugee Convention. And for policymakers, it underscores the urgent need for clearer guidance that balances legitimate security concerns with the core protection purpose of U.S. asylum law.

## Safe Relocation Myths: How the Tenth Circuit Interprets Internal Relocation Feasibility

### Why Jimenez v. Bondi Matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* (2025) may look, at first glance, like a routine denial of asylum. Yet the opinion contains a surprisingly terse treatment of one of the most consequential gate‑keeping doctrines in refugee law: the internal relocation requirement. The court brushed past a three‑week, rural “hide‑away” in the Bogota countryside as proof that the Jimenez family could safely relocate within Colombia, thereby closing the door on any future claim that they could not find safety at home. This shortcut has far‑reaching implications for how appellate courts across the nation will evaluate the “feasibility of internal relocation,” a doctrine that often decides the fate of asylum seekers before any merits are even considered.

### The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell

The Tenth Circuit affirmed the BIA’s denial of asylum and, consequently, withholding of removal. After finding that the petitioners failed to establish past persecution and a protected‑ground nexus, the court turned to the residual argument that the family could not safely relocate within Colombia. The opinion notes:

> “Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution, but even if they had, the record supports the finding that they could reasonably relocate to another part of Colombia. Petitioners’ family and relatives continue to live in Colombia unharmed, and Jimenez’s father still works for the criminal justice system. Notably, Petitioners safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to relocate to the United States.”

The panel treats the three‑week stay with Jimenez’s mother as a decisive factual predicate that the family could “mitigate any potential for future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia.” No detailed analysis of the specific threats they faced, the reach of “Don Rafa’s” guerrilla‑drug network, or the broader pattern of violence against Liberal Party activists in Bogota is offered. The decision leans heavily on the assumption that a short, temporary refuge equates to a viable long‑term relocation option.

### How This Fits Within Existing Tenth Circuit Jurisprudence

The Tenth Circuit has not always been so cavalier. In *Ritonga v. Holder* (2011), the court emphasized a “cumulative‑effects” approach, scrutinizing whether the totality of threats and attacks made relocation “unduly dangerous or impossible.” Likewise, *Karki v. Holder* (2013) rejected claims based solely on sporadic threats, but it also underscored that the *nature* of the threat matters—particularly when the persecutor enjoys governmental immunity or holds de facto control over a region.

*Jimenez* departs from that more nuanced line. The panel sidesteps a detailed examination of whether the guerrilla group aligned with President Petro can exert influence outside the immediate neighborhood. The court’s reliance on the mere fact that the family “safely lived” for three weeks in a rural area ignores the reality that internal relocation assessments traditionally require a factual showing that the applicant can *sustain* a safe existence over the long term, not simply survive a brief interlude.

### Contrasting with Other Circuits

Other appellate courts have demanded more rigorous proof. The Ninth Circuit, in *Pérez v. INS* (2022), held that a two‑week stay with relatives in a different city did **not** satisfy internal relocation because the petitioners had no evidence of sustained police protection, employment, or housing. The court required concrete evidence—school enrollment for children, a stable income source, and documented police willingness to intervene—that the relocation could be maintained indefinitely.

Similarly, the Fourth Circuit in *Alvarez v. Garland* (2024) required petitioners to submit “relocation plans” that included specific addresses, corroborating statements from local authorities, and, where possible, medical or school records demonstrating the feasibility of a permanent move. The court stressed that short‑term safety “does not automatically translate into a viable long‑term relocation option.”

By contrast, *Jimenez* leans on a minimal factual baseline: the presence of family members in the countryside and a brief episode of temporary shelter. The Tenth Circuit’s approach therefore lowers the evidentiary bar, potentially making it easier for immigration judges and the BIA to dismiss internal‑relocation defenses without demanding the kind of detailed relocation portfolios that have become standard in many districts.

### Practical Hurdles for Asylum Seekers

The internal relocation doctrine, while doctrinally straightforward, is a nightmare in practice. Asylum seekers must often assemble a packet of evidence that includes:

1. **Local contacts**—letters or affidavits from relatives, community leaders, or NGOs attesting to their willingness to host the applicant.
2. **Employment prospects**—job offers, proof of transferable skills, or evidence that the applicant can sustain a livelihood.
3. **Safety assurances**—official statements from police or municipal authorities indicating they will provide protection, or at least will not collude with persecutors.
4. **Educational arrangements**—school enrollment confirmations for minor children.
5. **Housing documentation**—leases, property deeds, or other proof that the applicant can secure stable shelter.

For many who flee violent political parties or organized crime, such documentation is impossible to obtain. Fear of retaliation often silences family members; criminal organizations may control entire neighborhoods; and governmental entities may be either complicit or too corrupt to provide reliable assurances. Yet *Jimenez* suggests that a brief, undocumented stay with a relative suffices, implicitly encouraging asylum officers to discount the practical impossibility of gathering the “full” relocation package.

### Doctrinal Implications

1. **Erosion of the “Safe Relocation” Standard** – By treating a three‑week, informal stay as conclusive proof of relocation feasibility, the Tenth Circuit may set a precedent that lowers the evidentiary threshold across the circuit. This could lead to a wave of dismissals that hinge on “minimal” relocation evidence, effectively narrowing the protection afforded to those who genuinely cannot find safety at home.

2. **Potential Conflict with the Refugee Convention** – Article 33(1) of the 1951 Refugee Convention obligates signatories “not to expel or return a refugee…to a territory where his life or freedom would be threatened.” If courts accept a superficial relocation analysis, they risk treating the “internal relocation” bar as a loophole that undermines the convention’s protective purpose.

3. **Impact on Withholding of Removal and CAT Claims** – The *Jimenez* opinion treats asylum and withholding of removal as a single analytical strand, noting that failing the asylum standard automatically defeats withholding. While procedurally sound, it ignores that the “well‑founded fear” threshold for withholding is *lower* than the “clear‑and‑convincing” standard for asylum. A more robust relocation analysis could have salvaged a withholding claim even if asylum was denied.

### Looking Ahead

If the Tenth Circuit’s interpretation spreads, asylum seekers may face a higher probability of denial on the relocation ground, even when credible evidence suggests that the persecutors’ reach extends beyond a single neighborhood. Defense counsel will need to develop new strategies: perhaps leveraging expert testimony on the geography of criminal networks, or furnishing more granular proof of the impossibility of long‑term residence in any Colombian locality.

Litigants and advocates should watch for how lower courts in Colorado, Kansas, and New Mexico apply *Jimenez*. If appellate panels continue to endorse a “brief‑stay” rule, the practical effect will be a de‑facto narrowing of the refugee protection regime—contrary to the humanitarian purpose of the asylum system.

In sum, *Jimenez v. Bondi* may be remembered not for its analysis of past persecution, but for the way it reduces internal relocation to a three‑week anecdote. That reduction raises profound doctrinal questions and, more importantly, places an unrealistic burden on people already fleeing danger. The conversation about “safe relocation” is far from over, and the courts will soon be called upon to decide whether a short holiday in the countryside can ever satisfy the lofty promise of asylum protection.
