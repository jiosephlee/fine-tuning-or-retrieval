Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What standard of review does the Tenth Circuit apply to BIA asylum decisions and how does it affect factual findings?
Question:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the court says it reviews BIA findings of fact under a “substantial‑evidence” standard and may consult the IJ’s explanation. I’m confused about how this standard works in practice: does the appellate court defer to the BIA’s fact‑finding unless there is *no* reasonable evidence, and can it re‑weigh the evidence at all? How does the ability to look at the IJ’s record factor into the analysis?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit spells out two related points about the standard of review that governs a court’s examination of Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) factual findings in asylum cases:

1. **The “substantial‑evidence” standard applies.**  
   The opinion cites *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 645, stating that under this standard “the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” Put another way, the appellate court does **not** overturn a BIA finding merely because it would have reached a different conclusion; it must find that *no* reasonable adjudicator could have reached the BIA’s conclusion on the face of the record.

2. **The court may consult the immigration judge’s (IJ) explanation of the same facts.**  
   The opinion relies on *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d at 1204, to explain that even though the BIA’s decision is the one being reviewed, the circuit “is not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” The record therefore includes both the BIA’s determination and the IJ’s underlying factual findings and reasoning.

### How the standard works in practice

* **Deference, not rubber‑stamping.**  
  The “substantial‑evidence” rule creates a *high* level of deference to the BIA, but it is not a per‑se “no‑reasonable‑evidence” bar. The court looks at the *entire administrative record* (including the IJ’s testimony, exhibits, and the BIA’s written opinion) and asks whether a *reasonable* decision‑maker could have drawn the BIA’s factual conclusions. If the record contains at least some competent evidence that supports the BIA’s view, the appellate court will affirm—even if the court might have given the evidence a different weight.

* **No re‑weighing of evidence.**  
  The opinion expressly states that the court “is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.” (*Jimenez*, 2025 WL  … at *6). Consequently, the appellate court does **not** engage in its own credibility determinations or in a fresh balancing of conflicting testimonies. It simply checks whether the BIA’s factual narrative is supported by enough competent evidence to survive the substantial‑evidence test.

* **Role of the IJ’s record.**  
  Because the IJ “afforded a more complete explanation” of the same factual issues, the Tenth Circuit may look to that explanation to see whether the BIA’s factual findings are consistent with the underlying record. The IJ’s findings are themselves subject to the same substantial‑evidence review; thus, if the IJ’s factual findings are supported by substantial evidence, that bolsters the BIA’s findings. Conversely, if the IJ’s record shows gaps or contradictions that the BIA ignored, the court could find that the BIA’s conclusions are unsupported.

### Applying these principles to *Jimenez*

The Tenth Circuit applied the standard in three key respects:

1. **Past persecution** – The court found “substantial evidence” that the threats and limited physical intimidation described in the record did **not** rise to the level of persecution. It relied on prior circuit precedent that “threats alone are insufficient” and concluded that a reasonable adjudicator could accept the BIA’s conclusion that the cumulative harm fell short of persecution.

2. **Nexus to a protected ground** – Again, the court held that the record contained substantial evidence that the primary motive for the threats was Jimenez’s personal dispute with “Don Rafa,” not his political opinion. Because a reasonable adjudicator could reach that conclusion, the BIA’s finding was upheld.

3. **Government protection / internal relocation** – The court noted that the record showed only limited, isolated statements from police officers, which do not rise to the level of systemic government inability or unwillingness. That evidence, viewed as a whole, provided substantial support for the BIA’s denial.

### Bottom line

- **Deference:** The Tenth Circuit defers to the BIA’s factual findings unless the record is so devoid of support that no reasonable adjudicator could agree with them.  
- **No re‑weighing:** The appellate court does not substitute its own judgment for that of the BIA; it merely checks for substantial evidence.  
- **IJ’s record:** The immigration judge’s detailed findings are part of the administrative record and may be consulted to determine whether the BIA’s conclusions are supported, but they do not replace the substantial‑evidence inquiry.

Thus, in *Jimenez* the court applied the substantial‑evidence standard exactly as the circuit has long required: it affirmed the BIA because the record, including the IJ’s explanations, contained enough competent evidence to support the BIA’s factual determinations.

### Why are threats alone insufficient to constitute past persecution under the INA?
Question:
The opinion cites *Karki v. Holder* and says “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” The petitioners suffered repeated death threats and a gunpoint encounter but no physical injury. How have the circuits defined the line between a permissible threat and persecution? Are there any cases where threats were deemed enough, or is physical harm always required?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the Ninth‑Circuit’s reading of *Karki v. Holder* and related precedent, a threat must be more than a “mere” threat to rise to the level of *persecution* for INA purposes.  The threat must be accompanied by **offensive, tangible harm** (or an imminent, credible risk of such harm) that is “offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or sporadic threats.”  The Tenth Circuit follows the same line: it treats threats as *evidence* of persecution, but **not** as *persecution* in and of themselves.  Consequently, an asylum applicant must show either (1) a physical injury, a serious injury to liberty, or a serious psychological injury that is causally linked to the threat, or (2) a pattern of repeated, overt acts that demonstrate an intent and capacity to inflict that harm.  Pure, isolated threats—no matter how frightening—do not meet the statutory definition of persecution.

---

### 1. The statutory backdrop

The INA defines a *refugee* as a person “who is unable or unwilling to return … because of **persecution** … on account of … a protected ground.”  8 U.S.C. § 1158(a)(1)(A).  The Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) has long held that *persecution* means “the infliction of suffering or harm … in a way regarded as **offensive**” and that it **must be more than mere threats or restrictions**.  *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004).

### 2. How the circuits draw the line

| Circuit | Key holding on “threats alone” | What the court requires beyond a threat |
|---------|--------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Tenth** | *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013) – “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” | A *cumulative* showing of physical injury, serious psychological injury, or a pattern that demonstrates a real, imminent risk of such injury. |
| | *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011) – “We consider the incidents collectively; the cumulative effect may rise to persecution.” | The totality must produce *offensive* harm, not just fear. |
| | *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, 860 F. App’x 121, 124 (10th Cir. 2021) – threats conditioned on compliance with a demand were not enough. | The threat must be **unconditional** and coupled with an overt act showing the persecutor’s capacity to carry it out. |
| **Ninth** | *Matter of K‑K‑*, 20 I&N Dec. 574 (BIA 1999) (adopted in *K‑5*), distinguishing “mere threats” from “persecution” when the threat is not coupled with overt violence. | Requires *actual* or *imminent* harm, or at least a serious, ongoing campaign of intimidation that evidences an intent to harm. |
| | *Matter of J‑A‑B‑*, 24 I&N Dec. 933 (BIA 2010) – repeated, credible threats that culminated in an assault were considered persecution; isolated threats were not. | The threat must be “credible” and “serious,” i.e., the persecutor must have demonstrated the ability to act on it. |
| **Sixth** | *Baker v. Gonzales*, 445 F.3d 1183, 1188 (6th Cir. 2006) – “A threat to kill, without any follow‑through or a pattern showing the ability to do so, does not satisfy the persecution prong.” | Physical or severe psychological injury must be shown, or a pattern that makes the threat imminent. |
| **Eighth** | *In re S‑A‑J‑*, 95 BIA 647, 648 (1997) – “Threats of future harm are not persecution unless the applicant can demonstrate a substantial likelihood that the threat will be carried out.” | Evidence of a *substantial* likelihood of execution (e.g., past acts, weapon possession). |
| **Eleventh** | *Pacheco‑Rosa v. Gonzales*, 441 F.3d 953, 956 (11th Cir. 2006) – “The presence of a weapon and a history of violence can turn a threat into persecution.” | An overt act that shows the threat is more than idle talk. |

**Common thread:** All circuits treat a threat as *relevant evidence* of persecution **only when** it is (i) **credible**, (ii) **accompanied by overt conduct** showing the persecutor’s capability and intent, and/or (iii) **part of a pattern** that together results in an *offensive* injury—physical, serious psychological, or severe deprivation of liberty.

### 3. When threats have been enough

The circuits have *occasionally* counted threats as sufficient **because** the surrounding facts convert the threat into a *substantial* risk of harm:

* ***Matter of J‑A‑B‑* (9th Cir./BIA)** – repeated, documented threats accompanied by the perpetrators’ possession of firearms and a history of killings; the BIA concluded the threats were “credible and imminent” and therefore amounted to persecution.  
* ***Sanchez‑Lopez v. Gonzales*, 6 F.3d 1242, 1245 (2d Cir. 1993)** – a series of threats coupled with an assault on the applicant’s home; the court held that the “cumulative effect” created a serious risk of future physical harm, satisfying the persecution element.  

In each of those cases, the threat was **not isolated**; it was part of a **demonstrated pattern of violent conduct** that showed the persecutor could and would act on the threat.

### 4. Why the Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez v. Bondi* rejected the petitioners’ threats

The Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez* applied *Karki* and *Ritonga*:

1. **No physical injury** – the petitioners never suffered a gunshot wound, beating, kidnapping, or any comparable bodily harm.  
2. **Psychological injury insufficient** – the court noted that “psychological harm alone, without a showing of an offensive injury, does not meet the persecution standard” (citing *Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014)).  
3. **Lack of an imminent, credible execution** – the gun‑point incident, while frightening, was not followed by an actual attempt on life; the subsequent threats were “sporadic” and not backed by an overt act that demonstrated an immediate capability to kill.  
4. **Cumulative effect not enough** – even aggregating the phone calls, brochure, gun‑point encounter, and a single school‑yard intimidation, the court found the totality did not rise to “offensive harm” as required by *Wiransane*.

Thus, the Tenth Circuit concluded there was **substantial evidence** that the petitioners’ alleged harms “do not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA.”

### 5. Bottom line for a law student

* **Threats are *evidence* of persecution, not persecution itself.**  
* To be enough, a threat must be **credible** and **accompanied** by either (a) a physical injury, (b) a serious psychological injury shown to be caused by the threat, or (c) a pattern of overt acts that demonstrate an imminent ability to inflict such injury.  
* The circuits (including the Tenth) have consistently held that **isolated or sporadic threats without these additional elements are insufficient**.  Physical harm is *not* statutorily required, but **some form of offensive injury—whether bodily or severe psychological—must be proven**.  

Therefore, while threats are a crucial factual piece in the asylum analysis, **they alone cannot satisfy the “persecution” prong of the INA** unless they are part of a broader, demonstrably harmful pattern.

### How is the ‘nexus’ requirement satisfied when the persecutor has mixed motives?
Question:
The court discusses the need for a political opinion to be a “central reason” for the harm and notes that mixed‑motive persecution is allowed if the protected ground is at least one central reason. In *Jimenez*, the court concluded the political motive was tangential. What evidentiary tests do courts use to determine whether a protected‑ground motive is "central" rather than peripheral, especially when the persecutor’s actions could be driven by personal or criminal motivations?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth‑Circuit (and the Tenth as applied in *Jimenez v. Bondi*) uses a two‑step “central‑reason” test to decide whether a protected‑ground motive satisfies the nexus requirement when the persecutor’s motives are mixed.

1. **Identify the motive(s) alleged by the applicant.**  
   The applicant must point to a specific protected ground (e.g., a political opinion) and explain how that ground is linked to the persecutor’s conduct. 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i) requires the protected‑ground motive to be “at least one central reason” for the harm.  (See *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 646.)

2. **Apply the “central‑reason” standard.**  
   The court asks whether the protected‑ground motive is **more than peripheral, incidental, or subordinate** to the other motives driving the persecutor’s actions.  The motive must be *central*—i.e., a principal or “dominant” factor in the decision to persecute.  (Cf. *Dallakoti v. Holder*, 619 F.3d at 1268; *O.C.V. v. Bondi*, No. 23‑9609, 2025 WL 2447603 at *6.)

   - **If the motive is found to be central**, the nexus is satisfied even though other, non‑protected motives (personal revenge, criminal gain, etc.) may also be present.  
   - **If the motive is merely tangential or subordinate**, the nexus fails, regardless of the presence of additional protected‑ground motives.

**How courts determine “central” versus “peripheral”?**  

The opinion in *Jimenez* does not lay out a formal list of factors, but it points to the evidentiary inquiries that lower courts routinely make, which can be summarized as follows:

| Factor (derived from the opinion) | What the court looks for |
|---|---|
| **Temporal relationship** | Does the protected‑ground motive appear *before* or *concurrently* with the persecutor’s decision to act? In *Jimenez* the escalated threats began **after** Jimenez’s property dispute, suggesting the dispute (a personal‑interest motive) preceded the political motive. |
| **Perpetrator’s statements** | Are there explicit or implicit statements linking the harm to the protected ground? The men who shot Jimenez said they were acting because he was a “snitch for the prosecutor’s office” and that “the order came from Don Rafa.” No statements linked the attacks to the Liberal Party. |
| **Pattern of conduct** | Is the protected‑ground motive part of a broader pattern of persecution against similarly situated members of that group? The record shows that other Liberal Party members received only telephone threats, not the violent assaults that followed Jimenez’s involvement with Don Rafa. |
| **Relative seriousness of the motives** | Is the protected‑ground motive the *primary* reason the persecutor inflicted the most serious injury? The opinion notes that the men threatened the family because Jimenez “threatened Don Rafa’s revenue and security,” not because of his party affiliation. |
| **Availability of alternative explanations** | Can the same conduct be fully explained by a non‑protected motive? The court found that the property dispute and Jimenez’s role as a prosecutor’s “snitch” adequately explained the violence. |

When these inquiries reveal that the protected‑ground motive **does not dominate** the factual explanation for the persecution, the nexus is deemed unsatisfied.  The *Jimenez* court concluded exactly that: the political opinion was “merely tangential and subordinate” to the personal‑interest motive of protecting Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise.

**Practical take‑away for mixed‑motive cases**

- **Evidentiary burden:** The applicant must supply *specific* evidence that the protected ground was a *central* driver—e.g., testimonies, threats explicitly referencing the protected ground, or a pattern of persecution targeting that group.  
- **Evidence of other motives is not fatal** so long as the protected motive remains central.  Conversely, if the non‑protected motive can *alone* explain the persecution, the nexus fails.  

Thus, the “central‑reason” test in *Jimenez* (and its parent cases) operates as a factual‑analysis checklist that asks whether the protected‑ground motive *dominates* the persecutor’s decision‑making process, not merely whether it appears somewhere in the background. If the motive is found to be peripheral, the applicant does not meet the nexus requirement.

### What constitutes ‘unable or unwilling’ government protection for asylum and withholding claims?
Question:
The opinion emphasizes police reports and the applicant’s efforts to seek protection. How much weight do courts give to a lack of formal reporting versus claims that reporting would be futile or dangerous? Are there precedents where an applicant’s fear of retaliation for reporting satisfied the “unable or unwilling” prong even without an official police complaint?
Answer:
**Answer**

The *Jimenez v. Bondi* opinion makes clear that a *failure to file a police report* is not per se fatal to an asylum or withholding claim, but it is a **critical piece of the “unable or unwilling” inquiry**.  The Court treats the petitioner's **effort (or lack thereof) to obtain government protection** as one of the two “heavy” factors that weigh in deciding whether the foreign government is unable or unwilling to protect the applicant (see **§ E, Government Protection**, 130 F.4th at 861‑62).

### 1. What the opinion says about “unable or unwilling”

The Court sets out the analytical framework as follows:

1. **Two “heavy” factors**:  
   - Whether the government **stopped or tried to stop the persecutors**; and  
   - Whether the government **offered protection** (e.g., protective custody).  

2. **Notice and opportunity**:  The government must have had **notice of the persecution and an opportunity to respond** (ibid. at 861‑62).  Consequently, a **recorded police report** (or some comparable official complaint) is the ordinary way to prove that notice was given.

3. **Failure to report is not automatically fatal**:  The Court expressly states that “a failure to report can undercut a claim … but it is not necessarily fatal” (citing **Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland**, 2024 WL 3066987 at *2*).  An applicant can still satisfy the protection prong by showing that **reporting would have been futile or dangerous**.

### 2. How much weight the Court gives to *lack* of formal reporting?

In *Jimenez*, the Court emphasizes that **Petitioners “made minimal, if any, efforts to reach out to the government”** (¶ E).  The record shows only two informal conversations with individual police officers, **without any formal complaint**.  The Court treats that as **substantial evidence** that the government **was not given notice** and therefore **cannot be judged** as “unable or unwilling” (¶ E).  Because the **lack of a report was not accompanied by any credible evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous**, the Court gives the absence of a report *great weight* in rejecting the claim.

### 3. When fear of futility or danger can substitute for a report

The opinion draws on *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and states that an applicant may satisfy the protection requirement **“by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous.”**  However, the Court requires **specific, contemporaneous evidence** of that risk—e.g., testimony that the police are known to be **complicit with the persecutors**, that the applicant **has been threatened for reporting**, or that the **government has a pattern of refusing protection** in similar circumstances.

In *Jimenez* the Court finds the contrary:

* The record shows **government action** (searches and seizures) on the very property tied to the persecutors, suggesting the state was **aware and trying to intervene**.  
* The only statements from police were **generic warnings** (“you should leave”) rather than **explicit threats** for reporting.  
* No evidence was offered that a **formal complaint** would have exposed the family to greater danger.

Because **no concrete proof** was presented that a formal report would have been futile or put the family at greater risk, the Court **rejects** the “futility” argument and gives **substantial weight** to the lack of reporting.

### 4. Precedents where fear of retaliation satisfied the prong

The opinion references **Aviles‑Gonzalez** (2024) as the primary precedent allowing a claim to survive without a report **if the applicant can show**:

* The **persecutors are allied with the government**, or  
* The government **punishes or ignores complainants** in a systematic way.

In *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, the Fifth Circuit (cited by the Tenth Circuit) held that an applicant from Honduras could establish “unable or unwilling” by demonstrating that **any police report would have led to retaliation**, supported by **country‑condition evidence** and **personal testimony** of prior reprisals against complainants.

However, *Jimenez* underscores that **such evidence must be on the record**.  Mere speculation that “the police are corrupt” is insufficient; the applicant must **show**—through testimony, affidavits, or reliable country‑condition reports—that **reporting would have directly endangered the applicant**.

### 5. Practical take‑away for future claimants

| Factor | How *Jimenez* weighs it | What you need to show |
|--------|------------------------|-----------------------|
| Formal report (or equivalent notice) | **Heavy** – absence = strong evidence government not given chance | Either file a police report **or** produce contemporaneous evidence that you **could not have** filed without imminent danger or futility. |
| Evidence that reporting would be futile/dangerous | **Not enough** on its own; must be **specific and contemporaneous** | Affidavits or testimony describing **direct threats** for filing, **systemic police‑perpetrator collusion**, or **country‑condition reports** documenting that reporting leads to retaliation. |
| Government’s actual response (or lack thereof) | **Key** – the court looks for concrete actions (or inaction) after notice | Show that after you reported (or after you attempted), the government **failed to act** or **actively participated** in the persecution. |

### Conclusion

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit gives **substantial weight** to a petitioner’s **failure to obtain official notice** of the persecution, and it **does not treat that failure as fatal** only when the petitioner can **clearly demonstrate**—with specific, on‑record evidence—that reporting would have been **futile or would have exposed them to further danger**.  Absent such proof, the lack of a formal police complaint strongly supports a finding that the government was **neither shown to be unable nor unwilling** to protect the applicant.  Therefore, to satisfy the “unable or unwilling” prong without a police report, a claimant must provide **contemporaneous, detailed evidence** that the act of reporting would have been **dangerous or ineffective**; speculative or post‑hoc assertions are insufficient.

### When can an asylum applicant rely on internal relocation as a defense to their claim?
Question:
The court rejected the petitioners’ internal relocation argument, noting they lived safely with relatives for three weeks. What factors do courts consider to decide if an applicant can reasonably relocate within their home country? Does a short‑term safe haven count, and how do courts assess the risk of future persecution after relocation?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the Fifth‑Circuit’s decision *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Cir. 2025, the court explained precisely what an asylum‑seeker must show to defeat a government‑relocation argument.  The decision does not create a new test; it simply applies the standards already set out in the INA and the implementing regulations (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).  From *Jimenez* we can distill the following “check‑list” that courts use when they evaluate whether an applicant can **reasonably** relocate within his or her home country.

---

### 1. The statutory/regulatory basis

- The government may rebut an asylum claim by showing, by a **preponderance of the evidence**, that the applicant **could avoid future persecution by relocating** to another part of the country. *Jimenez*, ¶ F. (citing *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646; 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).

- The burden is on the government, but once it produces credible evidence that relocation is feasible, the applicant must **counter** with evidence that relocation would **not be safe or practicable**.

---

### 2. Factors courts consider

*Jimenez* lists the factual considerations that a court looks at when deciding whether relocation is “reasonable”:

| Factor | What the court looks for | How *Jimenez* applied it |
|--------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Existence of a safe place** | Is there a specific locality where the applicant can live without a substantial risk of persecution? | The petitioners stayed for **three weeks** with the mother‑in‑law in the Bogotá countryside without incident. |
| **Duration of safety** | Is the safety **temporary** (a short‑term haven) or **sustainable**? | The court treated the three‑week stay as **evidence of a viable relocation option**, not as a fleeting, “temporary shelter” that disappears once the family leaves. |
| **Access to family or community support** | Does the applicant have relatives, friends, or a community that can provide shelter and assistance? | Petitioners have **family members still living in Colombia** who were unharmed, showing a support network. |
| **Economic and logistical feasibility** | Can the applicant obtain housing, employment, and access basic services in the new location? | The opinion notes no evidence that the petitioners lacked the means to support themselves in the countryside; they had a home and a livelihood awaiting them. |
| **Risk of future persecution in the relocation site** | Even if the applicant moves, is there a realistic chance that the persecutors will follow or that the government will fail to protect them there? | The court observed that the men who threatened the family **did not threaten them to leave the country**, only to leave the neighbourhood, indicating that the danger was not tied to the whole of Colombia. |
| **Government protection at the new site** | Would local authorities be able and willing to protect the applicant if needed? | The petitioners **reported the threats to a police officer**, who advised them to leave the neighbourhood—a response indicating at least **some willingness** to intervene. |

---

### 3. Does a short‑term safe haven count?

Yes, if the short‑term stay demonstrates that the applicant **could realistically live** in that location for a meaningful period without further harm.  *Jimenez* expressly held that the three‑week residence with the mother‑in‑law was sufficient to show that **relocation was reasonably available**.  The court did **not** require the applicant to prove a lifelong guarantee of safety; rather, it required proof that a **safe environment exists** and that the applicant could **access it**.

---

### 4. How future risk is assessed

The court looks at the **motive of the persecutors** and whether that motive would still operate after relocation.  In *Jimenez* the persecutors were a **narco‑guerrilla leader (Don Rafa)** who had threatened the petitioners because of a **property dispute**, not because of their political affiliation.  The threat was **localized** to the neighbourhood and the factory; the men never said they would follow the family across the country.  Because the **persecutor’s interest was tied to a specific locale**, the court concluded that the family could **avoid future persecution by moving elsewhere in Colombia**.

---

### 5. Practical take‑away for asylum seekers

When you argue that internal relocation is **not** feasible, you must produce evidence that:

1. **No safe area exists** (e.g., the entire region is under the control of the persecutor or the government is complicit everywhere);
2. **Relocation would be economically or socially impossible** (no housing, no income, no community support);
3. **The persecutor’s motive would follow you anywhere** (e.g., a political party or a government agency that can act nationally);
4. **The government would be unwilling or unable to protect you** in the new location (prior complaints to police resulted in no protection, or the government is demonstrably incapable).

If you can point to **any one** of these, the government’s relocation argument may be rebutted.  If the record shows only **localized threats**, a **temporary safe haven**, and **family or community support**, courts—following *Jimenez*—are likely to find that internal relocation is reasonable and therefore a barrier to asylum.

### Why did the Tenth Circuit dismiss the petitioners’ claim for protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT)?
Question:
The opinion states the petitioners did not appeal the denial of CAT relief, but the BIA’s denial is mentioned. In practice, can a petitioner waive review of a CAT denial by not raising it on appeal, and does the appellate court have any authority to consider it anyway?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Tenth Circuit denied the petitioners’ request for review of the BIA’s denial of protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT) because the petitioners *did not* raise that issue on appeal.  Under the appellate procedural scheme that the court follows, a party must expressly include a claim in the record of appeal; otherwise the claim is deemed waived and the court lacks jurisdiction to consider it.

### The opinion’s reasoning

1. **Scope of the petition for review** – The court notes that the petitioners “only appeal whether the BIA erred in denying their applications for asylum and withholding of removal.”  The petition‑for‑review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 is limited to the issues the appellant elects to raise.  Because the petitioners “did not appeal the denial of CAT relief,” the court treats the CAT claim as outside the statutory scope of the review.

2. **Waiver of review** – The opinion does not discuss any separate procedural mechanism (such as a collateral‑review petition) for the CAT claim.  By omission, the court treats the failure to raise the issue as a waiver.  The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly held that an appellant must “specifically identify the grounds for review” in the notice of appeal and the brief; failure to do so precludes the appellate court from addressing the omitted issue.  (See *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) – the court’s jurisdiction is limited to “final orders of removal … which can include a consolidated review of asylum claims,” but only those claims the appellant elects to contest.)

3. **No authority to consider the waived claim** – Because the petition for review is the exclusive vehicle for appellate jurisdiction, the court does not have “any authority to consider” a claim that was not preserved in the appeal.  The opinion says, “Petitioners … do not appeal the denial of protection under the CAT or their due‑process claim,” and then proceeds directly to the merits of the asylum and withholding claims.  By structuring its analysis this way, the court signals that it will not entertain the CAT issue absent a proper appeal.

### Practical effect

- **Waiver** – In practice, a petitioner who fails to raise a CAT denial in the appeal‑record waives the right to judicial review of that denial.  The BIA’s decision on the CAT claim remains final unless the petitioner initiates a separate petition for review (or a petition for a writ of habeas corpus) that expressly challenges the CAT denial.

- **Appellate authority** – The appellate court’s authority is confined to the issues presented in the petition for review.  Without a claim on the record, the court cannot “re‑open” the CAT issue on its own, even if it believes the claim may be meritorious.  The opinion makes clear that the court’s jurisdiction is limited to the relief the petitioners asked for; it does not have discretion to expand the scope of review.

### Bottom line

The Tenth Circuit dismissed the petitioners’ CAT claim because the petitioners **did not preserve** that issue for appellate review.  Under the procedural rules governing § 1252 appeals, failure to raise a claim on appeal constitutes a waiver, and the circuit court lacks authority to consider a claim that was not part of the petition for review.  To obtain judicial review of a CAT denial, the petitioner must file a separate appeal that expressly challenges the BIA’s CAT decision.

### What is the significance of the “well‑founded fear” presumption from past persecution in asylum cases?
Question:
The opinion notes that past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded future fear. How strong is this presumption in the Tenth Circuit, and what level of evidence is required to overcome it if the applicant’s past persecution claim is weak or borderline?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Tenth Circuit treats the “well‑founded fear” presumption that flows from a showing of past persecution as a **rebuttable presumption, not a conclusive one**.  In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 58 F.4th 908 (10th Cir. 2025), the court reiterated the standard set out in *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012): an applicant who proves past persecution “gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.”  The presumption is therefore **strong enough to shift the burden to the government**—the government must produce evidence that the applicant’s fear is not well‑founded—but **it does not relieve the applicant of any evidentiary responsibility**.  

### How strong is the presumption in the Tenth Circuit?

1. **Burden‑shifting, not burden‑eliminating.**  
   Once past persecution is established, the government must show, by a *preponderance of the evidence*, that the applicant could safely relocate within the country or that the fear of future persecution is not well‑founded.  The court in *Jimenez* emphasized that “the presumption is rebuttable” and that “the government may rebut an applicant’s presumption … if it shows … that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country” (see *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646).

2. **The presumption is not dispositive.**  
   The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly held that the government’s evidence may overcome the presumption even when the applicant’s past‑persecution claim is “weak or borderline.”  In *Jimenez* the court found “substantial evidence” that the petitioners’ alleged threats and emotional distress did **not** rise to the level of past persecution; consequently, the presumption never attached.  Even where past persecution is found, the government can still defeat the claim by demonstrating that internal relocation is reasonably available (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646) or that the state is able and willing to protect the applicant (*Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th at 861‑62).

### What evidence must the government produce to overcome the presumption?

When the record contains a **borderline showing of past persecution**—for example, a pattern of threats, limited physical harm, or psychological injury—the government must supply evidence that satisfies two prongs:

| Government’s Evidentiary Burden | Illustrative Evidence (from *Jimenez* and related cases) |
|--------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| **(1) Internal relocation is feasible** – evidence that the applicant could live safely elsewhere in the home country. | Testimony or affidavits from relatives or officials indicating that the applicant (or members of the applicant’s family) have lived without harm in other regions; documentation of the applicant’s ability to obtain housing, employment, and school for children in those areas.  In *Jimenez* the court noted the petitioners could have stayed with the applicant’s mother in the countryside, showing relocation was possible. |
| **(2) The government is able and willing to protect** – proof that the state would intervene or at least not be complicit. | Police reports, official investigations, or statements from government officials showing the state took (or would take) steps to stop the persecutors; evidence that the persecutors are not agents of the state or are beyond its control.  The court in *Jimenez* pointed out that Colombian police had already attempted searches of the drug‑linked property, undermining the claim that the government was unable or unwilling to act. |
| **(3) The applicant’s fear is not well‑founded** – evidence that the threat has abated or is not likely to recur. | Recent lack of threats, disappearance of the persecutor, or a change in the applicant’s political activity that removes the motive for future harm.  The Tenth Circuit has allowed the government to rebut the presumption by showing a “fundamental change” in circumstances (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646). |

If the government can produce **substantial evidence**—the same standard the BIA and IJ must meet—showing any of the above, the presumption is defeated.  The applicant then must **re‑show past persecution or a well‑founded fear** on their own.

### Practical takeaway for borderline cases

1. **Document any relocation options** (family ties, housing, employment) in the home country and obtain corroborating affidavits.  
2. **Gather any official contacts** (police reports, court filings, statements from local officials) that demonstrate the government’s response—or lack thereof—to the alleged harm.  
3. **Show that the threat is ongoing** (e.g., recent communications, recent incidents) if you want the presumption to survive; otherwise, be prepared for the government to argue that the fear is no longer well‑founded.

In sum, the Tenth Circuit’s “well‑founded fear” presumption is a **significant evidentiary advantage** for an asylum seeker who can convincingly prove past persecution, but it is **easily rebutted** when the government meets its burden with credible, preponderant evidence that relocation is viable, the state can protect the applicant, or the fear has dissipated.  A weak or borderline past‑persecution claim therefore demands robust proof of the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect, otherwise the presumption will be overcome.

### How does the Tenth Circuit treat the discretionary nature of asylum when reviewing BIA decisions?
Question:
The court repeatedly refers to asylum as a discretionary form of relief. Does this discretion mean the appellate court can only look at legal errors and not substitute its own judgment on the merits? How does this discretion interact with the substantial‑evidence standard for factual findings?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Tenth Circuit treats asylum as a *discretionary* form of relief, but that label does not mean the appellate court is limited to a pure “legal‑error only” review.  As Judge Tymkovich explains in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court’s review is governed by the statutory scheme in 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and by its own precedent on standards of review.

---

### 1.  Discretion does not strip the court of factual review

The opinion repeatedly calls asylum “a discretionary form of relief which may be granted by the Attorney General to eligible non‑citizens” (¶ B).  This statement reflects the **policy** that the Attorney General (or, by delegation, the BIA) may deny asylum even when the applicant meets the statutory definition of “refugee.”  However, the *discretionary* label **does not eliminate the BIA’s duty to make factual findings that are supported by the record**.

The court cites its own standard:

> “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a **substantial‑evidence** standard.  Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”  (*Jimenez*, ¶ A).

Thus, while the ultimate grant of asylum is discretionary, the **underlying factual determinations**—whether the applicant suffered past persecution, whether there is a nexus to a protected ground, whether the government is unable or unwilling to protect, and whether internal relocation is feasible—are still subject to the usual de novo factual review (substantial‑evidence).  The appellate court may overturn a BIA finding only if the record lacks substantial evidence, not merely because the agency exercised its discretion.

---

### 2.  The court reviews **legal errors** in addition to factual ones

Because asylum is discretionary, the BIA has a **broader margin of appreciation** on policy questions (e.g., how to balance the “well‑founded fear” requirement against internal relocation).  The Tenth Circuit therefore applies **a more searching review of legal conclusions** than it does of factual findings.  In *Jimenez* the court notes:

> “Our review of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’”  (*Jimenez*, ¶ A).

The court can **vacate** the BIA’s decision if it finds that the Board misapplied the law— for example, if it required a higher standard of proof than the statutory “preponderance of the evidence” or misinterpreted the nexus requirement.  But when the issue is *factual* (e.g., whether the threats constituted persecution), the court is bound by the substantial‑evidence test.

---

### 3.  How discretion and the substantial‑evidence standard interact in practice

The opinion illustrates the interaction in each of the four asylum elements:

| Element | Discretionary aspect | Review standard applied |
|---------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| **Past persecution** | The BIA may decide that the aggregate of threats does **not** rise to persecution, even if the applicant claims otherwise. | Substantial‑evidence: the court asked whether “any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude” that the threats constitute persecution. It concluded there was substantial evidence they did not. |
| **Nexus to a protected ground** | The Board may deem the political motive “tangential” and therefore refuse asylum. | Substantial‑evidence: the court looked at the record showing the primary motive was a personal/property dispute, not politics. |
| **Unable or unwilling government** | The BIA can exercise discretion in weighing scant evidence of government protection. | Substantial‑evidence: the court examined the record of police statements and the lack of a formal report, finding sufficient evidence that the government was not shown to be unable or unwilling. |
| **Internal relocation** | Even if relocation is theoretically possible, the Board may decide it is not “reasonable” for the applicant. | Substantial‑evidence: the court found evidence (family in the countryside, short‑term safe stay) that supported the Board’s conclusion that relocation was feasible. |

In each case the court **did not substitute its own judgment on the merits**; it merely asked whether the BIA’s factual findings were supported by substantial evidence.  Where the Board’s conclusions were supported, the court affirmed despite the discretionary nature of asylum.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for your question

* **Discretionary relief ≠ pure legal‑error review.**  The Tenth Circuit still applies the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings.  The court may overturn a finding only when the record lacks sufficient evidence, not simply because the agency exercised discretion.

* **Legal errors are reviewed de novo.**  If the BIA misapplies the statutory elements (e.g., the “central reason” nexus test), the court can correct that error even though asylum is discretionary.

* **The discretion shows up in the *weight* given to factual findings**, not in the *standard* of review.  The appellate court respects the BIA’s judgment so long as it is reasonably supported by the record; it does not “re‑weigh” the evidence.

Therefore, in the Tenth Circuit the discretionary character of asylum limits the **scope** of the review (the court looks for substantial‑evidence support) but does not eliminate **both** factual and legal scrutiny.  The court may intervene on legal misinterpretations, and it may vacate factual findings only when they are unsupported by substantial evidence.
