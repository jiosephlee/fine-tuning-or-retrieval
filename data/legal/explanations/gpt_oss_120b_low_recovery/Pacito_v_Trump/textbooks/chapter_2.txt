Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # Statutory Framework Governing Refugee Admissions and Resettlement  

In *Pacito v. Trump* the Ninth Circuit wrestled with two distinct statutory regimes: (1) the President’s authority to halt the admission of refugees under the immigration statutes, and (2) the Congress‑mandated (or authorized) requirement that the Government provide “reception and placement” services to refugees who are admitted.  Understanding the opinion requires a clear picture of the relevant provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), the Refugee Act of 1980, and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).  This chapter isolates the statutes the court looked to, explains the textual choices that matter (e.g., **shall** versus **may**), and shows how those provisions interacted with the procedural posture of the case.

---

## 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a) – Presidential Power to Suspend Entry of Aliens  

### Textual grant of authority  

*Section 1182(f)* reads, in pertinent part:  

> “Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or non‑immigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem appropriate.”  

*Section 1185(a)* authorizes the President, by executive order, to “direct the suspension of the entry of any alien or class of alien” consistent with the finding requirement in § 1182(f).

### How the Court applied the text  

The panel’s order quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* (585 U.S. 667 (2018)) to emphasize that the statutory language “exudes deference” and confers “ample power” on the President.  The Court’s analysis focused on the **single prerequisite**: the President must *find* that entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.”  The record shows President Trump made that finding in Executive Order 14163, § 3(a).  Accordingly, the panel concluded the President satisfied the statutory condition and that the language “does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.”  

Because the statute uses the permissive verb **may** for the suspension and the qualifying phrase “as he shall deem necessary,” the court treated the President’s discretion as virtually unreviewable on the merits.  The stay‑standard analysis therefore gave the government a strong showing of likely success on the merits of that claim, even though the court noted it would not conduct a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.”  

**Key take‑away for students:** When a statute grants power in *may* terms and imposes only a minimal factual predicate (“the President finds…”), appellate courts typically apply a highly deferential standard.  The question for a stay is whether the applicant can show that the statutory prerequisite has been met—not whether the policy choice is wise.

## The Refugee Act of 1980 and Its Incorporation into the INA  

The Refugee Act of 1980 (Pub. L. 96‑212) codified the United States’ historic commitment to admitting persons who qualify as refugees.  It was woven into the INA at **8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.**, creating a statutory scheme that (1) defines “refugee,” (2) sets the annual admission ceiling, and (3) establishes the procedural framework for refugee status determinations.  

In *Pacito*, plaintiffs argued that Executive Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act because it effectively “suspended” the program without adhering to the Act’s statutory scheme.  The Ninth Circuit, however, treated the Refugee Act **as a procedural and substantive overlay** on the broader immigration authority, not as a separate source of limitation on the President’s § 1182(f) power.  The court therefore focused on the textual grant of authority in § 1182(f) rather than on any “general rule” language from the Refugee Act.  

**Lesson:** The Refugee Act creates rights and procedures for prospective refugees, but it does **not** curtail the President’s statutory power to suspend admissions when the narrow § 1182(f) condition is satisfied.  Students should keep in mind that the Act can be “overridden” by a valid exercise of the suspension authority, unless Congress amends the INA to impose additional constraints.

## 8 U.S.C. § 1522 – Reception and Placement Services: Mandate or Authorization?  

### Statutory structure  

Section 1522 is the core of the statutory dispute in *Pacito*.  It is divided into several subsections:

| Subsection | Heading (as in the statute) | Key language |
|------------|----------------------------|--------------|
| **(a)** | **Conditions and consideration** | Uses **shall** and the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” |
| **(b)** | **Authorization for programs** | Repeats **may** (or “authorized”) for developing programs. |
| **(c)** | **Program of initial resettlement** | Uses **may** for grants and contracts. |
| **(d)** | **Unaccompanied minor refugees** | Mix of **shall** (legal responsibility) and **may** (to make necessary decisions). |
| **(e)** | **Cash assistance, medical assistance, and case management** | Contains both **shall** (Secretary shall develop projects) and permissive language. |

The tension in the opinion stems from the differing grammatical moods—**shall** (imperative) versus **may** (permissive)—and from the statutory title of § 1522: “**Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees**.”

### How the panel read the provision  

The majority treated § 1522 as imposing at least a **statutory duty** to provide reception and placement services, reasoning that the “shall” language in subsection (a) creates mandatory conditions that attach when the government elects to fund refugee programs.  Because Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for FY 2025 (later reduced by $800 million), the majority concluded there were *available appropriations* and thus the government must satisfy the “shall” conditions.

The dissent (Judge Lee) argued the opposite: the statute’s **title** and the repeated use of “authorize” signal that Congress intended only to give the Executive discretion to fund such programs, not to obligate it.  Lee emphasized that even the “shall” language in (a) is limited to “in providing assistance under this section,” i.e., it conditions the *exercise* of discretion, not the existence of the duty itself.

**Why the language matters:**  
* **“Shall”** typically imposes a mandatory duty.  
* **“May”** indicates discretion.  
When a statute contains both, courts look to the **overall structure, headings, and statutory purpose** to determine whether the duty is mandatory or merely optional.  In *Pacito*, the split reflects a classic statutory‑interpretation problem—students should be comfortable identifying which subsections use which verbs and understanding how a court may infer a *conditional* duty.

---

## Key Subsections of § 1522 and Their Interpretive Signals  

### § 1522(a) – Conditions and Consideration  

> “The Director… **shall**, to the extent of available appropriations, make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement… provide English language training… insure cash assistance… insure gender equity…”  

*Interpretive note*: The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifies the **shall**.  The duty exists only if funding exists.  The majority read this as a **mandatory** condition attached to any funded program; the dissent read it as a **conditional prerequisite** that only triggers when the government decides to fund.

### § 1522(b) – Authorization for Programs  

> “The Director **is authorized** to establish programs… ”  

*Interpretive note*: The word **authorized** (a permissive term) signals Congress is giving the Executive *permission* to create programs but not requiring it.  This subsection was central to Lee’s argument that the entire chapter is permissive.

### § 1522(c) – Program of Initial Resettlement  

> “The Director **may** enter into contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements… ”  

*Interpretive note*: The **may** language underscores discretion over the mechanisms (contracts, grants, etc.) used to deliver services.  It does not, by itself, create a duty to enter into any agreement.

### § 1522(d)(2)(B) – Unaccompanied Minor Refugees  

> “During any interim period… the Director **shall** assume legal responsibility… and **is authorized** to make necessary decisions…”  

*Interpretive note*: The **shall** imposes a duty *if* an unaccompanied minor is present and the interim period applies; the **is authorized** clause again frames the actual actions as discretionary.  The court noted the factual record did not show any such minors, weakening the plaintiffs’ claim under this subsection.

### § 1522(e)(7)(A) – Cash and Medical Assistance  

> “The Secretary **shall** develop and implement alternative projects for refugees… ”  

*Interpretive note*: The **shall** creates a duty to *develop* projects, but the statute leaves the *scope* and *content* of those projects to the Secretary’s discretion (“as needed”).  This hybrid language contributed to the split in the Ninth Circuit.

## APA Provisions Relevant to Agency Action and Termination of Contracts  

The plaintiffs also alleged violations of the **Administrative Procedure Act**, 5 U.S.C. §§ 551‑559.  Two APA concepts were pivotal:

1. **Rule‑making vs. non‑rulemaking actions** – The termination of cooperative agreements is a *non‑rulemaking* action (a “contracting decision”).  Under **§ 706(2)(A)**, the APA’s “notice‑and‑comment” requirements apply only to *rules*; agency contracts are generally **not** subject to those procedures, though agencies must still obey the **arbitrary‑and‑capricious** standard under **§ 706(1)** when reviewing agency actions.

2. **Arbitrary‑and‑Capricious Standard** – When a court reviews an agency’s decision to terminate contracts, it asks whether the agency considered the relevant factors, provided a reasoned explanation, and avoided clear errors of judgment.  In *Pacito*, the district court’s injunction forced the State Department to **reinstate** the agreements, effectively finding that the government’s termination was arbitrary because it left the statutory duties in § 1522 unfulfilled.

The Ninth Circuit’s order indicated that the government “is unlikely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” suggesting that the agency may have failed to demonstrate that the termination was based on a reasoned analysis of the statutory requirements and the available appropriations.

**Study tip:** When a case involves both an immigration‑statute claim and an APA claim, treat them as separate layers of analysis.  The *immigration* claim hinges on statutory text and deference; the *APA* claim hinges on procedural adequacy and the agency’s rational explanation.

## Congressional Appropriations for Refugee Assistance and the 2025 Rescissions  

Congress appropriated **$3.178 billion** for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 (Pub. L. 119‑4 § 1101(a)(11)).  The **Rescissions Act of 2025** later cut **$800 million** from that total.  The appropriations statute is crucial for two reasons:

* **Link to § 1522(a)** – The “to the extent of available appropriations” clause means that if the appropriations are insufficient to fund the mandatory services, the government may be excused from the **shall** duties.  The majority believed that, even after the rescission, enough funds remained to trigger the duty; the dissent argued that the rescission demonstrated congressional intent to reduce the obligation, thereby weakening the claim of a mandatory duty.

* **Policy context for the stay analysis** – The stay standards ask whether the government will suffer irreparable injury if the injunction remains.  The government argued that without the ability to reallocate the rescued funds, its ability to meet other national security and immigration priorities would be impaired.  The panel, weighing the **public‑interest** factor, found the broad presidential discretion in immigration matters outweighed the narrowed funding landscape.

**Take‑away:** Appropriations can transform permissive language into a mandatory duty (or vice‑versa) when the statute ties the duty to “available appropriations.”  Courts will scrutinize the amount of funding left after rescissions to determine whether the statutory condition is satisfied.

---

### Putting It All Together  

*Pacito v. Trump* illustrates the interplay of three statutory regimes:

1. **Immigration authority (§ 1182(f) & § 1185(a))** – a highly deferential, broadly worded presidential power to suspend refugee admissions, triggered by a simple factual finding.  
2. **Refugee resettlement framework (§ 1522)** – a mixed‑motive provision whose “shall” language creates conditional duties that hinge on the existence of appropriations and on the Executive’s decision to fund.  The Ninth Circuit split on whether the statute imposes a **mandatory** funding obligation or merely an **authorization**.  
3. **APA procedural safeguards** – a review standard that examines whether the agency’s termination of contracts was arbitrary or capricious, regardless of the broader immigration question.

For a law student, the key lesson is to read each statutory provision **in its own context**, identify the operative verbs, note any qualifying phrases (such as “to the extent of available appropriations”), and then see how the court balances those textual rules against the procedural standards for stays and for APA review.  In *Pacito*, the majority’s emphasis on the President’s unambiguous authority under § 1182(f) carried the stay analysis, while the dissent’s focus on the permissive language of § 1522 shaped the disagreement over the government’s duty to fund resettlement services.
