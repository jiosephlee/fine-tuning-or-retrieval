Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Procedural History and Parties  

In **Pacito v. Trump**, the Ninth Circuit was called upon to review a series of district‑court rulings that arose after the Trump Administration dramatically altered the United States Refugee Admissions Program (“USRAP”).  Understanding the procedural posture of the case requires a clear picture of the executive actions at issue, the parties who brought the suit, and the cascade of district‑court orders that set the stage for appellate review.  This chapter walks you through each of those components, providing the factual and procedural context you will need to follow the Ninth Circuit’s later analysis.  

## The January 20, 2025 Executive Order 14163 and Its Statutory Basis  

On the first day of his second term, President Donald J. Trump issued **Executive Order 14163**, titled *“Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.”*  The order, published in the Federal Register on January 30, 2025 (90 Fed. Reg. 8459), invoked **8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)**.  Section 1182(f) grants the President the power to *“suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or non‑immigrants … whenever the President finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.”*  Relying on that statutory authority, the order declared that the admission of refugees under USRAP *“would be detrimental to the interests of the United States”* and directed that refugee admissions be **suspended** pending further findings.  

The President’s finding—required by § 1182(f) but otherwise unqualified—constituted the legal backbone of the order.  The order did not merely propose policy; it exercised a statutory power that, under Supreme Court precedent (see *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018)), is entitled to a high degree of judicial deference.  

## Defunding of USRAP Under Executive Order 14169  

Four days after the refugee‑admissions suspension, the Administration issued **Executive Order 14169**, *“Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid,”* also published in the Federal Register (January 20, 2025, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619).  Although the order’s primary focus was foreign‑aid policy, it contained a provision that **suspended all funding for USRAP**, including the domestic resettlement services that support refugees once they arrive in the United States.  By cutting the program’s appropriations, the Administration effectively incapacitated the operational mechanisms that the Refugee Act of 1980 (the statutory framework embedded in the Immigration and Nationality Act) normally provides for refugee resettlement.  

## Identity of the Plaintiffs – Refugees and Resettlement Organizations  

The plaintiffs were a *diverse coalition* that reflected the two primary ways the executive actions harmed parties:  

1. **Individual Refugees** – a group of persons who either (a) had already been admitted to the United States under USRAP before January 20, 2025, or (b) had received conditional approval for resettlement but were still abroad.  Their claims centered on the loss of a lawful right to enter and, for those already admitted, the loss of statutory services that the Refugee Act guarantees.  

2. **Resettlement Organizations** – three non‑profit entities that held **cooperative agreements** with the Department of State to provide (i) overseas processing of refugee applications and (ii) domestic reception and placement services for refugees once admitted.  Because the Executive Orders terminated those agreements, the organizations asserted that the government had breached its statutory duty under **8 U.S.C. § 1522** to provide “reception and placement” services, and they also raised **Administrative Procedure Act (APA)** challenges to the abrupt termination of funding.  

Together, the plaintiffs sought declaratory and injunctive relief to (a) halt the suspension of refugee admissions, (b) reinstate funding for USRAP, and (c) require the government to restore the cooperative agreements that fund resettlement services.  

## District Court Preliminary Injunctions (February 25, February 28, March 24, 2025)  

### February 25 – Oral Preliminary Injunction  

Shortly after the complaint was filed in the **Western District of Washington**, the district judge **orally** entered a preliminary injunction on **February 25, 2025**.  The injunction barred the government from *“enforcing or implementing §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order 14163.”*  In effect, the judge ordered the Administration to stop applying the suspension of refugee admissions.  

### February 28 – Written Clarification  

Two days later, the district court issued a **written order** (February 28) that clarified the scope of the oral injunction.  The order extended the prohibition to the **suspension of USRAP funding**, meaning that the government could not withhold the appropriations needed for refugee resettlement services.  

### March 24 – Second Preliminary Injunction  

On **March 24, 2025**, the court issued a **second preliminary injunction** ordering the State Department to **reinstate all cooperative agreements** that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling.  This order was aimed at restoring the operational pipeline for both overseas processing and domestic placement services.  

Together, these three injunctions significantly constrained the Administration’s ability to implement the Executive Orders.  

## Government’s Notices of Appeal and Stay Motions  

Following the district court’s February 28 and March 24 orders, the United States **filed notices of appeal** on **February 28** and **March 25, 2025**, respectively.  The government simultaneously sought **stays** of the injunctions pending appeal.  

- **March 25 Motion for Stay** – A three‑judge *motions panel* of the Ninth Circuit considered the Government’s request.  Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel recognized the broad deference owed to the President under § 1182(f) and **denied a full stay**.  The panel granted a *partial* stay, carving out an exception for *“individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status … before January 20, 2025.”*  This limited carve‑out reflected the court’s attempt to balance the President’s authority with the plaintiffs’ alleged immediate harms.  

- **Subsequent Clarifications** – The panel issued **orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025** that refined the scope of the partial stay, ensuring that the carve‑out was applied consistently.  The panel also **directed full briefing** and placed the appeal on the “next available calendar.”  

## Subsequent District Court Orders (July 14 Enforcement Framework) and Ninth Circuit Stays  

While the appeal was pending, the district court took additional procedural steps:  

- **July 14, 2025 – Enforcement Framework** – The district judge adopted a **framework** for enforcing the limited carve‑out and appointed a **magistrate judge** to oversee its implementation.  This order was designed to give the government a mechanism to continue processing those “conditionally approved” refugees while the broader injunctions remained in place.  

- **July 17 – Emergency Motion to Enforce the Stay** – The government filed an **emergency motion** requesting that the district court **enforce, clarify, or amend** its earlier stay order.  The plaintiffs opposed the motion, arguing that it would undermine the injunctions.  

- **July 18 – Administrative Stay** – The Ninth Circuit, acting as a *panel*, issued an **administrative stay** of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order, effectively freezing the framework until the appellate court could consider the emergency motion.  

The Ninth Circuit subsequently **scheduled expedited oral argument** for September 3, 2025, and, after hearing arguments, issued the **Order** (the document reproduced above) that *vacated* its earlier administrative stay, *vacated* the March 25 stay (as amended), and *stayed* the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions *in their entirety* pending final resolution of the appeal.  

---

### Why This Procedural Trail Matters  

The procedural saga outlined above is not mere courtroom drama; it shapes the legal issues that the Ninth Circuit must resolve.  The **President’s statutory authority** under § 1182(f) frames the *merits* of the refugees’ challenge to the suspension of admissions.  By contrast, the **APA and statutory funding provisions** in **8 U.S.C. § 1522** frame the *separate* challenge concerning the termination of resettlement services.  The series of injunctions, stays, and appellate briefs demonstrate how the courts balance **executive discretion**, **statutory mandates**, and **irreparable injury**—the four *Nken* stay factors that the Ninth Circuit applied in its interim order.  

Understanding this procedural backdrop will enable you to follow the Ninth Circuit’s analysis of (1) the likelihood of success on the merits for each claim, (2) the irreparable harms alleged by the refugees and NGOs, (3) the potential injury to the United States and the public interest, and (4) the ultimate resolution of the stay and, eventually, the merits of the case.  

---  

*Key Takeaways*  

1. **Executive Order 14163** used the broad immigration‑suspension power of **§ 1182(f)** to halt refugee admissions.  
2. **Executive Order 14169** simultaneously defunded the USRAP, striking at the program’s operational capacity.  
3. Plaintiffs—**refugees** and **resettlement NGOs**—asserted violations of the **Refugee Act**, **8 U.S.C. § 1522**, and the **APA**.  
4. The district court issued **three preliminary injunctions** (Feb 25, Feb 28, Mar 24) that blocked the suspension and ordered reinstatement of funding and cooperative agreements.  
5. The **Government appealed** and sought **stays**; the Ninth Circuit granted a *partial* stay, later replaced by a *full* stay of the district court injunctions pending appeal.  
6. The **July 14 district‑court enforcement framework** and the **July 18 Ninth‑Circuit administrative stay** illustrate the courts’ ongoing attempt to manage the case while appellate review proceeds.  

With this procedural map in hand, you are ready to explore the Ninth Circuit’s substantive legal reasoning on the merits of the refugee‑admission and funding challenges.

# Statutory Framework Governing Refugee Admissions and Resettlement  

In *Pacito v. Trump* the Ninth Circuit wrestled with two distinct statutory regimes: (1) the President’s authority to halt the admission of refugees under the immigration statutes, and (2) the Congress‑mandated (or authorized) requirement that the Government provide “reception and placement” services to refugees who are admitted.  Understanding the opinion requires a clear picture of the relevant provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), the Refugee Act of 1980, and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).  This chapter isolates the statutes the court looked to, explains the textual choices that matter (e.g., **shall** versus **may**), and shows how those provisions interacted with the procedural posture of the case.

---

## 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a) – Presidential Power to Suspend Entry of Aliens  

### Textual grant of authority  

*Section 1182(f)* reads, in pertinent part:  

> “Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or non‑immigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem appropriate.”  

*Section 1185(a)* authorizes the President, by executive order, to “direct the suspension of the entry of any alien or class of alien” consistent with the finding requirement in § 1182(f).

### How the Court applied the text  

The panel’s order quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* (585 U.S. 667 (2018)) to emphasize that the statutory language “exudes deference” and confers “ample power” on the President.  The Court’s analysis focused on the **single prerequisite**: the President must *find* that entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.”  The record shows President Trump made that finding in Executive Order 14163, § 3(a).  Accordingly, the panel concluded the President satisfied the statutory condition and that the language “does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.”  

Because the statute uses the permissive verb **may** for the suspension and the qualifying phrase “as he shall deem necessary,” the court treated the President’s discretion as virtually unreviewable on the merits.  The stay‑standard analysis therefore gave the government a strong showing of likely success on the merits of that claim, even though the court noted it would not conduct a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.”  

**Key take‑away for students:** When a statute grants power in *may* terms and imposes only a minimal factual predicate (“the President finds…”), appellate courts typically apply a highly deferential standard.  The question for a stay is whether the applicant can show that the statutory prerequisite has been met—not whether the policy choice is wise.

---

## The Refugee Act of 1980 and Its Incorporation into the INA  

The Refugee Act of 1980 (Pub. L. 96‑212) codified the United States’ historic commitment to admitting persons who qualify as refugees.  It was woven into the INA at **8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.**, creating a statutory scheme that (1) defines “refugee,” (2) sets the annual admission ceiling, and (3) establishes the procedural framework for refugee status determinations.  

In *Pacito*, plaintiffs argued that Executive Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act because it effectively “suspended” the program without adhering to the Act’s statutory scheme.  The Ninth Circuit, however, treated the Refugee Act **as a procedural and substantive overlay** on the broader immigration authority, not as a separate source of limitation on the President’s § 1182(f) power.  The court therefore focused on the textual grant of authority in § 1182(f) rather than on any “general rule” language from the Refugee Act.  

**Lesson:** The Refugee Act creates rights and procedures for prospective refugees, but it does **not** curtail the President’s statutory power to suspend admissions when the narrow § 1182(f) condition is satisfied.  Students should keep in mind that the Act can be “overridden” by a valid exercise of the suspension authority, unless Congress amends the INA to impose additional constraints.

---

## 8 U.S.C. § 1522 – Reception and Placement Services: Mandate or Authorization?  

### Statutory structure  

Section 1522 is the core of the statutory dispute in *Pacito*.  It is divided into several subsections:

| Subsection | Heading (as in the statute) | Key language |
|------------|----------------------------|--------------|
| **(a)** | **Conditions and consideration** | Uses **shall** and the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” |
| **(b)** | **Authorization for programs** | Repeats **may** (or “authorized”) for developing programs. |
| **(c)** | **Program of initial resettlement** | Uses **may** for grants and contracts. |
| **(d)** | **Unaccompanied minor refugees** | Mix of **shall** (legal responsibility) and **may** (to make necessary decisions). |
| **(e)** | **Cash assistance, medical assistance, and case management** | Contains both **shall** (Secretary shall develop projects) and permissive language. |

The tension in the opinion stems from the differing grammatical moods—**shall** (imperative) versus **may** (permissive)—and from the statutory title of § 1522: “**Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees**.”

### How the panel read the provision  

The majority treated § 1522 as imposing at least a **statutory duty** to provide reception and placement services, reasoning that the “shall” language in subsection (a) creates mandatory conditions that attach when the government elects to fund refugee programs.  Because Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for FY 2025 (later reduced by $800 million), the majority concluded there were *available appropriations* and thus the government must satisfy the “shall” conditions.

The dissent (Judge Lee) argued the opposite: the statute’s **title** and the repeated use of “authorize” signal that Congress intended only to give the Executive discretion to fund such programs, not to obligate it.  Lee emphasized that even the “shall” language in (a) is limited to “in providing assistance under this section,” i.e., it conditions the *exercise* of discretion, not the existence of the duty itself.

**Why the language matters:**  
* **“Shall”** typically imposes a mandatory duty.  
* **“May”** indicates discretion.  
When a statute contains both, courts look to the **overall structure, headings, and statutory purpose** to determine whether the duty is mandatory or merely optional.  In *Pacito*, the split reflects a classic statutory‑interpretation problem—students should be comfortable identifying which subsections use which verbs and understanding how a court may infer a *conditional* duty.

---

## Key Subsections of § 1522 and Their Interpretive Signals  

### § 1522(a) – Conditions and Consideration  

> “The Director… **shall**, to the extent of available appropriations, make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement… provide English language training… insure cash assistance… insure gender equity…”  

*Interpretive note*: The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifies the **shall**.  The duty exists only if funding exists.  The majority read this as a **mandatory** condition attached to any funded program; the dissent read it as a **conditional prerequisite** that only triggers when the government decides to fund.

### § 1522(b) – Authorization for Programs  

> “The Director **is authorized** to establish programs… ”  

*Interpretive note*: The word **authorized** (a permissive term) signals Congress is giving the Executive *permission* to create programs but not requiring it.  This subsection was central to Lee’s argument that the entire chapter is permissive.

### § 1522(c) – Program of Initial Resettlement  

> “The Director **may** enter into contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements… ”  

*Interpretive note*: The **may** language underscores discretion over the mechanisms (contracts, grants, etc.) used to deliver services.  It does not, by itself, create a duty to enter into any agreement.

### § 1522(d)(2)(B) – Unaccompanied Minor Refugees  

> “During any interim period… the Director **shall** assume legal responsibility… and **is authorized** to make necessary decisions…”  

*Interpretive note*: The **shall** imposes a duty *if* an unaccompanied minor is present and the interim period applies; the **is authorized** clause again frames the actual actions as discretionary.  The court noted the factual record did not show any such minors, weakening the plaintiffs’ claim under this subsection.

### § 1522(e)(7)(A) – Cash and Medical Assistance  

> “The Secretary **shall** develop and implement alternative projects for refugees… ”  

*Interpretive note*: The **shall** creates a duty to *develop* projects, but the statute leaves the *scope* and *content* of those projects to the Secretary’s discretion (“as needed”).  This hybrid language contributed to the split in the Ninth Circuit.

---

## APA Provisions Relevant to Agency Action and Termination of Contracts  

The plaintiffs also alleged violations of the **Administrative Procedure Act**, 5 U.S.C. §§ 551‑559.  Two APA concepts were pivotal:

1. **Rule‑making vs. non‑rulemaking actions** – The termination of cooperative agreements is a *non‑rulemaking* action (a “contracting decision”).  Under **§ 706(2)(A)**, the APA’s “notice‑and‑comment” requirements apply only to *rules*; agency contracts are generally **not** subject to those procedures, though agencies must still obey the **arbitrary‑and‑capricious** standard under **§ 706(1)** when reviewing agency actions.

2. **Arbitrary‑and‑Capricious Standard** – When a court reviews an agency’s decision to terminate contracts, it asks whether the agency considered the relevant factors, provided a reasoned explanation, and avoided clear errors of judgment.  In *Pacito*, the district court’s injunction forced the State Department to **reinstate** the agreements, effectively finding that the government’s termination was arbitrary because it left the statutory duties in § 1522 unfulfilled.

The Ninth Circuit’s order indicated that the government “is unlikely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” suggesting that the agency may have failed to demonstrate that the termination was based on a reasoned analysis of the statutory requirements and the available appropriations.

**Study tip:** When a case involves both an immigration‑statute claim and an APA claim, treat them as separate layers of analysis.  The *immigration* claim hinges on statutory text and deference; the *APA* claim hinges on procedural adequacy and the agency’s rational explanation.

---

## Congressional Appropriations for Refugee Assistance and the 2025 Rescissions  

Congress appropriated **$3.178 billion** for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 (Pub. L. 119‑4 § 1101(a)(11)).  The **Rescissions Act of 2025** later cut **$800 million** from that total.  The appropriations statute is crucial for two reasons:

* **Link to § 1522(a)** – The “to the extent of available appropriations” clause means that if the appropriations are insufficient to fund the mandatory services, the government may be excused from the **shall** duties.  The majority believed that, even after the rescission, enough funds remained to trigger the duty; the dissent argued that the rescission demonstrated congressional intent to reduce the obligation, thereby weakening the claim of a mandatory duty.

* **Policy context for the stay analysis** – The stay standards ask whether the government will suffer irreparable injury if the injunction remains.  The government argued that without the ability to reallocate the rescued funds, its ability to meet other national security and immigration priorities would be impaired.  The panel, weighing the **public‑interest** factor, found the broad presidential discretion in immigration matters outweighed the narrowed funding landscape.

**Take‑away:** Appropriations can transform permissive language into a mandatory duty (or vice‑versa) when the statute ties the duty to “available appropriations.”  Courts will scrutinize the amount of funding left after rescissions to determine whether the statutory condition is satisfied.

---

### Putting It All Together  

*Pacito v. Trump* illustrates the interplay of three statutory regimes:

1. **Immigration authority (§ 1182(f) & § 1185(a))** – a highly deferential, broadly worded presidential power to suspend refugee admissions, triggered by a simple factual finding.  
2. **Refugee resettlement framework (§ 1522)** – a mixed‑motive provision whose “shall” language creates conditional duties that hinge on the existence of appropriations and on the Executive’s decision to fund.  The Ninth Circuit split on whether the statute imposes a **mandatory** funding obligation or merely an **authorization**.  
3. **APA procedural safeguards** – a review standard that examines whether the agency’s termination of contracts was arbitrary or capricious, regardless of the broader immigration question.

For a law student, the key lesson is to read each statutory provision **in its own context**, identify the operative verbs, note any qualifying phrases (such as “to the extent of available appropriations”), and then see how the court balances those textual rules against the procedural standards for stays and for APA review.  In *Pacito*, the majority’s emphasis on the President’s unambiguous authority under § 1182(f) carried the stay analysis, while the dissent’s focus on the permissive language of § 1522 shaped the disagreement over the government’s duty to fund resettlement services.

# Legal Issues Presented on Appeal  

The appeal in **Pacito v. Trump** asks the Ninth Circuit to resolve several distinct legal questions that arise from the district court’s preliminary injunctions and the government’s request for a stay.  For a law student learning to read appellate opinions, it is helpful to separate the issues into two broad categories: (1) the President’s immigration‑policy authority, and (2) the statutory and procedural requirements governing the funding and provision of refugee resettlement services.  Each issue is framed by the specific factual background of the case and the legal standards that the appellate court will apply.

## Whether the President Exceeded the Authority Granted by § 1182(f) by Suspending Refugee Admissions  

The core immigration‑policy question is whether Executive Order 14163, which suspends the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), is a permissible exercise of the power conferred on the President by **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)**.  That statute authorizes the President “to suspend the entry of…any class of aliens … whenever the President finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.”  The appellate court must determine:  

1. **Whether the statutory text grants the President unfettered discretion to suspend refugee admissions** once the “finding” requirement is satisfied; and  
2. **Whether the President’s factual finding – that refugee admissions are “detrimental to the interests of the United States” – meets the legal threshold**.  

The district court’s injunction enjoined enforcement of §§ 3(a)‑(c) and 4 of the executive order, essentially holding that the President exceeded his authority.  On appeal, the government argues that the finding requirement is satisfied on its face and that the courts must defer to the President’s judgment, invoking *Trump v. Hawaii* and the “presidential prerogative” doctrine.  The plaintiffs contend that the finding is unsubstantiated and that the suspension impermissibly overrides the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Immigration and Nationality Act’s procedural safeguards.

## Whether the District Court’s Preliminary Injunctions Are Proper Under the Standards for Injunctive Relief  

Even if the President possessed the statutory authority, the district court may still have acted improperly if its preliminary injunctions did not satisfy the four‑part test for injunctive relief articulated in **Rule 65 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure** and applied in cases such as *Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council* and *Nken v. Holder*.  The appellate court will evaluate each factor as applied to the district court’s order:  

1. **Likelihood of Success on the Merits** – Did the plaintiffs demonstrate a substantial likelihood that the executive order violates the Refugee Act, the INA, or the APA?  
2. **Irreparable Harm** – Did the plaintiffs show that without an injunction they would suffer injury that could not be remedied by money damages (e.g., loss of refugee status, disruption of resettlement services)?  
3. **Balance of Hardships** – How do the harms to the government and the public (including national security interests) compare with the harms to the plaintiffs?  
4. **Public Interest** – Does the public interest favor maintaining the status quo (the suspension) or restoring the refugee program?  

The Ninth Circuit’s discussion of the stay highlights that the first two factors are “most critical.”  The panel concluded that the government is “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions,” suggesting a weak showing of success on the merits for the plaintiffs.  The appellate court must decide whether the district court properly weighed these factors when it issued its preliminary injunctions.

## Whether the Defunding of USRAP and Termination of Cooperative Agreements Violate the APA  

The plaintiffs also allege that the administration’s defunding of USRAP and the abrupt termination of cooperative agreements with nonprofit resettlement partners constitute unlawful actions under the **Administrative Procedure Act (APA), 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq.**  The APA requires that agency actions be **lawful, reasonable, and not arbitrary or capricious**.  The key questions for the appellate court are:  

1. **Whether the Department of State’s decision to suspend funding and terminate contracts was a “final agency action” subject to APA review**; and  
2. **Whether the agency provided a sufficient explanatory record demonstrating that the action was based on a reasoned analysis** (see *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 422 (1983)).  

The government argues that the funding suspension is a lawful exercise of the President’s discretion under § 1182(f) and that the agency’s internal deliberations, though not fully disclosed, are adequate.  The plaintiffs contend that the abrupt termination without a notice‑and‑comment period, and without showing that the funding cut is “necessary and appropriate,” is arbitrary and capricious.  The Ninth Circuit must apply the APA’s substantive standard and determine whether the agency’s action was supported by the record.

## Whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 Imposes a Mandatory Duty on the Government to Provide Reception and Placement Services  

Beyond the immigration‑policy question, the case turns on the statutory interpretation of **8 U.S.C. § 1522**, which outlines the “conditions and considerations” for refugee resettlement services.  The plaintiffs assert that § 1522 creates a **mandatory** duty for the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) to provide “reception and placement” services, and that the termination of cooperative agreements violates that duty.  The government contends that the provisions are **authorizations**, not mandates, giving the executive branch discretion to fund or not fund the services.  

The appellate court must analyze:  

1. **The textual distinction between “shall” (imperative) and “may/authorizes” (permissive) language** in the statute;  
2. **The statutory structure**, including the “to the extent of available appropriations” limitation in § 1522(a)(1)(A), which conditions any duty on the existence of appropriated funds;  
3. **Congressional intent**, as reflected in the legislative history of the Refugee Act and the FY 2025 appropriations act, particularly the $800 million rescission that reduced funding for refugee assistance; and  
4. **Precedent on interpreting statutory “authorizations” versus “mandates”**, such as *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. | (2001) and *U.S. Dep’t of Labor v. Weiss*.  

Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasizes his view that the “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement” title and the permissive language indicate that § 1522 does **not** create a binding obligation.  The majority, however, reads the “shall” provisions as conferring a **mandatory condition** that the government must satisfy when it exercises the discretionary funding authority.  The Ninth Circuit’s ultimate holding will hinge on the interpretive approach it adopts.

## Standard of Review for the Stay (Nken v. Holder Factors) and for the Merits  

The appellate court must apply two distinct standards of review:

1. **Stay Review** – The panel’s order granting a stay is reviewed for **abuse of discretion**.  The court will revisit the **four‑factor** framework from *Nken v. Holder* (likelihood of success, irreparable injury, balance of hardships, and public interest).  Because the stay is an interlocutory remedy, the court gives considerable deference to the district court’s factual findings, but it must ensure that the panel correctly applied the factors.  

2. **Merits Review** – The underlying preliminary injunction and the APA challenges are reviewed under **de novo** standards for legal questions (e.g., whether § 1182(f) permits the suspension) and **clear error** for factual findings (e.g., the adequacy of the President’s “finding”).  Statutory interpretation of § 1522 also receives de novo review, while the APA’s arbitrary‑and‑capricious analysis is reviewed under a **substantial‑likelihood** standard: the appellate court must determine whether the agency’s decision was based on a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made.  

Understanding how these standards operate helps the student follow the appellate opinion’s structure: the court first addresses the procedural propriety of the stay, then moves to the substantive merits of the injunctions and the statutory interpretations at issue.  

---  

By isolating each of these questions, the student can see how the Ninth Circuit narrows the dispute into discrete legal issues, applies the appropriate doctrinal standards, and ultimately decides whether the district court’s injunctions should remain in place while the case proceeds on the merits.

# Court’s Analysis of Presidential Authority and Stay Standards  

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit was called upon to decide whether to stay the district court’s preliminary injunctions that blocked President Trump’s executive order suspending refugee admissions and defunding the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP).  The panel’s order offers a clear illustration of two fundamental procedural doctrines that law students must master: the doctrine of deference to the President in immigration matters, and the four‑factor test that governs the issuance of a stay pending appeal.  This chapter walks through the court’s reasoning, showing how the panel applied each principle to the facts before it.

---

## Deference Doctrine in Immigration (Trump v. Hawaii) and Its Application Here  

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), codified a high level of judicial deference to the political branches when they exercise the broad discretion granted by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).  The Court held that the “President’s authority to suspend the entry of aliens…exudes deference” and that courts should not engage in a “searching inquiry” into the justifications underlying a presidential finding.  The Ninth Circuit’s order invokes *Hawaii* repeatedly, treating the statutory grant of power in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as a “textual” authority that limits judicial review to whether the President has satisfied the bare statutory prerequisite—the “find” that admission of a class of aliens would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.”

In *Pacito*, the panel noted that the executive order at issue (Exec. Order No. 14163) was issued under the explicit authority of § 1182(f).  The order states that the President “determined” that refugee admissions would be detrimental and therefore suspended them pending further findings.  The court therefore asked a narrow question: **Did the President make the statutory finding required by § 1182(f)?**  The record showed a clear statement of the finding in the order itself, satisfying the text of the statute.  Consequently, the panel concluded that the President had met the only substantive requirement, and that any further assessment of the political or factual reasonableness of the determination was barred by the deference doctrine articulated in *Hawaii*.

---

## Interpretation of the “Find” Requirement in § 1182(f)  

Section 1182(f) provides that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may…suspend the entry…as he shall deem necessary.”  The Ninth Circuit read the word “find” in its ordinary, plain‑language sense: the President must make a formal, on‑record determination that the class of aliens (here, refugees) poses a detriment.  The court emphasized that the statute does not require the President to articulate supporting evidence or to demonstrate a particular level of factual certainty.  The mere articulation of a finding, coupled with the issuance of a proclamation or executive order, satisfies the statutory condition.

The panel therefore rejected the plaintiffs’ argument that the executive order was facially invalid because the President had not provided a detailed factual basis.  Citing *Hawaii*, the court held that the statutory text “does not impose a duty on the President to provide a ‘rational basis’ or to substantiate the finding with evidence.”  The finding in Exec. Order 14163—“entry into the United States of refugees … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States”—met the statutory threshold, making the order presumptively lawful unless the plaintiffs could show that the President exceeded the scope of the authority (e.g., by suspending refugee admissions for a reason unrelated to national interest).  The court found no such allegation in the record, and thus concluded that the government was “likely to prevail on the merits” of the challenge to the injunction.

---

## Application of Nken v. Holder Stay Factors  

Having determined that the government satisfied the statutory “find” requirement, the panel turned to the procedural question of whether the district court’s injunction should be stayed pending appeal.  The Ninth Circuit applied the four‑factor test articulated in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), which requires a court to consider:

1. **Likelihood of success on the merits**  
2. **Likelihood of irreparable injury if the stay is denied**  
3. **Balance of hardships**  
4. **Public interest**  

The order stresses that the first two factors are “the most critical.”  The panel evaluated each factor in turn.

### 1. Likelihood of Success on the Merits  

As discussed, the court found the President’s statutory authority under § 1182(f) to be clear and uncontroversial.  The government’s argument that the plaintiffs could not succeed on the merits of their challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions was therefore “strong.”  The panel quoted *Hawaii*—“the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority”—to underscore that the merits issue was essentially foreclosed in the government’s favor.

### 2. Likelihood of Irreparable Injury  

The court next examined whether the government would suffer an injury that could not be remedied by later monetary damages if the stay were denied.  The district court’s injunction had halted the President’s ability to suspend the refugee program, thereby compelling the executive branch to continue processing and admitting refugees despite the President’s finding of detriment.  The panel concluded that continuing the admissions program would cause “irreparable injury” to the government’s national‑security interests and to the executive’s discretion to manage immigration—injuries that could not be undone once refugees entered the United States.  Moreover, the injunction forced the government to expend resources on a policy the President had deemed contrary to the nation’s interests, a injury the court deemed both immediate and irreversible.

### 3. Balance of Hardships  

Having found that the first two factors heavily favored the government, the panel gave less weight to the remaining factors but still addressed them.  The plaintiffs argued that the injunction protected refugees from being barred from entry and from losing resettlement services.  The court acknowledged these hardships but held that they were “substantial” but not “irreparable,” especially given the government’s statutory authority to suspend admissions.  Conversely, the government would suffer not only the abstract injury to its discretion but also concrete administrative disruption and potential security risks, which the court deemed to outweigh the plaintiffs’ asserted hardships.

### 4. Public Interest  

The public‑interest analysis was intertwined with the national‑security considerations already noted.  The panel emphasized that the President’s discretion in immigration is rooted in the sovereign power to protect the nation, a core public interest.  While the court recognized the humanitarian interests of the refugees, it determined that the overarching public interest in allowing the President to act on his statutory authority to restrict entry carried greater weight in the balance.

---

## Why the Court Found the Government Likely to Succeed on the Merits of the Suspension Challenge  

Summarizing the merits analysis, the Ninth Circuit’s order identifies three pillars supporting the government’s likelihood of success:

1. **Statutory Textualism** – The plain language of § 1182(f) requires only a presidential “finding” of detriment.  The executive order supplied that finding, satisfying the statutory condition.  
2. **Supreme Court Precedent** – *Trump v. Hawaii* commands deference to the President’s immigration judgments, barring courts from second‑guessing the merits of the finding.  
3. **Absence of Overreach** – The court found no allegation that the President exceeded the scope of § 1182(f) (e.g., by banning all refugees for a purpose unrelated to national interest).  Consequently, the injunction appeared to enjoin a power the President was plainly authorized to exercise.

Because the merits issue was essentially a question of statutory construction that the court saw resolved in the government’s favor, the first Nken factor tipped heavily toward granting a stay.

---

## Assessment of Irreparable Injury to the Government and the Public Interest  

The irreparable‑injury analysis hinged on the idea that once refugees are admitted, the government cannot retroactively “undo” that admission.  The court characterized the continued operation of the refugee admissions program as a “harm that is not merely monetary” but one that affects the nation’s sovereign right to control its borders.  This injury is “irreparable” because it directly frustrates the President’s statutorily delegated power and cannot be repaired by later relief.

In the public‑interest calculus, the court placed the President’s discretion at the apex of the nation’s interests in immigration control.  While humanitarian concerns are undeniably important, the *Hawaii* line of authority makes clear that the judiciary’s role is not to substitute its own policy preferences for those of the political branches.  Accordingly, the panel concluded that the public interest “lies with the government’s ability to enforce the statutory immigration scheme as envisioned by Congress.”

---

### Putting It All Together  

The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* demonstrates how a court blends substantive immigration doctrine with procedural stay analysis.  By invoking *Trump v. Hawaii* and the plain‑text reading of § 1182(f), the panel affirmed that the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions is largely insulated from judicial scrutiny.  Applying the *Nken* stay test, the court gave decisive weight to the government’s strong likelihood of success on the merits and to the irreparable injury the government would suffer without a stay.  The remaining factors—balance of hardships and public interest—followed from those conclusions, leading the panel to issue a stay of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal.

Understanding this reasoning equips a law student to recognize when courts will defer to the executive in immigration matters and how the stay standards operate as a gatekeeper to appellate review.  In future cases, the same four‑factor analysis will be applied, but the weight given to each factor will depend heavily on the underlying merits of the statutory authority at issue.

# Interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and the APA Challenge  

In *Pacito v. Trump* the Ninth Circuit was called upon to decide two closely‑related questions. First, does the Refugee Act’s provision on “reception and placement” services, 8 U.S.C. § 1522, impose a mandatory statutory duty on the government to fund those services, or is it merely an authorization that the executive may exercise when it chooses? Second, does the government’s abrupt termination of the cooperative agreements that historically provided those services violate the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) because the agency failed to follow the procedural safeguards required for an unlawful contract termination?  

The majority and Judge Lee reached opposite conclusions on the first question, and the panel’s final order reflects the majority’s view while also addressing the APA claim. The following sections walk a law‑student through the statutory text, the interpretive tools the judges applied, and the procedural standards governing agency contract actions under the APA.  

---

## Textual Reading of “shall” versus “may” in §§ 1522(a)‑(e)

Statutory interpretation begins with the ordinary meaning of the language used. The relevant subsections of § 1522 contain a mixture of “shall” and “may” verbs:

| Subsection | Language | Typical effect |
|------------|----------|----------------|
| § 1522(a) | “the Director **shall** … make available sufficient resources … to the extent of available appropriations” | Appears mandatory, but conditioned on the existence of appropriations and on the Director actually providing assistance. |
| § 1522(b)‑(e) | “the Director **is authorized** … **may** make …” | Clearly permissive; Congress gives the Director discretion to fund or not fund specific programs. |

The majority stressed that the presence of “shall” in § 1522(a) creates a **mandatory condition** that attaches whenever the government elects to fund refugee‑resettlement programs. The “to the extent of available appropriations” phrase merely limits the obligation to the amount of money Congress actually provides; it does not transform a mandatory duty into a discretionary one.

Judge Lee, by contrast, argued that the “shall” language is limited to **procedural considerations**—the Director must follow those procedures *if* the agency decides to act. Because the statute’s **title** (“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”) and the pervasive “may/authorized” language in §§ 1522(b)‑(e) signal congressional intent to give the executive broad discretion, Lee concluded that the statute does not create a funding mandate. In Lee’s view, the “shall” in § 1522(a) functions as a *condition* on an *optional* program, not as a stand‑alone command to spend.

---

## The Majority’s View: § 1522 Imposes Mandatory Conditions When Funding Is Provided

The panel majority read the statute as a **conditional mandatory scheme**:

1. **Statutory Structure** – The statute is organized so that § 1522(a) sets *baseline requirements* that apply *whenever* the Director provides assistance. The “shall” commands are therefore interpreted as **operative** once the discretionary funding provisions in §§ 1522(b)‑(e) are triggered.  

2. **Interpretive Presumption Against Implied Repeal** – Because the Refugee Act is a comprehensive scheme for resettlement, the majority rejected the notion that Congress intended a “gap” where the government could simply stop providing services. The statutory scheme “makes sense” only if the mandatory conditions are enforceable whenever Congress appropriates money for refugee programs.  

3. **Legislative Purpose** – The legislative history (not reproduced here) emphasizes that Congress sought to ensure that refugees admitted under the USRAP receive a “minimum set of services” to achieve self‑sufficiency. The majority read the “shall” language as reflecting that purpose: once the government decides to admit refugees, it must also meet the statutory service obligations.  

4. **Effect of the Funding Reduction** – Even though FY 2025 appropriations were reduced by $800 million, the remaining $2.378 billion represents “available appropriations” within the meaning of the statute. Thus, the mandatory conditions continue to apply to the portion of the program that remains funded.  

Based on this reading, the majority concluded that the government’s termination of the cooperative agreements—without any alternative mechanism to satisfy § 1522(a)’s conditions—constitutes a breach of a mandatory statutory duty. Consequently, the court ordered the government to reinstate the agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.”

---

## Judge Lee’s Dissent: § 1522 as an Authorization, Not a Command

Judge Lee’s dissent hinges on a **different textual emphasis** and a different view of congressional intent:

1. **Authorization vs. Mandate** – Lee points out that the **title** of the statute expressly calls it an “Authorization.” In statutory construction, titles are given controlling weight when they illuminate ambiguous language. The repeated use of “may” and “is authorized” in §§ 1522(b)‑(e) reinforces that Congress intended to give the executive discretion.  

2. **“Shall” as Procedural, Not Substantive** – Lee argues that the “shall” in § 1522(a) merely obliges the Director to consider certain factors *if* the agency proceeds with funding. The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” is read as a further limitation, suggesting that the mandatory language is **contingent** on the existence of a discretionary funding decision.  

3. **No Independent Funding Requirement** – Because the statute does not contain a stand‑alone command to spend, Lee holds that the government may lawfully discontinue the cooperative agreements. The statutory provision does not “require” the agency to provide services; it simply sets **conditions** the agency must meet should it choose to fund them.  

4. **Policy Implications** – Lee emphasizes that imposing a mandatory spending requirement on the executive would unduly curtail the President’s discretion under § 1182(f), which the majority already recognized as “vast.”  

Lee therefore would have vacated the district court’s injunctions **in their entirety**, including the portion concerning the provision of services, because he saw no statutory basis for compelling the government to maintain the agreements.

---

## Role of “to the Extent of Available Appropriations” and Its Limiting Effect

Both sides agree that the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” is a **budgetary qualifier**. The majority treats it as a **quantitative ceiling**—the mandatory duties apply only to the amount Congress actually provides. The dissent, however, reads it as a **qualitative limit** that underscores the discretionary nature of the duty: if Congress does not appropriate money for a particular service, the “shall” obligations never arise.

In practice, this phrase means that the court must first determine whether any appropriation exists for the specific service at issue. In *Pacito*, the record shows that FY 2025 appropriations, though reduced, still funded refugee‑resettlement programs. Therefore, under the majority’s reading, the mandatory conditions survive the budget cut and must be honored. Lee’s approach would treat the reduction as evidence that Congress may have intended to scale back the program, thereby giving the executive more leeway to terminate contracts.

---

## APA Standards for Unlawful Termination of Contracts and the Need for Notice/Fact‑Finding

Beyond the statutory interpretation, the plaintiffs asserted an **APA claim**: the State Department’s abrupt termination of the cooperative agreements violated the procedural safeguards required for agency actions that affect contractual rights.

1. **APA’s “Arbitrary and Capricious” Standard** – Under 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A), a court may set aside an agency action that is “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” The agency must explain its decision, consider relevant factors, and make a reasoned choice.  

2. **Contract‑Termination Requirements** – When an agency terminates a contract, the APA (and the Federal Acquisition Regulation, though not directly at issue here) requires **notice of proposed termination**, an **opportunity to comment**, and a **statement of the basis** for the action. The district court’s preliminary injunction highlighted that the government had not provided any substantive justification for the termination beyond a vague statement that a new agency was being sought.  

3. **Notice and Fact‑Finding** – The majority found that the government’s termination was **procedurally deficient** because it occurred without the notice and fact‑finding the APA demands. Even if the agency claimed it could rely on its broad discretionary authority, the lack of a reasoned explanation rendered the action “arbitrary and capricious.”  

4. **Resulting Remedy** – Because the termination was procedurally improper and, under the majority’s reading, violated a mandatory statutory duty, the panel ordered **reinstatement** of the cooperative agreements *pending* final resolution of the appeal. This is a classic equitable remedy in APA cases: the status quo is restored while the court resolves the underlying legal questions.

Judge Lee agreed that the agency’s termination was procedurally flawed but argued that the court should not compel the government to **fund** the services. Accordingly, Lee would have dismissed the mandatory‑funding portion of the injunction while still requiring the agency to follow proper APA procedures before any future termination.

---

## Court’s Order Directing Reinstatement of Resettlement Agreements Pending Final Resolution

The panel’s final **interim order** reflects the majority’s synthesis of the two issues:

* **Stay of the District Court Injunctions** – The Ninth Circuit stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24, 2025 injunctions *except* for the portion requiring the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements. This selective stay preserves the status quo for the service‑provision dispute while allowing the government to continue the broader suspension of refugee admissions pending appeal.  

* **Mandate to Reinstate** – By directing the government to reinstate the agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522,” the court enforces its view that § 1522 imposes a **binding obligation** when funding is available. The order does not dictate the precise terms of the agreements; it merely requires the agencies to restore the pre‑termination framework until the merits are finally decided.  

* **APA Compliance** – The order implicitly reminds the agencies that any future termination must satisfy APA procedural requirements—notice, an opportunity to be heard, and a reasoned explanation—otherwise the court will again intervene.  

* **Scope of the Stay** – The stay is **temporary**; it remains in effect “pending final resolution of this appeal.” Once the Ninth Circuit renders its merits opinion, the stay will either dissolve (if the majority’s view prevails) or be superseded by a new directive (if the dissent’s reasoning gains traction on appeal).  

For students, the key takeaway is that the Court can issue a **partial stay** that preserves certain elements of a lower‑court injunction while modifying others, based on the appellate panel’s interim assessment of the legal issues. The order is a practical tool that balances the need to prevent irreparable harm (to refugees who would otherwise lack services) with the government’s interest in maintaining its policy discretion over refugee admissions.

---

### Putting It All Together  

* **Statutory Interpretation:** The majority reads the “shall” language of § 1522(a) as a mandatory condition that activates whenever Congress funds refugee‑resettlement programs, whereas Judge Lee treats the entire section as a permissive authorization.  

* **Budgetary Limitation:** “To the extent of available appropriations” caps the mandatory duty but does not nullify it when any appropriation exists.  

* **APA Concerns:** The government’s termination of cooperative agreements lacked the required notice and reasoning, violating the “arbitrary and capricious” standard.  

* **Remedial Order:** By staying the district court’s injunctions except for the service‑provision component, the Ninth Circuit effectively enforces the majority’s reading of § 1522 while preserving the broader policy dispute for later resolution.  

Understanding how the panel parsed the interplay between statutory text, congressional purpose, and procedural law provides a clear window into appellate review of executive immigration actions and the limits of agency discretion under the APA.

# Holding, Disposition, and Significance  

The Ninth Circuit’s order in **Pacito v. Trump** resolves a procedural dispute that arose after the district court in the Western District of Washington entered two preliminary injunctions blocking the Trump Administration’s suspension of the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) and the termination of cooperative agreements that provide “reception and placement” services to refugees.  The appellate court’s holding is limited to the stay it issued pending final appellate review; the court has not yet addressed the ultimate merits of the plaintiffs’ constitutional and statutory claims.  Nevertheless, the order clarifies the scope of the stay, reinstates certain funding mechanisms, and signals how the court will balance presidential immigration authority against statutory funding mandates in future litigation.

## Vacatur of Prior Administrative Stays and Issuance of a New Stay Pending Appeal  

The panel first **vacated all of its earlier administrative stays**—the July 18, 2025 stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and the March 25 stay (as amended on April 21 and May 9).  By vacating those stays the court removed the earlier, piecemeal restraints that had allowed the government to proceed with limited aspects of its policy while the appeal was pending.  In their place, the Ninth Circuit entered a **new, comprehensive stay** that applies “pending final resolution of this appeal.”  The stay **covers the district court’s February 28 and March 24, 2025 preliminary injunctions in their entirety**, except for the limited carve‑out the panel had previously articulated (the carve‑out concerned individuals who were *conditionally* approved for refugee status before the executive order took effect).  In other words, the district court’s injunctions remain in force, but their operation is temporarily halted while the appellate court considers the parties’ appeals.

The order’s language underscores the Court’s reliance on the **four‑factor stay standard** articulated in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009): (1) a strong likelihood of success on the merits, (2) irreparable injury absent a stay, (3) the balance of harms, and (4) the public interest.  The panel found that, given the President’s clear statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the government’s apparent inability to demonstrate that it can meet its statutory service obligations, the stay was warranted.  By vacating its earlier, more narrowly‑tailored stays and issuing a single, umbrella stay, the court eliminated the administrative confusion that had arisen from the overlapping orders.

## Partial Stay of District Court Injunctions – What Remains Enjoined  

Although the Ninth Circuit’s stay temporarily **suspends enforcement of the district court’s injunctions**, the underlying injunctions remain on the record.  The district court’s orders still **enjoin**:

1. **The suspension of refugee admissions** under Executive Order 14163 §§ 3(a)–(c) and 4; and  
2. **The termination of cooperative agreements** that provide reception, placement, and other resettlement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States.  

Because the appellate stay is “pending final resolution,” the injunctions will take effect again if the Ninth Circuit ultimately affirms them on the merits, or they will be vacated if the appellate court reverses.  The stay therefore does **not** alter the substantive legal conclusions of the district court; it merely pauses their practical effect while the appeal proceeds.

## Mandate to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements for Refugee Resettlement Services  

The most consequential operative directive in the order is the Court’s **order directing the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements** that were terminated on February 26, 2025.  The Ninth Circuit reasoned that, even if the President may lawfully suspend the entry of new refugees, the **statutory obligations of 8 U.S.C. § 1522** require the government to provide “reception and placement services” to refugees who have already been admitted.  The Court noted that the administration had not demonstrated a viable alternative mechanism for delivering those services, nor could it show that it could meet its statutory duties without the agreements.

Accordingly, the order commands the Department of State (and, by delegation, the Office of Refugee Resettlement within HHS) to **reactivate the pre‑existing contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements** with the three nonprofit organizations that were plaintiffs.  The reinstated agreements must provide the “usual and customary services” previously furnished—employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and other supports required by §§ 1522(a)–(e).  This directive is limited to **refugees who have already been admitted**; it does not compel the government to create new mechanisms for future admissions, which remain suspended under the executive order.

## Implications for Future Challenges to Executive Suspension of Refugee Admissions  

The Ninth Circuit’s order, while procedural, offers a preview of how the court is likely to treat the **substantive challenge to the President’s suspension authority**.  The panel’s discussion of the stay standard emphasizes that the government “is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions,” citing the broad deference accorded to the President under *Trump v. Hawaii* and the plain language of § 1182(f).  This suggests that, on the merits, the Court will **uphold the President’s power to suspend refugee admissions** so long as the statutory condition—that the President “finds” the entry would be detrimental—is satisfied.

Future litigants who seek to overturn a similar suspension will therefore need to **focus on the adequacy of the President’s factual finding** and on any procedural violations (e.g., failure to consult Congress) rather than on the abstract scope of presidential discretion.  Moreover, the order signals that the Court will **separate the admission question from the funding question**; even if the suspension stands, the government may still be required to fund services for refugees already in the United States.

## Potential Impact on Statutory Interpretation of “Authorization” versus “Mandate” in Refugee Law  

Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores an unresolved **statutory interpretation dispute**: whether § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty (“shall”) or merely an authorization (“may”) to fund resettlement services.  The majority’s order treats the provisions as **mandatory** to the extent that they impose conditions on the use of appropriated funds—i.e., once the government elects to fund refugee resettlement, it must provide the services described.  By ordering reinstatement of the agreements, the panel effectively reads § 1522 as creating a **binding obligation** to deliver services once refugees have been admitted.

If the Ninth Circuit eventually rules on the merits, its interpretation will become precedent for how lower courts treat the “authorization” language throughout the Refugee Act.  A **mandatory reading** would expand congressional oversight of executive implementation, potentially limiting the President’s ability to cut funding without judicial review.  Conversely, a **permissive reading**—as advocated by Judge Lee—would preserve broader executive flexibility.  Practitioners should monitor the forthcoming opinion for the Court’s final statutory construction, as it will dictate the leeway agencies have in designing or eliminating refugee‑service programs.

## Procedural Takeaways for Litigants Seeking Stays in Immigration Cases  

The Pacito order provides several practical lessons for attorneys handling **stay motions in immigration litigation**:

| Takeaway | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Early and thorough briefing** | The panel relied heavily on the parties’ briefs to assess the likelihood of success on the merits and the presence of irreparable harm.  A well‑crafted stay brief that links the statutory basis of the executive action to the plaintiff’s injury is essential. |
| **Clear identification of the “carve‑out”** | The district court’s limited carve‑out for conditionally approved refugees created confusion that the appellate court had to resolve.  Litigants should anticipate and explicitly address any partial relief the district court may grant. |
| **Documented irreparable injury** | The government’s inability to show it could provide required services without the cooperative agreements was decisive.  Plaintiffs should quantify or otherwise demonstrate concrete harms that would result from enforcement of the district court’s injunction. |
| **Public‑interest framing** | The panel highlighted both domestic and foreign interests.  Positioning the case within broader national‑security or humanitarian contexts can tip the balance of the fourth factor. |
| **Awareness of procedural posturing** | The government’s multiple stays (July 18, March 25, etc.) illustrate that parties may seek incremental relief.  Courts may prefer a single, comprehensive stay over a series of fragmented orders. |

By internalizing these procedural nuances, litigants can better structure their stay motions to meet the rigorous *Nken* standard and avoid the pitfalls of contradictory or overlapping orders.

---  

In sum, the Ninth Circuit’s order **vacates earlier administrative stays, imposes a new stay pending appeal, and mandates the reinstatement of refugee‑resettlement cooperative agreements**.  While the court has not yet ruled on the ultimate constitutionality of the President’s suspension of refugee admissions, its reasoning foreshadows a strong deference to executive discretion under § 1182(f).  The order also clarifies that statutory provisions governing refugee services may be read as mandatory once the government elects to fund them, a point that will likely shape future statutory interpretation and the scope of judicial oversight over executive immigration policy.
