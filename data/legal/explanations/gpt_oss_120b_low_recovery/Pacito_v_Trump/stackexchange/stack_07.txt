Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the significance of the “limited carve‑out” for individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before Jan. 20, 2025, in the stay order?
Question:
The panel stayed the preliminary injunction “to the extent the district court’s order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved….” I’m unclear on why the court treated that group differently and what legal reasoning supports carving out that subset while staying the rest.
Answer:
**Answer**

The “limited carve‑out”‑the portion of the stay that *does not* apply to people who were **conditionally approved for refugee status before 20 January 2025**‑exists because the Ninth Circuit concluded that, while the government is likely to succeed on the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions, it is **not** likely to succeed on the plaintiffs’ statutory claim that the government must continue to fund “reception and placement” services for those refugees who have already been admitted.

### Why the court treated that group differently

1. **Two distinct legal questions**  
   The district court’s preliminary injunction covered (a) the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under **Exec. Order 14163** and (b) the termination of the State Department’s cooperative agreements that provide resettlement services.  

   The Ninth Circuit applied the *stay‑analysis* (Nken v. Holder) separately to each prong.  
   * For the **suspension of admissions**, the panel found the government “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge,” because § 1182(f) gives the President wide discretion once he makes the required “finding” that entry would be detrimental. (See “The government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order 14163’s suspension….”)  
   * For the **reception‑and‑placement services**, the panel said “the government is **not likely to prevail** on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.” The reason is the uncertainty about whether § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty to fund those services. (See “For reasons to be explained … the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.”)

2. **Statutory interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522**  
   The majority read § 1522 as creating a **statutory duty** to provide certain services to refugees **who have already been admitted**. Because Congress appropriated funds for those services, the government must keep the cooperative agreements in place until a new provider can be found. (See “the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services … to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.”)

   Judge Lee’s dissent argues that § 1522 is merely an **authorization**, not a mandate, but the majority’s majority view controls the stay order.

3. **Effect on the stay**  
   Because the court believes the government will likely win on the immigration‑admission issue but may lose on the statutory‑service issue, it **stays the entire district‑court injunction except** for the subset of individuals whose status was *conditionally approved before the Jan. 20, 2025 cutoff*. Those individuals are the ones for whom § 1522’s mandatory‑service reading applies; they have a concrete expectation of receiving resettlement services that the government may be obligated to fund.

### Legal reasoning that supports the carve‑out

- **Four‑factor stay test** (Nken): the court found the government satisfied the first two factors (likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury) for the *admissions* prong, but **did not** for the *services* prong.  
- **Deference to the President** (Trump v. Hawaii): the court emphasizes that the President’s authority under § 1182(f) “exudes deference,” so the injunction blocking the suspension is presumptively improper.  
- **Statutory duty under § 1522**: The majority treats the language “shall … to the extent of available appropriations” as creating a **mandatory** obligation to provide reception and placement services *once* a refugee has been admitted. Because the government has terminated the cooperative agreements, reinstatement is required for those already admitted (or conditionally approved) before the moratorium took effect.

### Practical significance

- **People conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025** retain the right to the resettlement services that were already promised under the pre‑moratorium program. The government must keep the existing contracts alive until a new provider is in place.  
- **All other plaintiffs** (those whose applications were pending after Jan. 20, 2025 or who were not yet conditionally approved) remain subject to the stay of the district court’s injunction; the suspension of refugee admissions and the termination of the cooperative agreements continue while the appeal is pending.  
- The carve‑out therefore **limits the scope of the stay** to avoid irreparable harm to a class of refugees who have a vested interest in receiving statutorily mandated assistance, while preserving the broader deference the court gives to the President’s immigration discretion.

In short, the “limited carve‑out” reflects the panel’s nuanced view that the government’s authority to block refugee admissions is strong, but its statutory obligations to fund resettlement services for refugees already conditionally approved are uncertain. The stay therefore protects that latter class of refugees while leaving the rest of the injunction on hold.
