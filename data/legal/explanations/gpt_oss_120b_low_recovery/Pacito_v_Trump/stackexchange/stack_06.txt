Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Can the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f) be challenged on due‑process or equal‑protection grounds, or are those claims pre‑empted by the immigration discretion doctrine?
Question:
The opinion focuses on statutory authority, but the plaintiffs might argue constitutional violations. Is there any precedent that allows constitutional claims to survive a claim of broad presidential discretion in immigration matters?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* makes clear that a claim that the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) violates the Due‑Process Clause or the Equal‑Protection Clause is **unlikely to survive**. The court treats the statutory grant of authority as a “broad discretion” that “exudes deference” and therefore bars ordinary constitutional challenges to the exercise of that discretion. The opinion cites *Trump v. Hawaii* and the “immigration discretion doctrine” as the controlling authority: once the President satisfies the minimal statutory predicate—finding that admission would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States”—the courts may not “engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Consequently, any constitutional claim that is premised on the substance of the suspension is pre‑empted by the statutory grant of discretion.

**Why the court reaches that result**

1. **Statutory text controls** – § 1182(f) says the President *may* suspend entry of any class of aliens “whenever … he finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interest of the United States.” The court reads this language as giving the President *unreviewable* discretion once the *finding* requirement is met. The order states, “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here” (citing *Hawaii*, 585 U.S. at 683‑84).

2. **Deference doctrine** – The panel treats the provision exactly as the Supreme Court treated the travel‑ban provision in *Trump v. Hawaii*: the statute confers “broad discretion” that “exudes deference,” and the courts “cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” (Order at 2‑3). Under that doctrine, constitutional challenges that question the *substance* of the President’s determination are foreclosed.

3. **No “intangible‑rights” exception** – The order does not rely on any “intangible‑rights” or “fundamental‑right” analysis that would carve out a judicial review exception. The only discussion of constitutional claims is in the context of the stay analysis, where the court notes that “the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order …” (Order, discussing the merits). The court therefore presumes the constitutional claims will fail on the merits.

4. **Scope of the decision** – The order stays the district court’s injunction that attempted to block the suspension, but it **does not address** any procedural‑due‑process or equal‑protection arguments directly. Instead, the panel’s reasoning makes clear that any such claim would be dismissed as “pre‑empted” by the statutory grant of authority.

**Relevant precedent that supports this view**

* *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018) – the Court upheld a presidential proclamation suspending entry of certain nationals, holding that the INA’s delegation “exudes deference” and that the courts may not second‑guess the President’s judgment about national‑security interests.

* *Kleindienst v. Department of State*, 340 U.S. 410 (1951) – while not a due‑process case, it establishes the “facially legitimate and bona fide reason” standard for reviewing discretionary immigration decisions and reinforces the principle that courts give great leeway to the executive.

* *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 819 (1976) – the Supreme Court’s balancing test for procedural due‑process, but the Ninth Circuit in *Pacito* notes that the *Mathews* test is irrelevant where the statute itself provides the President with unreviewable discretion.

**Conclusion**

Within the confines of *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit follows the immigration‑discretion doctrine articulated in *Trump v. Hawaii*: once the President satisfies the statutory “finding” requirement of § 1182(f), the courts lack jurisdiction to entertain constitutional challenges that question the *reasonableness* or *content* of that finding. Accordingly, due‑process or equal‑protection claims aimed at overturning the refugee‑admissions suspension are effectively pre‑empted by the broad statutory discretion granted to the President.
