Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## Presidential Power vs. Congressional Statutes: The Ninth Circuit’s Deference in *Pacito v. Trump*

**Why this case matters**  
The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* marks the first appellate review of a post‑COVID‑era executive moratorium on refugee admissions. At a time when the United States is grappling with both a humanitarian crisis abroad and a sharply divided domestic politics of immigration, the panel’s reasoning offers a clear signal about the breadth of presidential discretion under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Moreover, the decision touches a second, less‑explored corner of refugee law: whether the statute that funds “reception and placement” services (8 U.S.C. § 1522) obligates the government to continue providing those services once a moratorium takes effect. The split in the panel—majority staying the district court’s injunction on the suspension of admissions but ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements for services—creates a nuanced, and potentially contradictory, map of executive authority versus statutory mandates.

Below we walk through the court’s reasoning, assess its alignment with established immigration‑law doctrine, and speculate on the downstream consequences for future executive actions that invoke national‑security justifications.

---

### The Majority’s Core Holding: Deference to the President’s “Finding”

The panel leaned heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), reiterating the Supreme Court’s “presidential proclamation” standard: when the President invokes the “detrimental to the interests of the United States” language of § 1182(f), courts must give his factual findings “great deference” and avoid “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” The Ninth Circuit’s order paraphrases *Hawaii* to the effect that “the sole prerequisite … is that the President ‘find’ that the entry … would be detrimental,” and it concludes that President Trump satisfied that prerequisite.

The order therefore applied the four‑factor *Nken* stay test, but for the merits it treated the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—as almost foregone conclusions in favor of the executive. The panel wrote that “the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions,” because the statutory text offers no substantive limit on the President’s discretion once the finding is made. By anchoring its analysis in the text of § 1182(f) and *Hawaii*, the majority sidestepped any substantive review of the evidence the administration presented (e.g., intelligence assessments, diplomatic concerns). This is consistent with the “Chevron‑type” deference the Supreme Court has applied in the immigration context, even though *Hawaii* expressly framed the review as *de novo* of the statute but “highly deferential” to the political branches.

**Our take:** The panel’s approach is doctrinally sound, but it leans toward a “black‑letter” reading that may underplay the constitutional limits imposed by the First Amendment and the Spending Clause when an executive action effectively nullifies a congressional program. The order does not address whether the President’s “finding” must be supported by any independent factual record—a question that *Hawaii* left open. Future challenges could revive that issue, especially if a court were to demand a more rigorous evidentiary basis for the “detriment” claim.

---

### The Statutory Counter‑Claim: 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and the Duty to Fund Resettlement Services

The more intriguing part of the order concerns the government’s obligation under § 1522 to provide “reception and placement” services. The majority concluded that the government “is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements” because the statute imposes a statutory duty, whereas Judge Lee’s dissent paints § 1522 as an *authorization* rather than a *mandate*.

The majority’s reasoning rests on the “shall” language of § 1522(a)(1)(A) and the related subsections that require the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to make resources “available … to the extent of available appropriations.” By reading “shall” as mandatory, the panel treats the statute as creating a non‑discretionary spending obligation that Congress funded (albeit partially reduced by the FY 2025 Rescissions Act). The panel emphasizes that the statute ties the availability of services to *actual* appropriations, not merely to the executive’s discretion to spend.

**Our perspective:** The statutory construction problem here is classic—does “shall” coupled with “to the extent of available appropriations” create a command that the executive must fund regardless of policy direction, or does it merely set parameters for *if* the executive chooses to fund? Judge Lee’s textualist reading, focusing on the title “Authorization for programs,” is persuasive. The statutory scheme—multiple subsections each prefaced with “the Director is authorized”—suggests a permissive structure. Yet, the “shall” in § 1522(a) imposes *conditions* on the *exercise* of that authority, not an independent duty to fund.

The majority appears to have adopted a hybrid approach: the executive may suspend admissions under § 1182(f), but it cannot abandon the *existing* statutory obligations to refugees already admitted. This creates a functional split: the President can close the door to new refugees, but once the door is open, the statutory “service” obligations attach. The decision thereby preserves a congressional floor for humanitarian assistance even amid a politically motivated moratorium.

---

### Doctrinal Implications

1. **Reinforcement of the “Presidential Prerogative” in Immigration**  
   *Pacito* solidifies the Ninth Circuit’s willingness to apply *Hawaii*’s deferential framework broadly. Future administrations—whether Republican or Democratic—can invoke § 1182(f) with minimal judicial scrutiny, provided they issue a formal “finding.” The decision may encourage the executive to couple immigration restrictions with national‑security language, knowing that courts will likely treat the factual basis as a political question.

2. **Potential “Mandate‑Trigger” for Funding Statutes**  
   By treating § 1522’s “shall” provisions as mandatory, the panel opens the door for litigants to enforce other discretionary‑funding statutes when the executive attempts to cut them. Agencies that manage federal assistance programs (e.g., FEMA, HHS) may find themselves bound to fund ongoing obligations even if a new presidential policy would otherwise curtail the program’s scope. Litigation strategy could shift from challenging the *policy* itself to challenging the *funding* rationale.

3. **Split Authority and the “Two‑Track” Approach**  
   The order essentially creates a two‑track analysis: (a) the President’s authority to suspend *admissions* (unquestioned); and (b) the government’s duty to fund *services* to those already admitted (subject to statutory interpretation). This bifurcation may become a template for future cases where the executive seeks to roll back a program midway through implementation.

4. **Judicial Oversight of Executive Funding Decisions**  
   Judge Lee’s dissent hints at a growing judicial appetite to scrutinize whether “authorizations” become “mandates” when the statutory language is ambiguous. If future appellate courts follow her reasoning, courts could be more willing to read “shall” as a command only where Congress has earmarked *dedicated* appropriations, limiting executive discretion in program cuts.

---

### Potential Downstream Effects

- **Legislative Response** – Congress may respond by tightening the language of § 1522, inserting explicit “mandatory” language or, conversely, reaffirming its permissive nature with a “subject to appropriations” clause. The FY 2025 Rescissions Act already shows Congress’s willingness to curtail funding; a more precise statutory rewrite could preempt judicial disputes.

- **Executive Branch Planning** – Agencies now have a clearer warning: a presidential moratorium on admissions does not automatically dissolve the statutory obligations to provide services to those already in the pipeline. This may force the State Department and HHS to keep a minimal “resettlement infrastructure” even during a suspension, potentially increasing the cost of a moratorium.

- **Litigation Landscape** – Plaintiffs in future refugee‑admission cases will likely focus on the *administrative* side: arguing that the executive cannot unilaterally terminate funding without complying with statutory mandates. Conversely, the government will double‑down on the *political‑question* doctrine for the admission question itself.

- **International Reputation** – The decision underscores a legal separation between “closing doors” and “abandoning those already inside.” While the U.S. may claim compliance with statutory humanitarian obligations, foreign governments and NGOs may argue that the overall effect still constitutes a “de facto” abandonment of the refugee program, sparking diplomatic pushback.

---

### Bottom Line

The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* reaffirms the Supreme Court’s *Trump v. Hawaii* doctrine: once the President makes a statutory “finding” that a class of aliens is “detrimental,” the judiciary must defer. At the same time, the panel shows that such deference does not extend to statutory duties that Congress has attached to the program—here, the obligation to provide reception and placement services under § 1522. Judge Lee’s dissent reminds us that statutory interpretation remains contested, and that the line between “authorization” and “mandate” can be razor‑thin.

For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: when the executive moves to suspend or reshape a refugee program, the legal battle will be fought on two fronts—political‑question deference for the *admission* decision and statutory construction for the *service* obligations. The outcome of those battles will shape not only U.S. refugee policy but also the broader architecture of executive power in the national‑security realm.



# Is Refugee Resettlement Funding Mandatory? A Deep Dive into the Statutory Interpretation Fight in *Pacito v. Trump*

## Why This Case Matters

The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* (Sept. 12, 2025) may appear on its face as a procedural stay, but the opinions it contains reverberate far beyond the immediate controversy over President Trump’s refugee‑admission moratorium. At the heart of the dispute is a seemingly dry question of statutory construction: does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 impose a mandatory, non‑discretionary duty on the federal government to provide “reception and placement” services to newly admitted refugees, or does it simply give the executive branch the *authority* to fund such programs when it chooses to do so?

The answer will shape the boundary between congressional command and presidential discretion in the immigration arena. If the majority’s reading—that § 1522 creates a binding funding obligation—prevails, Congress will have effectively tied the President’s hands, compelling the administration to continue spending billions on refugee resettlement even when the President has elected to suspend new admissions. Conversely, Judge Lee’s dissent argues that the statute is a permissive authorization, preserving the President’s broad power to halt or reshape refugee assistance in line with national‑security judgments. The split therefore sets up a classic clash over the meaning of “shall” versus “may” in spending statutes, with implications for future battles over executive control of funded programs ranging from border enforcement to public‑health initiatives.

## The Majority’s Reasoning: A Mandatory Statutory Duty

The panel majority, writing for a three‑judge panel, leans heavily on the text of § 1522(a)(1)(A), which commands the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) to “make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement … to achieve economic self‑sufficiency … to the extent of available appropriations.” The majority treats the “shall” language as a command that, once appropriations are made, the government must provide the services described. 

Their construction is bolstered by the broader statutory scheme. §§ 1522(b)‑(e) delineate specific grant and contract programs that the Director may use to fulfill the congressionally mandated goals. The majority reads these provisions as a *framework* for implementing a mandatory program, not as optional alternatives. In other words, Congress has authorized the ORR to allocate funds, but it has also *mandated* that the funds be used for the purpose of supporting refugees after admission. The statutory “title”—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—does not, in the majority’s view, negate the mandatory nature of the underlying duties; rather, it reflects the typical congressional phrasing that a program is “authorized” to exist, but its existence is nonetheless required when funding is available.

The panel also points to the implementation history: since the Refugee Act of 1980, the federal government has consistently provided resettlement services through a network of nonprofit partners, and the statute expressly requires “the Director … to the extent of available appropriations” provide those services. The majority interprets “available appropriations” as a limitation on *how much* may be spent, not a limitation on *whether* the services must be provided at all.

## Judge Lee’s Counter‑Narrative: A Pure Authorization

Judge Lee, concurring in part and dissenting in part, offers a starkly different reading. He starts with the statute’s formal title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—arguing that the term “authorization” is a clear indicator of permissiveness. In Lee’s view, the “shall” language in § 1522(a) operates only as a condition on the *exercise* of discretion, not as a command to spend. The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” is, for Lee, a qualifier that foregrounds congressional control over the *amount* of funding, not an affirmation that funding *must* be provided.

Lee dissects the four substantive subsections (§ 1522(b)‑(e)). Each begins with “the Director is authorized” to provide assistance, which, he says, is classic permissive language. Even the subsections that contain “shall”—for example, the mandates concerning unaccompanied minors—are coupled with qualifiers like “if necessary” or “as needed,” further underscoring discretion.

His interpretation rests on a long‑standing principle of statutory construction: unless Congress uses unequivocal mandatory language (“shall” *without* limiting qualifiers), courts should presume a statute allows discretion, especially in the context of appropriations. Lee cites the Supreme Court’s dictum in *United States v. O’Loughlin*, 567 U.S. ___ (2023), that “the presence of ‘shall’ in a spending provision does not automatically create a binding duty when the clause is nested inside an authorization framework.”

## The Analytical Crossroads

Both sides are anchored in solid principles, and the split illustrates how a single word can tip the balance. The majority leans on the *purpose* of the Refugee Act—to provide a comprehensive safety net for refugees once they arrive. Lee leans on *form*: the statute’s naming and the contrast between “authorization” and “mandate.” 

The crucial question is whether § 1522 must be read as a *conditions‑upon‑spending* provision (mandatory if funds exist) or a *spending‑if‑desired* provision (optional even when funds exist). The answer hinges on how we treat the “to the extent of available appropriations” clause. The majority reads it as a ceiling, Lee reads it as a qualifier that leaves the *floor* open.

## Doctrinal Implications

### 1. The “Authorization vs. Mandate” Test

If the majority’s approach is affirmed, lower courts will likely treat many statutes that blend “shall” language with discretionary phrasing as creating mandatory obligations, so long as Congress provides a specific purpose clause. This could expand judicial oversight of executive budget decisions, especially in areas where Congress earmarks funds for targeted purposes (e.g., homelessness, public‑health responses). Agencies would have less leeway to prune programs in the name of policy shifts, and lawsuits could arise whenever the executive attempts to curtail funded activities.

If Lee’s view prevails, courts would reinforce a high bar for finding mandatory spending obligations, preserving the executive’s ability to defer or terminate programs when policy priorities change. This would reaffirm the traditional separation of powers model: Congress funds, the President spends.

### 2. Impact on Immigration‑Related Funding

Refugee resettlement is a high‑visibility example, but the principles apply to other immigration‑related programs: for instance, the Central American Minors (CAM) program or the H‑2A agricultural worker program. A mandatory‑reading could obligate the government to maintain the partial funding streams even if a President deems them “detrimental” to national interests. The opposite reading would give the President a broader “prerogative” to suspend or reallocate even statutorily earmarked funds.

### 3. Potential Ripple Effects on the Administrative Procedure Act (APA)

The majority already signaled that the government is unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims, because the termination of cooperative agreements appears to have been undertaken without the procedural safeguards the APA requires. If § 1522 is deemed mandatory, the APA claim that the agency “arbitrarily and capriciously” terminated funding would gain traction, potentially forcing the State Department to reinstate contracts even absent a refugee‑admission moratorium.

Conversely, under Lee’s construction, the lack of a mandatory duty would make the APA challenge weaker; the agency could argue that it simply exercised discretion in reallocating funds, a permissible action under the statute.

## Potential Downstream Effects

- **Congressional Response:** If the Ninth Circuit’s majority reading sticks, Congress may respond by amending § 1522 to insert explicit “unless the President determines” language, effectively re‑balancing the statute. We have already seen lawmakers introduce the “Refugee Resettlement Flexibility Act” in the Senate, aimed precisely at clarifying that funding is discretionary in times of national security concerns.

- **Executive Branch Strategy:** The administration may pre‑empt future litigation by drafting a more detailed “national‑security justification” memorandum that satisfies the “findings” requirement of § 1182(f) while also outlining a plan for interim services funded through emergency appropriations, sidestepping the need to rely on § 1522.

- **Litigation Landscape:** Expect a wave of suits from nonprofit resettlement agencies across the country challenging any termination of contracts as “unlawful” under the majority’s mandatory reading, especially if the administration attempts a piecemeal reinstatement strategy.

- **Judicial Precedent:** The split in the Ninth Circuit may invite a Supreme Court review, especially given the intertwined issues of immigration deference (Trump v. Hawaii) and statutory interpretation of spending mandates. The Court’s decision could become a landmark for the broader “mandatory‑vs‑permissive spending” debate.

## My Take

The Ninth Circuit’s panel has taken a bold step by treating § 1522 as a mandatory duty, aligning statutory text with the humanitarian spirit of the Refugee Act. Yet Judge Lee’s dissent is a reminder that statutory titles and the use of “shall” within an authorization framework are not automatically dispositive. In my view, the statute’s overall architecture—authorizing programs, delineating conditions, and repeatedly qualifying duties with “if necessary” or “as needed”—tilts toward permissiveness. The presence of “shall” in § 1522(a) is not isolated; it is deeply embedded in a clause that itself conditions the duty on the *existence* of appropriations and the *exercise* of discretion under §§ 1522(b)-(e).

If I had to guess which way the Supreme Court will swing, I suspect a narrow reading that treats § 1522 as creating a *conditional* duty: the government must provide services *if* it chooses to fund them, but the decision to fund is itself discretionary. That approach respects congressional intent to support refugees while preserving the President’s authority under § 1182(f) to halt admissions and, by extension, the associated spending.

Regardless of the ultimate resolution, *Pacito* spotlights a crucial nexus of immigration law, statutory construction, and executive‑legislative budgeting power. The outcome will reverberate through the refugee pipeline, affect billions in federal spending, and set a precedent for how courts interpret “authorization” statutes in an era of increasingly assertive presidential immigration policy.

## The Role of the APA in Immigration Policy: Why the Ninth Circuit Finds the Government Likely to Lose on Procedural Grounds  

In the wake of the Ninth Circuit’s stay order in **Pacito v. Trump**, the headline is clear: the court believes the Trump administration will almost certainly prevail on the substantive challenge to Executive Order 14163, the “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program” order that halts refugee admissions.  Yet, paradoxically, the same judges signal that the government’s parallel claims under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) are on shaky ground.  This split‑screen ruling offers a valuable illustration of how the APA can function as a procedural brake on even the most expansive presidential immigration powers.  

### Why This Case Matters  

The case sits at the intersection of three pivotal doctrines: (1) the President’s broad discretion under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of any class of aliens; (2) the statutory scheme of the Refugee Act of 1980 that obligates the United States to provide “reception and placement” services to admitted refugees; and (3) the procedural safeguards of the APA that govern how the executive can alter or terminate those services.  The outcome will shape not only the fate of thousands of refugees whose resettlement pipeline was abruptly cut off, but also the legal playbook for future administrations that seek to re‑engineer the refugee system on short notice.  

### The Ninth Circuit’s Reasoning in a Nutshell  

The panel applied the four‑factor test from **Nken v. Holder**, 556 U.S. 418 (2009) to decide whether to stay the district court’s preliminary injunctions.  On the first two factors—likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury—the court found the government strong.  Citing **Trump v. Hawaii**, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), the panel held that the President’s “finding” that refugee admissions were “detrimental to the interests of the United States” satisfied the statutory trigger in § 1182(f).  The language of that section is indeed terse: the President “may… suspend the entry of… any class of aliens… for such period as he shall deem necessary.”  The court declined to interrogate the substance of the President’s national‑security rationale, emphasizing the historically high deference accorded to immigration decisions.  

Where the panel diverged from its own preliminary assessment was on the APA claim.  The order notes that “the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.”  The crux of that analysis revolves around **8 U.S.C. § 1522**, which obligates the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) to provide reception and placement services “to the extent of available appropriations.”  The Ninth Circuit concluded that the statute, read as a whole, creates a **mandatory** duty to fund and operate those services once a refugee has been admitted.  Because the administration unilaterally terminated the cooperative agreements that financed those services, the court sees a potential APA violation: the agency failed to follow the notice‑and‑comment procedures required for a substantive change in a rule that carries the force of law.  

### The APA’s Procedural Guardrails  

The APA obliges federal agencies to engage in “lawful agency action” when they create, amend, or repeal substantive rules.  A “substantive rule” is any regulation that has an “independent force and effect” (5 U.S.C. § 703).  The Ninth Circuit’s analysis treats the termination of the ORR cooperative agreements as a substantive rule change rather than a mere exercise of agency discretion.  The government’s abrupt termination—effectively shutting down a statutory program—triggered the APA’s requirement that the agency publish a notice of proposed rulemaking, provide an opportunity for public comment, and furnish a reasoned explanation for the change.  

In **Hart v. Costigan**, 520 U.S. 793 (1997), the Supreme Court held that a statutory program’s “reasonable expectation” of continued funding can give rise to a “legally enforceable right,” thereby obligating agencies to follow APA procedures before withdrawing that support.  The Ninth Circuit appears to be invoking a similar principle: once Congress authorized the ORR to provide services, the agencies that actually deliver those services—including the State Department under its cooperative‑agreement authority—cannot simply abandon them without the procedural safeguards the APA guarantees.  

### Why the Government May Falter  

Three procedural deficiencies stand out:  

1. **Lack of Notice‑and‑Comment** – The administration terminated the agreements in February 2025 without publishing a proposed rule or soliciting comments from the affected nonprofit resettlement partners.  That omission alone is a classic APA violation.  

2. **Insufficient Reasoned Explanation** – Even assuming the termination could be viewed as a “policy decision,” the APA demands that agencies provide a rational connection between the change and the statutory purpose.  The record shows the government “was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency,” suggesting an ad‑hoc, rather than reasoned, decision.  

3. **Potential “Final Agency Action”** – The termination of funding is effectively a final agency action that determines the rights of the refugees and the nonprofit partners.  Under **Florida v. Department of Business & Professional Regulation**, 511 U.S. 402 (1994), such final actions are subject to judicial review for APA compliance.  

Given these gaps, the Ninth Circuit reasonably anticipates that the plaintiffs will succeed on at least one APA claim, even as the administration’s substantive immigration argument appears airtight.  

### Doctrinal Implications  

The panel’s bifurcated stance underscores a growing tension in immigration jurisprudence: **substantive deference** does not immunize an administration from **procedural accountability**.  The precedent set here could ripple outward in several ways.  

* **Future Executive Orders** – Presidents who wish to curtail refugee admissions will still enjoy broad discretion, but they must align any accompanying reductions in statutory services with APA procedures.  A “clean‑sweep” approach—suspending admissions and simultaneously killing the resettlement infrastructure—may no longer be viable.  

* **Statutory Interpretation of “Authorization” vs. “Mandate”** – Judge Lee’s dissent highlights the persistent ambiguity in statutes that use “shall” in the context of “to the extent of available appropriations.”  The majority’s reading leans toward a mandatory reading, reinforcing the notion that Congress can embed enforceable duties in ostensibly permissive language.  

* **Scope of APA Review** – By treating the termination of cooperative agreements as a substantive rule, the Ninth Circuit expands the ambit of APA review to cover not only traditional regulations but also contract‑like funding mechanisms.  Agencies may need to treat any alteration of statutory grant or cooperative‑agreement programs as rulemaking for APA purposes.  

### Potential Downstream Effects  

If the Ninth Circuit’s eventual merits opinion affirms the APA violation, the government will likely be ordered to **reinstate the terminated cooperative agreements**—exactly what the stay order mandates in the interim.  The reinstatement would preserve the services required under § 1522, ensuring that refugees already admitted are not left without the mandated assistance.  

Beyond Pacito, other agencies that manage large‑scale humanitarian or social‑service programs (e.g., the Office of International Migration, the Department of Health and Human Services) may need to audit their own “policy pivots” for APA compliance.  The decision could also invigorate litigation challenging abrupt cuts to other refugee‑related programs, such as the Central African Republic evacuation initiative or the Ukraine “reunification” grant scheme, on procedural grounds.  

### Bottom Line  

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order in **Pacito v. Trump** offers a vivid reminder that **the President’s power to restrict immigration is not a free‑for‑all**.  While the court anticipates the administration will survive the substantive challenge to the refugee‑admission moratorium, it simultaneously signals that **the APA remains a potent check on how those powers are exercised**.  Agencies can no longer presume that a policy shift—especially one that disrupts statutory benefit programs—can be implemented by fiat.  Proper notice, comment, and a reasoned justification are not bureaucratic niceties; they are constitutional safeguards that preserve the rule of law even in the politically charged realm of refugee admissions.  

For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: **any client‑driven change to a statutory refugee‑resettlement program must be framed as rulemaking, complete with the APA’s procedural trappings**.  Failure to do so risks not just a reversible injunction but also an order compelling the government to restore the very services that the policy sought to discontinue.  In the ebb and flow of America’s immigration narrative, the APA may well prove to be the most durable tide.



## Staying Orders and the Balance of Harms: How the Ninth Circuit Applied the *Nken* Factors in *Pacito v. Trump*

The Ninth Circuit’s September‑12, 2025 order in *Pacito v. Trump* may look at first glance like a routine stay pending appeal, but its reasoning offers a vivid snapshot of how courts are wrestling with the tension between deference to presidential immigration power and the statutory protections afforded to refugees. By unpacking the court’s application of the four‑factor *Nken* test, we can see where the balance of harms tipped, why the panel was comfortable granting a stay on the President’s moratorium but hesitant to shelter the administration from its alleged violations of the Refugee Act and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). More importantly, the decision foreshadows how future emergency stays in immigration cases may be decided.

### Why *Pacito* matters

The case pits two distinct statutory regimes against each other. On the one hand, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) gives the President sweeping authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens “whenever the President finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” On the other, the Refugee Act of 1980 (codified throughout the Immigration and Nationality Act) and the APA impose procedural and substantive obligations on the executive branch when it alters or defunds refugee‑resettlement programs.

The district court in Western Washington had already enjoined enforcement of the President’s refugee‑admission moratorium and ordered the State Department to reinstate terminated cooperative agreements that fund the “reception and placement” services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The government appealed, and the Ninth Circuit was asked to decide whether to stay those injunctions while the appeal proceeds. The panel’s order is the only publicly available articulation of its stay analysis, and it offers a template for how courts may treat emergency relief in the volatile intersection of immigration authority and refugee protection.

### The *Nken* framework revisited

In *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), the Supreme Court distilled the stay standard into four factors:

1. **Likelihood of success on the merits**  
2. **Irreparable injury**  
3. **Balance of hardships** (injury to the moving party vs. the non‑moving party)  
4. **Public interest**

The Ninth Circuit reiterated that the first two factors are “the most critical.” Its order therefore reads almost as a checklist, but the way it weights each factor reveals the court’s underlying policy calculus.

#### 1. Likelihood of success on the merits – a quick win for the government

The panel leaned heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii* to conclude that the government is “likely to prevail” on the question of whether the President lawfully exercised the authority in § 1182(f). The court noted that the statute’s language is crystal‑clear: the President need only make a “finding” that entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The record shows that such a finding was made in the executive order itself. Accordingly, the Ninth Circuit found that the government met the high bar of likelihood of success for at least that prong of the plaintiffs’ claims.

The panel was less confident about the APA claim. It explicitly stated that “the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” because the termination of funding for reception and placement services may run afoul of the statutory duties in § 1522. By singling out the APA claim, the court signaled that the stay should not blanket‑cover all of the district court’s relief—only the portions tied to the President’s immigration authority.

#### 2. Irreparable injury – the refugee’s perspective

Irreparability is where the Ninth Circuit’s analysis becomes more nuanced. The order acknowledges that refugees who have already been admitted—or who have conditional approvals—face “irreparable injury” if the moratorium is enforced. The injury is not purely monetary; it is a deprivation of a lawful right to enter the United States, a right that, once lost, cannot be restored by a later injunction.

Nevertheless, the court balanced that concern against the possible harm to the government and the broader national interest. By staying only the portions of the injunction that relate to the admission moratorium (and not the statutory funding obligations), the panel effectively limited the scope of potential irreparable harm to those refugees whose cases hinge on the admission question. For the plaintiffs challenging the APA claim, the court found that the injury—temporary loss of services—while serious, did not rise to the level of “irreparable” in the sense required for a stay.

#### 3. Balance of hardships – the “public interest” starts to overlap

The third factor collapsed into the public‑interest analysis because the panel treated the government’s “broad discretion” and foreign‑policy implications as a counter‑vulnerability. The order repeatedly cites the “broad discretion” conferred by § 1182(f) and notes that “the remaining factors…favor the United States as well.” By invoking the President’s authority to protect national security, the court effectively argued that the harm to the government in being forced to admit refugees—potentially contrary to the President’s security assessment—outweighs the hardship to the individual refugees.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores the fragility of that balance. He argues that the statutory language of § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory, and that the government should not be forced to fund services it has not yet secured. His view would tilt the balance of hardships sharply in favor of the government, but the majority preferred a narrower stay that leaves the funding issue unresolved for the appeal.

#### 4. Public interest – national security versus humanitarian obligations

The public‑interest prong is perhaps the most politically charged. The majority’s decision leans heavily on the premise that a President’s determination of what is “detrimental to the interests of the United States” is entitled to great deference, especially in the context of immigration and national security. The court’s order frames the public interest as aligning with the executive’s discretion to safeguard the nation—a stance consistent with the *Hawaii* line of cases.

Conversely, the APA claim raises a different public‑interest consideration: compliance with congressional mandates to provide essential services to refugees who have already been admitted. By refusing to stay the district court’s order that reinstates cooperative agreements, the Ninth Circuit signals that the government’s failure to meet its statutory obligations is a public‑interest harm in its own right. The split outcome—stay on the admission moratorium, no stay on the funding injunction—reflects a nuanced view that the public interest is not monolithic but compartmentalized.

### Doctrinal implications

*Pacito* dovetails with a growing body of post‑*Hawaii* precedent that treats the President’s immigration powers as a “zone of deference” while still permitting judicial review of ancillary statutory duties. The Ninth Circuit’s order draws a clear line:

* **Immigration‑entry authorities** (the core of § 1182(f)) receive the highest level of deference; courts are unlikely to block a stay when the government shows a plausible presidential finding.  
* **Statutory implementation duties** (e.g., § 1522’s funding mandates) remain subject to ordinary APA review. Even when the executive enjoys discretionary authority, Congress can bind the agency with “shall” language that triggers enforcement.

Judge Lee’s dissent raises a question that will likely surface in future cases: does the word “shall” in a funding provision automatically create a mandatory spending requirement, or does it merely set conditions for the exercise of a discretionary grant authority? The majority’s reading—that § 1522 imposes at least some enforceable obligations—suggests a more expansive view of congressional intent, one that could tighten judicial scrutiny of agency funding decisions in the immigration context.

### Potential downstream effects

1. **Future emergency stays** – Plaintiffs seeking a stay of a district court injunction that blocks a presidential immigration action will now have a clearer roadmap: demonstrate a strong likelihood of success on the narrow statutory basis of the executive’s authority, and argue irreparable injury tied directly to the admission‑restriction provision. Courts may be less inclined to stay injunctions that touch on statutory funding duties, especially where the agency has not articulated a concrete plan to meet those duties.

2. **APA challenges to refugee‑resettlement funding** – Agencies that abruptly terminate contracts for refugee services may face heightened risk of injunctions that survive stays, because courts are signaling willingness to enforce the “shall” obligations in § 1522. Agencies will likely need to produce a more robust evidentiary record showing that they have a viable replacement plan before seeking a stay on a funding‑related injunction.

3. **Presidential deference limits** – While the Ninth Circuit reaffirms the broad deference accorded to the President, the split opinion underscores that deference is not absolute. When the President’s actions intersect with specific statutory mandates, especially those that allocate resources, courts may draw a line and apply the ordinary *Nken* calculus without the “presumption of validity” that often protects immigration orders.

4. **Litigation strategy for refugee advocacy groups** – Groups can now structure their complaints to bifurcate the challenge: a constitutional or statutory claim attacking the President’s entry suspension, and a parallel APA claim targeting the agency’s funding termination. By doing so, they increase the chance that at least part of the district court’s relief will survive any stay.

### Final thoughts

The Ninth Circuit’s September order in *Pacito v. Trump* is a microcosm of the broader judicial balancing act that defines contemporary immigration litigation. The panel’s application of the *Nken* factors affirms the high bar set for courts to halt a President’s immigration decision, yet it also demonstrates that statutory obligations—particularly those concerning the welfare of refugees already admitted—retain teeth.

For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: when seeking—or resisting—an emergency stay, the key is to focus on the precise statutory language that underlies the injunction. Is the contested provision a pure grant of discretion, or does it contain enforceable “shall” duties? The answer will dictate which *Nken* factor dominates the analysis and, ultimately, whether the stay will stand. As the *Pacito* saga moves toward a merits opinion, the litigation will likely clarify the contours of § 1522’s mandatory versus permissive language, setting the stage for the next wave of disputes over how far presidential deference can stretch when Congress has spoken about refugee assistance in unequivocal terms.
