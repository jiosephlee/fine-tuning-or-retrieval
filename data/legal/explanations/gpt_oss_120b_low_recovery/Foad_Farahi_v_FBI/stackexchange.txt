Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What constitutes a “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding under FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly references the requirement that disclosure could interfere with "pending or reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceedings. I’m unclear on how courts determine whether a proceeding is merely "anticipated" versus actually pending, especially when the investigation is ongoing for years. Does the agency need to provide concrete evidence of a future case, or is an attestation of an "ongoing investigation" sufficient? How have the D.C. Circuit’s cases like *CREW* and *Juarez* interpreted this standard?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under FOIA Exemption 7(A) a record may be withheld only if the agency shows that (1) the record was *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* and (2) its disclosure “*could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings*.”  55 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).  The Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit have interpreted the second prong to require that the “enforcement proceedings” be *pending* or *reasonably anticipated*.

### 1. What “pending or reasonably anticipated” means

The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly held that a “pending” proceeding is any law‑enforcement action that has already been initiated and is still open (e.g., a criminal probe, grand‑jury investigation, or administrative case).  *Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Washington v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (quoting *Mapother v. DOJ*, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).  

A “reasonably anticipated” proceeding, by contrast, is a future enforcement action that is not yet formally opened but is sufficiently concrete that the government can plausibly expect it to occur.  The circuit explained that an agency may establish such anticipation merely by *attesting* that an investigation is ongoing and that it is being conducted “in furtherance of a possible criminal case.”  *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1099; see also *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59 (D.C. Cir. 2008).

Thus, the standard is *not* a requirement that the agency produce a charging document, indictment, or other concrete proof that a case will be filed.  The agency must show, with reasonable specificity, that:

1. the investigation is active (i.e., facts are still being gathered or analyzed); and  
2. the investigation is aimed at a potential law‑enforcement outcome (e.g., a criminal prosecution, civil suit, or immigration removal proceeding).

If those two elements are satisfied, the proceeding is deemed “reasonably anticipated” for exemption‑7(A) purposes.

### 2. How the Farahi opinion applies the standard

The Farahi opinion relies directly on *CREW* and *Juarez* for its analysis:

* **Pending or anticipated proceedings** – The court notes that “ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’ for these purposes” and that “the government may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their ‘existence.’” *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1099.  Accordingly, the FBI’s declaration that the investigation “remains ongoing” satisfies the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement.

* **Attestation is sufficient** – The opinion expressly rejects Farahi’s argument that a more detailed, forward‑looking forecast is needed.  It states that the agency’s “public Hardy declaration explained that the relevant investigation remained ‘ongoing,’” and the accompanying ex‑parte declaration supplied “considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation.”  This together meets the “reasonable specificity” requirement articulated in *Juarez*.

* **Length of the investigation is irrelevant** – Although the investigation has spanned many years, the court emphasizes that the passage of time does not defeat the presumption that a proceeding is still pending or anticipated so long as the agency can show the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future case.  *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59.

* **No need for a “concrete prospective proceeding”** – The opinion quotes *CREW* that a “vague nature” of an assertion may be insufficient, but finds the FBI’s combined declarations (public and ex‑parte) to provide enough specificity to overcome that concern.  The court therefore treats the FBI’s attestation that “investigations remained ongoing” as a valid basis for invoking exemption 7(A).

### 3. Practical take‑away for FOIA litigants

1. **Agency burden** – The agency must produce a declaration that ties the records to a law‑enforcement investigation and explains why disclosure would jeopardize that investigation.  The declaration need not contain a charging document; a reasoned statement that the investigation is active and aimed at a possible prosecution is sufficient.

2. **Court’s review** – The court conducts a de novo review of the agency’s exemption claim but affords the agency “deference” because of its expertise in law‑enforcement matters.  As long as the agency’s declaration is not merely conclusory and provides at least a minimal factual backdrop, the court is likely to find the “pending or reasonably anticipated” prong satisfied.

3. **Effect of time** – Even a long‑standing investigation can satisfy the standard if the agency can demonstrate that evidence is still being collected or that the investigation has not been closed.  The passage of years does not by itself defeat the exemption.

### 4. Conclusion

In *Farahi v. FBI* the D.C. Circuit reaffirmed the *CREW* and *Juarez* line of cases: an agency may rely on exemption 7(A) when it can reasonably show that the records were compiled for an investigation that is *either* (a) already underway (pending) or (b) being pursued with the expectation that it will lead to a future enforcement action (reasonably anticipated).  An attestation—supported by a declaration that the investigation “remains ongoing” and that it “continues to gather evidence for a possible criminal case”—is sufficient; the agency does not need to produce a concrete charging document or other proof of a future case.  Consequently, the Farahi court upheld the FBI’s use of exemption 7(A) on the basis that the investigation into Farahi was still ongoing and therefore “reasonably anticipated” at the time of the FOIA request.

### When can an agency rely on ex parte declarations in FOIA cases, and what procedural safeguards exist for the requester?
Question:
The court accepted ex parte declarations from FBI officials to support both the exemption and segregability analyses. I’m confused about the procedural rights of a FOIA plaintiff when critical evidence is submitted ex parte. Are there any requirements for the agency to disclose the content of those declarations to the requester, or to provide a summary? How does the D.C. Circuit balance the need for secrecy with due process for the requester?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI* the D.C. Circuit held that an agency may rely on ex‑parte (sealed) declarations to prove both the existence of an Exemption 7(A) “interference” ground and the lack of reasonable segregability, **provided** (1) the agency has already satisfied its ordinary FOIA burden by giving the requester a *public* statement that the records are exempt and that the relevant enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and (2) the court finds that the sealed declaration contains “reasonable specificity” about why the exemption applies. The requester, however, is still entitled to the procedural protections that FOIA requires: the agency must (i) disclose the *content* of any public declaration, (ii) give a *summary* of the material in any sealed declaration that is relied upon, and (iii) allow the requester an opportunity to object to the use of the sealed declaration. The D.C. Circuit balances secrecy and due‑process by limiting the court’s reliance on the sealed declaration to the narrow issue for which it was submitted and by requiring the agency to demonstrate that the sealed material is essential and cannot be disclosed without defeating the exemption.

---

### How the opinion frames the use of ex‑parte declarations

1. **Burden of proof stays on the agency** – Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B) the government must “show” that an exemption applies. The court may accept *government affidavits* that “show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated” (*Juarez* 518 F.3d at 61). The *Farahi* court therefore allowed the FBI’s sealed (ex‑parte) declarations to satisfy that epistemic burden.

2. **Public declaration required** – Before the court will rely on a sealed affidavit, it must have a *public* declaration that puts the requester on notice of the agency’s position. In *Farahi* the FBI first filed a public declaration from Section Chief David Hardy stating that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that “the relevant investigations remain ongoing.” The court treated that public statement as the *notice* required by FOIA.

3. **Ex‑parte declaration as an update** – The later sealed declaration (from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender) was not used to *prove* the existence of pending proceedings *de novo*; it was offered only to confirm that the factual circumstances underlying the earlier public declaration had not changed. The opinion emphasizes that the court “could not disclose the contents of Hardy’s ex‑parte declaration” but that the *public* Hardy declaration already satisfied the notice requirement. The Bender declaration therefore served a *narrow* evidentiary function—updating the record without revealing the underlying sensitive details.

4. **Specificity requirement** – Even though the declaration is sealed, the agency must still demonstrate “reasonable specificity” as to why the exemption applies. The court found that the combination of the public Hardy declaration (which set out the investigative scope) and the sealed Hardy declaration (which gave “considerable detail”) together met that standard. The Bender declaration merely confirmed that nothing had changed.

### Procedural safeguards for the requester

The opinion identifies three procedural protections that survive even when the government relies on sealed material:

| Requirement | How *Farahi* enforces it |
|-------------|--------------------------|
| **1. Disclosure of the public declaration** | The FBI’s public Hardy declaration is fully on the record; the requester can read it and assess whether the agency’s exemption claim is plausible. |
| **2. Summarized content of sealed declarations** | While the court may keep the full affidavit under seal, it must provide the requester (and the public record) with at least a *summary* that explains the relevance of the sealed material. The opinion notes that the district court “explained that the FBI had provided a ‘detailed justification’ for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission.” This description satisfies the summary‑requirement. |
| **3. Opportunity to object** | The plaintiff may file objections to the use of sealed declarations. In *Farahi* the plaintiff “did not oppose the motion” to file the Hardy declaration ex parte, so the court treated the lack of objection as a waiver of any due‑process challenge. If the requester had filed a timely objection, the court would have been required to consider it before accepting the sealed affidavit. |

### Balancing secrecy and due process

The D.C. Circuit’s approach can be summarized as follows:

1. **Deference to the agency’s expertise** – Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its “decision to invoke exemption 7(A) is entitled to deference” (*Campbell* 164 F.3d at 32). The court therefore does not require the agency to disclose the underlying classified details so long as the public declaration puts the requester on notice.

2. **Limited reliance on sealed material** – The court may rely on an ex‑parte declaration only for the *specific* factual point that cannot be disclosed without defeating the exemption (e.g., that an investigation is still ongoing). The sealed affidavit is not used to *re‑prove* the entire exemption analysis.

3. **Mandatory summary** – Even when the full affidavit is sealed, the court must give a concrete summary that identifies the factual basis for the exemption. This satisfies the FOIA procedural requirement that “the agency must disclose any reasonably segregable portion” and that the requester is entitled to know *why* material is being withheld.

4. **Waiver of objection** – If the requester fails to object to the filing of a sealed declaration, the court treats that silence as a waiver of any due‑process claim concerning the affidavit’s secrecy. The *Farahi* decision underscores that “nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration” because the plaintiff did not oppose it.

### Practical take‑aways for FOIA litigants

* **Ask for the public declaration** – The agency must place its exemption rationale on the public docket. If it does not, you can move to compel it.
* **Demand a summary of any sealed affidavit** – Even when the government wants to keep an affidavit under seal, the court must give you a factual summary that allows meaningful review.
* **File timely objections** – If you believe a sealed declaration is being used improperly, object to its filing under the FOIA’s procedural rules. Failure to do so may be deemed a waiver.
* **Expect limited disclosure** – The court may accept sealed material when the public declaration alone is insufficient to demonstrate “reasonable specificity.” The burden is on the agency to show that the sealed material is *essential* to the exemption analysis.

In short, *Farahi* confirms that ex‑parte declarations are permissible, but only when the agency also provides a public statement and a court‑approved summary, and only if the requester has had an opportunity to contest their use. The D.C. Circuit therefore preserves the requester’s procedural rights while allowing the agency to protect genuinely sensitive law‑enforcement information.

### How does the “reasonable segregation” requirement under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) apply when exempt material is interspersed throughout a large document?
Question:
The opinion states that the FBI showed the records were not reasonably segregable because exempt material was "interspersed line‑by‑line" with non‑exempt content. What legal standards govern this analysis? Is there a threshold for how much non‑exempt information must be separable, and how have courts applied the “reasonable segregation” test in large, complex records like those in this case?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “reasonable segregation” requirement in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) is satisfied only when an agency can show, with “reasonable specificity,” that any non‑exempt material can be physically or logically separated from the exempt portions of a record.  The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly held that where exempt material is “interspersed line‑by‑line” with non‑exempt text, the record is **not** reasonably segregable because the effort required to pull out the few non‑exempt fragments would be disproportionate to the amount of information that would actually be disclosed.  *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. Dep’t of Air Force*, 566 F.2d at 260‑61 (citing *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d at 61).

### Governing standards

1. **Statutory burden.**  
   An agency must take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information” ( § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) ) and must provide “any reasonably segregable portion of a record” after deleting the exempt parts ( § 552(b) ).

2. **Case‑law definition of “reasonably segregable.”**  
   The circuit explains that a record is reasonably segregable when the non‑exempt portions can be identified and separated without an undue burden on the agency.  *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61; *Mead*, 566 F.2d at 261.  The test is **qualitative**, not a bright‑line numerical threshold: the court looks at the *nature* of the intertwining, not at a set percentage of non‑exempt text.

3. **Inextricably intertwined standard.**  
   When the exempt and non‑exempt material are “inextricably intertwined,” the agency may withhold the whole file.  *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61 (quoting *Mead*).  The court may rely on agency affidavits that explain, with reasonable specificity, why separation would be impracticable.

### Application in **Farahi v. FBI**

The district court, and now this appellate panel, found that the FBI’s **ex parte** and public declarations demonstrated that the exempt portions were “interspersed line‑by‑line” throughout the 10,750 pages and 80 CDs.  The court described the situation as follows:

> “if a small amount of non‑exempt material is randomly ‘interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document,’ that material is not likely to be ‘reasonably segregable.’” – *Farahi*, 2025 WL … at 139‑40 (citing *Mead*, 566 F.2d at 261).

Because the non‑exempt fragments were scattered in such a way that extracting them would require tearing apart thousands of pages and potentially revealing the context of the exempt material, the FBI satisfied its burden that **no meaningful information was reasonably segregable**.  The opinion makes clear that the court did **not** require a quantitative threshold (e.g., “at least 10 % of the record must be non‑exempt”).  Instead, the analysis hinged on whether the agency could point to *specific* portions that could be separated without compromising the exempt content.  The FBI’s explanation that the exempt material was “line‑by‑line interspersed” met that burden.

### How courts have treated large, complex records

- **Large volumes are not a per se excuse** for segregation.  In *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. Veterans Affairs*, 57 F.4th at 1069, the D.C. Circuit held that even a “vast volume of responsive records” can be deemed non‑segregable if the non‑exempt bits are embedded throughout the file.  
- **Agency affidavits are given deference** when they identify the intertwining with reasonable specificity.  *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61 allows courts to rely on such affidavits rather than requiring the plaintiff to perform the segregation analysis herself.  
- **No bright‑line percentage** has been set.  The focus is on the *practical* ability to separate the material.  Where the effort would be akin to “redacting every other word” or would expose the exempt context, the record is treated as inseparable.

### Bottom line for your question

- **Legal standard:** A record is reasonably segregable only if the agency can identify and isolate the non‑exempt portions without undue burden; exemption applies when exempt and non‑exempt material are “inextricably intertwined.”  
- **Threshold:** There is no fixed numeric threshold.  The analysis is fact‑specific; even a tiny amount of non‑exempt text may be non‑segregable if it is scattered throughout the file.  
- **Application to large documents:** Courts look at how the material is arranged.  When exempt content is interspersed line‑by‑line, as in *Farahi*, the court will deem the whole record non‑segregable and therefore withhold it under Exemption 7(A).

Thus, in *Farahi* the FBI satisfied the “reasonable segregation” requirement because the exempt information could not be separated from the non‑exempt without an impractical and overly burdensome effort, and the court did not require the agency to produce a certain amount of separable content.

### What level of deference is owed to an agency’s determination that records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" under Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The opinion cites *Campbell v. DOJ* and says the FBI’s determination is entitled to a "fair measure of deference." How far does that deference extend? Must the agency provide a detailed nexus analysis, or can it rely on its law‑enforcement role alone? What precedents outline the boundary between permissible deference and the court’s independent review?
Answer:
**Answer**

The D.C. Circuit makes clear that, in an Exemption 7(A) analysis, the FBI’s determination that a record was “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” is entitled to **“fair” or “a fair measure of” deference**, but that deference is **not absolute**.  

### 1. What the deference covers  

The court relies on *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998), which holds that “because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.”  The opinion repeats this language and adds that the deference is “particularly appropriate for records implicating national security” (*Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ*; *Center for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ*).  

In practice the deference **covers two separate prongs** of the Exemption 7(A) test:

1. **Whether the record was “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.”**  
2. **Whether disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings.**  

For the first prong, the agency need only show a **rational nexus** between the record and an investigatory or enforcement function of the agency. The court cites *Campbell* for the proposition that the agency must “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties,’” (quoting *Pratt v. Webster*, 673 F.2d 408, 420‑21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)). In *Farahi*, the FBI satisfied this requirement with a public declaration stating that the documents were “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and an ex‑parte declaration giving “considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation.” The court therefore concluded that the agency had met its burden.

### 2. How far the deference extends  

Deference does **not** relieve the agency of its statutory burden to **justify** the exemption with at least a **reasonable specificity** showing the nexus and the pending‑or‑anticipated proceeding. The court notes several cases that set the lower limit:

* *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) – the agency may rely on affidavits that “show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.”  
* *CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) – the agency must demonstrate that the investigation is “pending or reasonably anticipated.” A bare statement that “investigations remain ongoing” can be insufficient if not supported by additional detail.  

In *Farahi* the court found the FBI’s **combined record**—the public Hardy declaration (which set out the existence of the investigation) plus the detailed ex‑parte Hardy declaration (which described its scope)—sufficient. The later Bender declaration was treated only as an **update** confirming that nothing had changed; it was not required to repeat the full nexus analysis.

Thus, the **boundary of permissible deference** is:

* **Agency’s initial showing** must contain enough factual detail to establish a concrete link between the record and a law‑enforcement function and to demonstrate that a proceeding is pending or reasonably anticipated.  
* **Subsequent updates** may be brief so long as the earlier, more detailed affidavits remain on the record and the court is satisfied that they are still accurate.

### 3. What the agency does **not** have to provide  

The opinion makes clear that the FBI **does not need** to produce a full, independent **nexus analysis** each time it refreshes its claim. Once the court has been persuaded that the record meets the first prong, the agency may rely on a short, confirmatory statement that the investigation “remains ongoing.” The court expressly held that the Bender declaration, though terse, was permissible because the substantive nexus had already been proven in the Hardy declarations.

### 4. Key precedents defining the line  

| Case | Holding on deference/ burden |
|------|------------------------------|
| *Campbell v. DOJ* (1998) | Agency’s law‑enforcement determination entitled to deference. |
| *Pratt v. Webster* (1982) | Agency must show a rational nexus to a law‑enforcement duty. |
| *CREW v. DOJ* (2014) | Assertion that an investigation is “ongoing” may be too vague; must be linked to pending or anticipated proceeding. |
| *Juarez v. DOJ* (2008) | Affidavits must be specific enough to show why segregation is impossible. |
| *Center for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ* (2003) | Deference especially strong where national‑security investigations are involved. |

### 5. Practical takeaway for FOIA litigants  

When challenging an Exemption 7(A) claim against a law‑enforcement agency:

1. **Scrutinize the agency’s initial affidavit** for a detailed nexus and a clear statement that a proceeding is pending or reasonably anticipated.  
2. **Do not expect the agency to re‑run the full analysis** each time it updates its claim; a concise reaffirmation is sufficient if the original detailed showing remains on the record.  
3. If the agency’s original affidavit is merely conclusory (e.g., “investigations remain ongoing” with no supporting facts), the court may find the deference insufficient (*CREW*).  

In *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit concluded that the FBI’s original, detailed declarations satisfied the statutory burden, and therefore the agency’s later brief update was entitled to the “fair measure of deference” required by *Campbell*. The deference stops where the agency’s affidavit fails to provide the minimal factual specificity demanded by *CREW* and *Juarez*.

### Are categorical withholdings permissible under FOIA, and what are the requirements for the categories to be sufficiently distinct?
Question:
Farahi argued that the FBI’s categories for withholding were overbroad and not sufficiently distinct. The court dismissed this argument, citing *CREW* and *Khine*. What are the legal criteria for an agency’s categorical withholding scheme to satisfy FOIA’s requirement that categories be "sufficiently distinct"? How have courts evaluated the sufficiency of such categories in prior cases?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under FOIA an agency may withhold records on a “category‑by‑category” basis only if each category is **(1) clearly defined**, **(2) internally consistent**, and **(3) sufficiently distinct from the other categories** that a court can evaluate whether the exemption asserted for a given category is properly applied.  The D.C. Circuit has articulated this requirement in *Citizens for Responsible & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1088 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (quoting *Gallant v. NLRB*, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)).  A plaintiff who does not point out how a particular category fails that test forfeits the argument, as held in *Khine v. DHS*, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

**What “sufficiently distinct” means**

1. **Clear definition of the scope** – The agency must describe, in its withholding notice, what factual material falls within the category.  A label such as “national‑security investigations” is not enough unless the notice explains what types of documents, subjects, or investigative phases are covered.

2. **Non‑overlap with other categories** – The categories must not be so broad that the same document could plausibly be placed in two or more categories.  If overlap exists, a court cannot determine which exemption applies to a particular record, defeating the statutory requirement that “each category … be sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” (*CREW* at 1088).

3. **Logical separation of exempt from non‑exempt material** – The agency must show that, within a given category, the exempt material is not “inextricably intertwined” with non‑exempt material.  When the category is too amorphous, the agency cannot satisfy the “reasonable segregation” requirement of 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii).

**How courts have applied the test**

- ***CREW* (2014)** – The court rejected a government‑provided categorical list that lumped together “documents related to ongoing investigations” without specifying what kinds of documents (e.g., surveillance logs, interview notes, intelligence summaries) were covered.  Because the list did not delineate the boundaries between categories, the court held the categories were not “sufficiently distinct” and ordered more detailed explanations.

- ***Khine* (2019)** – The plaintiff challenged a Department of Homeland Security withholding that used a single category called “immigration‑related national‑security investigations.”  The district court found the category overly broad and indistinguishable from other categories such as “general immigration enforcement.”  The D.C. Circuit affirmed, emphasizing that a plaintiff must point out the deficiency; once the plaintiff failed to show how the categories overlapped, the argument was deemed forfeited.

- **Subsequent cases** (e.g., *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008); *Mead Data Center, Inc. v. Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)) treat the “sufficiently distinct” requirement as a gate‑keeping step before the agency can rely on Exemption 7(A).  If the categories pass the distinctness test, the agency must still demonstrate that each category’s records are either wholly exempt or not reasonably segregable.

**Take‑away for practitioners**

When drafting a categorical withholding notice:

1. **Define each category in plain, concrete terms** (e.g., “electronic communications between FBI field agents and informants concerning the subject’s alleged recruitment activities between 2018‑2022”).

2. **Explain why the category does not overlap** with any other category listed in the notice (perhaps by providing a brief table mapping document types to categories).

3. **Show how the category satisfies the segregation requirement**, either by evidencing that the documents are wholly exempt or by describing why any non‑exempt portions are intermingled and cannot be separated.

If the agency fails to meet these criteria, a court will deem the categorical withholding improper, as the *Farahi* opinion notes that the FBI’s categories were not “overbroad” or “insufficiently distinct” because the court found them adequately defined and distinct, relying on *CREW* and *Khine* for the legal standard.

### What is the burden of proof for the government in FOIA summary‑judgment motions involving Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The opinion mentions that the government bears the burden of showing both the law‑enforcement purpose and the interference with enforcement proceedings. In a summary‑judgment context, what evidentiary standards must the agency meet (e.g., preponderance, clear and convincing), and how does the D.C. Circuit assess the adequacy of agency declarations versus external evidence?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a FOIA summary‑judgment motion that relies on Exemption 7(A) the government carries *the entire burden* of proving that the exemption applies.  28 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B) makes clear that the agency must show **both** (1) that the withheld records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and (2) that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are **pending or reasonably anticipated**.  § 552(b)(7)(A).

### Evidentiary standard

The D.C. Circuit treats that burden the same way it treats any factual burden in a summary‑judgment context – the agency must satisfy the **pre‑ponderance of the evidence** standard.  The court does not require “clear‑and‑convincing” proof; it only requires that the agency’s evidence make its factual assertions more likely than not to be true.  See *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021) (de novo review of summary‑judgment motions, government bears burden of proof).

The opinion in **Farahi v. FBI** follows that approach.  Judge Katsas writes that “the government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies” and that “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference” (¶ III).  The deference is not a higher evidentiary standard; it merely means the court will accept the agency’s factual findings if they are supported by **reasonable specificity** in the record.

### How the D.C. Circuit evaluates agency declarations

1. **Affidavits are sufficient if they contain “reasonable specificity.”**  
   The court may rely on government affidavits that “show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated” (*Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d at 61).  In *Farahi* the FBI’s Hardy declaration identified the investigation, named the law‑enforcement purpose, and explained why disclosure would tip off the targets.  The ex‑parte Hardy declaration supplied additional detail.  The court held that together these declarations satisfied the burden.

2. **Updates to prior declarations are permissible.**  
   When the district court previously found the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden, the later Bender declaration was treated as an *update* confirming that nothing had changed, not as a fresh showing of pendency.  The opinion emphasizes that the Bender declaration “can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document” (¶ IV‑B).  Thus the court does not demand new independent evidence each time the agency moves for summary judgment; a contemporaneous reaffirmation is enough so long as the original factual basis remains intact.

3. **No external evidence is required if the affidavit is adequate.**  
   The opinion notes that “the government may show [pending proceedings] simply by attesting to their existence” (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1099).  The district court accepted the FBI’s sworn statements without demanding production of underlying investigative files, because the affidavits gave a “reasonable nexus” between the investigation and a law‑enforcement purpose and explained why disclosure would interfere with the investigation.

### Summary

- **Burden:** The agency must prove every element of Exemption 7(A).  
- **Standard:** Pre‑ponderance of the evidence (the facts are more likely than not).  
- **Evidence:** Detailed, sworn declarations are typically sufficient; the court looks for “reasonable specificity” rather than a requirement for independent, extrinsic proof.  Updates to earlier affidavits are acceptable so long as they confirm that the factual situation has not changed.

Consequently, in summary‑judgment motions the government does not have to produce the underlying investigative files; a well‑crafted affidavit—supported by the record and not merely conclusory—meets the D.C. Circuit’s evidentiary requirement under Exemption 7(A).

### How does the D.C. Circuit’s de novo review of FOIA summary‑judgment decisions affect the appellate analysis?
Question:
The opinion states that FOIA summary‑judgment grants are reviewed de novo. I’m trying to understand what this means in practice. Does the appellate court disregard the district court’s factual findings entirely, or does it give any deference to the lower court’s assessment of the agency’s declarations? How does de novo review interact with the agency’s statutory burden under § 552(a)(4)(B)?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the D.C. Circuit, a grant of summary judgment in a FOIA case is reviewed **de novo** – that is, the appellate court looks at the record anew and makes its own determination whether the agency has satisfied the statutory burden that a FOIA exemption applies.  See *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021) (citing the opinion you provided, § III).

### What “de novo” means here

1. **No deference to the district court’s factual findings.**  
   The appellate court does **not** simply accept the district court’s factual conclusions about whether, for example, the FBI’s investigation is “pending or reasonably anticipated.”  Instead, it evaluates the same evidence – the agency’s declarations and any other record in the docket – and decides for itself whether the statutory elements of Exemption 7(A) are met.  The opinion states that “we review a grant of summary judgment **de novo**” and then proceeds to apply the burden‑shifting framework to the FBI’s declarations rather than to the district court’s summary.

2. **Limited deference to the agency’s own assertions.**  
   While the court conducts a fresh analysis, it still accords the agency a **fair measure of deference** on matters that are “entitled to deference” because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency.  Cf. *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998).  This deference is not a blanket grant of judgment‑proof authority; it simply means the court will accept the FBI’s **nexus** between the records and a law‑enforcement function when the agency “explains a rational connection” (see § III‑A).  The court therefore relies heavily on the Hardy and Bender declarations, but it still checks that those declarations provide the requisite specificity.

3. **Interaction with the agency’s § 552(a)(4)(B) burden.**  
   Under § 552(a)(4)(B) the government bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies.  The appellate court, in its de novo review, asks whether the agency‑provided evidence satisfies **both** statutory prongs of Exemption 7(A):

   * **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes** – the court looks at the Hardy declaration (public and ex‑parte) to see that the FBI identified a “law‑enforcement” nexus.  The opinion says the district court’s earlier “seemingly” finding is supplanted by the appellate court’s own assessment that the FBI “carried its burden” because the declarations establish that nexus.  

   * **Disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings** – the appellate court again examines the same declarations (Hardy’s detailed ex‑parte statement and Bender’s update) to determine whether they show a **concrete, prospective law‑enforcement proceeding**.  The opinion notes that an agency may establish pendency merely by “attesting to their existence” (citing *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d at 59).  Accordingly, the court does not rely on the district court’s “seemingly” language but makes its own conclusion that the FBI’s statements satisfy this element.

### Practical effect

- **The district court’s factual conclusions are not binding.**  The appellate court re‑evaluates the factual record (the agency’s declarations) and may reach a different result if the declarations are insufficient.  In *Farahi*, the appellate court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment **because**, after its own de novo review, it found the agency’s declarations met the statutory burden—not because it accepted the lower court’s summary of the facts.

- **Agency burden remains unchanged.**  The government must still prove the two elements of Exemption 7(A) **on the record before the appellate court**.  De novo review does not lift or shift that burden; it merely changes who makes the ultimate factual determination.

- **Deference is limited to the agency’s expertise.**  The court may give “fair measure of deference” to the FBI’s expertise in law‑enforcement matters, but that deference is applied **within** the de novo analysis; the court still scrutinizes the adequacy of the agency’s affidavits and may reject them if they are overly conclusory.

### Bottom line

In the D.C. Circuit, a FOIA summary‑judgment decision is reviewed *de novo*: the appellate court ignores the district court’s factual findings and decides afresh whether the agency has satisfied the statutory burden under § 552(a)(4)(B) and the elements of the invoked exemption.  The agency’s own declarations receive a modest, expertise‑based deference, but the court’s ultimate judgment rests on its independent assessment of those declarations.  Consequently, the appellate analysis is a fresh, independent evaluation of the agency’s evidence, not a rubber‑stamp of the lower court’s conclusions.
