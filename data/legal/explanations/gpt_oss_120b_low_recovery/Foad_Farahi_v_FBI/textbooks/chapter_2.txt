Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: The FOIA Statutory Scheme and Exemption 7(A)

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) balances two competing interests: the public’s right to know what the federal government is doing and the government’s need to withhold certain information.  Understanding this balance is essential for reading any FOIA opinion, including *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*.  This chapter walks you through the statutory framework that underlies the Court’s analysis and then concentrates on Exemption 7(A)—the provision the Court relied on to uphold the FBI’s withholdings.

## 2.1 FOIA’s “right to know” provision and statutory text (552(a)(3), (b))

FOIA’s core command is found at **28 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3)**, which states that “any person” may *“inspect and copy any agency record … unless the record (or any portion thereof) is ... specifically exempted from disclosure by law.”*  The statute therefore creates a **presumption of openness**: agencies must disclose all records that are responsive to a request, and the burden is on the agency to show that an exemption applies.

The definition of “record” is set out in **§ 552(b)**, which includes any *“any item, collection, or grouping of information, regardless of physical form or characteristics, that is either created or obtained by an agency and that is under agency control.”*  This broad definition captures paper documents, electronic files, and even audio or video recordings—precisely the types of material the FBI located in response to Farahi’s request (over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs).

Because the statute is written in a “right‑to‑know” language, any exception to disclosure must be expressly enumerated.  The Court in *Farahi* therefore began its analysis by confirming that the FBI’s withholdings could not be justified by any ground other than a statutory exemption.

## 2.2 Enumerated exemptions and the agency’s burden to invoke them (552(a)(4)(B))

FOIA lists nine categorical exemptions in **§ 552(b)**, each addressing a distinct class of sensitive information (e.g., national security, internal personnel rules, trade secrets).  The **agency’s burden** for every exemption is set out in **§ 552(a)(4)(B)**: the agency *“shall bear the burden of proving that the record is exempt.”*  This is a **strict burden of proof**—the agency must present competent evidence, often in the form of sworn declarations, that satisfies the statutory elements of the exemption it invokes.

In *Farahi*, the FBI invoked **Exemption 7(A)**.  The district court therefore required the FBI to produce declarations that established (1) the records were *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* and (2) disclosure *could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings* that were *pending or reasonably anticipated*.  The Court’s ultimate affirmation of the district court’s grant of summary judgment underscores that the agency met its burden.

## 2.3 Text of Exemption 7(A) – compiled for law‑enforcement purposes & interference with proceedings

Exemption 7(A) reads:

> “Records or portions of records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes … if the release of such records *could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings*.”  28 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).

Two elements must be satisfied:

1. **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.**  The record must have been created *“in furtherance of a law‑enforcement investigation or prosecution.”*  The Supreme Court has emphasized a “nexus” between the record and the agency’s law‑enforcement function.  In *Farahi*, the FBI’s declarations described the file as assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and linked the material to ongoing investigations of Farahi’s alleged terrorist affiliations.  The Court accepted that nexus, noting that the FBI’s status as a law‑enforcement agency grants its determinations a “fair measure of deference.”

2. **Reasonable expectation of interference with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.**  The statute does not require the agency to prove that disclosure *will* interfere, only that it *could* reasonably be expected to do so.  The Court in *Farahi* found the FBI’s explanation—that releasing the material would tip off targets and jeopardize an ongoing terrorism investigation—sufficient to satisfy this element.

## 2.4 Judicial interpretation of “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings (CREW precedent)

The phrase *“pending or reasonably anticipated”* has been clarified by precedent.  In **Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Government v. DOJ**, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (the *CREW* decision), the Court held that for Exemption 7(A) to apply, the agency must show that the underlying enforcement action is either already under way (*pending*) or that the agency has a *reasonable basis* to anticipate that a proceeding will occur in the future.  The *CREW* opinion emphasized that a bare assertion that “investigations are ongoing” may be insufficient; the agency must provide *some specificity* about the nature of the anticipated proceeding.

In *Farahi* the district court initially flagged the FBI’s Hardy declaration as “seemingly” satisfying the *pending or reasonably anticipated* requirement but ordered an update because of the passage of time.  The FBI responded with fresh declarations from Bender, confirming that the investigation remained active as of March 2023.  The appellate Court accepted this update, reasoning that the new declaration functioned as an *update* to the earlier, more detailed Hardy statements rather than a stand‑alone justification.  The Court therefore reiterated that an agency may satisfy the *pending or anticipated* prong by *updating* an earlier sufficient showing, provided the update does not merely repeat a conclusory assertion.

## 2.5 Relationship of Exemption 7(A) to national‑security investigations

Exemption 7(A) is distinct from the national‑security exemptions (e.g., Exemptions 1 and 3), but courts have consistently accorded **heightened deference** to law‑enforcement agencies when the records pertain to national‑security or counter‑terrorism investigations.  The D.C. Circuit has stated that “the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national‑security concerns are implicated… is especially strong” (see *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927‑28 (D.C. Cir. 2003)).  This deference does not waive the agency’s burden; rather, it means that the court will accept the agency’s sworn statements as credible unless there is a reason to doubt them.

In *Farahi*, the Court noted the FBI’s specialized expertise in terrorism investigations and the sensitivity of the alleged connections to foreign terrorist organizations.  Accordingly, the Court gave “fair measure of deference” to the FBI’s determinations that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure would jeopardize the investigation.

## 2.6 The “reasonable steps” segregation requirement (552(a)(8)(A)(ii))

Even when an exemption applies to an entire document, FOIA obligates agencies to make **reasonable efforts** to separate (or “segregate”) any non‑exempt portions and release them.  The statute says that an agency must *“take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information”* and that *“any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided … after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”*  28 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii).

The segregation analysis proceeds in two stages:

1. **Identify the exempt and non‑exempt material.**  The agency must provide a clear categorical or line‑by‑line explanation of why particular portions fall under the exemption.  In *Farahi*, the district court required the FBI to explain its categorical withholding scheme, but Farahi failed to demonstrate that the categories were overly broad, leading the Court to deem the FBI’s categorization satisfactory.

2. **Assess reasonableness of segregation.**  The agency must show that the non‑exempt material is **reasonably segregable**—i.e., it can be isolated without rendering the exempt material “inextricably intertwined.”  The D.C. Circuit has articulated that a “tiny amount of non‑exempt text interspersed line‑by‑line” does not satisfy the segregation requirement (see *Mead Data Center, Inc. v. Dep’t of the Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)).  In *Farahi*, the district court concluded, based on the FBI’s detailed ex parte declaration, that the exempt material was not reasonably segregable because the non‑exempt portions were scattered throughout the massive files and could not be released without disclosing the core investigative content.

The appellate Court affirmed this finding, emphasizing that the FOIA’s purpose is to disclose *information*, not merely isolated words that would compromise the integrity of a law‑enforcement investigation.

---

**Bottom line for students:** The *Farahi* opinion illustrates the practical application of FOIA’s statutory scheme.  The agency bears the burden of proving each exemption element; the Court gives deference to the agency’s expertise, especially in national‑security contexts; and even when an exemption applies, agencies must still attempt to segregate any releasable material.  Mastery of these concepts will enable you to follow the Court’s reasoning in any FOIA case.
