Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Segregability — When Non‑Exempt Information Must Be Released  

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* the D.C. Circuit was asked to decide whether the FBI had satisfied its statutory duty to “segregate and release” any non‑exempt material that lay hidden in a massive file of roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs.  The court’s discussion provides a clear illustration of how segregation operates in practice, what the law requires of an agency, and how a court evaluates the agency’s justification.  This chapter walks you through those points, grounding each rule in the opinion and showing how the judges applied them.

## 4.1 Statutory segregation requirement and its policy rationale  

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) imposes a two‑step duty on agencies once they determine that a record contains exempt material.  First, the agency must identify the exemption that applies.  Second, **U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)** commands the agency to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release any reasonably segregable portion of a record” that is *not* covered by the exemption.  The statutory language mirrors the FOIA’s overall policy: the public’s right of access is paramount, but it must be balanced against legitimate government interests in secrecy.  The segregation requirement is the mechanism that preserves that balance.  Instead of a blanket denial, the agency must carve out the non‑exempt “information” and give it to the requester, thereby ensuring that the public does not lose more than is necessary to protect the interest protected by the exemption.

The *Farahi* opinion repeatedly cites § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and the parallel provision in § 552(b) that “any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided… after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”  The court treats the duty as non‑negotiable: if the agency cannot show that the non‑exempt material is reasonably separable, it must withhold the entire record under the applicable exemption—in this case Exemption 7(A).

## 4.2 What constitutes a “reasonably segregable” portion  

The D.C. Circuit has fashioned a practical definition of “reasonably segregable” through a line of cases that the *Farahi* court relies upon.  Two seminal decisions are instructive:

* **Mead Data Central, Inc. v. Department of the Air Force**, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977).  The court held that when exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with non‑exempt material—so that the two are interleaved line‑by‑line—segregation is not reasonable.  The focus is on *information* rather than the mere presence of any non‑exempt words.  The court said, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down” (Mead at 261 n.55).  The implication is that the agency must be able to excise whole sections, pages, or documents that contain only exempt material; isolated sentences or phrases embedded in a larger exempt narrative do not satisfy the requirement.

* **Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. Department of Veterans Affairs**, 57 F.4th 1061 (D.C. Cir. 2023).  This later case reaffirmed the Mead standard and clarified that “reasonable segregation” is judged on a *case‑by‑case* basis, looking at the specific record structure.  The court emphasized that an agency’s claim that a few words can be redacted is insufficient; there must be a “meaningful” portion of the record that can be released without compromising the exempt interest.

In *Farahi*, the district court applied these principles when it concluded that the FBI’s justification—found in Bender’s supplemental ex‑parte filing—demonstrated that exempt material could not be reasonably separated from non‑exempt material.  The court noted that the “protected records were not reasonably segregable” because the exempt content was “interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document,” echoing Mead and Perioperative.

## 4.3 Agency‑level category‑by‑category analysis  

Before the court evaluates whether the material can be segregated, the agency must “identify distinct categories” of records and explain how each category is treated.  The requirement comes from **Citizens for Responsible & Ethics in Washington v. Department of Justice**, 746 F.3d at 1088 (the “CREW” decision).  CREW held that categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each.

The purpose of the rule is two‑fold:

1. **Transparency to the court** – By breaking the file into discrete, labeled groups (e.g., “interrogation transcripts,” “electronic surveillance logs,” “intelligence assessments”), the agency gives the judge a roadmap for evaluating each exemption claim.

2. **Avoiding over‑broad withholding** – If the agency lumps together disparate documents under a single, amorphous category, it can hide non‑exempt material behind the exemption claim.

In *Farahi*, the FBI’s initial FOIA response used categories, but Farahi challenged the adequacy of those categorizations.  The appellate court found that Farahi “forfeited his arguments” because he offered no specific basis to prove the categories were overbroad (citing *Khine v. Department of Homeland Security*, 943 F.3d at 967).  Consequently, the court accepted the FBI’s categorical breakdown as sufficient for the segregation analysis.

## 4.4 The district court’s assessment of the FBI’s segregation justification via Bender’s ex‑parte filing  

When the district court revisited the summary‑judgment motion, it faced two unanswered questions: (1) whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated, and (2) whether any non‑exempt material could be disclosed.  The FBI responded with a *public* declaration from Joseph Bender (Acting Section Chief, RIDS) and a *sealed* ex‑parte declaration that elaborated on the factual basis for both points.

**Key elements of the court’s assessment:**

1. **Reliance on the updated declaration** – The court accepted Bender’s statements that the investigations “remain ongoing” as an *update* to the earlier Hardy declarations, not as the sole foundation for the pending‑proceedings analysis.  Because the district court had already found the Hardy declarations “seemingly” sufficient, Bender’s brief comment was deemed adequate to show that nothing had changed.

2. **Segregability analysis** – The district court turned to Bender’s ex‑parte filing for a “detailed justification” of why the exempt portions could not be separated.  Although the public opinion does not reproduce the sealed content, the court’s order references that the filing explained the *intertwined* nature of the records.  The court found this explanation met the “reasonable specificity” standard required for an agency affidavit (see *Juarez v. Department of Justice*, 518 F.3d at 61).

3. **Application of Mead/Perioperative** – By concluding that the exempt material was “interspersed line‑by‑line,” the court directly applied the two‑step test: (i) identify the exempt content, (ii) determine whether that content can be isolated without destroying the informational value of the remaining material.  The court determined the answer was negative, and therefore the FBI satisfied its statutory duty.

The appellate court affirmed this reasoning, emphasizing that the district court had correctly applied the segregation standard and that the FBI’s filings contained the requisite level of detail.

## 4.5 Limits of segregation when exempt material is interspersed line‑by‑line  

The “line‑by‑line” limitation is not a technical requirement that the exemption be found on a single line of text; rather, it is a conceptual shortcut for asking whether the non‑exempt information can be **meaningfully** extracted.  Courts look for:

* **Structural separability** – Are there whole pages, sections, or documents that contain only non‑exempt facts?  If yes, the agency must release those.
* **Functional separability** – Even if a document mixes exempt and non‑exempt material, can the agency redact the exempt paragraphs while preserving the rest?  If the redactions would produce a document that is “gibberish” or devoid of context, segregation is unreasonable.
* **Practical feasibility** – The agency must consider the burden of segregation.  A request that would require piecemeal redaction of thousands of pages, each with a handful of exempt sentences, is unlikely to meet the “reasonable steps” standard.

In *Farahi*, the district court’s finding that the FBI’s records were “inextricably intertwined” indicates that the agency could not point to any discrete, self‑contained portion that would convey useful information once the exempt material was removed.  The court’s reliance on the sealed Bender declaration suggests that the FBI detailed the technical impossibility of extracting meaningful non‑exempt content—perhaps because the records consist of investigative notebooks, analyst memos, and raw intelligence that are all interdependent.

## 4.6 Consequences of failing to segregate: full withholding under Exemption 7(A)  

When an agency cannot demonstrate reasonable segregation, the FOIA mandates that the entire record be withheld under the applicable exemption.  The statutory text is explicit: “Any reasonably segregable portion… shall be provided… after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”  If nothing is “reasonably segregable,” the deletion step does not produce any material to turn over, and the agency may lawfully retain the whole record.

The *Farahi* court’s judgment illustrates the practical impact:

1. **No partial disclosure** – Even though the FBI’s file spanned 10,750 pages, the court concluded that no “meaningful information” could be released.  Consequently, Farahi received none of the requested documents.

2. **Reaffirmation of exemption scope** – By allowing the FBI to withhold the entire file, the court reinforced the principle that Exemption 7(A) does not merely protect the *existence* of an investigation but also protects any *information* that, if disclosed, would jeopardize that investigation.  The segregation requirement cannot be used to “carve out” non‑exempt trivia if the overall document is so entangled that the exempt core would be compromised by any release.

3. **Judicial deference to agency expertise** – The appellate court highlighted that the FBI’s expertise and the nature of national‑security investigations merit “deference” (citing *Campbell v. Department of Justice*).  This deference is especially salient when the agency must assess the practical feasibility of segregation.

4. **Strategic implication for FOIA litigants** – Plaintiffs must be prepared to challenge the agency’s claim of inseparability.  That means requesting the agency produce detailed explanations—ideally with redacted exemplars—that show precisely why each purportedly non‑exempt portion cannot be isolated.  Without such specificity, the court may find the agency’s assertion insufficient and order partial release.

In sum, the *Farahi* decision demonstrates that the segregation requirement is a substantive hurdle for agencies: they must not only invoke an exemption but also prove that the remaining material can be meaningfully disclosed.  When the record is so interwoven that only a thin layer of exempt text separates the rest, the law permits the agency to withhold the whole file, and the court will uphold that decision so long as the agency’s affidavit meets the “reasonable specificity” standard articulated in *Juarez* and *Mead*.  

---  

**Take‑away points for you, the law student:**  

* Segregation is a *mandatory* step, not a discretion left to the agency.  
* “Reasonably segregable” requires more than a cursory redaction; the non‑exempt portion must retain informational value on its own.  
* Agencies must break the request into distinct categories and explain, category by category, how each is treated.  
* Courts will scrutinize the agency’s affidavit (public and ex‑parte) for sufficient detail; a terse statement is rarely enough unless it builds on earlier, more detailed declarations.  
* If the record fails the segregation test, the entire document may be lawfully withheld under the applicable exemption, as the *Farahi* court affirmed.  

Understanding these principles will equip you to evaluate future FOIA segregation disputes and to craft persuasive arguments—whether you are representing a requesting party or defending an agency’s position.
