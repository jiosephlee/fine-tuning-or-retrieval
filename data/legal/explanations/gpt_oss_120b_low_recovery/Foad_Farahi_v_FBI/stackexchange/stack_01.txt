Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What constitutes a “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding under FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly references the requirement that disclosure could interfere with "pending or reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceedings. I’m unclear on how courts determine whether a proceeding is merely "anticipated" versus actually pending, especially when the investigation is ongoing for years. Does the agency need to provide concrete evidence of a future case, or is an attestation of an "ongoing investigation" sufficient? How have the D.C. Circuit’s cases like *CREW* and *Juarez* interpreted this standard?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under FOIA Exemption 7(A) a record may be withheld only if the agency shows that (1) the record was *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* and (2) its disclosure “*could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings*.”  55 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).  The Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit have interpreted the second prong to require that the “enforcement proceedings” be *pending* or *reasonably anticipated*.

### 1. What “pending or reasonably anticipated” means

The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly held that a “pending” proceeding is any law‑enforcement action that has already been initiated and is still open (e.g., a criminal probe, grand‑jury investigation, or administrative case).  *Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Washington v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (quoting *Mapother v. DOJ*, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).  

A “reasonably anticipated” proceeding, by contrast, is a future enforcement action that is not yet formally opened but is sufficiently concrete that the government can plausibly expect it to occur.  The circuit explained that an agency may establish such anticipation merely by *attesting* that an investigation is ongoing and that it is being conducted “in furtherance of a possible criminal case.”  *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1099; see also *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59 (D.C. Cir. 2008).

Thus, the standard is *not* a requirement that the agency produce a charging document, indictment, or other concrete proof that a case will be filed.  The agency must show, with reasonable specificity, that:

1. the investigation is active (i.e., facts are still being gathered or analyzed); and  
2. the investigation is aimed at a potential law‑enforcement outcome (e.g., a criminal prosecution, civil suit, or immigration removal proceeding).

If those two elements are satisfied, the proceeding is deemed “reasonably anticipated” for exemption‑7(A) purposes.

### 2. How the Farahi opinion applies the standard

The Farahi opinion relies directly on *CREW* and *Juarez* for its analysis:

* **Pending or anticipated proceedings** – The court notes that “ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’ for these purposes” and that “the government may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their ‘existence.’” *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1099.  Accordingly, the FBI’s declaration that the investigation “remains ongoing” satisfies the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement.

* **Attestation is sufficient** – The opinion expressly rejects Farahi’s argument that a more detailed, forward‑looking forecast is needed.  It states that the agency’s “public Hardy declaration explained that the relevant investigation remained ‘ongoing,’” and the accompanying ex‑parte declaration supplied “considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation.”  This together meets the “reasonable specificity” requirement articulated in *Juarez*.

* **Length of the investigation is irrelevant** – Although the investigation has spanned many years, the court emphasizes that the passage of time does not defeat the presumption that a proceeding is still pending or anticipated so long as the agency can show the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future case.  *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59.

* **No need for a “concrete prospective proceeding”** – The opinion quotes *CREW* that a “vague nature” of an assertion may be insufficient, but finds the FBI’s combined declarations (public and ex‑parte) to provide enough specificity to overcome that concern.  The court therefore treats the FBI’s attestation that “investigations remained ongoing” as a valid basis for invoking exemption 7(A).

### 3. Practical take‑away for FOIA litigants

1. **Agency burden** – The agency must produce a declaration that ties the records to a law‑enforcement investigation and explains why disclosure would jeopardize that investigation.  The declaration need not contain a charging document; a reasoned statement that the investigation is active and aimed at a possible prosecution is sufficient.

2. **Court’s review** – The court conducts a de novo review of the agency’s exemption claim but affords the agency “deference” because of its expertise in law‑enforcement matters.  As long as the agency’s declaration is not merely conclusory and provides at least a minimal factual backdrop, the court is likely to find the “pending or reasonably anticipated” prong satisfied.

3. **Effect of time** – Even a long‑standing investigation can satisfy the standard if the agency can demonstrate that evidence is still being collected or that the investigation has not been closed.  The passage of years does not by itself defeat the exemption.

### 4. Conclusion

In *Farahi v. FBI* the D.C. Circuit reaffirmed the *CREW* and *Juarez* line of cases: an agency may rely on exemption 7(A) when it can reasonably show that the records were compiled for an investigation that is *either* (a) already underway (pending) or (b) being pursued with the expectation that it will lead to a future enforcement action (reasonably anticipated).  An attestation—supported by a declaration that the investigation “remains ongoing” and that it “continues to gather evidence for a possible criminal case”—is sufficient; the agency does not need to produce a concrete charging document or other proof of a future case.  Consequently, the Farahi court upheld the FBI’s use of exemption 7(A) on the basis that the investigation into Farahi was still ongoing and therefore “reasonably anticipated” at the time of the FOIA request.
