Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### When can an agency rely on ex parte declarations in FOIA cases, and what procedural safeguards exist for the requester?
Question:
The court accepted ex parte declarations from FBI officials to support both the exemption and segregability analyses. I’m confused about the procedural rights of a FOIA plaintiff when critical evidence is submitted ex parte. Are there any requirements for the agency to disclose the content of those declarations to the requester, or to provide a summary? How does the D.C. Circuit balance the need for secrecy with due process for the requester?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In *Foad Farahi v. FBI* the D.C. Circuit held that an agency may rely on ex‑parte (sealed) declarations to prove both the existence of an Exemption 7(A) “interference” ground and the lack of reasonable segregability, **provided** (1) the agency has already satisfied its ordinary FOIA burden by giving the requester a *public* statement that the records are exempt and that the relevant enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and (2) the court finds that the sealed declaration contains “reasonable specificity” about why the exemption applies. The requester, however, is still entitled to the procedural protections that FOIA requires: the agency must (i) disclose the *content* of any public declaration, (ii) give a *summary* of the material in any sealed declaration that is relied upon, and (iii) allow the requester an opportunity to object to the use of the sealed declaration. The D.C. Circuit balances secrecy and due‑process by limiting the court’s reliance on the sealed declaration to the narrow issue for which it was submitted and by requiring the agency to demonstrate that the sealed material is essential and cannot be disclosed without defeating the exemption.

---

### How the opinion frames the use of ex‑parte declarations

1. **Burden of proof stays on the agency** – Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B) the government must “show” that an exemption applies. The court may accept *government affidavits* that “show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated” (*Juarez* 518 F.3d at 61). The *Farahi* court therefore allowed the FBI’s sealed (ex‑parte) declarations to satisfy that epistemic burden.

2. **Public declaration required** – Before the court will rely on a sealed affidavit, it must have a *public* declaration that puts the requester on notice of the agency’s position. In *Farahi* the FBI first filed a public declaration from Section Chief David Hardy stating that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that “the relevant investigations remain ongoing.” The court treated that public statement as the *notice* required by FOIA.

3. **Ex‑parte declaration as an update** – The later sealed declaration (from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender) was not used to *prove* the existence of pending proceedings *de novo*; it was offered only to confirm that the factual circumstances underlying the earlier public declaration had not changed. The opinion emphasizes that the court “could not disclose the contents of Hardy’s ex‑parte declaration” but that the *public* Hardy declaration already satisfied the notice requirement. The Bender declaration therefore served a *narrow* evidentiary function—updating the record without revealing the underlying sensitive details.

4. **Specificity requirement** – Even though the declaration is sealed, the agency must still demonstrate “reasonable specificity” as to why the exemption applies. The court found that the combination of the public Hardy declaration (which set out the investigative scope) and the sealed Hardy declaration (which gave “considerable detail”) together met that standard. The Bender declaration merely confirmed that nothing had changed.

### Procedural safeguards for the requester

The opinion identifies three procedural protections that survive even when the government relies on sealed material:

| Requirement | How *Farahi* enforces it |
|-------------|--------------------------|
| **1. Disclosure of the public declaration** | The FBI’s public Hardy declaration is fully on the record; the requester can read it and assess whether the agency’s exemption claim is plausible. |
| **2. Summarized content of sealed declarations** | While the court may keep the full affidavit under seal, it must provide the requester (and the public record) with at least a *summary* that explains the relevance of the sealed material. The opinion notes that the district court “explained that the FBI had provided a ‘detailed justification’ for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable in Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission.” This description satisfies the summary‑requirement. |
| **3. Opportunity to object** | The plaintiff may file objections to the use of sealed declarations. In *Farahi* the plaintiff “did not oppose the motion” to file the Hardy declaration ex parte, so the court treated the lack of objection as a waiver of any due‑process challenge. If the requester had filed a timely objection, the court would have been required to consider it before accepting the sealed affidavit. |

### Balancing secrecy and due process

The D.C. Circuit’s approach can be summarized as follows:

1. **Deference to the agency’s expertise** – Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its “decision to invoke exemption 7(A) is entitled to deference” (*Campbell* 164 F.3d at 32). The court therefore does not require the agency to disclose the underlying classified details so long as the public declaration puts the requester on notice.

2. **Limited reliance on sealed material** – The court may rely on an ex‑parte declaration only for the *specific* factual point that cannot be disclosed without defeating the exemption (e.g., that an investigation is still ongoing). The sealed affidavit is not used to *re‑prove* the entire exemption analysis.

3. **Mandatory summary** – Even when the full affidavit is sealed, the court must give a concrete summary that identifies the factual basis for the exemption. This satisfies the FOIA procedural requirement that “the agency must disclose any reasonably segregable portion” and that the requester is entitled to know *why* material is being withheld.

4. **Waiver of objection** – If the requester fails to object to the filing of a sealed declaration, the court treats that silence as a waiver of any due‑process claim concerning the affidavit’s secrecy. The *Farahi* decision underscores that “nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration” because the plaintiff did not oppose it.

### Practical take‑aways for FOIA litigants

* **Ask for the public declaration** – The agency must place its exemption rationale on the public docket. If it does not, you can move to compel it.
* **Demand a summary of any sealed affidavit** – Even when the government wants to keep an affidavit under seal, the court must give you a factual summary that allows meaningful review.
* **File timely objections** – If you believe a sealed declaration is being used improperly, object to its filing under the FOIA’s procedural rules. Failure to do so may be deemed a waiver.
* **Expect limited disclosure** – The court may accept sealed material when the public declaration alone is insufficient to demonstrate “reasonable specificity.” The burden is on the agency to show that the sealed material is *essential* to the exemption analysis.

In short, *Farahi* confirms that ex‑parte declarations are permissible, but only when the agency also provides a public statement and a court‑approved summary, and only if the requester has had an opportunity to contest their use. The D.C. Circuit therefore preserves the requester’s procedural rights while allowing the agency to protect genuinely sensitive law‑enforcement information.
