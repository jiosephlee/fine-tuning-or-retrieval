## Exemption 7(A) in the Age of Counter‑terrorism: Why the D.C. Circuit Still Defers to the FBI  

*By [Your Name],* *July 9 2026*  

### Why the case matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in **Foad Farahi v. FBI** (No. 23‑5243, Sept. 30 2025) is the latest in a line of decisions that grant the FBI broad latitude to invoke **FOIA Exemption 7(A)**—the “law‑enforcement” exemption—when the agency says disclosure would “interfere with enforcement proceedings.”  While the court’s reasoning is not novel, the decision is noteworthy for three reasons. First, it re‑affirms a bright‑line deference standard even when the agency’s justification rests on a brief, ex‑parte declaration. Second, it underscores the Court’s willingness to treat “ongoing investigations” as a catch‑all for “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings, even after several years of delay. Finally, the opinion illustrates how the statutory balance between transparency and national‑security secrecy continues to tilt toward the latter in the counter‑terrorism context, raising fresh questions about the practical limits of FOIA’s “public‑interest” override.  

In short, Farahi does not change the law, but it sharpens the blade of deference that the judiciary wields against the FBI in Exemption 7(A) cases. For practitioners, journalists, and scholars, the decision is a reminder that the battle for disclosure now hinges less on the merits of the underlying investigation and more on the procedural scaffolding—affidavits, categories, and segregability analyses—that agencies deploy to keep records under lock and key.  

### The court’s reasoning, step by step  

#### 1. Agency’s burden and the “law‑enforcement purpose” prong  

The D.C. Circuit reiterated that the burden rests squarely on the government to show that the withheld records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.”  U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).  Judge Katsas leaned on a well‑established line of precedent—*Campbell v. DOJ* (1998) and *Clemente v. FBI* (2017)—which grants the FBI a “fair measure of deference” simply because the agency is a law‑enforcement entity.  The court found the FBI’s initial Hardy declaration, which linked the records to an investigation of “national security and international terrorism,” to satisfy the required nexus.  

#### 2. The “interfere with enforcement proceedings” prong  

Exemption 7(A) also demands that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere” with **pending or reasonably anticipated** enforcement proceedings.  The D.C. Circuit relied heavily on *CREW v. DOJ* (2014) and *Juarez v. DOJ* (2008), which interpret “pending” to include investigations that are still gathering evidence for a prospective case.  The court accepted the FBI’s assertion—first in Hardy’s public declaration and then refreshed by Bender’s ex‑parte filing—that the investigation remained “ongoing” as of March 2023.  

Farahi’s argument that the passage of time rendered the FBI’s claim “conclusory” was rejected on two grounds. First, the court treated Bender’s brief update not as a stand‑alone showing of pendency but as a continuation of the substantive factual foundation already laid by Hardy. Second, the court emphasized the “breadth” of the investigation, citing the FBI’s public allegations that Farahi was connected to multiple designated terrorist organizations.  In the court’s view, such a wide‑ranging probe plausibly remains active for years, satisfying the “reasonably anticipated” standard.  

#### 3. Segregability and the “reasonable steps” requirement  

Even when an exemption applies, FOIA obliges agencies to “segregate and release” any non‑exempt material.  U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii).  The district court, and now the appellate court, concluded that the FBI’s second‑hand ex‑parte submission sufficiently explained why the exempt portions were “inextricably intertwined” with the non‑exempt.  The opinion invoked the classic *Mead* (1977) line that “the focus of FOIA is information, not simply words which the Government has written down.”  Because the allegedly releasable material was scattered line‑by‑line throughout massive files, the court found it “not reasonably segregable.”  

#### 4. Procedural deference to ex‑parte declarations  

Finally, the D.C. Circuit addressed Farahi’s objection to the reliance on ex‑parte affidavits.  The court noted that the plaintiff did not oppose the use of such declarations, and that “ex‑parte declarations… are the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.”  The decision therefore reaffirmed an already‑well‑settled principle: when a FOIA claim implicates national‑security investigations, courts may rely heavily on confidential, agency‑provided statements.  

### Our take: is the deference doctrine sustainable?  

The Farahi opinion is a textbook illustration of **judicial forbearance** in the face of agency expertise.  On the one hand, the Court’s approach is consistent with the statutory text of Exemption 7(A), which was crafted to protect exactly the kind of investigative secrecy the FBI alleges it needs.  On the other hand, the opinion raises two structural concerns.  

**First, the “ongoing investigation” shortcut.**  The courts have long treated the existence of an active probe as a black‑box justification for withholding.  Yet the very nature of an “ongoing” investigation is indeterminate; an agency can keep a case “open” on paper for years while in practice the investigative hunt has run cold.  By accepting a terse, ex‑parte affirmation that “nothing has changed,” the D.C. Circuit sidesteps the substantive inquiry into whether the underlying case truly remains “pending” in a meaningful sense.  Future litigants may find themselves stuck behind the same procedural barrier, without a realistic avenue to compel at least a minimal “status report” from the agency.  

**Second, the segregability hurdle.**  The *Mead* standard—requiring “reasonable” segregation—has been stretched to its limits.  In practice, the FBI can argue that any intermingling of exempt and non‑exempt text renders the entire file non‑segregable, regardless of how trivial the non‑exempt portions might be.  The Farahi court’s reliance on this argument, without demanding a more granular redaction plan, effectively grants the FBI a “whole‑file” shield.  This outcome is at odds with the FOIA’s purpose of maximizing disclosure, and it may embolden agencies to adopt a “dump‑and‑cover” strategy: produce massive volumes of documents with only generic categorical explanations, then claim inseparability.  

Both concerns feed into a broader policy question: **does the current deference regime preserve the statutory balance between transparency and secrecy, or has it tipped irreversibly toward secrecy?**  The answer may depend on how lower courts handle future challenges that focus on the *quality* of agency evidence rather than its mere existence.  If courts continue to accept “ex‑parte updates” at face value, the practical effect will be a chilling pre‑emptive bar on FOIA requests that touch on national‑security investigations.  

### Doctrinal implications and downstream effects  

1. **Reaffirmation of the “pending or reasonably anticipated” test** – The decision reiterates that a mere assertion of an “ongoing” investigation satisfies the pendency requirement, provided the agency offers a contemporaneous affidavit.  Plaintiffs will need to press for more concrete proof—such as docket numbers, charging documents, or evidence of active investigative steps—if they hope to overcome the exemption.  

2. **Strengthening of the “inextricably intertwined” segregation standard** – By accepting the FBI’s claim that the exempt material is woven throughout the record, the court provides a robust shield for large, heterogeneous files.  Agency counsel may be encouraged to produce even larger compilations, knowing that a “no‑segregable” argument is likely to succeed.  

3. **Potential “ex‑parte‑only” litigation track** – The opinion normalizes the practice of filing sealed, ex‑parte declarations to support FOIA withholding.  While statutory, this practice limits the ability of litigants to cross‑examine or test the veracity of the agency’s statements, thereby reducing the procedural safeguards FOIA was meant to guarantee.  

4. **Impact on immigration‑related FOIA suits** – Farahi’s background in removal and asylum proceedings, intertwined with a counter‑terrorism investigation, highlights a growing nexus between immigration enforcement and national‑security claims.  Future FOIA suits filed by immigration detainees may encounter the same deference, meaning that the public’s ability to scrutinize the intersection of immigration law and counter‑terrorism policy will remain severely constrained.  

### Looking ahead  

The Farahi ruling does not create new legal doctrine, but it codifies a comfortable status quo for the FBI when invoking Exemption 7(A).  For FOIA advocates, the challenge now is to **re‑frame the strategic battlefield**.  Rather than focus solely on the agency’s statements about “ongoing investigations,” plaintiffs may need to demand more precise, case‑specific metrics of pendency—such as recent investigative actions, subpoenas, or surveillance warrants—and to push agencies toward *functional* segregation (redacting exempt passages while releasing the rest).  

Congress could also step in.  Amendments clarifying the scope of “pending or reasonably anticipated” (perhaps by imposing a temporal limitation, e.g., three‑year “activity window”) would give courts a firmer footing to reject overbroad claims of ongoing investigations.  A statutory “segregability safe harbor” that obliges agencies to produce a redacted version of any file larger than a certain size could also blunt the “whole‑file” shield.  

Until such reforms materialize, the D.C. Circuit’s deference will continue to tilt the FOIA balance in favor of the executive, especially in the counter‑terrorism arena.  For now, the message to would‑be FOIA requesters is clear: **the FBI’s self‑assessment, bolstered by a few affidavits, is often enough to keep even the most massive files out of public view.**

## Pending or Anticipated? The Elusive Standard for “Interference” Under FOIA Exemption 7(A)

### Why This Case Matters

When a citizen asks the government for his own file, the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) promises a straightforward answer: either you get the records or the agency points to an exemption.  The *Farahi v. FBI* decision, handed down by the D.C. Circuit in September 2025, shows just how much leeway the courts give to law‑enforcement agencies when they invoke Exemption 7(A), the “interference with enforcement proceedings” carve‑out.  The opinion is a textbook illustration of how the “pending or reasonably anticipated” test—first articulated in *Mapother* and refined in *CREW*—can be stretched to protect a vast trove of material even after years of delay.  For litigants who hope to force agencies to disclose files once the “investigation appears to be over,” the ruling is a stark reminder that “ongoing” can be a moving target.

### The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell

The D.C. Circuit broke the case down into the three statutory elements of Exemption 7(A):

1. **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.**  
2. **Disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.**  
3. **Those proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.”**

The panel gave the FBI heavy deference on the first element, noting that the agency’s own declarations tied Farahi’s file to a national‑security investigation involving alleged ties to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Al Qaeda, and other terrorist actors.  Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, the court said, its determination that the records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” requires only a “rational nexus”—a low bar that the agency easily met.

The second and third elements are where the opinion dwells.  The court leaned on existing D.C. Circuit precedent that an agency need not produce a grand docket of criminal charges; a mere attestation that an investigation “remains ongoing” can satisfy the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement.  The panel accepted a brief, public declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender stating that the investigation “remains ongoing” as sufficient proof that the inquiry was still “pending” as of March 2023.  The earlier, more detailed (but sealed) declarations from Section Chief David Hardy were treated as the substantive foundation of the claim, with Bender’s note simply updating the status.

On the segregation question, the court reiterated that when exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with non‑exempt text, the agency may withhold the whole document.  The judges found the FBI’s explanations—largely derived from the sealed Hardy declaration—to be “reasonably specific” enough to satisfy the statutory burden.

### A Critical Look at the “Pending or Reasonably Anticipated” Test

The decision rests on a fairly amorphous standard that the D.C. Circuit has long treated as flexible.  In *CREW*, the court warned that a vague claim of “ongoing investigation” may not be enough, but here it seemed to quietly reverse that caution.  The panel’s analysis effectively says:

*If an agency can point to any internal memo or declaration that some investigative effort has not been “formally closed,” the FOIA exemption is triggered, even if the original investigative thrust dates back more than two decades.*

That reading raises two concerns.

First, **the risk of perpetual exemption.**  By allowing a single, unelaborated statement to stand for “pending” status, the court opens the door for agencies to keep files under the shield of Exemption 7(A) indefinitely.  The statute was designed to protect the *shape* of an investigation, not to give a permanent “black box” for any file whose subject has ever been of interest to law‑enforcement.  A more rigorous approach would demand a concrete indication of a specific, actionable proceeding—e.g., a pending indictment, a grand‑jury subpoena, or a target list with a defined investigative timeline.

Second, **the lack of a temporal constraint.**  The record in *Farahi* stretches back to 2002, when the subject first entered removal proceedings.  The court’s willingness to treat a 2023 declaration as proof that the “investigation remains ongoing” glosses over the fact that the original investigative leads may have long since fizzled.  The “reasonably anticipated” limb of the test is supposed to capture investigations that are still likely to result in prosecution, but the opinion provides no substantive analysis of whether the FBI’s current investigative posture meets that threshold.

In short, the decision leans heavily on *deference* to the executive, especially in national‑security contexts, but it does so at the cost of a meaningful, fact‑based inquiry into whether the exemption truly applies.

### Doctrinal Implications

#### 1. **Reinforcement of Agency Deference**

The opinion underscores that, at least in the D.C. Circuit, agency expertise in criminal and national‑security investigations carries a presumption of correctness.  The panel cites *Campbell* and *CNSS* to stress that “the government may withhold records in their entirety when non‑exempt portions are inextricably intertwined.”  Future FOIA plaintiffs will find it increasingly difficult to pry apart interwoven documents without a wrestling match over sealed declarations that the agency need not disclose.

#### 2. **Erosion of the “Segregability” Safeguard**

The ruling confirms that the agency’s burden on segregation is satisfied by “reasonable specificity” in an affidavit, even when that specificity is hidden behind an ex‑parte seal.  Courts have historically demanded that the agency show at least a line‑by‑line justification for why a particular paragraph is exempt.  Here, the D.C. Circuit appears satisfied with a narrative that the entire file is “intertwined” with the investigation.  This trend may lead to more blanket withholdings, especially in terrorism‑related cases where the government can invoke both national‑security and law‑enforcement exemptions.

#### 3. **The “Pending” Standard Becomes a Sliding Scale**

By treating a brief declaration that “nothing has changed” as proof of “pending” status, the decision effectively lowers the evidentiary bar for agencies.  The *CREW* caution about vague assertions is muted; the panel simply points to the earlier, more detailed Hardy declaration for context.  Absent a concrete showing that the FBI is actively gathering evidence for a specific case, the “pending” label could be applied to any historical investigation that never received a formal closure notice.

### What This Means for Future FOIA Litigants

A FOIA plaintiff hoping to argue that an investigation has “effectively ended” will now have to do more than point to the passage of time.  The burden will shift to producing internal agency documents—often sealed—that demonstrate a formal termination, a decision not to prosecute, or a clear shift in investigative focus.  The plaintiff’s best weapon may be a request for a “final determination” or “closing memo,” but agencies are under no statutory obligation to produce such a document, especially when citing Exemption 7(A).

Moreover, the decision signals that **ex parte affidavits** will continue to be a potent tool for the government.  Courts are comfortable accepting sealed statements as the factual substrate for exemption analyses, as long as the plaintiff does not object on the record.  Practically, this means that FOIA plaintiffs must be prepared to contest the *use* of ex parte material in the appellate record, or risk forfeiting the issue altogether.

### Potential Downstream Effects

* **Increased Litigation Over Segregability** – As agencies perfect the “inextricably intertwined” argument, plaintiffs may focus more on demanding line‑by‑line reviews and moving away from “category‑wide” withholdings.  Courts will have to balance the administrative burden on agencies against the FOIA’s transparency goals.

* **Calls for Legislative Clarification** – The “pending or reasonably anticipated” phrase has long been a source of ambiguity.  Lawmakers may propose amendments that require agencies to provide at least a high‑level description of the investigative stage (e.g., “pre‑indictment” vs. “post‑indictment”) when invoking Exemption 7(A).

* **Potential Split in the Circuits** – While the D.C. Circuit continues its tradition of strong deference, other appellate courts (e.g., the Ninth Circuit) have shown more willingness to demand concrete evidence of pending proceedings.  If a petition for certiorari is filed, the Supreme Court could be asked to resolve the tension between a flexible, deference‑heavy approach and a stricter, fact‑driven standard.

### Concluding Thoughts

*Farahi* is less about the specifics of one Iranian national’s file and more about the broader trajectory of FOIA jurisprudence in the national‑security arena.  By affirming the FBI’s blanket use of Exemption 7(A) based on a minimal update that an investigation “remains ongoing,” the D.C. Circuit reinforces a doctrine that can effectively lock away entire investigative archives indefinitely.  For advocates of transparency, the decision is a setback; for agencies protecting sensitive law‑enforcement techniques, it is a victory.

The real test will come when a plaintiff can produce a concrete agency document showing that an investigation has formally closed—perhaps a “termination of investigation” memo or a court dismissal.  Until then, the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard will remain an elastic, executive‑friendly shield, and FOIA requesters will have to brace for a long, opaque battle in the courts.



## Segregability After the Fact: When “Inextricably Intertwined” Becomes a Catch‑All  

*By [Your Name], Guest Contributor*  

### Why Farahi Matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s affirmation of the FBI’s FOIA withholding in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* is a vivid reminder that the “segregability” requirement—one of the most technically demanding hurdles for FOIA litigants—can be stretched to swallow an entire, massive dossier. Farahi’s 10,750‑page file, plus 80 CDs of “potentially responsive” material, was deemed wholly exempt under Exemption 7(A) because the court concluded that **no reasonably segregable portion existed**. That pronouncement does not merely resolve a single request; it signals how future plaintiffs may confront the practical reality that, when a file is alleged to be “inextricably intertwined” with exempt content, the government’s burden to prove non‑segregability can be met with relatively minimal factual support.  

For practitioners, journalists, and scholars who routinely pursue FOIA records tied to national‑security investigations, the Farahi decision crystallizes three intertwined issues: (1) the statutory and case‑law standards governing segregation; (2) the shifting evidentiary burden on agencies; and (3) the downstream consequences for transparency when the “catch‑all” language of *Mead* and *Juarez* is invoked in large, classified files.  

### The Legal Landscape of Segregability  

FOIA’s text obligates agencies, after invoking an exemption, to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The Supreme Court has never defined “reasonable segregation,” but the D.C. Circuit has built a framework that courts and litigants now rely upon.  

1. **The Agency’s Initial Burden** – Once an exemption is asserted, the agency must show two things: (a) the record was “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes,” and (b) the exemption applies to the whole record **or** that the exempt portions are **“inextricably intertwined”** with the non‑exempt portions, making segregation impracticable. *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d at 61 (quoting *Mead Data Center*, 566 F.2d at 260).  

2. **The “Inextricably Intertwined” Standard** – The court looks for a **“rational nexus”** between exempt and non‑exempt material. If the exempt information is interspersed line‑by‑line, or if redaction would render the remaining text unintelligible, segregation may be denied. *Mead* emphasized that FOIA is about “information, not simply words which the Government has written down.”  

3. **The Agency’s Evidentiary Duty** – The burden then shifts to the agency to **specifically identify** the exempt portions, explain why they are intertwined, and demonstrate that any segregation would be futile. *CREW v. DOJ* (2014) required that categories used to withhold be “sufficiently distinct” to permit judicial review.  

In *Farahi*, the district court found that the FBI’s “detailed justification” (submitted ex parte by Acting Section Chief Bender) satisfied this burden, even though the public record contains only a terse affirmation that the investigation remains ongoing. The court implicitly accepted that the FBI’s internal, sealed declaration—explainably opaque because of the classified nature of the material—provided enough specificity to meet the *Mead* standard.  

### How the Court Applied the Standard  

The appellate panel’s opinion offers a concise summary of the lower court’s analysis:  

* The FBI’s declarations (Hardy’s public and ex‑parte statements, plus Bender’s update) together established that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that “the relevant investigation remained ongoing.”  
* The district court concluded that the exempt material could not be separated without compromising the investigative value of the file, citing the risk that any disclosed snippet could “tip off” targets.  
* The court rejected Farahi’s argument that the passage of time rendered the “ongoing” assertion insufficient, holding that the agency’s earlier, more detailed attestations, combined with the updated declaration, created a continuous chain of proof.  

What is striking is the court’s reliance on **agency affidavits** rather than an independent evidentiary review of the actual documents. The opinion notes that “the FBI’s public Hardy declaration explained…that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere…by tipping off the targets,” and that “the Bender declaration confirmed that the investigation remained ongoing as of March 2023.” The court did **not** demand the production of a redacted sample or a line‑by‑line segregation analysis, even though the file spanned thousands of pages.  

### Analytical Take‑aways  

#### 1. Deference to Law‑Enforcement Assertions Is Cemented  

The panel reiterated that “because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” This echoes *Campbell v. DOJ* (1998) and *CNSS v. DOJ* (2003). While deference is not absolute—courts can still invalidate overly broad exemptions—it sets a high bar for plaintiffs to pierce the shield. In practice, the deference doctrine allows agencies to rely on **aggregate statements** about an ongoing investigation, without furnishing granular evidence of each segregable piece.  

#### 2. The “Update” Doctrine Lowers the Threshold for Ongoing Investigations  

Farahi demonstrates that an agency can meet the “pending or reasonably anticipated” prong with a **mere update** to an earlier declaration, provided the original showing was “sufficiently detailed.” This creates a practical shortcut: once an agency has made a robust initial showing, a short “nothing has changed” affidavit can sustain the exemption indefinitely, even as years pass.  

#### 3. The “Inextricably Intertwined” Standard Becomes a Blanket Shield  

Because the court accepted the FBI’s assertion that the exempt material was “inextricably intertwined” without a public, itemized analysis, future agencies may argue that any large, classified file automatically meets this threshold. The key will be whether the agency can produce a **“detailed justification”** that, while sealed, is sufficiently specific to survive appellate review. The Farahi decision suggests that “detailed” can be satisfied by an internal memorandum that outlines the investigation’s scope and the risk of disclosure, without disclosing the actual document excerpts.  

### Doctrinal Implications  

1. **Erosion of the Segregability Counterbalance** – FOIA’s segregation requirement was intended to prevent agencies from using a single exemption to sweep away entire files. Farahi signals a potential erosion of that counterbalance, especially where national‑security investigations are involved.  

2. **Potential Expansion of the “Exemption‑by‑Reference” Strategy** – Agencies may increasingly rely on a handful of internal affidavits to blanket‑cover massive records, citing *Mead* and *Juarez* as justification. Plaintiffs will need to demand more specific “redaction logs” or “segregation matrices,” but courts may be reluctant to force such production when it would jeopardize investigations.  

3. **Impact on Litigation Strategy** – FOIA litigants may need to pivot from a “line‑by‑line segregation” approach to one that attacks the **adequacy of the agency’s affidavit** itself. Demonstrating that the affidavit is conclusory, or that the agency failed to identify distinct categories, could become the primary avenue for success.  

### Practical Effects for Plaintiffs  

* **Higher Evidentiary Bar** – Plaintiffs must now front‑load their case with a demand for an agency’s **segregation methodology**, not merely the existence of non‑exempt material. Failure to pinpoint categories that are “sufficiently distinct” can result in forfeiture, as Farahi’s court noted.  

* **Increased Reliance on Ex Parte Filings** – Because agencies can use sealed declarations to avoid disclosing the very material at issue, plaintiffs must anticipate that much of the evidentiary battle will occur “behind closed doors.” While courts have recognized the necessity of ex parte filings in these contexts, the lack of a public record makes it harder for litigants to “show cause” why segregation is possible.  

* **Strategic Use of “Partial” FOIA Requests** – To avoid the “all‑or‑nothing” trap of a massive dossier, requestors might consider **targeted, narrowly defined requests** that isolate specific document types (e.g., “email correspondence dated X–Y”). This can force agencies to justify segregation on a smaller scale, where the “inextricably intertwined” argument is less persuasive.  

### Looking Ahead  

The *Farahi* decision sits at the nexus of FOIA doctrine and national‑security secrecy. Its affirmation of the FBI’s broad segregation defense may incentivize agencies to adopt a **“no‑segregation” posture for any large, classified file**. If left unchallenged, this could render FOIA’s transparency promise largely ineffective for subjects of counterterrorism investigations.  

Future courts will have to balance the deference owed to law‑enforcement agencies against the statutory mandate that “any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided.” The line may be drawn where plaintiffs can demonstrate that an agency’s affidavit is **barely more than a conclusory statement**, or where the claimed intertwining is **logically impossible** (e.g., when a file contains a few paragraphs of purely administrative correspondence amidst classified investigative notes).  

Until then, the *Farahi* precedent equips the government with a sturdy, if controversial, tool: a well‑drafted ex parte affidavit that tells the court the investigation is still alive and the records are too tangled to untangle. For FOIA advocates, the fight now turns to **forcing agencies to make that affidavit more than a shield**—to compel a granular, defensible segregation analysis that respects both investigative integrity and the public’s right to know.



# Ex‑Parte Declarations and FOIA: A Double‑Edged Sword for Transparency

## Why This Case Matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s September 30, 2025 opinion in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* spotlights a fault line that has long haunted Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation: the reliance on sealed, ex‑parte declarations to sustain exemptions. While the Court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for the FBI, it did so on a record that the plaintiff, an Iranian national subject to a sprawling national‑security investigation, could not fully see or rebut. The decision underscores how the judicial deference granted to the executive in national‑security cases can, when paired with ex‑parte filings, effectively shut the door on meaningful adversarial contest. For journalists, scholars, and litigants alike, the ruling raises pressing questions about the procedural safeguards that are—or are not—built into FOIA suits involving sensitive law‑enforcement material.  

## The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell  

The D.C. Circuit applied the standard three‑part test for Exemption 7(A):  

1. **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.**  
2. **Disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.**  
3. **The proceedings must be pending or reasonably anticipated.**  

The opinion gives the FBI broad latitude to satisfy the first prong, noting that “the agency specializes in law enforcement” and therefore its determination warrants “fair measure of deference.”  

The second and third prongs rested on a series of declarations. The district court first relied on a public declaration from Section Chief David Hardy, which asserted that the records were part of an ongoing national‑security investigation. When the district court sought an updated assessment, the FBI filed a supplemental, sealed ex‑parte declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender, essentially stating that the investigation “remains ongoing” as of March 2023. The appellate court accepted these filings at face value, concluding that the FBI had shown a “reasonable nexus” between the records and a still‑pending investigation, and that the material could not be reasonably segregated.  

## Analytical Perspective: Deference Meets Secrecy  

### The Deference Problem  

Deference to an agency’s “law‑enforcement expertise” is a well‑trod doctrine. *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998) and *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111 (D.C. Cir. 2017) both articulate that courts should not act as “second‑guessers” of an agency’s investigative judgments. Yet, this deference is premised on the assumption that the agency’s assertions are subject to meaningful scrutiny. When the very evidence supporting those assertions is hidden behind a seal, the balance tips in favor of the government.  

In *Farahi*, the Court treated the Bender declaration as a “simple update” to the earlier Hardy statements. The Court noted that Hardy’s earlier, public declaration already established pendency, so Bender’s terse “investigation remains ongoing” was sufficient. This reasoning effectively narrows the *CREW* “vague nature” warning—*Citizens for Resp. & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014)—by allowing a bare assertion to stand when coupled with a prior, more detailed ex‑parte filing that the plaintiff cannot examine.  

### Segregability and the “Inextricably Intertwined” Standard  

The opinion also embraced the agency’s claim that the records were “not reasonably segregable.” The D.C. Circuit reiterated the *Mead* line: “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down.” While that principle is sound, the court’s acceptance of the FBI’s segregation analysis hinged on the same sealed ex‑parte declaration. No independent, on‑the‑record test was applied to determine whether a meaningful chunk of non‑exempt material could have been released absent the secrecy of the underlying investigative details.  

### The Ex‑Parte Procedural Gap  

FOIA statutes provide that “any reasonably segregable portion … shall be provided” after the exempt portions are deleted. The statutory text also imposes a procedural burden on agencies: they must “provide a reasonable justification” for why segregation is not feasible. The *Farahi* opinion concedes that the FBI met this burden, but does so on a record that the plaintiff—and by extension, the public—cannot access. The result is a procedural asymmetry: the government can introduce sealed evidence to support a blanket withholding, while the plaintiff must contest it without seeing it.  

## Doctrinal Implications  

1. **Broadening the Exemption 7(A) Safe Harbor.** By accepting a minimal update to a prior declaration as sufficient, the D.C. Circuit may have lowered the evidentiary threshold for showing “pending” or “reasonably anticipated” proceedings. Future FOIA litigants will find it harder to pry open files where the government can rely on a single, sealed statement that “the investigation continues.”  

2. **Reinforcing the “Inextricably Intertwined” Doctrine.** The decision signals that courts are willing to accept the government’s claim that exempt material is inseparable from non‑exempt content, even when the underlying justification is sealed. This could lead agencies to adopt a “catch‑all” approach, labeling entire voluminous records as non‑segregable because some portion is sensitive.  

3. **Eroding Judicial Oversight.** The de novo standard for summary‑judgment review, as the opinion reminds us, normally places the burden squarely on the agency. Yet the Court’s heavy reliance on ex‑parte declarations effectively curtails the court’s own fact‑finding role, turning the review into a rubber‑stamp of the agency’s sealed narrative.  

## Potential Downstream Effects  

- **Chilling Effect on FOIA Requesters.** Litigants may be discouraged from pursuing FOIA suits against national‑security agencies, knowing that the government can wield sealed declarations to forestall discovery.  
- **Precedential Shield for Future Ex‑Parte Filings.** District courts in the D.C. Circuit are likely to follow *Farahi*’s template, allowing agencies to file supplemental, ex‑parte affidavits as “updates” without providing the plaintiff an opportunity to cross‑examine.  
- **Impact on Transparency Litigation Beyond FOIA.** The reasoning may spill into other statutes that hinge on “reasonable steps” to disclose, such as the Privacy Act or the Freedom of Information Improvement Act, where agencies can cite ex‑parte declarations to justify nondisclosure.  

## Toward Fairer FOIA Litigation: A Reform Blueprint  

1. **Mandatory In‑Camera Review of Ex‑Parte Declarations.** Courts should compel parties to submit sealed affidavits for private, in‑camera examination, giving the judge the chance to assess credibility and relevance without exposing the content publicly.  

2. **Limited “Redacted Summary” Requirement.** When an ex‑parte declaration is the sole basis for a withholding, the agency should be required to produce a redacted summary that conveys the factual basis (e.g., “the investigation concerns X, Y, Z”) without jeopardizing the investigation. This would give plaintiffs a foothold for meaningful objections.  

3. **Heightened Burden for “Ongoing” Assertions.** The Court should require more than a blanket assertion that “the investigation remains ongoing.” A concise factual description of what remains pending—e.g., a grand jury subpoena, an outstanding arrest warrant, or a pending charge—should be necessary to satisfy the “pending or reasonably anticipated” prong.  

4. **Enhanced Segregability Scrutiny.** Courts ought to apply a “reasonable segregation” test that looks at the file as a whole, rather than relying on the agency’s statement that non‑exempt portions are “intertwined.” Where practicable, judges should order a “redacted‑first” approach: the agency must produce a version with all identified exempt material blacked out, allowing the plaintiff to challenge the adequacy of the redactions.  

5. **Periodic Review of “Secret” Exemptions.** In cases where the government invokes Exemption 7(A) for years, courts could set a “review clock” (e.g., every two years) to reassess whether the alleged pending investigation still exists, preventing indefinite lock‑up of records.  

## Conclusion  

*Farahi* underscores a stark tension at the heart of FOIA: the desire to protect sensitive law‑enforcement techniques versus the democratic imperative of transparency. The D.C. Circuit’s willingness to accept sealed, ex‑parte declarations as the linchpin of an Exemption 7(A) analysis tilts the balance heavily toward secrecy. While deference to the executive is appropriate in genuine national‑security contexts, it must be coupled with procedural safeguards that preserve the adversarial nature of FOIA litigation. Implementing in‑camera review, redacted summaries, and stricter pleading requirements for “ongoing” investigations would help restore that balance, ensuring that the “right to know” does not become an empty promise when the government can simply file a sealed affidavit and walk away.
