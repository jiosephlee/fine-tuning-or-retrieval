Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## When Penalties Belong to the State: The Eighth Circuit’s Interpretation of Minnesota’s Wage Penalty Schemes  

*By [Your Name], Lawfare / Volokh Conspiracy contributor*  

### Why This Case Matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s October 6, 2025 opinion in **Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC** may not be a headline‑grabbing decision about the scope of overtime liability, but it has outsized significance for wage‑and‑hour litigants throughout the Midwest.  By holding that the **Minnesota Payment of Wages Act** (MPWA) and the **Minnesota Wage Theft Act** (MWTA) limit the recovery of “average‑daily‑wage” (ADW) penalties to the state’s Labor Commissioner—not to the aggrieved employee— the court has effectively stripped plaintiffs of a powerful leverage tool that many class actions have historically used to compel compliance and secure injunctive relief.  

For attorneys who routinely “stack” liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive claims to pressure employers, the decision redraws the battlefield.  Moreover, the reasoning sheds light on how courts may interpret similar penalty schemes in other states, especially those that, like Minnesota, have bifurcated enforcement authority between an administrative agency and private plaintiffs.  

Below, I walk through the Eighth Circuit’s statutory construction, assess the strength of its legislative‑history arguments, and consider the downstream ripple effects for wage‑and‑hour practice in the region.  

### The Court’s Reasoning on MPWA Penalties  

#### Plain‑Language Reading  

The majority began with the unambiguous language of **Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101(a)** and **181.171**.  Section 181.101(a) squarely reserves the *authority to assess and collect* ADW penalties to the Commissioner, stating that the Commissioner may “charge and collect the wages earned … and a penalty” after a demand for payment is served and the employer fails to comply within ten days.  The statute further mandates that any money collected “must be paid to the employee concerned,” but it does **not** grant the employee a private right of action to *seek* the penalty in the first place.  

The court leaned heavily on Minnesota precedent that “when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect” (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d at 33).  Under that principle, the Eighth Circuit read the MPWA as giving the Commissioner *exclusive* enforcement power for ADW penalties.  Consequently, an employee’s civil action may recover wages and commissions, but the penalty component is off‑limits unless the Commissioner initiates the enforcement process.  

#### Legislative History as a Supporting Tool  

Recognizing that statutory construction is not limited to text, the court examined the legislature’s own “tool‑kit” for imposing penalties.  It pointed to **§ 181.13(a)** and **§ 181.14**, which expressly authorize the Commissioner to assess penalties in discharge and resignation contexts.  The majority argued that the legislature’s decision to embed penalty‑imposing authority in those sections—while leaving it *out* of § 181.101—demonstrates a purposeful, not accidental, distinction.  

The opinion cited **Lagasse v. Horton**, 982 N.W.2d at 199 n.2, emphasizing that when a legislature knows how to achieve a particular policy objective in one provision, its silence in another signals a deliberate choice.  The Eighth Circuit read Minnesota’s omission of a private‑right‑to‑penalty clause in § 181.101 as a clear legislative intent to keep the penalty mechanism within the administrative sphere.  

#### The “Must Be Paid to the Employee” Clause  

A frequent point of confusion is the statutory language that any penalty “must be paid to the employee concerned.”  The court differentiated **who** collects the penalty from **who** receives it.  Because the Commissioner is the sole enforcer, the penalty—once levied—is disbursed to the employee, but the *right to impose* the penalty never belongs to the employee.  This distinction mirrors the analysis in **Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.**, 570 F. Supp. 3d at 695, where the district court similarly held that the employee’s claim is limited to wages, not to the statutory penalty itself.  

### The MWTA Claim: No Penalties, No Problem  

The court’s treatment of the **Minnesota Wage Theft Act** was brief but consistent.  Cohen relied on **§ 181.032(f)** and **(d)(4)**, arguing that the failure to give proper notice of the overtime policy change triggered civil penalties.  The majority found no statutory language authorizing a private‑right‑to‑penalty claim under the MWTA either, and, given the parties’ stipulation that all wages were paid, dismissed the claim without further discussion.  The opinion’s narrow focus underscores a broader judicial trend: unless a state statute expressly grants a private remedy for penalties, courts will interpret the enforcement role as belonging to the state agency.  

### The MFLSA Issue: Standing and Mootness  

While the MPWA and MWTA analyses are relatively straightforward, the opinion’s handling of the **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act** (MFLSA) penalties raises interesting procedural questions.  Cohen argued that, notwithstanding his stipulation that overtime wages and liquidated damages had been paid, he remained entitled to statutory penalties—money that, under the MFLSA, is payable to the Commissioner.  

The Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s summary‑judgment grant on this ground, citing standing concerns.  It noted that civil penalties can serve a “citizen‑plaintiff” function—deterring future violations—but that a plaintiff must demonstrate a continuing personal stake.  Because the parties had not resolved whether Consilio’s offending overtime policy remained in effect, the court deemed the issue potentially moot and sent the case back for a jurisdictional determination.  

The takeaway for litigants is clear: even when a statute permits civil penalties, the plaintiff must show that the underlying wrongful conduct is *ongoing* or that a future risk of recurrence exists.  Otherwise, the court may deem the penalty claim moot and strip the plaintiff of that avenue of relief.  

### Doctrinal Implications  

1. **Limiting Private Penalty Claims** – The decision reinforces a *strict* construction approach: if a statute does not expressly grant a private right to seek a penalty, courts will read that right away.  This contrasts with a more expansive view seen in some jurisdictions where courts have inferred penalty‑seeking rights from broader remedial language.  

2. **Agency‑Centric Enforcement** – By emphasizing the Commissioner’s exclusive role, the Eighth Circuit aligns Minnesota with a growing cohort of states—such as Illinois and California—where labor agencies are the primary penalty enforcers.  Plaintiffs must now consider agency coordination as a prerequisite to leveraging penalties.  

3. **Strategic Shift in Class Actions** – Wage‑and‑hour class actions often rely on the threat of hefty statutory penalties to induce settlement.  With the penalty stream redirected to the state, plaintiffs may find their bargaining chips dulled, potentially leading to more protracted negotiations over injunctive relief or higher liquidated‑damage demands.  

4. **Potential for Legislative Response** – The opinion’s reliance on legislative silence could spur Minnesota lawmakers to amend § 181.101 (or the MWTA) to create a private-right‑to‑penalty provision, especially if employer groups lobby against the “penalty‑to‑the‑state” model.  We may see a future amendment that expressly allows employees to recover ADW penalties directly, mirroring statutes in other states that have adopted such language.  

### Practical Takeaways for Practitioners  

- **Screen Statutes Early** – When drafting pleadings, verify whether the relevant state wage law grants a private right to penalties.  If the language mirrors Minnesota’s § 181.101, assume the penalty is agency‑only unless the statute says otherwise.  

- **Engage the Commissioner** – Consider filing a demand with the Minnesota Labor Commissioner concurrently with the lawsuit.  A successful agency demand can trigger the penalty‑collection mechanism, after which the amount can be passed to the employee, strengthening the plaintiff’s overall recovery.  

- **Preserve the “Ongoing Harm” Argument** – In MFLSA‑type cases, maintain evidence that the offending policy remains in place or that the employer has not abandoned the illegal practice.  This will help defeat mootness challenges to statutory‑penalty claims.  

- **Re‑evaluate Injunctive Strategies** – Since penalties may no longer be available as a pressure point, plaintiffs should sharpen the injunctive component of their complaints, perhaps by seeking declaratory relief that the employer’s policy violates specific statutory provisions.  

### Looking Ahead  

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in **Cohen** may be a harbinger of a broader judicial consensus: private‑right‑to‑penalty provisions will be *read narrowly* and only enforced when they are unmistakably spelled out.  For the Midwest wage‑and‑hour landscape, that means attorneys must adapt, leaning more heavily on agency collaboration, robust evidentiary records of ongoing violations, and strategic injunctive pleading.  

Whether Minnesota’s legislature will respond by amending the MPWA or MWTA to restore a private penalty avenue remains to be seen.  Until then, the state's “penalties belong to the state” rule stands—a reminder that, in wage‑and‑hour law, the devil is often in the statutory detail.
