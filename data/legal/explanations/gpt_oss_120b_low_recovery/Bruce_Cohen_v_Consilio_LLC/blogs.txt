## When Penalties Belong to the State: The Eighth Circuit’s Interpretation of Minnesota’s Wage Penalty Schemes  

*By [Your Name], Lawfare / Volokh Conspiracy contributor*  

### Why This Case Matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s October 6, 2025 opinion in **Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC** may not be a headline‑grabbing decision about the scope of overtime liability, but it has outsized significance for wage‑and‑hour litigants throughout the Midwest.  By holding that the **Minnesota Payment of Wages Act** (MPWA) and the **Minnesota Wage Theft Act** (MWTA) limit the recovery of “average‑daily‑wage” (ADW) penalties to the state’s Labor Commissioner—not to the aggrieved employee— the court has effectively stripped plaintiffs of a powerful leverage tool that many class actions have historically used to compel compliance and secure injunctive relief.  

For attorneys who routinely “stack” liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive claims to pressure employers, the decision redraws the battlefield.  Moreover, the reasoning sheds light on how courts may interpret similar penalty schemes in other states, especially those that, like Minnesota, have bifurcated enforcement authority between an administrative agency and private plaintiffs.  

Below, I walk through the Eighth Circuit’s statutory construction, assess the strength of its legislative‑history arguments, and consider the downstream ripple effects for wage‑and‑hour practice in the region.  

### The Court’s Reasoning on MPWA Penalties  

#### Plain‑Language Reading  

The majority began with the unambiguous language of **Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101(a)** and **181.171**.  Section 181.101(a) squarely reserves the *authority to assess and collect* ADW penalties to the Commissioner, stating that the Commissioner may “charge and collect the wages earned … and a penalty” after a demand for payment is served and the employer fails to comply within ten days.  The statute further mandates that any money collected “must be paid to the employee concerned,” but it does **not** grant the employee a private right of action to *seek* the penalty in the first place.  

The court leaned heavily on Minnesota precedent that “when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect” (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d at 33).  Under that principle, the Eighth Circuit read the MPWA as giving the Commissioner *exclusive* enforcement power for ADW penalties.  Consequently, an employee’s civil action may recover wages and commissions, but the penalty component is off‑limits unless the Commissioner initiates the enforcement process.  

#### Legislative History as a Supporting Tool  

Recognizing that statutory construction is not limited to text, the court examined the legislature’s own “tool‑kit” for imposing penalties.  It pointed to **§ 181.13(a)** and **§ 181.14**, which expressly authorize the Commissioner to assess penalties in discharge and resignation contexts.  The majority argued that the legislature’s decision to embed penalty‑imposing authority in those sections—while leaving it *out* of § 181.101—demonstrates a purposeful, not accidental, distinction.  

The opinion cited **Lagasse v. Horton**, 982 N.W.2d at 199 n.2, emphasizing that when a legislature knows how to achieve a particular policy objective in one provision, its silence in another signals a deliberate choice.  The Eighth Circuit read Minnesota’s omission of a private‑right‑to‑penalty clause in § 181.101 as a clear legislative intent to keep the penalty mechanism within the administrative sphere.  

#### The “Must Be Paid to the Employee” Clause  

A frequent point of confusion is the statutory language that any penalty “must be paid to the employee concerned.”  The court differentiated **who** collects the penalty from **who** receives it.  Because the Commissioner is the sole enforcer, the penalty—once levied—is disbursed to the employee, but the *right to impose* the penalty never belongs to the employee.  This distinction mirrors the analysis in **Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.**, 570 F. Supp. 3d at 695, where the district court similarly held that the employee’s claim is limited to wages, not to the statutory penalty itself.  

### The MWTA Claim: No Penalties, No Problem  

The court’s treatment of the **Minnesota Wage Theft Act** was brief but consistent.  Cohen relied on **§ 181.032(f)** and **(d)(4)**, arguing that the failure to give proper notice of the overtime policy change triggered civil penalties.  The majority found no statutory language authorizing a private‑right‑to‑penalty claim under the MWTA either, and, given the parties’ stipulation that all wages were paid, dismissed the claim without further discussion.  The opinion’s narrow focus underscores a broader judicial trend: unless a state statute expressly grants a private remedy for penalties, courts will interpret the enforcement role as belonging to the state agency.  

### The MFLSA Issue: Standing and Mootness  

While the MPWA and MWTA analyses are relatively straightforward, the opinion’s handling of the **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act** (MFLSA) penalties raises interesting procedural questions.  Cohen argued that, notwithstanding his stipulation that overtime wages and liquidated damages had been paid, he remained entitled to statutory penalties—money that, under the MFLSA, is payable to the Commissioner.  

The Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s summary‑judgment grant on this ground, citing standing concerns.  It noted that civil penalties can serve a “citizen‑plaintiff” function—deterring future violations—but that a plaintiff must demonstrate a continuing personal stake.  Because the parties had not resolved whether Consilio’s offending overtime policy remained in effect, the court deemed the issue potentially moot and sent the case back for a jurisdictional determination.  

The takeaway for litigants is clear: even when a statute permits civil penalties, the plaintiff must show that the underlying wrongful conduct is *ongoing* or that a future risk of recurrence exists.  Otherwise, the court may deem the penalty claim moot and strip the plaintiff of that avenue of relief.  

### Doctrinal Implications  

1. **Limiting Private Penalty Claims** – The decision reinforces a *strict* construction approach: if a statute does not expressly grant a private right to seek a penalty, courts will read that right away.  This contrasts with a more expansive view seen in some jurisdictions where courts have inferred penalty‑seeking rights from broader remedial language.  

2. **Agency‑Centric Enforcement** – By emphasizing the Commissioner’s exclusive role, the Eighth Circuit aligns Minnesota with a growing cohort of states—such as Illinois and California—where labor agencies are the primary penalty enforcers.  Plaintiffs must now consider agency coordination as a prerequisite to leveraging penalties.  

3. **Strategic Shift in Class Actions** – Wage‑and‑hour class actions often rely on the threat of hefty statutory penalties to induce settlement.  With the penalty stream redirected to the state, plaintiffs may find their bargaining chips dulled, potentially leading to more protracted negotiations over injunctive relief or higher liquidated‑damage demands.  

4. **Potential for Legislative Response** – The opinion’s reliance on legislative silence could spur Minnesota lawmakers to amend § 181.101 (or the MWTA) to create a private-right‑to‑penalty provision, especially if employer groups lobby against the “penalty‑to‑the‑state” model.  We may see a future amendment that expressly allows employees to recover ADW penalties directly, mirroring statutes in other states that have adopted such language.  

### Practical Takeaways for Practitioners  

- **Screen Statutes Early** – When drafting pleadings, verify whether the relevant state wage law grants a private right to penalties.  If the language mirrors Minnesota’s § 181.101, assume the penalty is agency‑only unless the statute says otherwise.  

- **Engage the Commissioner** – Consider filing a demand with the Minnesota Labor Commissioner concurrently with the lawsuit.  A successful agency demand can trigger the penalty‑collection mechanism, after which the amount can be passed to the employee, strengthening the plaintiff’s overall recovery.  

- **Preserve the “Ongoing Harm” Argument** – In MFLSA‑type cases, maintain evidence that the offending policy remains in place or that the employer has not abandoned the illegal practice.  This will help defeat mootness challenges to statutory‑penalty claims.  

- **Re‑evaluate Injunctive Strategies** – Since penalties may no longer be available as a pressure point, plaintiffs should sharpen the injunctive component of their complaints, perhaps by seeking declaratory relief that the employer’s policy violates specific statutory provisions.  

### Looking Ahead  

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in **Cohen** may be a harbinger of a broader judicial consensus: private‑right‑to‑penalty provisions will be *read narrowly* and only enforced when they are unmistakably spelled out.  For the Midwest wage‑and‑hour landscape, that means attorneys must adapt, leaning more heavily on agency collaboration, robust evidentiary records of ongoing violations, and strategic injunctive pleading.  

Whether Minnesota’s legislature will respond by amending the MPWA or MWTA to restore a private penalty avenue remains to be seen.  Until then, the state's “penalties belong to the state” rule stands—a reminder that, in wage‑and‑hour law, the devil is often in the statutory detail.



## Standing, Mootness, and Civil Penalties: Why the MFLSA Claim Was Sent Back for a Jurisdictional Ruling  

*By [Your Name], Law & Policy Analyst*  

### Why This Decision Matters  

When an employer has already paid back‑pay and liquidated damages for overtime violations, does an employee still have a live claim for the statutory penalties that the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) reserves for the state?  The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in **Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC**, 2025 WL XXXX (8th Cir. Oct. 6 2025), says “maybe.”  By vacating the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim and remanding for a jurisdictional analysis, the panel forces lower courts to grapple with two intertwined doctrinal questions that have been largely dormant in wage‑and‑hour litigation: (1) does a plaintiff have standing to pursue civil penalties that are payable to the state, and (2) does the plaintiff’s claim become moot once the employer has corrected the underlying wage arrears but may retain the unlawful policy?  

The answer has immediate practical stakes for employers who employ “premium‑pay‑elimination” policies, for plaintiffs seeking punitive deterrence, and for courts deciding whether to let a case die after back‑pay is satisfied.  Moreover, the opinion spotlights the tension between the **Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw** standing framework—originally articulated for federal environmental civil penalties—and the MFLSA’s unique scheme that directs penalties to the Commissioner of Labor rather than to the aggrieved worker.  

### The Court’s Reasoning, Step by Step  

#### 1. The Statutory Landscape  

The MFLSA mirrors the federal Fair Labor Standards Act in authorizing a civil penalty of **$42,000** (as of the court’s date) for each violation, but the statute directs that **the penalty be paid to the Commissioner**, not to the employee.  The plaintiff’s claim therefore rests on the proposition that a private employee can sue for a remedy that benefits the state.  The Eighth Circuit noted that the MFLSA’s text does not expressly grant a private right of action for penalties; it merely provides the Commissioner's enforcement authority.  

#### 2. Standing Under Friends of the Earth  

The panel borrowed the Supreme Court’s test from **Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw**, which allows a private plaintiff to assert a standing claim for civil penalties when (a) the plaintiff has suffered an injury, and (b) the penalty serves a remedial or deterrent purpose that benefits the plaintiff’s interests.  The court acknowledged that the **Laidlaw** analysis is a useful yardstick but cautioned that the MFLSA’s penalty regime is *statutorily distinct*—penalties are not a “private” remedy but a *public* enforcement tool.  

Because the plaintiff had already been made whole for the wage claim, the only alleged injury was the continued risk of future overtime violations under the same policy.  The court found that the parties had not conclusively resolved whether Consilio’s “premium‑pay‑elimination” policy remained in effect.  Without that factual determination, the court could not definitively say whether Cohen suffered a concrete injury sufficient for standing.  

#### 3. The Mootness Problem  

Even if standing were found, the court raised the classic mootness question: **Is there a “live controversy” once the employee has received all back‑pay and liquidated damages?**  The Seventh Circuit (and other circuits) have held that civil‑penalty actions may survive remedial payments because the penalty’s deterrent function is independent of the plaintiff’s personal loss.  However, the Eighth Circuit emphasized that **dismissal for lack of jurisdiction is appropriate when the plaintiff cannot demonstrate a continuing personal stake**.  

Cohen’s own stipulation that he “has been paid for all overtime wages … and does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages” undercut his claim of personal injury.  The court observed that any lingering controversy hinged on whether the policy remained in force—a factual dispute that the district court had not addressed.  Consequently, the panel found that the lower court’s summary‑judgment grant on the MFLSA penalty claim was premature, not because the law was unclear, but because the jurisdictional prerequisites—standing and a non‑moot controversy—were unresolved.  

#### 4. Procedural Consequences  

The opinion **vacates** the district court’s judgment on the MFLSA claim and **remands** for a determination of (1) whether Cohen has standing to sue for a penalty that benefits the state, and (2) whether the claim is moot given the current status of Consilio’s overtime policy.  The concurring judge’s separate note that the district court’s order on attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest should likewise be revisited underscores how closely procedural posture and substantive rights are intertwined in wage‑and‑hour cases.  

### My Take: A Cautious but Forward‑Looking Approach  

The Eighth Circuit’s decision is a **necessary pause** rather than a defeat for plaintiffs.  By insisting that the district court first address standing and mootness, the panel safeguards the constitutional limits on judicial power while preserving the possibility that statutory penalties can serve a deterrent role even after back‑pay is satisfied.  

Two points deserve emphasis:  

1. **The “public‑interest” nature of the penalty does not automatically grant a private right of action.**  Courts have been reluctant to extend standing to private litigants for civil penalties unless the statute unambiguously provides such a right.  Minnesota’s legislative history—seen in the MPWA discussion earlier in the opinion—shows that the legislature *knew* how to empower employees with penalties when it wanted to, but *chose not* to do so in the MFLSA.  This suggests that the presumption should be against a private right of action for MFLSA penalties.  

2. **Mootness can be sidestepped if the plaintiff can show a “reasonable probability” of future injury.**  The Supreme Court in **City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle** stressed that voluntary cessation does not moot a claim if the conduct could reasonably be expected to recur.  Here, the key fact is whether Consilio’s policy remains on the books.  If the employer simply *re‑instated* the policy after paying Cohen, the claim would survive.  Conversely, a clear, documented policy change could render the penalty claim moot.  

Given these nuances, the remand will likely produce a fact‑finding stage (perhaps via a limited evidentiary hearing) to determine the current status of Consilio’s policy.  The district court will also have to apply the **Friends of the Earth** test, balancing Cohen’s narrowed injury against the state’s interest in deterrence.  

### Doctrinal Ripple Effects  

#### a. Wage‑and‑Hour Penalty Jurisprudence  

If the district court ultimately finds that Cohen **does** have standing, the Eighth Circuit will join a growing minority of courts treating civil‑penalty statutes as giving rise to a private right of action *for enforcement purposes only*.  That would push plaintiffs in other states—like Illinois or New York, which also have civil‑penalty provisions payable to labor departments—toward similar standing arguments.  

If, however, the court rules that standing is lacking, the decision will reinforce the prevailing view that **civil penalties are exclusively a government enforcement tool**.  Plaintiffs would then have to rely on the threat of state agency action, or possibly on collective‑action statutes that permit private enforcement of penalties.  

#### b. Mootness in Remedial Wage Cases  

The opinion sharpens the line between *remedial* claims (back‑pay, liquidated damages) and *deterrent* claims (penalties).  Future litigants will need to preserve factual disputes about ongoing policy violations early—ideally via discovery—so that the case does not become moot once the employer “cures” the past violation.  We may see more “conditional” settlement language, where employers agree not only to pay back‑wages but also to *retract* the offending policy, thereby insulating themselves from later penalty suits.  

#### c. Interaction with State Enforcement Agencies  

Because the MFLSA penalties flow to the Commissioner, there is an implicit incentive for the state to intervene.  If courts start allowing private plaintiffs to sue for those penalties, the Commissioner’s enforcement discretion could be undermined or duplicated.  State labor agencies may respond by issuing more detailed guidance on when and how they will pursue penalties, or by convening joint enforcement actions with private plaintiffs.  

### Looking Ahead  

The **Cohen** decision is a textbook example of how procedural doctrines—standing and mootness—can shape the substantive reach of wage‑and‑hour statutes.  It reminds us that **remedies matter**, not merely the amount of back‑pay.  For employers, the prudent path is to *both* pay any past wage deficiencies and *formally rescind* any policies that run afoul of state overtime rules.  For plaintiffs, the takeaway is to preserve the factual record that the offending policy persists and to frame the penalty claim as a *deterrent* interest that the plaintiff shares with the state.  

Ultimately, the Eighth Circuit’s vacatur and remand will produce a fact‑intensive inquiry that could settle the lingering question: **Can a private employee sue for a civil penalty that enriches the state, or must that enforcement be left solely to the Commissioner?**  The answer will reverberate through the wage‑and‑hour landscape across the Midwest and beyond.

## The Perils of Stipulating “All Claims Resolved” Before Summary Judgment: Lessons from *Cohen v. Consilio*

**Why this case matters**  
Wage‑and‑hour disputes are fertile ground for hefty statutory penalties, liquidated damages, and injunctive relief. In the Eighth Circuit’s October 2025 decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the parties—by mutual stipulation—agreed that Cohen had already been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed. The district court, taking that stipulation at face value, sailed through the summary‑judgment (SJ) phase on the overtime‑wage claims and left the more speculative penalty and injunctive issues for later. On appeal, the circuit affirmed the SJ on the wage‑wage claims but vacated and remanded the judgment on the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties, citing unresolved jurisdiction and standing concerns.  

The outcome underscores a strategic hazard: stipulating that “all claims are resolved” can truncate the factual record on issues that remain genuinely in dispute—particularly statutory penalties and equitable relief. Litigators who rely on such stipulations without adequate safeguards may find themselves facing unexpected remands, wasted resources, and, in some cases, exposure to liability that could have been avoided with clearer drafting.

Below, I walk through the court’s reasoning, flag the doctrinal pitfalls, and offer practical guidance for drafting stipulations in wage‑and‑hour cases (and beyond).

---

## 1. The court’s anatomy of the stipulation

### 1.1. A “clean‑sheet” for overtime wages  

The parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages was uncontroversial. Both sides even submitted a payment schedule documenting the $256,010.01 Consilio paid to Cohen and his co‑workers. Because the dispute centered on whether any *additional* overtime compensation existed, the district court felt no need to probe further and granted SJ on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA wage‑wage claims.

### 1.2. Penalties left in limbo  

Crucially, the stipulation said nothing about *penalties*—the statutory damages that, under Minnesota law, flow to the state commissioner (MPWA) or the Commonwealth (MFLSA). Cohen argued that he was entitled to average‑daily‑wage penalties under the MPWA and to the MFLSA’s $42,000 penalty provision. Consilio countered that the statutes did not create a private right to those penalties. The district court, perhaps buoyed by the “all claims resolved” language, dismissed the penalty issues without detailed analysis, reasoning that the parties had agreed no further relief remained.

### 1.3. Injunctive relief never reached the table  

Cohen’s injunctive request—to rescind the illegal overtime policy—was likewise sidelined. He shifted his sought relief between the complaint and the SJ brief, and the district court, noting the procedural irregularity, declined to address it. The appellate court agreed the issue was not properly before it.

---

## 2. The Eighth Circuit’s appellate reversal

### 2.1. Standing and jurisdiction for penalties  

Judge Kelly’s opinion treats the MFLSA penalty claim as a classic standing problem. Even though penalties are payable to the state, the plaintiff must still demonstrate a personal stake. The court leaned on *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* to show that civil penalties can serve a remedial purpose, but it also emphasized that standing is *facts‑driven*. Because the parties had not resolved whether Consilio’s policy remained in effect, the court could not determine whether Cohen’s injury was ongoing—an essential element of standing. Consequently, the district court’s SJ on the penalty claim was vacated, and the case sent back for a jurisdictional determination.

### 2.2. The MPWA penalty analysis survived  

On the MPWA side, the court applied a straightforward textualist approach: § 181.101 authorizes the commissioner—not the employee—to seek daily‑average‑wage penalties. The court noted the Legislature’s ability to grant private penalties in other statutes (e.g., §§ 181.13, 181.14) and concluded that the absence of such language in § 181.101 indicates a clear intent to limit enforcement to the commissioner. The district court’s SJ therefore stood.

### 2.3. The injunctive claim left untouched  

Because Cohen never properly amended his complaint to articulate a concrete injunctive request, the appellate court declined to rescue the claim. This illustrates the procedural trap: stipulations cannot conjure a claim that was never properly before the court.

---

## 3. Doctrinal implications

### 3.1. Stipulations are not a “blanket‑kill” for all relief  

The *Cohen* decision reaffirms that a stipulation that “all claims are resolved” is only as strong as the language it contains. Courts will not assume that parties intended to foreclose *all* potential relief, especially when statutory penalties or equitable remedies are on the table. The Eighth Circuit’s willingness to vacate the SJ on the MFLSA penalties shows that appellate courts will scrutinize whether a stipulation truly eliminates the *jurisdictional* basis for a claim.

### 3.2. The “private‑penalty” distinction  

Minnesota’s wage‑law framework creates a bifurcation: some statutes empower employees to sue for penalties (e.g., the MWTA’s § 181.032), while others vest enforcement exclusively in the commissioner (e.g., § 181.101). Courts will look to the *plain language* and *legislative history* to determine the intended private right. Practitioners must therefore tailor their stipulations to the specific statutory scheme—blanket language can backfire when the statute’s penalty mechanism is ambiguous.

### 3.3. Standing remains fact‑intensive even for “government‑paid” penalties  

The appellate analysis underscores that a plaintiff’s personal stake is not automatically satisfied because the penalty is payable to the state. The plaintiff must show a concrete injury—typically the continued risk of illegal conduct or the loss of a statutory right. This aligns with the Supreme Court’s standing doctrine post‑*Lujan* and *Friends of the Earth*. Litigants should be prepared to prove ongoing exposure, or else risk a mootness or jurisdictional dismissal.

---

## 4. Practical takeaways for litigators

1. **Draft stipulations with surgical precision**  
   - Specify exactly which heads of relief are *settled* (e.g., “all overtime wages and liquidated damages”).  
   - Explicitly *exclude* penalties, injunctive claims, or any other statutory remedies you intend to keep alive.  
   - Include a clause stating that the stipulation does not waive any right to seek statutory penalties unless the parties expressly agree to that term.

2. **Anticipate statutory nuance**  
   - When negotiating a settlement in a wage‑and‑hour case, identify which statutes generate private penalties and which do not.  
   - If the employer wishes to avoid penalties, consider a separate “penalty waiver” provision, acknowledging the statutory source and the parties’ intent.

3. **Preserve the factual record for penalties**  
   - Even if you stipulate that wages are paid, retain discovery on the *policy* itself—whether the illegal overtime scheme remains in effect.  
   - Courts can deem a penalty claim moot only when the offending conduct is *clearly* ceased; vague or future‑policy language leaves the door open for further litigation.

4. **Handle injunctive relief early**  
   - If you seek an injunction, file an amended complaint *before* moving for SJ.  
   - Avoid “flip‑flopping” on the requested form of relief; consistency signals to the court that the injunction is an integral part of the claim, not an afterthought.

5. **Plan for appellate review**  
   - Expect that appellate courts will scrutinize any “all claims resolved” stipulation for *overbreadth*.  
   - Keep a contemporaneous docket of the parties’ negotiations, especially any side‑letter agreements, to demonstrate the parties’ actual intent if the matter reaches an appellate bench.

---

## 5. Looking ahead

The *Cohen* decision may prompt both employers and employee‑representing firms to revisit their settlement templates. In jurisdictions with similar statutory structures—Illinois, New York, California—practitioners should expect parallel disputes over the scope of private penalties and the adequacy of stipulations. Moreover, as courts continue to enforce rigorous standing requirements for civil penalties, plaintiff counsel will need to build a stronger factual foundation demonstrating ongoing risk.

In the end, *Cohen v. Consilio* offers a cautionary tale: a well‑intentioned stipulation that aims to streamline litigation can inadvertently preserve the very issues litigants hoped to extinguish. By drafting stipulations that are both precise and comprehensive, wage‑and‑hour practitioners can avoid costly remands, preserve the efficiencies of summary judgment, and, most importantly, safeguard their clients’ substantive rights.



## Injunctions in Wage‑and‑Hour Disputes: Why the Eighth Circuit Refused to Grant Relief in Cohen’s Case  

The Eighth Circuit’s recent opinion in **Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC**, 8th Cir. 2025, offers a vivid reminder that, even when a plaintiff has a strong substantive claim, procedural missteps can shut the door on the most potent remedy—an injunction. Cohen’s attempt to force Consilio to rescind its “premium overtime” policy was foiled not because the court doubted the policy’s legality, but because the claim was never properly presented at the right stage of the litigation. Below we unpack the procedural dynamics that barred injunctive relief, situate the decision within the broader landscape of injunctive standards in wage‑and‑hour law, and outline the steps plaintiffs should take to preserve an injunction when a case pivots toward statutory penalties or damages.

### Why This Case Matters  

In wage‑and‑hour litigation, injunctions serve a dual purpose: they instantly correct ongoing unlawful conduct and they deter future violations. Courts have routinely ordered employers to reinstate overtime pay, to cease misclassification, or to destroy unlawful policies. Consequently, a decision that bars an injunction—especially on purely procedural grounds—has ripple effects for every employee who seeks immediate relief from a pervasive pay practice. Cohen’s case underscores how the timing of claim amendments and the requirements of Rule 12(b)(6) and Rule 15(a) can be as decisive as the merits.

### The Procedural Roadblock: Claim Amendment After Summary Judgment  

Cohen’s original complaint asked for both monetary relief (unpaid overtime, liquidated damages, penalties) **and** an affirmative injunction requiring Consilio to withdraw its August 2019 overtime policy. By the time the case reached the summary‑judgment phase, the parties had stipulated that all overtime wages and liquidated damages had been paid. Cohen then shifted focus, seeking “the rescission of the illegal policy” as his sole injunctive request. He raised this request not in a formally amended complaint, but in his **summary‑judgment briefing**, essentially attempting to “add” a new remedy at the appellate stage.

The Eighth Circuit, following well‑established procedural doctrine, refused to consider the injunction. The court invoked **Fed. R. Civ. P. § 15(a)**, which requires that a plaintiff amend the pleading **before** a responsive pleading is served, or later with the court’s leave. More importantly, the court stressed that **new claims**—or new **relief sought**—cannot be introduced through an opposition brief. As Judge Kobes wrote, “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.” The appellate court therefore concluded that the injunction request was procedurally barred and not before the court for review.

### The Injunctive Standard in Wage‑and‑Hour Cases  

Even if the procedural hurdle had been cleared, Cohen would still have needed to satisfy the classic four‑prong test for a preliminary injunction (as articulated in *Winter v. NRDC*, 555 U.S. 420 (2009) and applied in the Eighth Circuit in *United States v. C.D. Savile Corp.*, 704 F.3d 987 (8th Cir. 2013)):

1. **Likelihood of success on the merits** – In wage‑and‑hour cases this often hinges on whether the employer’s policy runs afoul of the FLSA or, in Cohen’s case, the Minnesota statutes (MPWA, MFLSA, MWTA).  
2. **Irreparable harm** – The plaintiff must show that monetary damages are inadequate. Courts have recognized that continued wage violations cause ongoing injury to employees’ livelihoods, satisfying this prong.  
3. **Balance of equities** – Courts weigh the employer’s interest in maintaining its payroll practices against the employee’s lost wages.  
4. **Public interest** – Enforcing wage and hour laws is considered a strong public policy.

Cohen’s case would likely have cleared the first two prongs—he could point to the employer’s unilateral policy change and the statutory prohibition on eliminating overtime premiums. The third and fourth prongs are usually favorable to employees, especially where the employer’s conduct is clearly contrary to state wage law. Nonetheless, the procedural misstep meant the court never reached this substantive analysis.

### The Strategic Pitfall of “Pivoting” to Penalties  

Cohen’s case also illustrates a strategic danger for plaintiffs: **pivoting** from an injunction to a damages‑only claim can unintentionally waive injunctive relief. After the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime and liquidated damages, Cohen asserted that his remaining remedy was statutory penalties under the MFLSA. By focusing the opposition brief on the MFLSA penalty issue and omitting any discussion of the injunction, Cohen effectively signaled to the court that the injunctive claim was no longer contested—a classic “waiver by omission.”  

Courts have warned that defendants can move to dismiss an unasserted claim for lack of a timely pleading (see *Taylor v. Dearborn Real Estate Co.*, 942 F.2d 1155 (8th Cir. 1991)). While Cohen could have filed a **motion to amend** his complaint before the district court’s summary‑judgment deadline, the parties’ stipulation that “the most expedient and efficient path forward would be … to decide the summary‑judgment motion” created a tacit understanding that the case would close once the monetary claims were resolved. In that context, the appellate court was correct to treat the injunction request as procedurally untimely.

### Doctrinal Implications  

The Cohen decision reinforces two doctrinal points that will shape future wage‑and‑hour litigation in the Eighth Circuit:

1. **Injunctive relief is not an automatic fallback** – Plaintiffs must **preserve** the injunction claim from the outset, either by stating it in the complaint or by amending the pleading **before** any dispositive motion is filed.  
2. **Stipulations and settlement‑style agreements can waive procedural rights** – Even informal stipulations, like the parties’ agreement to resolve the case via summary judgment, can be interpreted as a waiver of unasserted claims. Plaintiffs must explicitly carve out any relief they wish to retain, or ask the court to enter a **protective order** preserving the claim for later determination.

### Practical Takeaways for Practitioners  

- **Draft the complaint with a clear injunctive prayer**. If you anticipate needing an injunction, articulate the specific conduct to be enjoined and the statutory basis.  
- **File a motion to amend before summary judgment** if you need to add or clarify injunctive relief. The Eighth Circuit is willing to grant leave to amend when there is good cause and no undue prejudice.  
- **Avoid blanket stipulations** that “resolve all claims” without expressly excluding injunctive relief. A precise stipulation can preserve the injunction while still allowing for damages.  
- **Consider early motion practice**. If your case hinges on an injunction, file a **pre‑motion for preliminary injunction** contemporaneously with any motion for summary judgment, or at least before the court rules on the merits of monetary claims.  
- **Maintain the “injunctive” thread in all briefs**. Even if the main focus is penalties, a brief should reference the pending injunctive claim to avoid waiver through omission.

### Potential Downstream Effects  

The decision is likely to prompt more cautious pleading strategies in the Midwest. Employers may feel emboldened to rely on settlement stipulations that sweep away injunctive claims, knowing that plaintiffs must be meticulous to preserve them. Conversely, employee advocates will likely push for **court‑issued scheduling orders** that force parties to address injunctive claims early, preventing a “wait‑and‑see” approach that could allow unlawful policies to remain in effect while damages are litigated.

In the long run, the Cohen opinion may nudge the Eighth Circuit toward a more **procedurally strict** stance on injunctive relief in wage‑and‑hour cases, aligning with the circuit’s broader emphasis on finality and efficiency. Plaintiffs who overlook the procedural gatekeepers risk ending up with money in the bank but no guarantee that the offending policy will ever be dismantled.

### Bottom Line  

Cohen’s case is a textbook example of how **procedural precision** can trump substantive merit when seeking injunctions in wage‑and‑hour disputes. The Eighth Circuit did not rule on the legality of Consilio’s “premium overtime” policy; it simply refused to entertain a claim that was never properly before the court. For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: **file, amend, and argue your injunction early**, and never assume that a settlement of back‑pay automatically preserves the right to a court‑ordered policy change. The injunction remains one of the most powerful tools in the employee’s arsenal—provided it survives the procedural gauntlet.
