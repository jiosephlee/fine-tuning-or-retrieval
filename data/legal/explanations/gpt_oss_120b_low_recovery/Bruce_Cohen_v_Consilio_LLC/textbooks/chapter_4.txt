Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: The MPWA Penalty Issue – Who May Seek Daily‑Average‑Wage Penalties?

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit confronted a seemingly simple question: does Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (“MPWA”) permit an aggrieved employee, rather than the state Commissioner of Labor and Industry, to pursue the statutory “average‑daily‑wage” penalties that the Act authorizes?  The court’s answer—no—rests on a close reading of the statutory language, well‑established rules of statutory construction in Minnesota, and a comparison with other state wage statutes that explicitly empower employees to obtain penalties.  This chapter walks law‑students through each component of the court’s analysis, showing how the judges moved from the text of § 181.101(a) to the ultimate holding that the district court correctly granted summary judgment on Cohen’s MPWA claim.

---

## 4.1. Text of § 181.101(a) and Its Limitation to Commissioner‑Initiated Demand

Section 181.101(a) is the operative provision governing the assessment and collection of penalties under the MPWA.  Its text can be broken into three sequential steps:

1. **Employee Demand** – “When an employer fails to pay wages … the employee may file a demand for payment with the Commissioner.”  
2. **Commissioner Action** – “If the employer does not pay the wages within ten (10) days after service of the demand, the Commissioner may charge and collect … the wages earned … and a penalty.”  
3. **Disposition of Collected Money** – “Any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.”

Two features of this language are dispositive.  First, the statutory **right to impose a penalty** is expressly **conditioned on the Commissioner’s action**—the phrase “the Commissioner may charge and collect … a penalty” makes clear that the authority to assess the penalty resides solely with the Commissioner.  Second, the **only party who may receive the money** is the employee, but the **only party who may impose the penalty** is the Commissioner.  The statute therefore creates a **two‑step enforcement scheme**: an employee initiates a demand; the Commissioner, not the employee, decides whether a penalty attaches and then forwards the penalty proceeds to the employee.

Cohen’s argument rested on the broader purpose clause of the MPWA and the ordinary language of “civil action seeking redress” in § 181.171, but the court emphasized that **the plain text of § 181.101(a) overrides any broader inference**.  When a statute’s language is “plain and unambiguous,” Minnesota courts apply the text as the legislature’s ultimate expression of intent (see **Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015)**).  The Eighth Circuit, borrowing that principle, concluded that the statutory scheme left no room for an employee to independently sue for the daily‑average‑wage penalty.

---

## 4.2. Interpretation of “Civil Action Seeking Redress” Under § 181.171

Section 181.171 creates a **private right of action** for employees: “A person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain MPWA provisions, including § 181.101.  The provision also states that an employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” and that “an employee may also recover civil penalties or damages, so long as they are provided for in the section of the MPWA that the employer allegedly violates.”

The appellate court parsed this language carefully:

* The **right to bring a civil action** is limited to **seeking redress for the substantive violation**—typically unpaid wages or commissions.  
* The **reference to “civil penalties or damages”** is **contingent on whether the underlying violated section itself furnishes a penalty provision**.  In other words, the private right of action can only incorporate penalties that the **statute itself authorizes**.

Because § 181.101(a) **does not itself authorize the employee to assess a penalty**—it reserves that authority for the Commissioner—the “civil penalties” language in § 181.171 cannot be read to create a new, employee‑driven penalty right.  The court therefore held that § 181.171 **does not expand** the enforcement mechanism laid out in § 181.101(a); it merely provides a procedural avenue for the employee to **recover what the statute already provides**, namely the unpaid wages (and any penalty the Commissioner may impose).

---

## 4.3. Precedent on Statutory Construction in Minnesota (Allan, Burkstrand)

The court’s reliance on **statutory construction principles** is grounded in a line of Minnesota precedent that treats clear statutory language as controlling:

* **Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d at 33** – When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, “it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.”  
* **Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001)** – The “plain meaning rule” dictates that courts first look to the ordinary meaning of the words, and only if ambiguity exists should they turn to extrinsic aids.

Applying these rules, the Eighth Circuit found **no ambiguity** in § 181.101(a).  The phrase “the Commissioner may … charge and collect … a penalty” is unqualified and exclusive; it does not contain language such as “or the employee may” that would suggest a dual enforcement pathway.  Accordingly, the court declined to engage in any extensive legislative history analysis because the text itself offered a **clear directive**.

---

## 4.4. Legislative Comparison: Statutes That Explicitly Grant Employee‑Penalty Rights vs. MPWA’s Silence

To underscore that the MPWA’s silence on employee‑initiated penalties is intentional, the court examined **parallel Minnesota wage statutes** that **do** empower employees to seek statutory penalties directly:

1. **Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a)** – Provides that a **discharged employee** may recover **unpaid wages or commissions** *and* “may charge and collect a penalty” after a demand is made.  
2. **Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.14, subd. 2** – Extends the same employee‑penalty right to employees who resign or quit.  

These provisions are **explicit**: they contain the phrase “may charge and collect a penalty” attached to the employee’s own demand, without reference to the Commissioner.  The court cited **Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022)**, which notes that the legislature “knows how” to empower employees when it wishes to do so.  By contrast, the MPWA **omits** any such employee‑penalty language, delegating the penalty function solely to the Commissioner.

The juxtaposition serves two purposes:

* It **demonstrates legislative intent**—the drafters deliberately chose a different enforcement model for the MPWA.  
* It **reinforces the textual analysis**—the absence of employee‑penalty language in § 181.101(a) is not an oversight but a deliberate design choice, affirmed by the presence of employee‑penalty language elsewhere in the wage code.

---

## 4.5. Court’s Conclusion and Affirmation of the District Court’s MPWA Ruling

Having established that **(1) § 181.101(a) gives the Commissioner exclusive authority to assess penalties, (2) § 181.171 does not create a new employee‑penalty cause of action, and (3) the legislature has expressly provided employee‑penalty rights in other statutes when it intended to**, the Eighth Circuit concluded that **Cohen cannot recover the average‑daily‑wage penalties under the MPWA**.

The court therefore affirmed the district court’s summary‑judgment grant on the MPWA claim, holding that no genuine issue of material fact existed as to whether Cohen possessed a statutory right to the penalties.  The decision underscores a broader lesson for practitioners: **when a statute delineates a specific enforcement mechanism, that mechanism controls**, and employees must look to the statutory text (and any express employee‑penalty provisions) before asserting a private right to penalties.

--- 

### Takeaways for the Reader

1. **Read the operative provision first.**  § 181.101(a)’s explicit grant of penalty authority to the Commissioner is dispositive.  
2. **Apply the plain‑meaning rule.**  Minnesota courts give effect to unambiguous language without resorting to policy arguments.  
3. **Use legislative comparison wisely.**  The presence of employee‑penalty language in other statutes signals that its omission in the MPWA is intentional.  
4. **Recognize the limits of § 181.171.**  The private right of action there does not expand the statutory penalties; it merely provides a procedural route to enforce what the statute already allows.

By following these analytical steps, a law student can reliably navigate similar penalty‑authority questions in wage‑law litigation and appreciate how appellate courts balance statutory text, construction principles, and legislative context.
