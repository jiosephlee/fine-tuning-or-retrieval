# Standing in the Shadow of Administrative Discretion: Why AFL’s Claim Failed  

*By [Your Name], Legal Analyst*  

## Why the case matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer**, 24‑5168 (2025), is a cautionary tale for any organization that hopes to compel an oversight agency to “do its job.”  America First Legal (AFL) sought to force the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate a Department of Justice (DOJ) FOIA policy it alleged was arbitrary.  The court turned on a more fundamental question: does a plaintiff—even one that has already suffered a FOIA denial—have Article III standing to challenge an agency’s refusal to open an internal investigation?  The answer was a resounding **no**.  By applying the Lujan test with a strict view of procedural injury and redressability, the panel reaffirmed the high barrier to “informational standing” and signaled that the courts will not become a backstop for agency discretion.

## The court’s analytical roadmap  

### 1. The Lujan framework, applied bluntly  

The D.C. Circuit walked through the classic three‑pronged Lujan test—injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability—reminding us that each element must be satisfied *independently* of any speculative chain of events.  The majority emphasized that a plaintiff must show a concrete, particularized injury that is “actual or imminent” and not merely conjectural.  The court found AFL’s alleged injuries—failure to obtain requested DOJ records and denial of OSC‑generated notices—were too attenuated to meet that threshold.

### 2. Procedural‑injury doctrine does not create a shortcut  

AFL leaned on the “relaxed” standing standards that sometimes apply in procedural‑right cases, arguing that the statute (5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)) gave it a protected right to an OSC investigation.  The panel rejected this line of reasoning, citing *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau*, and the D.C. Circuit’s own precedent that a procedural right must be tethered to a concrete interest.  The court held that a “procedural right in vacuo”—the abstract entitlement to an agency‑initiated investigation—does not itself confer standing.

### 3. Causation and redressability collapse under speculation  

Even assuming a procedural right existed, the court dissected AFL’s causal theory.  To succeed, AFL would need to show that OSC’s investigation would *necessarily* trigger corrective action under §§ 1214/1215, that the Merit Systems Protection Board would then sanction DOJ employees, and that DOJ would subsequently produce the requested records.  The panel labeled this a “speculative chain” that cannot satisfy the causation requirement, invoking *Heckler v. Chaney* and the Supreme Court’s recent clarification in *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* that courts cannot compel agencies to exercise discretionary enforcement powers.

Redressability suffered a similar fate.  The D.C. Circuit stressed that a judgment must bind the parties — in this case, OSC — and that coercing OSC to investigate would not, by itself, guarantee that DOJ would produce the documents.  The court cited *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the principle that a court’s decree need not persuade third parties; it must directly affect the respondent.

### 4. No informational entitlement under § 1216  

AFL’s second standing theory hinged on “informational standing”—the notion that a plaintiff harmed by denial of information they are statutorily entitled to receive suffers a concrete injury.  The majority dismissed this because § 1216 does not create a right to receive the investigative notices or findings that OSC might produce.  The decision leaned on *Zivotofsky v. Sec’y of State* and *FEC v. Akins* to underscore that a statutory right to *receive* information is a prerequisite for this type of standing, which AFL simply did not have.

## Our take: the decision is good law, but perhaps too restrictive  

The opinion faithfully applies standing doctrine, preserving the constitutional limit on federal courts.  The court rightly refuses to turn the judiciary into a supervisory body over agency discretion; that role belongs to the agencies themselves, Congress, and the political process.

However, the decision may be **overly formalistic** when it comes to the practical realities of FOIA enforcement.  The FOIA’s own enforcement mechanism (the OSC trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) is designed to give aggrieved requesters a conduit to oversight when an agency’s withholding appears arbitrary.  By treating the statutory “shall investigate” language in § 1216 as discretionary and therefore non‑justiciable, the court effectively immunizes agencies that claim procedural loopholes—such as the DOJ’s “no‑consent email search” policy—from judicial scrutiny, unless the agency itself moves to invoke the FOIA‑specific trigger.  This creates a paradox: a private party cannot sue to enforce a statutory duty that the agency can ignore unless a court first finds the agency’s refusal to investigate unambiguously illegal, which it cannot do without standing.

In other words, the court’s standing analysis may **undermine the remedial purpose of § 1216**.  If an agency can sidestep the statutory investigation requirement by pointing to a different statutory trigger that it claims is unmet, private litigants are left without a meaningful avenue to enforce the law.

## Doctrinal implications  

1. **Reaffirmation of the high standing bar for informational injuries** – The decision joins a line of D.C. Circuit rulings that place the “informational standing” exception on a narrow ledge.  Plaintiffs must now point to an explicit statutory entitlement to the information they seek, not merely to a procedural benefit that might incidentally produce it.

2. **Further entrenchment of the “discretionary enforcement” principle** – By invoking *Heckler* and refusing to compel OSC to investigate, the panel strengthens the doctrine that agencies may decline to enforce their own statutes when they have discretionary authority.  Future challenges to OSC’s refusal to act on FOIA violations will face the same standing hurdle.

3. **Potential narrowing of the FOIA‑OSC nexus** – The opinion could be read to limit the reach of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).  If courts accept that OSC need not investigate absent the specific FOIA trigger, requesters may find it harder to bring “internal‑investigation” suits, pushing them instead toward the more cumbersome route of direct FOIA litigation.

## Downstream effects and strategic considerations  

- **Litigants will pivot to direct FOIA suits** – Organizations like AFL are likely to focus their resources on suing the agency that actually withholds records, rather than trying to compel OSC to act.  This may increase docket pressure on district courts handling FOIA cases.

- **Congress may feel pressure to clarify OSC’s duties** – Legislators concerned about agency‑wide FOIA compliance could amend § 1216 to explicitly tie OSC’s investigative authority to a requirement to provide procedural notice to requesters, thereby creating a concrete statutory right that satisfies standing.

- **Oversight agencies may codify internal policies** – To shield themselves from future lawsuits, agencies could adopt written policies stating that OSC investigations are discretionary unless a FOIA court order meets the § 552 trigger, thereby reinforcing the discretionary shield highlighted in this case.

- **Potential for a Supreme Court revisit** – The tension between the statutory “shall investigate” language and the court’s standing analysis could attract Supreme Court attention, especially if future cases present a more concrete injury—perhaps a scenario where an agency’s failure to investigate leads to demonstrable harm that is directly traceable to the omission.

## Conclusion  

*America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* is a textbook illustration of the standing doctrine in action: the courts will not entertain a case unless the plaintiff can point to a personal, concrete injury that the court can realistically remedy.  By applying the Lujan test with rigor and refusing to stretch procedural‑injury doctrine, the D.C. Circuit preserved Article III limits but at the cost of narrowing the enforceability of OSC’s statutory mandate.  For litigants and lawmakers alike, the decision serves as both a warning and a prompt: either craft claims that meet the strict standing requirements or push for legislative clarification that creates a genuine, enforceable entitlement to agency investigations.  Until then, the shadow of administrative discretion will continue to loom over FOIA‑related oversight suits.



## Statutory Interpretation of 5 U.S.C. § 1216: Does the OSC Have a Mandatory FOIA‑Investigation Duty?

**By [Your Name] – October 2025**  

*In America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer, the D.C. Circuit threw out a district‑court order compelling the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) alleged policy violation. The appellate panel resolved the case on the basis that the plaintiff, America First Legal (AFL), lacked Article III standing—but the opinions also contain a meticulous reading of the statutory language that raises a larger, unresolved question: Does § 1216(a)(3) create a non‑discretionary duty for OSC to open an investigation whenever a requester alleges an “arbitrary or capricious” FOIA withholding, irrespective of whether the FOIA‑specific trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) has been satisfied?*  

Below, I walk through the courts’ competing constructions of § 1216, place them in the context of longstanding interpretive rules governing “shall” language, and explore what the decision means for the future of OSC’s enforcement authority—and for FOIA litigants more broadly.

---

### 1. Why This Case Matters

FOIA’s enforcement regime is famously fragmented. Congress gave the OSC a parallel, statutory avenue to police “arbitrary or capricious” withholdings (5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)), but the same section also ties OSC’s investigative power to the procedural trigger that lives in the FOIA itself—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i). If Congress intended OSC to act whenever a private party alleges an arbitrary withholding, it would provide a potent, independent check on agency compliance, especially for requesters who lack the resources to bring a separate civil suit. Conversely, if the statutory language merely mirrors the FOIA trigger, OSC’s role remains limited to a “back‑stop” that only activates after a court has already ruled on the merits.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision does not settle the substantive question; it sidesteps it by dismissing the case for lack of standing. Yet the opinion supplies a detailed textual analysis that will likely be the foundation for any future challenge. Understanding how the court parsed “shall … conduct an investigation” versus “may investigate … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” is essential for practitioners who hope to leverage OSC as a FOIA enforcement tool.

---

### 2. The Competing Readings of § 1216(a)(3) and § 1216(c)

#### 2.1 The “Mandatory‑Investigation” View (District Court)

The district court read § 1216(a)(3) as imposing a *mandatory* duty:

> “OSC ‘shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.’”

The court emphasized the plain‑meaning of “shall” as command‑like, citing *United States v. Am. Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 292 U.S. 250 (1934), and held that the text does not condition the duty on the existence of a FOIA court order. The court further argued that § 1216(c) merely clarifies the *procedural* mechanics of the investigation (i.e., OSC may use the same remedial scheme as for prohibited personnel practices) but does not introduce a discretionary filter.

#### 2.2 The “Discretionary‑Trigger” View (Appellate Court)

Judge Katsas, writing for the D.C. Circuit, took a more nuanced approach. He read § 1216(a)(3) as **granting** OSC authority to investigate *if* the conditions in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are met:

> “The statutory scheme of § 1216 is designed to parallel the FOIA’s own enforcement provision. The reference to ‘arbitrary or capricious withholding’ must be read in light of the FOIA trigger that requires a court order, fee award, and a specific factual finding.”

The appellate opinion treated “shall” as *imperative* only within the confines of the statutory context. In other words, OSC *shall* investigate *when* the statutory conditions are satisfied; otherwise, the duty does not arise. The court leaned on the principle that a statute’s operative clause must be read in concert with the surrounding provisions, especially when Congress explicitly references another statute (here, the FOIA) as a prerequisite.

#### 2.3 The Role of § 1216(c)

Both opinions agree that § 1216(c) merely mirrors the procedural safeguards of § 1214/§ 1215. The district court saw it as confirming that, once an investigation is triggered, OSC follows the same notice and remedial scheme as it would for a prohibited personnel practice. The appellate court, however, stressed that § 1216(c) does *not* create a new substantive right to investigation; it only delineates the *process* if an investigation is lawfully initiated.

---

### 3. The “Shall” Rule: How Courts Usually Treat Imperative Language

The Supreme Court has consistently held that “shall” signals a *mandatory* obligation, but not every “shall” creates a *stand‑alone* duty. In *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946), the Court noted that a “shall” clause must be read in context to determine whether it imposes a duty, creates a limitation, or merely describes a permissible action.

The D.C. Circuit’s appellate reasoning aligns with this contextual approach. By anchoring the “shall” to the FOIA trigger, the court treats the statute as a *conditional* command—*shall* if *and only if* the antecedent condition (the FOIA trigger) occurs. This mirrors the Court’s analysis in *Nat’l Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius*, 567 U.S. ~ (2012), where a “shall” provision was held to be contingent on a statutory precondition.

Conversely, the district court’s reading reflects a more literalist stance, treating “shall” as an absolute command irrespective of surrounding language. This methodological split is not new; it mirrors the divide seen in *Garrett v. United States*, 131 S. Ct. 252 (2002), over whether “shall” in the federal sentencing guidelines created a mandatory duty or merely a strong recommendation.

---

### 4. Interplay with FOIA’s Own Enforcement Provision (§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i))

FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) expressly ties OSC’s involvement to a *court‑ordered* finding of arbitrary or capricious withholding. The statute compels OSC to act “promptly” once those conditions are satisfied. The question, then, is whether § 1216(a)(3) creates an *independent* pathway that bypasses the FOIA trigger, or merely provides a parallel procedural channel once the trigger is met.

The appellate panel treats the FOIA provision as the *gatekeeper*: without a judicial determination of arbitrariness (or a fee award and a specific factual finding), OSC’s “shall” never comes into force. This reading preserves the FOIA’s “court‑first” enforcement model and avoids creating a dual, potentially conflicting, enforcement scheme.

The district court, by contrast, viewed § 1216(a)(3) as an *independent* investigative mandate, arguing that Congress “intended” OSC to have authority to examine alleged policy violations *before* a litigant obtains a court order. The court relied on legislative history indicating that Congress was concerned about “systematic” FOIA violations that might otherwise go unaddressed.

Both interpretations are plausible; the statutes are undeniably ambiguous. The crux of the disagreement is whether Congress used the FOIA trigger as a *condition precedent* (the appellate view) or merely as a *reference point* to define the type of conduct OSC may investigate (the district court view).

---

### 5. Doctrinal Implications

#### 5.1 For Standing Doctrine

Even if the mandatory‑investigation reading were adopted, plaintiffs would still need to overcome the standing hurdle. The D.C. Circuit’s analysis is a reminder that procedural injuries alone rarely satisfy Article III requirements unless the plaintiff can show a concrete, particularized interest that is *directly* impaired. A future case that successfully pleads standing will likely need to demonstrate that the denial of an OSC investigation *immediately* harms a protected interest—perhaps by showing that OSC’s statutory duty includes a *right* to receive investigative updates, rather than a mere discretionary power.

#### 5.2 For OSC’s Enforcement Role

If the mandatory‑investigation view prevails, OSC could become a more proactive FOIA watchdog. Requesters could file a complaint directly with OSC, forcing an investigation without first obtaining a court order. This would echo the “private right of action” model that exists for many other statutes (e.g., the False Claims Act). However, it would also raise concerns about agency burden and potential duplication of effort with the FOIA’s existing judicial enforcement pathway.

#### 5.3 For Agency FOIA Policies

A mandatory‑investigation duty could incentivize agencies to tighten their FOIA search procedures. Knowing that OSC can initiate investigations on policy grounds alone would likely deter agencies from adopting overly restrictive email‑search policies, such as the “no‑search‑without‑consent” rule at issue here.

---

### 6. Potential Downstream Effects

1. **Legislative Clarification** – Congress may feel compelled to amend § 1216 to expressly state whether the FOIA trigger is a prerequisite. A clarifying amendment would eliminate the current interpretive deadlock.

2. **Strategic Litigation** – Plaintiffs may begin to structure FOIA suits to invoke both the FOIA’s judicial trigger *and* a direct OSC complaint, creating a “dual‑track” strategy that pressures agencies on two fronts.

3. **Judicial Review of OSC Discretion** – Assuming a future case survives standing, courts may be asked to review OSC’s discretionary choices under § 1216(c). The *Heckler v. Chaney* doctrine suggests that courts will be reluctant to compel agency action, but a clear statutory command (“shall”) could narrow the deference owed to OSC.

4. **Agency Administrative Burden** – A mandatory duty could significantly increase OSC’s workload, potentially prompting Congress to allocate additional resources or to set procedural thresholds (e.g., a minimum number of alleged violations before an investigation is launched).

---

### 7. Conclusion

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer leaves the substantive question of OSC’s FOIA‑investigation duty unresolved, but the opinions provide a rich textual roadmap for future courts. The D.C. Circuit’s contextual reading of “shall” aligns with modern statutory interpretation principles and preserves the FOIA’s court‑first enforcement scheme. The district court’s literalist approach, however, reflects a policy‑driven view that Congress wanted an independent, proactive FOIA watchdog.

Until the Supreme Court—or Congress—speaks, litigants must focus first on the standing gate. For those who can overcome that hurdle, the case will likely become a testbed for the “mandatory” versus “discretionary” reading of § 1216. In the meantime, FOIA requesters should continue to press agencies directly and consider supplementing their efforts with OSC complaints, recognizing that the agency’s current discretion may limit the efficacy of that route.

*The battle over § 1216’s exact meaning is far from over, and its resolution will shape the balance of power between transparency advocates and the federal bureaucracy for years to come.*



## The Role of Discretionary Enforcement in the Civil Service Reform Act: Lessons from the OSC’s Refusal to Investigate  

**By [Your Name] – [Date]**  

### Why This Case Matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s October 2025 opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* may look like a technical standing decision, but its implications ripple far beyond the parties. At issue was whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) must investigate alleged Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations “as a matter of duty” under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). The court concluded that the plaintiff, America First Legal (AFL), lacked Article III standing, leaving untouched the substantive question of OSC’s investigative obligation. Nonetheless, the opinion reaffirmed a long‑standing theme in administrative law: agencies enjoy broad discretion in enforcing their own statutes, and courts will not compel that discretion unless Congress has spoken in unmistakably mandatory language.  

For anyone who follows FOIA enforcement, civil‑service oversight, or the broader balance between executive flexibility and accountability, the decision is a reminder that the “Heckler‑Chaney” doctrine—*Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821 (1985)—still governs the contours of agency action. The opinion also surfaces a practical worry: if the OSC can ignore a statutory “shall‑investigate” clause by invoking its own discretion, FOIA litigants may find one of their few non‑court enforcement avenues effectively shut down.

Below we walk through the court’s reasoning, evaluate its alignment with precedent, and explore what the decision means for the future of FOIA enforcement and civil‑service oversight.

### The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell  

Judge Katsas began by framing the dispute: AFL alleged that OSC improperly refused to launch an investigation under § 1216(a)(3), which says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The district court had found that the statutory language was not limited by the FOIA’s “trigger” provision in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), and remanded for further consideration.  

On appeal, the D.C. Circuit sidestepped the merits and focused on standing. Applying *Lujan* and *Steel* standards, the panel held that AFL could not demonstrate a concrete, particularized injury. The court rejected two standing theories:

1. **Informational injury from DOJ’s withholding** – the injury was traceable to the DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain linking OSC’s investigation to eventual disclosure was speculative. The court invoked *Heckler* to stress that an agency’s discretionary decision not to investigate cannot be compelled absent a clear statutory command.  

2. **Informational injury from the lack of OSC’s procedural notices** – § 1216 does not create a right to receive the procedural information that § 1214 provides in prohibited‑personnel‑practice investigations. Since OSC’s § 1216 investigations are discretionary, no concrete injury exists absent a statutory entitlement.  

Finding no injury, the panel vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

### How This Fits Within the “Heckler‑Chaney” Paradigm  

*Heckler* articulated that an agency’s decision **whether** to enforce is generally unreviewable, unless the agency’s action is “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” The D.C. Circuit’s analysis mirrors that logic: it treated OSC’s refusal to investigate as an exercise of discretion, not a categorical statutory duty.  

The opinion also leans on recent Supreme Court guidance in *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), which emphasized that causation must not rely on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” By tying the standing analysis to that principle, the court underscored that even a “shall” provision does not automatically convert a discretionary function into a mandatory one.  

That is not to say the court ignored the language of § 1216. It acknowledged the “shall” but noted that the statute simultaneously grants OSC “discretion” to determine the appropriate investigative path (see § 1216(c)). The panel read the statute as a **grant of authority**, not a **mandate**—a classic *Heckler* distinction.

### Policy Concerns: Accountability vs. Flexibility  

#### A Potential Chilling Effect on FOIA Enforcement  

OSC’s primary enforcement role is to police “prohibited personnel practices,” a narrow category that historically includes retaliation against whistleblowers. By extending its remit to “arbitrary or capricious” FOIA withholdings, Congress signaled an intent to give FOIA oversight an extra layer of protection. Yet, the D.C. Circuit’s decision suggests that, in practice, OSC can retreat behind its discretionary gatekeeper role.  

If agencies interpret “shall investigate” as a permissive grant, FOIA litigants lose a valuable avenue for pre‑emptive enforcement. The usual FOIA remedy—court‑ordered production—remains available, but such litigation is costly, time‑consuming, and often limited to the records at issue. A robust OSC investigative function could have acted as an early warning system, nudging agencies toward compliance before a lawsuit erupted. The court’s deference may therefore dampen FOIA’s deterrent effect.

#### Executive Flexibility and the Need for Discretion  

On the other side of the ledger, agencies need leeway to allocate limited resources. The OSC’s docket is already crowded with whistleblower complaints, merit‑system disputes, and other personnel matters. Compelling OSC to launch investigations into every alleged FOIA misstep could overwhelm the office, diverting attention from its core mission. The decision preserves that flexibility, allowing OSC to prioritize cases where statutory enforcement mechanisms (e.g., § 1214 corrective actions) are most appropriate.

### Legislative Reform: Clarifying OSC’s Obligations  

The tension highlighted by *America First Legal* points to an opportunity for Congress to amend the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) and FOIA enforcement provisions. Here are three concrete proposals:

1. **Separate Mandatory FOIA Enforcement Provision** – Insert language that makes OSC’s duty to investigate FOIA withholdings “mandatory” and “independent of the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger.” The statute could specify that OSC must issue a “notice of investigation” within a set timeframe and report its findings to the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB).  

2. **Statutory Right to Procedural Information** – Amend § 1216(c) to require OSC, when exercising its FOIA‑investigative authority, to provide complainants with the same notice and procedural safeguards mandated in § 1214. This would create a concrete injury when OSC fails to comply, satisfying standing requirements and giving courts a clearer basis for review.  

3. **Congressional Oversight Reporting** – Require OSC to submit an annual report to the Senate and House Judiciary Committees summarizing the number of FOIA investigations opened, closed, and the outcomes (e.g., corrective actions, disciplinary recommendations). Transparency would incentivize OSC to act and allow Congress to monitor whether the office is fulfilling its FOIA oversight role.  

These reforms would preserve OSC’s discretion where appropriate while carving out a narrower, enforceable duty for FOIA‑related inquiries. By attaching a concrete procedural right to the investigative process, Congress would also alleviate the standing hurdles that have so far blocked judicial review.

### The Road Ahead  

The D.C. Circuit’s opinion does not close the door on OSC’s FOIA enforcement responsibilities; it merely reaffirms that, under current law, OSC retains broad discretion. For litigants, the practical takeaway is that a FOIA suit remains the primary lever to compel agency disclosure, while OSC investigations serve more as an ancillary option that may or may not be invoked.

For policymakers and oversight advocates, the decision is a call to action. If the goal is a more accountable federal bureaucracy—one that respects the public’s right to know—Congress must speak more clearly. By tightening the statutory language around OSC’s investigative duties, Congress can preserve the flexibility agencies need while ensuring that the promise of “shall investigate” translates into actual oversight.

In the meantime, FOIA practitioners should continue to push for enforcement through the courts, while keeping an eye on any future legislative moves that might recalibrate the balance between agency discretion and statutory accountability. The *America First Legal* case reminds us that the judiciary will respect the agency’s room to maneuver, but it also shows that when that room feels too expansive, the remedy may ultimately lie not in the courts but on Capitol Hill.



## FOIA, the OSC, and the Future of Judicial‑Recusal Transparency  

**Why this case matters**  
At first glance, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* appears to be a routine standing dispute settled on procedural grounds. Yet the opinions on both the district and D.C. Circuit courts raise a substantive question that could reshape how litigants obtain agency records on judicial‑recusal investigations: whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is statutorily obliged to investigate an alleged Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violation even when the “trigger” provision of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is not satisfied. The answer—no, at least for now—leaves an opaque DOJ policy in place: the Department of Justice will not search employee emails for FOIA‑responsive material without the employee’s consent. That policy, if left unchecked, can blunt public insight into whether federal prosecutors have appropriately recused themselves from matters involving political allies, a cornerstone of the rule of law.

Below we walk through the court’s reasoning, assess its doctrinal underpinnings, and explore the practical implications for future FOIA challenges to agency policies that sit at the intersection of personnel practice and information disclosure.

---

### The court’s reasoning in a nutshell  

Judge Katsas began by framing the dispute as one of Article III standing, not of statutory interpretation. The D.C. Circuit affirmed that a plaintiff must show (1) a concrete, particularized injury, (2) causation, and (3) redressability. The court applied the “relaxed” standing analysis that courts sometimes grant for procedural‑right claims, but emphasized that even that lenient standard requires a real, tangible injury tied to the statutory entitlement.

**First standing theory – the “information‑from‑DOJ” injury**  
AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s email‑search policy prevented the foundation from receiving the records it sought under FOIA. The court rejected this, noting that the injury is traceable directly to DOJ—not OSC. To claim causation, AFL would need to prove that OSC’s investigation would have compelled DOJ to produce the emails, a chain of events that (a) involves discretionary decisions by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and DOJ, and (b) is speculative. The court cited *Heckler v. Chaney* for the principle that courts cannot compel an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion, and *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* for the requirement that causation not be based on conjecture about independent actors.

**Second standing theory – the “information‑about‑the‑investigation” injury**  
AFL also claimed a right to the notices and procedural information that would accompany an OSC investigation under § 1216(c), borrowing the language of § 1214’s notice requirements. The court pointed out that § 1216 does not create an entitlement to receive those procedural documents; it merely gives OSC discretion to investigate. Because OSC may lawfully decline to follow § 1214’s notice regime, AFL lacks a concrete injury. The court reinforced that “procedural rights in a vacuum” do not confer standing (citing *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*).

Having found no injury in either theory, the panel concluded that there is no Article III case or controversy, and therefore the district court’s judgment must be vacated and dismissed for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

---

### Doctrinal takeaways

1. **Standing as a gatekeeper for FOIA policy disputes**  
   The decision underscores how standing continues to be the primary obstacle for litigants seeking judicial review of agency FOIA policies. Even when a statute—here, § 1216(a)(3)— appears to impose a duty on an agency, the doctrine of discretion (Heckler) and the lack of a concrete entitlement can nullify a plaintiff’s claim. In effect, the ruling reaffirms that agencies are largely insulated from court‑ordered investigations absent the statutory trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

2. **The “trigger” provision remains controlling**  
   The court’s analysis bolsters the view that the four‑prong trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F) (court order, attorneys’ fees, finding of arbitrary or capricious withholding, and a request for disciplinary action) is the sole gateway for OSC enforcement under FOIA. The D.C. Circuit rejected the district court’s reading that § 1216 “mandates” an investigation irrespective of the trigger. This reading aligns with longstanding administrative‑law precedent that agencies may not be forced to exercise enforcement discretion.

3. **Procedural‑right claims must be tied to a concrete statutory entitlement**  
   The panel leaned heavily on *Summers* and *Akers* (the “procedural right in vacuo” doctrine). Unless a statute confers a substantive right to receive information about an agency’s investigative process, a plaintiff cannot sustain a procedural‑injury claim. This distinction will dominate future FOIA litigation that seeks to compel agencies to disclose internal investigative logs, emails, or decision‑making memos.

4. **Potential narrowing of “relaxed” standing for procedural injuries**  
   Some lower courts have been generous in granting standing for procedural violations (e.g., *Center for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ.*). This opinion signals a modest retreat: the D.C. Circuit is willing to apply the relaxed test but still demands a concrete interest that is “affected” by the procedural breach. The bar is now higher for litigants who cannot point to a directly enumerated entitlement.

---

### What this means for transparency of judicial‑recusal investigations

The immediate practical effect is that the DOJ can continue its “no‑consent‑search” policy for employee emails without fear of being forced into an OSC investigation. That policy is especially salient for recusal inquiries, where investigators often need to verify whether a DOJ attorney communicated privately with a judge or litigant. By insulating email records from routine FOIA searches, the DOJ can effectively keep those communications out of public view unless a court order or other trigger forces disclosure.

**Comparative agency practices**  
Other agencies—such as the Department of Health and Human Services and the Federal Trade Commission—have taken less restrictive stances, allowing email searches when the request is narrowly tailored and does not implicate privacy interests beyond the employee’s expectations. The DOJ’s approach diverges from the general FOIA “search and produce” paradigm and may invite scrutiny under the “arbitrary or capricious” standard of FOIA’s own adjudication provisions (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(6)(A)). However, absent a standing case, courts are unlikely to compel a policy change.

**Potential litigation pathways**  

1. **Statutory amendment** – Congress could amend § 1216 to expressly require OSC investigations whenever an agency adopts a policy that effectively narrows FOIA rights, regardless of the trigger language. Such an amendment would create a concrete entitlement and likely survive the standing hurdle.

2. **Alternative plaintiffs** – Entities that suffer a “direct” injury—perhaps a journalist denied access to a specific email that is material to a news story—might have a stronger standing claim. The injury must be traceable to the agency’s refusal to search, not merely to OSC’s inaction. If a plaintiff can show that the agency’s policy directly caused the denial of records, that could satisfy causation.

3. **Class‑action or “entity‑standing” claims** – Some courts have entertained standing for organizations that represent the informational interests of a class of members. While the D.C. Circuit remains cautious, strategic framing of the claim as a “representative” injury could, in theory, broaden the pool of litigants.

4. **Administrative challenges** – Plaintiffs could file a formal FOIA appeal challenging the policy as “unreasonable” under the FOIA’s “reasonable determination” standard, then appeal the agency’s decision to the Federal Circuit. This route sidesteps standing because it proceeds through the agency’s own adjudicatory mechanisms, although the ultimate relief would still be limited to production of records, not a forced OSC investigation.

---

### Looking ahead: Will courts bite on FOIA‑policy disputes without standing?

The *AFL* decision suggests that, for the foreseeable future, standing will remain the “gatekeeper” for FOIA policy challenges that rely on the OSC’s enforcement powers. Courts appear unwilling to reinterpret § 1216 to override the statutory trigger, and they are equally reluctant to fashion a new procedural‑right doctrine that would give litigants a seat at the table.

That said, the decision may catalyze legislative action. Lawmakers who are concerned about the opacity of DOJ recusal investigations could draft amendments that either (a) broaden the scope of “arbitrary or capricious” findings to include policies that blanket‑exclude email searches, or (b) impose a mandatory OSC investigative duty whenever an agency adopts a policy that materially restricts FOIA access.

In the meantime, practitioners will need to be strategic about the plaintiffs they select and the procedural avenues they pursue. Direct FOIA appeals, targeted “information‑access” suits against the agency itself, and, where possible, the use of the “record‑keeping” exception (if the email is deemed a “record” under FOIA) may offer the most viable paths to disclosure.

---

### Bottom line

*America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* does not reshape the law of FOIA or the OSC’s enforcement powers in any substantive way; it reaffirms that those powers are tied to a narrowly defined trigger. Yet the case spotlights a policy gap—DOJ’s refusal to search employee emails without consent—that can shield potentially significant information about judicial recusal from public view. Unless Congress steps in or a litigant can demonstrate a concrete informational injury directly tied to the policy, the status quo is likely to persist. For advocates of transparency, the battle now shifts from the courtroom to the Capitol and to the front lines of FOIA litigation strategies.
