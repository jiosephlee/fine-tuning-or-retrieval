Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Procedural Background and the Parties’ Claims  

In order to understand the appellate court’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, it is essential first to grasp the factual and procedural context that brought the dispute before the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. This chapter walks through the statutory framework governing the Office of Special Counsel (OSC), the relevant FOIA enforcement provision, the concrete requests made by America First Legal Foundation (AFL), the district court’s handling of those requests, and the procedural posture on appeal.  

## The Office of Special Counsel (OSC): Statutory Mandate and Enforcement Powers under the Civil Service Reform Act  

The OSC is a federal independent investigative and prosecutorial agency created to safeguard the integrity of the civil service. Its authority derives from the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, codified principally at 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214‑1216. The Act empowers OSC to investigate “prohibited personnel practices” (PPPs) committed by federal employees and, when appropriate, to bring enforcement actions before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB).  

Two portions of the Act are most salient here:  

1. **Section 1214** – Provides the procedural scheme for “corrective actions” when a PPP is found. It requires OSC to notify the complaining party of investigative progress and to give the agency an opportunity to respond.  

2. **Section 1216** – Extends OSC’s investigative authority to “certain other forms of misconduct” by federal employees. Subsection (a)(3) expressly commands OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” i.e., a FOIA violation. Subsection (c) further clarifies that OSC may pursue the same corrective or disciplinary remedies available under §§ 1214 and 1215 as it would for a traditional PPP.  

Thus, while OSC’s primary mission is to address personnel misconduct, Congress also tasked it with a limited FOIA‑enforcement role, allowing OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary withholdings and, if warranted, seek corrective or disciplinary relief.  

## Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and Its Specific Enforcement Provision (552(a)(4)(F)(i))  

FOIA, 5 U.S.C. §§ 552‑552a, establishes a presumptive right of public access to agency records, subject to nine statutory exemptions. Enforcement is generally “exhaustive”—the aggrieved party must first seek relief through the agency’s own appeal process and, if still dissatisfied, may file a civil action in district court.  

FOIA contains a unique, narrow enforcement trigger for OSC in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). When a court orders an agency to produce records it had improperly withheld, awards attorneys’ fees, **and specifically finds that the agency acted “arbitrarily or capriciously” with respect to the withholding**, the statute obligates OSC to “promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action against the responsible employee is warranted. In other words, OSC’s FOIA‑enforcement role is conditional on a judicial finding of arbitrariness; absent that finding, OSC has no mandatory duty to act.  

The distinction between the statutory duties under §§ 1216(a)(3) and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is at the heart of the case. The former appears to create a standalone investigative duty, whereas the latter ties OSC’s action to a court’s finding. The district court and the appellate court differ on how these provisions interact.  

## America First Legal Foundation’s (AFL) FOIA Request to the DOJ and the DOJ’s Partial Compliance  

AFL, a nonprofit advocacy organization, sought DOJ records concerning the department’s internal process for judicial recusals. Specifically, it requested **emails exchanged between an outside attorney and the Civil Division’s employees**. The DOJ denied the request on a novel policy ground: it claimed that an agency may not search **employee** emails for responsive material without the employee’s consent.  

AFL sued the DOJ under FOIA, alleging that the policy was an impermissible restriction on disclosure. While the litigation was pending, the DOJ began producing some of the requested emails and continued to produce additional material as the case progressed. The adequacy of the DOJ’s production is **not** contested in the appeal; the issue is whether the agency’s underlying policy—refusing to search employee emails without consent—should be investigated and potentially remedied by OSC.  

## AFL’s Request for OSC to Investigate Alleged Illegal FOIA Policy Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)  

Relying on the language of § 1216(a)(3), AFL sent a formal request to OSC, asking the agency to **investigate the DOJ’s policy** as an alleged arbitrary and capricious FOIA withholding. AFL argued that the statutory phrase “shall… conduct an investigation” creates an **imperative** duty for OSC, regardless of whether any FOIA‑court finding exists under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).  

OSC, however, rejected the request. Its position was that the three “predicates” in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—a court order compelling production, an award of attorneys’ fees, and a finding of arbitrariness—had not been satisfied; therefore, OSC lacked jurisdiction to open an investigation. OSC further contended that even if § 1216 imposes a duty, § 1216(c) reserves to OSC the **discretion** to determine whether and how to investigate, consistent with prior case law.  

## OSC’s Refusal to Investigate and the District Court’s Partial‑Grant, Partial‑Dismiss Outcome  

AFL sued OSC, alleging that OSC’s refusal violated its statutory right to an investigation under § 1216. The United States District Court for the District of Columbia entered a mixed judgment:  

* **Partial Grant** – The court held that the language of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does **not** restrict OSC’s broader investigative authority under § 1216. Accordingly, the district court concluded that OSC’s refusal was based on a **legal error**.  

* **Partial Dismiss** – Despite finding an error, the district court declined to order OSC to investigate. It reasoned that § 1216(c) gives OSC **discretionary** authority over whether to pursue an investigation and how to conduct it. Consequently, the court **remanded** the case to OSC for further consideration of its discretionary choices.  

After remand, OSC again declined to investigate. AFL then moved for entry of final judgment, prompting the district court to dismiss AFL’s remaining claims, holding that the procedural avenues for compelling OSC had been exhausted.  

## Procedural Posture: District Court’s Remand, Second Refusal, Entry of Judgment, and Appeal to the D.C. Circuit  

The procedural timeline can be summarized as follows:  

1. **Initial FOIA suit against DOJ** – AFL sues; DOJ produces some records; dispute centers on DOJ’s email‑search policy.  
2. **AFL’s request to OSC** – AFL petitions OSC to investigate the policy under § 1216(a)(3).  
3. **OSC’s refusal** – OSC declines, citing lack of the three predicates in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).  
4. **District court action** – Issues a mixed opinion: finds OSC’s reliance on § 552 erroneous but emphasizes OSC’s discretion under § 1216(c); remands.  
5. **Second OSC refusal** – OSC again declines to investigate after remand.  
6. **Entry of final judgment** – District court dismisses AFL’s claims, concluding that no further relief is available.  
7. **Appeal** – AFL appeals the dismissal to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. The appellate court reviews the district court’s legal conclusions **de novo** (i.e., afresh) because the issues are pure questions of law, but it first must determine whether the district court even had jurisdiction to hear the case.  

The D.C. Circuit therefore framed its analysis around **Article III standing**. If AFL lacks a constitutionally cognizable injury, the district court’s judgment is void for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction, rendering any further discussion of OSC’s discretion moot.
