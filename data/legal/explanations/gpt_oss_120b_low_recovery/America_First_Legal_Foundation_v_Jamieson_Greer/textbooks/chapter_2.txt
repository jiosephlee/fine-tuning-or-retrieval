Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Governing Law – Standing Doctrine and the Limits of Procedural Rights  

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the D.C. Circuit was called upon to decide whether the plaintiff, America First Legal (AFL), could sue the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) for refusing to open a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) investigation.  The court’s answer hinged on the Constitution’s case‑or‑controversy requirement and, specifically, on whether AFL satisfied the elements of Article III standing.  This chapter unpacks the standing doctrine that the court applied, explains the “relaxed” standards for procedural‑injury claims, and shows why those standards were insufficient in this case.

## Article III Case‑or‑Controversy Requirement and the Lujan Test  

Article III, § 2 of the United States Constitution limits the federal judiciary to adjudicating actual “Cases” and “Controversies.”  The Supreme Court has translated this limitation into a four‑part test—often called the *Lujan* test—requiring a plaintiff to demonstrate:

1. **Injury in fact** – an invasion of a legally protected interest that is concrete, particularized, and either actual or imminent.  
2. **Causation** – a fairly direct causal connection between the injury and the defendant’s conduct.  
3. **Redressability** – a favorable judicial decision must be likely to remedy the injury.  
4. **Zone of interest** (implicit in many decisions) – the plaintiff’s interest must fall within the purpose of the statute or constitutional provision invoked.

These elements must each be plausibly alleged at the pleading stage.  The *Lujan* framework is applied uniformly, whether the plaintiff claims a substantive right (e.g., a statutory entitlement to benefits) or a procedural right (e.g., a statutory duty to investigate).  If any element is missing, the court lacks subject‑matter jurisdiction and must dismiss the case for lack of standing.

## Pleading Standing at the Motion‑to‑Dismiss Stage  

When a case is challenged under Rule 12(b)(6), the plaintiff must set forth **plausible** facts supporting each element of standing.  The D.C. Circuit has articulated this pleading standard in *Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack*, 808 F.3d 905, 913 (D.C. Cir. 2015).  The court looks for “more than sheer speculation” but does not require the plaintiff to prove the claim at this early stage.  The plaintiff’s complaint, together with any supplemental briefing ordered by the court, must set out enough factual context to allow the judge to infer that the injury is real, the defendant caused it, and the court can redress it.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the district court ordered both parties to file supplemental briefs precisely because standing had not been addressed in the original pleadings.  The appellate court then evaluated whether those briefs supplied the requisite factual allegations.

## Procedural‑Injury Standing: Relaxed Standards and Their Limits  

Procedural rights—statutory guarantees that a government agency will follow a particular process—receive somewhat softer standing analysis.  Courts recognize that a procedural violation can be a *means* of protecting a concrete interest, and they therefore relax the *imminence* and *redressability* prongs while preserving the core requirements of injury‑in‑fact and causation.

The D.C. Circuit illustrates this approach in *Center for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005).  There, the court held that plaintiffs alleging a violation of a procedural right “designed to protect their threatened concrete interest” need not show that the injury is imminent in the same way as a purely substantive claim.  Nonetheless, the plaintiff must still demonstrate:

* That the procedural right is **statutorily conferred** and **meant to safeguard a concrete interest**; and  
* That the **violation of the procedural right** caused a **concrete injury** to that interest.

A parallel limitation appears in *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009).  The Supreme Court warned that a “procedural right in vacuo”—that is, a right to a government process that does not itself grant any substantive benefit—does not, by itself, confer Article III standing.  The plaintiff must show that the denial of the process harms a distinct, legally protected interest.

In *America First Legal Foundation*, AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 deprived it of a procedural entitlement.  The court applied the relaxed standards, but found that AFL still failed to identify a **concrete interest** that the procedural violation harmed.

## The “Procedural Right in Vacuo” Principle  

A “procedural right in vacuo” arises when a statute creates a duty for the government to act (e.g., to investigate) without giving the plaintiff any direct entitlement to the outcome of that duty.  The Supreme Court’s language in *Summers* makes clear that such a bare procedural guarantee is insufficient for standing:

> “The deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient to confer Article III standing.” (*Summers*, 555 U.S. at 496).

The principle serves two purposes. First, it prevents courts from issuing advisory opinions about how agencies *should* act when no plaintiff can demonstrate personal harm. Second, it preserves the constitutional separation of powers by limiting judicial interference with agency discretion absent a concrete dispute.

AFL’s claim hinged on a statutory provision—§ 1216(a)(3)—that instructs OSC to “conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings.  The court observed that the provision **mandates a duty** but does **not create an entitlement** to receive the investigation’s results, nor does it guarantee any remedial action.  Consequently, the procedural right to an investigation was “in vacuo” for AFL because the statute did not tie that right to a concrete, protectable interest that AFL possessed.

## The Informational‑Standing Doctrine  

When plaintiffs seek access to government information, courts apply an “informational standing” analysis.  The doctrine, articulated in cases such as *American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld*, 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011), and *Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State*, 444 F.3d 614, 618 (D.C. Cir. 2006), holds that a plaintiff may establish injury‑in‑fact by showing:

1. A **statutory entitlement** to the information; and  
2. That the government’s refusal **denied** that entitlement.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Akins v. State of Iowa* (commonly cited as *Akins*), 524 U.S. 11 (1998), reinforced this principle, emphasizing that a plaintiff’s grievance must be “tied to an injury that is personal and concrete.”

In *America First Legal Foundation*, AFL asserted two informational injuries:

* **First**, that DOJ’s alleged failure to produce the requested emails violated AFL’s FOIA right.  
* **Second**, that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of the procedural notices and information that would accompany a § 1214 investigation.

The court rejected both theories.  Regarding the first, the injury was traced to DOJ, not OSC, breaking the causal link required for standing.  Regarding the second, the court noted that § 1216 does **not** create a statutory entitlement to the information produced in a § 1214‑style investigation; thus, AFL could not claim informational standing absent such an entitlement.  The “informational‑standing doctrine” therefore did not rescue AFL’s claim.

## Why AFL’s Standing Claims Failed  

Putting the doctrines together, the D.C. Circuit’s analysis unfolded as follows:

1. **Injury‑in‑Fact** – AFL could not point to a concrete, particularized injury.  Its alleged procedural injury was a “right in vacuo,” and its informational injury was either attributable to DOJ (not OSC) or lacked a statutory entitlement under § 1214.  

2. **Causation** – The causal chain AFL proposed (OSC investigates → OSC recommends corrective action → MSPB disciplines DOJ employees → DOJ releases records) was speculative.  The Supreme Court has warned that causation “cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (*FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024)).  Because the chain involved discretionary decisions by OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and DOJ, the plaintiff could not establish a direct causal link.

3. **Redressability** – Even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, the court could not guarantee that such an order would produce the records AFL sought.  Redressability hinges on whether the judgment “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury.  The speculative nature of the chain again blocked this prong (*Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC*, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025)).

Because AFL failed to satisfy any of the three essential elements—injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability—the court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing.  Accordingly, the district court’s judgment was vacated, and the case was remanded for dismissal on jurisdictional grounds.

---

**Take‑away for Law Students**  
When evaluating standing, always start with the *Lujan* elements and ask:  

* Does the plaintiff have a concrete, personal injury?  
* Is the defendant’s conduct the cause of that injury?  
* Will a favorable judgment likely cure the injury?  

If the plaintiff’s claim rests on a procedural right, confirm that the procedural right is not “in vacuo” and that its denial harms a distinct substantive interest.  Finally, remember that informational standing requires a clear statutory entitlement to the information at issue.  *America First Legal Foundation* demonstrates how a court applies these principles in a real‑world dispute involving agency investigations and FOIA.
