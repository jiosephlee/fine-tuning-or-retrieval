Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What procedural steps are required when a district court dismisses a case for lack of standing after a supplemental briefing order?
Question:
The appellate court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction. In practice, what does the district court need to do next? Does it issue a new order, a summary judgment, or a dismissal with prejudice? How does this affect any possibility of a future appeal?
Answer:
**Answer**

When the appellate court vacates the district court’s judgment and **remands “with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction,”** the district court’s only remaining duty is to enter a **jurisdictional dismissal** of the case.  

The opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* makes this clear.  After concluding that AFL lacked Article III standing – and therefore that the district court never had subject‑matter jurisdiction – the D.C. Circuit *“vacate[s] the district court’s judgment and remand[s] with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.”*  The circuit does not tell the district court to “re‑try” the case, issue a summary‑judgment ruling, or grant any further relief; it simply orders a **dismissal** because the court never had the power to hear the claim.

### What the district court must do

1. **Enter a dismissal order** – The appropriate procedural vehicle is a **Rule 12(b)(1) dismissal** (or the analogous “lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction” ground under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure).  
   * The dismissal must be **with prejudice** because the court lacks the jurisdiction to entertain the claim at all.  A jurisdictional dismissal is not a “dismissal on the merits” that could be reopened; it is a final bar to the case in that court.  

2. **State the basis** – The order should briefly recite the appellate court’s finding that the plaintiff failed to establish Article III standing, and therefore the district court never possessed subject‑matter jurisdiction.  No additional factual findings or legal analysis are required.

3. **Seal the docket** – Because the case is dismissed for jurisdictional reasons, the docket is closed.  No further briefing, discovery, or motions (including a motion for summary judgment) are permitted.

### Effect on further appeals

* **No further appeal from the district court** – A dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction is *non‑appealable* because the district court is simply acknowledging that it never had the power to decide the case.  The appellate court’s decision is already the final word on the jurisdictional issue.

* **Renewed filing in another forum** – The plaintiff may try to bring a new action in a court that properly has jurisdiction (for example, by establishing a concrete injury and redressability).  The prior dismissal, however, is **preclusive** only insofar as it shows the earlier suit was barred by lack of standing; it does not foreclose a fresh suit that overcomes the standing defect.

### Summary

In practice, after a circuit court vacates and remands “with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction,” the district court must:

* Issue a **Rule 12(b)(1) dismissal** (i.e., an order dismissing the case for lack of jurisdiction),  
* Do so **with prejudice**, and  
* Close the case, leaving no further appellate route from that judgment.

The only way the plaintiff could proceed is to file a new action that satisfies the standing requirements identified by the appellate court.
