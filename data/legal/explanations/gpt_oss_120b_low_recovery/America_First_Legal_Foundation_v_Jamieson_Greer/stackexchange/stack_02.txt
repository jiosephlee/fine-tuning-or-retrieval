Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What is the proper test for procedural‑injury standing under the “relaxed” standard, and why did the D.C. Circuit reject AFL’s claim?
Question:
The opinion cites cases like *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education* and *Fla. Audubon Society v. Bentsen* for a relaxed standing analysis when a procedural right is violated. What are the elements a plaintiff must prove under that relaxed test, and how did AFL fail to meet them according to the court? Specifically, why was the alleged informational injury not enough?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “relaxed” standing analysis that the D.C. Circuit cites in *Center for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d at 1157, and *Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen*, 94 F.3d at 664‑65, applies only when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a **procedural statutory right** that is *designed to protect* a *concrete* interest.  
Even under that relaxed test the plaintiff must still satisfy the three classic Article III elements:

1. **Injury in fact** – a concrete, particularized “actual or imminent” injury, not a bare procedural grievance.  
2. **Causation** – the injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct.  
3. **Redressability** – a favorable court order must be likely to cure the injury.

The “relaxation” merely allows the court to be somewhat less strict about *imminence* and about whether the procedural right itself is the injury; the plaintiff must still show that the procedural violation caused harm to a concrete interest and that the court can remedy that harm. *Center* expressly states that the court “relaxes—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.”

---

### How AFL failed to meet each prong

#### 1. Injury in fact  

AFL claimed two “informational injuries”:

* **(a) The FOIA records it sought from DOJ.**  
  The court says this injury is *not* caused by OSC’s refusal to investigate. The records are a right that belongs to AFL **under FOIA**, not under the procedural right granted by § 1216. Because the injury is traceable to DOJ’s own withholding, not to any statutory entitlement that OSC’s procedural duty creates, AFL does not have a concrete injury stemming from OSC’s conduct.

* **(b) The “notice and information” that would be generated by an OSC investigation.**  
  Even if OSC were required to investigate under § 1216(a)(3), the statute does **not** create an entitlement to receive the procedural notices that § 1214 imposes in prohibited‑personnel‑practice investigations. The court notes that OSC’s decision to apply § 1214’s procedural safeguards to a § 1216 investigation is discretionary (citing *Carson v. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d at 495‑96). Because no statutory right to that information exists, AFL’s alleged injury is too abstract to be “concrete and particularized.”

Thus, AFL satisfied neither the “informational injury” claim under the relaxed standard.

#### 2. Causation  

For the first theory, the opinion calls the causal chain “speculative and attenuated.” AFL would have to prove that OSC’s investigation would *prompt* the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to discipline DOJ employees, which would then *cause* DOJ to produce the records. The court points out that:

* The chain relies on the independent choices of **three** agencies (OSC, MSPB, DOJ).  
* Supreme Court precedent (*FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. at 383) bars reliance on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors.  
* *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. at 831‑32, makes clear that an agency’s discretionary enforcement decisions are not subject to judicial compulsion.

Because AFL cannot show that OSC’s (hypothetical) investigation is the *proximate* cause of DOJ’s withholding, the causation prong fails.

For the second theory, the only alleged cause is OSC’s refusal to provide procedural notices. Since no statutory entitlement to those notices exists, there is no “but‑for” link between OSC’s conduct and any concrete injury.

#### 3. Redressability  

Even assuming the injury existed, the court finds that a favorable judgment would not likely cure it. Redressability requires that the court’s order be *capable of remedying* the injury, not merely that it “might persuade” other agencies to act. The opinion emphasizes that:

* A judicial order cannot bind agencies that are not parties (MSPB, DOJ).  
* The discretionary nature of OSC’s investigative power means that even a court order compelling an investigation would not guarantee that OSC would issue the notices AFL wants or that the MSPB would take enforcement action.  
* The Supreme Court’s recent guidance (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. at 293‑94) limits redressability to the concrete effect of the judgment on the parties, not on third‑party actors.

Consequently, AFL’s alleged injuries are not redressable by the relief it seeks.

---

### The bottom line  

The “relaxed” procedural‑injury test still demands a **concrete injury, a causal connection, and a realistic chance that a court order will fix the injury**. AFL’s claims:

* lack a concrete injury because the statutes at issue do not grant it an entitlement to the information it alleges it is missing;  
* fail the causation requirement because the link between OSC’s (non‑)investigation and DOJ’s withholding is speculative; and  
* are not redressable because the court cannot compel the independent agencies whose actions would be needed to cure the alleged harm.

For those reasons, the D.C. Circuit correctly concluded that AFL has no Article III standing and dismissed the case.
