Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does the causation/redressability analysis differ for informational injuries versus procedural injuries in Article III standing?
Question:
The court discusses both an informational injury (failure to get FOIA records) and a procedural injury (failure to receive OSC investigation notices). It finds both lacking causation and redressability, but I'm unclear on the standards applied to each type of injury. What is the distinction between the two analyses, and why can’t a court’s order compelling OSC to investigate satisfy either requirement?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Court treats the two “injuries” AFL asserts in very different ways because they fall under two different strands of Article III standing doctrine.

* An **informational injury** (the failure to get the FOIA records) is judged under the ordinary “red‑blue‑red” test of *Luján*: the plaintiff must show a concrete, particularized harm, that the injury is caused by the defendant, and that a favorable judicial decree is likely to remedy it. The Court finds both causation and redressability missing – the injury is traceable to the DOJ, not to OSC, and any chain that would link an OSC order to DOJ’s eventual production is speculative.

* A **procedural injury** (the denial of OSC’s investigation notices) is evaluated under the “relaxed” standard that applies when a plaintiff alleges a statutory **procedural right**. Even under that more lenient analysis, the plaintiff must still demonstrate (i) a concrete interest that is injured by the procedural violation, and (ii) that the court’s order can realistically cure that injury. The Court holds that AFL has no statutory entitlement to the notice‑and‑information regime of § 1214, because § 1216 investigations are discretionary and do not create a right to receive those procedural documents. Consequently, AFL lacks a concrete injury, and a court order compelling an investigation would not be redressable.

Because neither theory satisfies its respective standing requirements, the Court concludes that AFL lacks Article III standing altogether. A judicial mandate ordering OSC to investigate cannot cure either the informational injury (the DOJ’s refusal to produce records) or the procedural injury (the lack of a statutory right to notice), so the order would not meet the causation or redressability prongs for either analysis.
