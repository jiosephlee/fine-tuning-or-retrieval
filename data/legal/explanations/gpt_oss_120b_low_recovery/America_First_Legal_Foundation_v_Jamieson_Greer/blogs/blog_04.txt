Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## FOIA, the OSC, and the Future of Judicial‑Recusal Transparency  

**Why this case matters**  
At first glance, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* appears to be a routine standing dispute settled on procedural grounds. Yet the opinions on both the district and D.C. Circuit courts raise a substantive question that could reshape how litigants obtain agency records on judicial‑recusal investigations: whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is statutorily obliged to investigate an alleged Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violation even when the “trigger” provision of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is not satisfied. The answer—no, at least for now—leaves an opaque DOJ policy in place: the Department of Justice will not search employee emails for FOIA‑responsive material without the employee’s consent. That policy, if left unchecked, can blunt public insight into whether federal prosecutors have appropriately recused themselves from matters involving political allies, a cornerstone of the rule of law.

Below we walk through the court’s reasoning, assess its doctrinal underpinnings, and explore the practical implications for future FOIA challenges to agency policies that sit at the intersection of personnel practice and information disclosure.

---

### The court’s reasoning in a nutshell  

Judge Katsas began by framing the dispute as one of Article III standing, not of statutory interpretation. The D.C. Circuit affirmed that a plaintiff must show (1) a concrete, particularized injury, (2) causation, and (3) redressability. The court applied the “relaxed” standing analysis that courts sometimes grant for procedural‑right claims, but emphasized that even that lenient standard requires a real, tangible injury tied to the statutory entitlement.

**First standing theory – the “information‑from‑DOJ” injury**  
AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s email‑search policy prevented the foundation from receiving the records it sought under FOIA. The court rejected this, noting that the injury is traceable directly to DOJ—not OSC. To claim causation, AFL would need to prove that OSC’s investigation would have compelled DOJ to produce the emails, a chain of events that (a) involves discretionary decisions by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and DOJ, and (b) is speculative. The court cited *Heckler v. Chaney* for the principle that courts cannot compel an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion, and *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* for the requirement that causation not be based on conjecture about independent actors.

**Second standing theory – the “information‑about‑the‑investigation” injury**  
AFL also claimed a right to the notices and procedural information that would accompany an OSC investigation under § 1216(c), borrowing the language of § 1214’s notice requirements. The court pointed out that § 1216 does not create an entitlement to receive those procedural documents; it merely gives OSC discretion to investigate. Because OSC may lawfully decline to follow § 1214’s notice regime, AFL lacks a concrete injury. The court reinforced that “procedural rights in a vacuum” do not confer standing (citing *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*).

Having found no injury in either theory, the panel concluded that there is no Article III case or controversy, and therefore the district court’s judgment must be vacated and dismissed for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

---

### Doctrinal takeaways

1. **Standing as a gatekeeper for FOIA policy disputes**  
   The decision underscores how standing continues to be the primary obstacle for litigants seeking judicial review of agency FOIA policies. Even when a statute—here, § 1216(a)(3)— appears to impose a duty on an agency, the doctrine of discretion (Heckler) and the lack of a concrete entitlement can nullify a plaintiff’s claim. In effect, the ruling reaffirms that agencies are largely insulated from court‑ordered investigations absent the statutory trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

2. **The “trigger” provision remains controlling**  
   The court’s analysis bolsters the view that the four‑prong trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F) (court order, attorneys’ fees, finding of arbitrary or capricious withholding, and a request for disciplinary action) is the sole gateway for OSC enforcement under FOIA. The D.C. Circuit rejected the district court’s reading that § 1216 “mandates” an investigation irrespective of the trigger. This reading aligns with longstanding administrative‑law precedent that agencies may not be forced to exercise enforcement discretion.

3. **Procedural‑right claims must be tied to a concrete statutory entitlement**  
   The panel leaned heavily on *Summers* and *Akers* (the “procedural right in vacuo” doctrine). Unless a statute confers a substantive right to receive information about an agency’s investigative process, a plaintiff cannot sustain a procedural‑injury claim. This distinction will dominate future FOIA litigation that seeks to compel agencies to disclose internal investigative logs, emails, or decision‑making memos.

4. **Potential narrowing of “relaxed” standing for procedural injuries**  
   Some lower courts have been generous in granting standing for procedural violations (e.g., *Center for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ.*). This opinion signals a modest retreat: the D.C. Circuit is willing to apply the relaxed test but still demands a concrete interest that is “affected” by the procedural breach. The bar is now higher for litigants who cannot point to a directly enumerated entitlement.

---

### What this means for transparency of judicial‑recusal investigations

The immediate practical effect is that the DOJ can continue its “no‑consent‑search” policy for employee emails without fear of being forced into an OSC investigation. That policy is especially salient for recusal inquiries, where investigators often need to verify whether a DOJ attorney communicated privately with a judge or litigant. By insulating email records from routine FOIA searches, the DOJ can effectively keep those communications out of public view unless a court order or other trigger forces disclosure.

**Comparative agency practices**  
Other agencies—such as the Department of Health and Human Services and the Federal Trade Commission—have taken less restrictive stances, allowing email searches when the request is narrowly tailored and does not implicate privacy interests beyond the employee’s expectations. The DOJ’s approach diverges from the general FOIA “search and produce” paradigm and may invite scrutiny under the “arbitrary or capricious” standard of FOIA’s own adjudication provisions (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(6)(A)). However, absent a standing case, courts are unlikely to compel a policy change.

**Potential litigation pathways**  

1. **Statutory amendment** – Congress could amend § 1216 to expressly require OSC investigations whenever an agency adopts a policy that effectively narrows FOIA rights, regardless of the trigger language. Such an amendment would create a concrete entitlement and likely survive the standing hurdle.

2. **Alternative plaintiffs** – Entities that suffer a “direct” injury—perhaps a journalist denied access to a specific email that is material to a news story—might have a stronger standing claim. The injury must be traceable to the agency’s refusal to search, not merely to OSC’s inaction. If a plaintiff can show that the agency’s policy directly caused the denial of records, that could satisfy causation.

3. **Class‑action or “entity‑standing” claims** – Some courts have entertained standing for organizations that represent the informational interests of a class of members. While the D.C. Circuit remains cautious, strategic framing of the claim as a “representative” injury could, in theory, broaden the pool of litigants.

4. **Administrative challenges** – Plaintiffs could file a formal FOIA appeal challenging the policy as “unreasonable” under the FOIA’s “reasonable determination” standard, then appeal the agency’s decision to the Federal Circuit. This route sidesteps standing because it proceeds through the agency’s own adjudicatory mechanisms, although the ultimate relief would still be limited to production of records, not a forced OSC investigation.

---

### Looking ahead: Will courts bite on FOIA‑policy disputes without standing?

The *AFL* decision suggests that, for the foreseeable future, standing will remain the “gatekeeper” for FOIA policy challenges that rely on the OSC’s enforcement powers. Courts appear unwilling to reinterpret § 1216 to override the statutory trigger, and they are equally reluctant to fashion a new procedural‑right doctrine that would give litigants a seat at the table.

That said, the decision may catalyze legislative action. Lawmakers who are concerned about the opacity of DOJ recusal investigations could draft amendments that either (a) broaden the scope of “arbitrary or capricious” findings to include policies that blanket‑exclude email searches, or (b) impose a mandatory OSC investigative duty whenever an agency adopts a policy that materially restricts FOIA access.

In the meantime, practitioners will need to be strategic about the plaintiffs they select and the procedural avenues they pursue. Direct FOIA appeals, targeted “information‑access” suits against the agency itself, and, where possible, the use of the “record‑keeping” exception (if the email is deemed a “record” under FOIA) may offer the most viable paths to disclosure.

---

### Bottom line

*America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* does not reshape the law of FOIA or the OSC’s enforcement powers in any substantive way; it reaffirms that those powers are tied to a narrowly defined trigger. Yet the case spotlights a policy gap—DOJ’s refusal to search employee emails without consent—that can shield potentially significant information about judicial recusal from public view. Unless Congress steps in or a litigant can demonstrate a concrete informational injury directly tied to the policy, the status quo is likely to persist. For advocates of transparency, the battle now shifts from the courtroom to the Capitol and to the front lines of FOIA litigation strategies.
