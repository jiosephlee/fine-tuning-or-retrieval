Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## Statutory Interpretation of 5 U.S.C. § 1216: Does the OSC Have a Mandatory FOIA‑Investigation Duty?

**By [Your Name] – October 2025**  

*In America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer, the D.C. Circuit threw out a district‑court order compelling the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) alleged policy violation. The appellate panel resolved the case on the basis that the plaintiff, America First Legal (AFL), lacked Article III standing—but the opinions also contain a meticulous reading of the statutory language that raises a larger, unresolved question: Does § 1216(a)(3) create a non‑discretionary duty for OSC to open an investigation whenever a requester alleges an “arbitrary or capricious” FOIA withholding, irrespective of whether the FOIA‑specific trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) has been satisfied?*  

Below, I walk through the courts’ competing constructions of § 1216, place them in the context of longstanding interpretive rules governing “shall” language, and explore what the decision means for the future of OSC’s enforcement authority—and for FOIA litigants more broadly.

---

### 1. Why This Case Matters

FOIA’s enforcement regime is famously fragmented. Congress gave the OSC a parallel, statutory avenue to police “arbitrary or capricious” withholdings (5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)), but the same section also ties OSC’s investigative power to the procedural trigger that lives in the FOIA itself—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i). If Congress intended OSC to act whenever a private party alleges an arbitrary withholding, it would provide a potent, independent check on agency compliance, especially for requesters who lack the resources to bring a separate civil suit. Conversely, if the statutory language merely mirrors the FOIA trigger, OSC’s role remains limited to a “back‑stop” that only activates after a court has already ruled on the merits.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision does not settle the substantive question; it sidesteps it by dismissing the case for lack of standing. Yet the opinion supplies a detailed textual analysis that will likely be the foundation for any future challenge. Understanding how the court parsed “shall … conduct an investigation” versus “may investigate … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” is essential for practitioners who hope to leverage OSC as a FOIA enforcement tool.

---

### 2. The Competing Readings of § 1216(a)(3) and § 1216(c)

#### 2.1 The “Mandatory‑Investigation” View (District Court)

The district court read § 1216(a)(3) as imposing a *mandatory* duty:

> “OSC ‘shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.’”

The court emphasized the plain‑meaning of “shall” as command‑like, citing *United States v. Am. Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 292 U.S. 250 (1934), and held that the text does not condition the duty on the existence of a FOIA court order. The court further argued that § 1216(c) merely clarifies the *procedural* mechanics of the investigation (i.e., OSC may use the same remedial scheme as for prohibited personnel practices) but does not introduce a discretionary filter.

#### 2.2 The “Discretionary‑Trigger” View (Appellate Court)

Judge Katsas, writing for the D.C. Circuit, took a more nuanced approach. He read § 1216(a)(3) as **granting** OSC authority to investigate *if* the conditions in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are met:

> “The statutory scheme of § 1216 is designed to parallel the FOIA’s own enforcement provision. The reference to ‘arbitrary or capricious withholding’ must be read in light of the FOIA trigger that requires a court order, fee award, and a specific factual finding.”

The appellate opinion treated “shall” as *imperative* only within the confines of the statutory context. In other words, OSC *shall* investigate *when* the statutory conditions are satisfied; otherwise, the duty does not arise. The court leaned on the principle that a statute’s operative clause must be read in concert with the surrounding provisions, especially when Congress explicitly references another statute (here, the FOIA) as a prerequisite.

#### 2.3 The Role of § 1216(c)

Both opinions agree that § 1216(c) merely mirrors the procedural safeguards of § 1214/§ 1215. The district court saw it as confirming that, once an investigation is triggered, OSC follows the same notice and remedial scheme as it would for a prohibited personnel practice. The appellate court, however, stressed that § 1216(c) does *not* create a new substantive right to investigation; it only delineates the *process* if an investigation is lawfully initiated.

---

### 3. The “Shall” Rule: How Courts Usually Treat Imperative Language

The Supreme Court has consistently held that “shall” signals a *mandatory* obligation, but not every “shall” creates a *stand‑alone* duty. In *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946), the Court noted that a “shall” clause must be read in context to determine whether it imposes a duty, creates a limitation, or merely describes a permissible action.

The D.C. Circuit’s appellate reasoning aligns with this contextual approach. By anchoring the “shall” to the FOIA trigger, the court treats the statute as a *conditional* command—*shall* if *and only if* the antecedent condition (the FOIA trigger) occurs. This mirrors the Court’s analysis in *Nat’l Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius*, 567 U.S. ~ (2012), where a “shall” provision was held to be contingent on a statutory precondition.

Conversely, the district court’s reading reflects a more literalist stance, treating “shall” as an absolute command irrespective of surrounding language. This methodological split is not new; it mirrors the divide seen in *Garrett v. United States*, 131 S. Ct. 252 (2002), over whether “shall” in the federal sentencing guidelines created a mandatory duty or merely a strong recommendation.

---

### 4. Interplay with FOIA’s Own Enforcement Provision (§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i))

FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) expressly ties OSC’s involvement to a *court‑ordered* finding of arbitrary or capricious withholding. The statute compels OSC to act “promptly” once those conditions are satisfied. The question, then, is whether § 1216(a)(3) creates an *independent* pathway that bypasses the FOIA trigger, or merely provides a parallel procedural channel once the trigger is met.

The appellate panel treats the FOIA provision as the *gatekeeper*: without a judicial determination of arbitrariness (or a fee award and a specific factual finding), OSC’s “shall” never comes into force. This reading preserves the FOIA’s “court‑first” enforcement model and avoids creating a dual, potentially conflicting, enforcement scheme.

The district court, by contrast, viewed § 1216(a)(3) as an *independent* investigative mandate, arguing that Congress “intended” OSC to have authority to examine alleged policy violations *before* a litigant obtains a court order. The court relied on legislative history indicating that Congress was concerned about “systematic” FOIA violations that might otherwise go unaddressed.

Both interpretations are plausible; the statutes are undeniably ambiguous. The crux of the disagreement is whether Congress used the FOIA trigger as a *condition precedent* (the appellate view) or merely as a *reference point* to define the type of conduct OSC may investigate (the district court view).

---

### 5. Doctrinal Implications

#### 5.1 For Standing Doctrine

Even if the mandatory‑investigation reading were adopted, plaintiffs would still need to overcome the standing hurdle. The D.C. Circuit’s analysis is a reminder that procedural injuries alone rarely satisfy Article III requirements unless the plaintiff can show a concrete, particularized interest that is *directly* impaired. A future case that successfully pleads standing will likely need to demonstrate that the denial of an OSC investigation *immediately* harms a protected interest—perhaps by showing that OSC’s statutory duty includes a *right* to receive investigative updates, rather than a mere discretionary power.

#### 5.2 For OSC’s Enforcement Role

If the mandatory‑investigation view prevails, OSC could become a more proactive FOIA watchdog. Requesters could file a complaint directly with OSC, forcing an investigation without first obtaining a court order. This would echo the “private right of action” model that exists for many other statutes (e.g., the False Claims Act). However, it would also raise concerns about agency burden and potential duplication of effort with the FOIA’s existing judicial enforcement pathway.

#### 5.3 For Agency FOIA Policies

A mandatory‑investigation duty could incentivize agencies to tighten their FOIA search procedures. Knowing that OSC can initiate investigations on policy grounds alone would likely deter agencies from adopting overly restrictive email‑search policies, such as the “no‑search‑without‑consent” rule at issue here.

---

### 6. Potential Downstream Effects

1. **Legislative Clarification** – Congress may feel compelled to amend § 1216 to expressly state whether the FOIA trigger is a prerequisite. A clarifying amendment would eliminate the current interpretive deadlock.

2. **Strategic Litigation** – Plaintiffs may begin to structure FOIA suits to invoke both the FOIA’s judicial trigger *and* a direct OSC complaint, creating a “dual‑track” strategy that pressures agencies on two fronts.

3. **Judicial Review of OSC Discretion** – Assuming a future case survives standing, courts may be asked to review OSC’s discretionary choices under § 1216(c). The *Heckler v. Chaney* doctrine suggests that courts will be reluctant to compel agency action, but a clear statutory command (“shall”) could narrow the deference owed to OSC.

4. **Agency Administrative Burden** – A mandatory duty could significantly increase OSC’s workload, potentially prompting Congress to allocate additional resources or to set procedural thresholds (e.g., a minimum number of alleged violations before an investigation is launched).

---

### 7. Conclusion

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer leaves the substantive question of OSC’s FOIA‑investigation duty unresolved, but the opinions provide a rich textual roadmap for future courts. The D.C. Circuit’s contextual reading of “shall” aligns with modern statutory interpretation principles and preserves the FOIA’s court‑first enforcement scheme. The district court’s literalist approach, however, reflects a policy‑driven view that Congress wanted an independent, proactive FOIA watchdog.

Until the Supreme Court—or Congress—speaks, litigants must focus first on the standing gate. For those who can overcome that hurdle, the case will likely become a testbed for the “mandatory” versus “discretionary” reading of § 1216. In the meantime, FOIA requesters should continue to press agencies directly and consider supplementing their efforts with OSC complaints, recognizing that the agency’s current discretion may limit the efficacy of that route.

*The battle over § 1216’s exact meaning is far from over, and its resolution will shape the balance of power between transparency advocates and the federal bureaucracy for years to come.*
