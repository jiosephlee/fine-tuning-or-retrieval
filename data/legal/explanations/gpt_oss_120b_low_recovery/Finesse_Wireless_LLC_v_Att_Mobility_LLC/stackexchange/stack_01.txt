Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What standard does the Federal Circuit apply when reviewing a district court’s denial of a JMOL of noninfringement?
Question:
In the opinion, the court says it reviews denials of JMOL de novo under Fifth Circuit law, citing Wi‑LAN and Apple cases. I’m confused about how the de novo standard works in practice: does the appellate court look at the evidence itself, or only at whether the district court made a legal error? How does the “substantial evidence” test interact with the de novo review?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit applies **de novo review** to a district court’s denial of a Rule 50(a) motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of non‑infringement, but the *substantial‑evidence* standard still governs the underlying factual findings that the district court relied on.  

### 1. De novo review of the JMOL decision  

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court states:

> “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit. The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL **de novo**, reversing ‘only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.’”  
> *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (citing Fifth Circuit law).

“De novo” means the appellate court makes its own legal determination about whether the elements of the claim are satisfied. It does **not** defer to the district court’s legal analysis of the claim‑construction or the burden‑of‑proof questions. The Federal Circuit therefore asks:

1. **Did the plaintiff meet the burden of proving each claim limitation?**  
2. **If the plaintiff’s evidence is insufficient, would a reasonable jury, viewing the record in the light most favorable to the verdict, have been compelled to find infringement?**

If the answer to (2) is “no,” the appellate court reverses the denial of JMOL.

### 2. How the appellate court treats the evidence  

Although the legal conclusion is reviewed de novo, the **evidence itself is not re‑examined in the abstract**. The Federal Circuit looks at the record **as the jury saw it**—the testimony, expert reports, and exhibits—to decide whether a reasonable jury could have reached the infringement verdict. This is the “substantial‑evidence” step.

The opinion explains the interaction:

> “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for **substantial evidence**. ‘A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.’”  
> *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022).

Thus, the appellate court:

* **Applies the de novo legal standard** (e.g., the meaning of “signals of interest” or the requirement that three separately identifiable signals be multiplied seven times).  
* **Simultaneously asks whether the factual record meets the substantial‑evidence threshold** for each of those legally‑defined elements. If the record is so lacking that no reasonable jury could have found infringement, the denial of JMOL is reversed—even though the court is not “re‑weighing” the evidence in the way a trial judge would.

### 3. Practical effect in *Finesse*  

For the ’134 patent, the Federal Circuit examined Dr. Wells’s testimony and concluded that the expert “never testified that x1 and x2 correspond to the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’” Because the record did not contain **substantial evidence** that the accused radios sampled both types of signals, the court found that a reasonable jury could not have reached an infringement verdict and therefore reversed the denial of JMOL.

For the ’775 patent, the court looked at the expert’s explanation of the seven required multiplications. It held that the evidence was “confusing and contradictory” and that “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.” Again, the legal issue (whether the claim requires seven distinct multiplications) was decided de novo, but the factual determination that the evidence did not meet the substantial‑evidence standard drove the reversal.

### 4. Summary of the standard  

| Step | Standard | What the appellate court does |
|------|----------|-------------------------------|
| **Legal determination** (claim construction, element‑by‑element analysis) | **De novo** | Analyzes the law afresh, independent of the district court’s legal reasoning. |
| **Factual support for each element** | **Substantial‑evidence** (Fifth Circuit) | Reviews the trial record to see whether a reasonable jury could have found the element satisfied. If the evidence is insufficient, the JMOL denial is reversed. |

In short, the Federal Circuit’s de novo review of a JMOL denial means it decides the legal questions itself, but it still requires that the underlying evidentiary record contain **substantial evidence** that would have allowed a reasonable jury to reach the infringement conclusion. If that evidentiary threshold is not met, the appellate court will reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL.
