## When Expert Testimony Fails: The Federal Circuit’s Threshold for Substantial Evidence in Patent Infringement  

*By [Your Name]*  

### Why Finesse Wireless v. AT&T matters  

The Federal Circuit’s September 24, 2025 opinion in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is a warning shot for patent litigators who depend on expert testimony to bridge the gap between a patent’s language and a defendant’s technology.  By vacating a $166 million verdict on the grounds that the jury’s finding of infringement was not supported by “substantial evidence,” the court reminded us that an expert’s “slide‑show” cannot stand in for a clear, logical mapping of each claim limitation to the accused device.  The decision is particularly consequential for two reasons.  

First, it sharpens the Fifth Circuit’s “reasonable juror” test—borrowed from *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455 (Fed. Cir. 2016)—by insisting that expert testimony must be internally consistent and must actually satisfy every claim limitation, not merely suggest a plausible correspondence.  

Second, it expands the Federal Circuit’s jurisprudence on the reliability of expert evidence in patent infringement cases, joining a line of decisions (e.g., *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008); *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019)) that treat contradictory or speculative testimony as insufficient to meet the substantial‑evidence threshold.  

Patent owners and defendants alike will need to reassess how they structure expert reports, how they foreground the factual underpinnings of each limitation, and how they anticipate the “light most favorable to the verdict” test on appeal.

### The Federal Circuit’s reasoning in a nutshell  

The panel, writing for a unanimous court, dissected the expert testimony of Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse’s chosen RF specialist.  The patents at issue—U.S. Pat. Nos. 7,346,134 (‘134) and 9,548,775 (‘775)—each contain a multi‑step method claim that demands a precise sequence of technical actions (sampling both “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” in the ’134, and performing seven distinct third‑order multiplications of three signals in the ’775).  

#### The ’134 claim  

The court first reviewed whether the accused Nokia radios actually *sample* a passband that includes both classes of signals.  Dr. Wells testified that the RF ADC receives two signals on a “red path”: a downlink transmit reference (the “signal of interest”) and a “modeled PIM path” (the “interference‑generating signal”).  Under cross‑examination, counsel exposed a fatal flaw: the modeled PIM path is *generated downstream* of the ADC, i.e., after sampling has already occurred.  As the court noted, “signals cannot be sampled before they are generated.”  Dr. Wells’ subsequent attempts to salvage his testimony—by invoking vague references to “x1 and x2” without ever tying those variables to the statutory language— left the record riddled with contradictions.

Relying on *Johns Hopkins v. Datascope*, the court held that the expert’s self‑contradictory testimony was not “clear and convincing” enough to satisfy the burden of proof.  The jury’s finding of infringement therefore lacked the requisite “substantial evidence”—in other words, a reasonable juror could not have arrived at the verdict given the conflicting expert statements.

#### The ’775 claim  

The second patent required that the accused system digitally multiply three *separately identifiable* signals in seven distinct third‑order products.  Dr. Wells identified three multiplications in the Nokia document, then argued that each of those three corresponds to multiple of the seven claimed products.  The court found no evidentiary bridge linking the three observed multiplications to the full set of seven; the Nokia document listed ten multiplications, but there was no explanation of which subset satisfied the claim.  Moreover, the expert failed to demonstrate that the three “signals” (S1, S2, S3) were in fact distinct— the accused radios only employed two unique inputs, with a copy of one treated as the third.  The Federal Circuit concluded that the expert’s speculation about “mapping” the three observed products onto the claimed seven was insufficient to meet the “reasonable juror” standard.

#### The damages component  

Because the court reversed the JMOL rulings on both patents, the $166 million award evaporated.  The decision underscores an important procedural point: if a court determines that no reasonable juror could have found infringement, any accompanying damages award is automatically invalidated.

### How this fits into the broader “substantial evidence” doctrine  

The Fifth Circuit’s “reasonable juror” test, as adopted by the Federal Circuit, asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, is so compelling that no rational juror could disagree.  *Finesse* shows that the test is not a rubber‑stamp for the jury’s view of the expert’s credibility; rather, the appellate court will scrutinize the logical chain linking testimony to each claim limitation.  

In *Johns Hopkins*, the Federal Circuit rejected an expert who offered a “partial” description of the accused device that conflicted with the device’s actual operation.  The *Finesse* panel applied the same logic, emphasizing that an expert who vacillates between “x1 and x2 represent the two signals” and “the downlink TX reference is the signal of interest” does not provide the kind of clear, reliable foundation required for a jury to find infringement.  

*Cont’l Circuits* introduced a higher bar for claim‑scope surrender: an amendment that merely adds language—“three signals S1, S2, and S3”—does not automatically disavow coverage of products with only two distinct inputs, unless the amendment is “clear and unambiguous.”  The *Finesse* court applied that principle, refusing to read a surrender into the ’775 claims, but still finding that the expert failed to prove the seven multiplications were performed.

Collectively, these cases form a three‑part checklist for appellate review of infringement verdicts:  

1. **Claim construction** – must be correct and applied consistently.  
2. **Factual support** – each limitation must be tied to reliable, non‑contradictory evidence.  
3. **Substantial evidence** – the totality of proof must be such that a reasonable juror could not have reached a different conclusion.

### Practical takeaways for litigators  

1. **Expert reports must be internally coherent.**  A single ambiguous paragraph can unravel an entire infringement theory.  Counsel should enforce a “consistency check” before deposition, ensuring that the expert can explain, without equivocation, how each claim term maps onto the accused device’s architecture.  

2. **Avoid “piecing together” testimony.**  In *Finesse*, the plaintiff’s case relied on stitching together statements made ninety pages apart.  Courts will not accept a “patchwork” argument; each limitation must be addressed in a single, continuous logical narrative.  

3. **Document the generation point of every signal.**  The ‘134 case hinged on the timing of signal generation relative to sampling.  When a claim’s limitation involves “sampling” or “receiving,” the expert must be able to locate the exact hardware block where that action occurs, and show that the block processes the claimed class of signals.  

4. **Quantify the number of distinct operations.**  For multiplicative claims like the ’775, an expert should produce a traceable flowchart or code excerpt that unmistakably shows seven third‑order products, not just a generic statement that “the device does a lot of multiplications.”  

5. **Prepare for “light most favorable” review.**  Even if the expert is persuasive in front of a jury, appellate courts will strip away the dramatic courtroom effects and focus on the raw record.  Depositions, affidavits, and demonstrative exhibits must survive that scrutiny.  

### Doctrinal implications and downstream effects  

The *Finesse* opinion reinforces the Federal Circuit’s willingness to overturn large verdicts when the underlying expert foundation is shaky.  This may lead to a modest but noticeable shift in trial strategy: parties will likely invest more heavily in pre‑trial “expert reliability” hearings (under Daubert/General Electric standards) and in “claim‑by‑claim” expert reports rather than relying on a single, catch‑all narrative.  

Moreover, the decision could influence how patent owners draft claims that rely on functional language such as “generating” or “sampling.”  By placing the onus of proving that the accused device performs those functions on the patentee’s expert, courts may encourage more concrete, structure‑oriented claim language that reduces interpretive ambiguity.  

Finally, the vacated damages award sends a clear message to juries and judges alike: a verdict that rests on a house of cards will not stand.  Future juries may be instructed to scrutinize expert testimony more closely, and judges may be more predisposed to grant JMOL motions when the evidentiary record contains glaring contradictions.  

### Bottom line  

*Finesse Wireless v. AT&T* illustrates that “substantial evidence” is not a mere formality; it is a substantive gatekeeper that demands clear, consistent, and technically precise expert testimony.  For patent litigators, the ruling is a call to tighten expert reports, double‑check every claim‑element mapping, and anticipate that appellate courts will dissect expert testimony with a scalpel.  In the high‑stakes world of patent infringement, the expert’s credibility can be the difference between a multimillion‑dollar victory and an appellate reversal.



## Claim Construction vs. Claim Scope: Why the ‘Three‑Signal’ Amendment Didn't Disavow Two‑Signal Devices  

**By [Your Name] — July 9 2026**

*The Federal Circuit’s decision in **Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC** (Fed. Cir. 2025) offers a vivid reminder that a claim amendment that adds language about “three separately identifiable signals” is not automatically a *clear and unambiguous* relinquishment of coverage for implementations that use only two signals. The court’s analysis, rooted in the Cont’l Circuits v. Intel “disavowal” test, re‑affirms a flexible, pragmatic approach to claim‑scope amendments—one that patent owners can leverage to tighten claim language without sacrificing breadth.*  

---

### Why This Case Matters  

At stake was a $166 million verdict for alleged infringement of two Finesse Wireless patents covering passive‑intermodulation (PIM) cancellation in cellular radios. The Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) on non‑infringement, primarily because the jury’s finding that the accused devices satisfied the “three‑signal” limitation was not supported by substantial evidence.  

Beyond the immediate reversal, the opinion clarifies **how courts treat claim‑amendment language that appears to limit scope**. In the patent world, claim amendments are a double‑edged sword: they can overcome prior‑art rejections, but they also risk trimming the patentee’s own coverage. Finesse’s amendment—adding a requirement that the three signals be “separately identifiable”—was scrutinized under the *disavowal* framework. The Federal Circuit concluded that the amendment fell short of the “clear and unambiguous” standard, preserving coverage for two‑signal implementations.  

For practitioners, the decision provides a roadmap for drafting amendments that narrow claim language enough to satisfy the examiner, yet remain broad enough to withstand later infringement challenges.

---

### The Court’s Reasoning, Step by Step  

1. **The Procedural Context**  
   The Fifth Circuit’s de novo standard for JMOL led the Federal Circuit to re‑examine the claim construction of the ‘775 patent. The crucial limitation was **“digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2 and S3”** in a series of seven third‑order multiplications.

2. **The Patent‑Prosecution History**  
   - The original claims read “two or three signals.”  
   - During prosecution, after a **Filipovic** rejection (which taught a two‑signal solution for active IMPs), the applicant amended the claims to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3.”  
   - The examiner rejected the “two‑or‑three” language as obvious; the applicant responded by arguing that Filipovic dealt only with *active* IMPs, whereas the invention targets *passive* IMPs. The amendment was allowed.

3. **Applying the *Disavowal* Test**  
   The Federal Circuit leaned on **Cont’l Circuits v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019)**, which asks whether an amendment is a *clear and unambiguous* statement that the patentee disclaims a particular embodiment. The test requires an *express* or *necessarily implied* surrender of coverage, not merely a *preference* for a narrower scope.

4. **Why the Amendment Was Not a Disavowal**  
   - **No Explicit Limitation on the Number of Unique Signals** – The amendment required three *separately identifiable* signals, but it did *not* say those signals must be *distinct* in the sense of coming from three different physical sources. “Separately identifiable” can be satisfied by a signal and its complex conjugate or modulus, both of which the accused radios generated.  
   - **Prosecution Arguments Were Not “Clear and Unambiguous”** – Finesse argued that the prior art was limited to active IMP mitigation, not that the invention *could not* be implemented with two physical inputs. The record shows the amendment was a *strategic* response to an obviousness rejection, not a categorical disclaimer of two‑signal devices.  
   - **No Explicit Reference to “Two‑Signal” Structures** – The PTO correspondence never said, “We abandon any embodiment that uses only two signals.” In Intel, the court struck down a disclaimer where the applicant *explicitly* said, “We do not claim embodiments that use fewer than three signals.” Finesse’s language fell short.

5. **Resulting Holding**  
   Because the amendment failed the *disavowal* threshold, the court held that the claim language still covered implementations that used only two unique inputs, provided the three “separately identifiable” signals could be derived from those inputs. Consequently, the jury’s finding that the accused radios performed the required seven multiplications was unsupported, leading to a JMOL reversal.

---

### Analytical Perspective  

The Federal Circuit’s approach strikes a measured balance. It respects **the presumption that claim‑amendments are not automatically narrowings**, while still protecting third parties from unpredictable claim creep. By requiring *clear and unambiguous* language to constitute a disavowal, the court shields patentees from inadvertent self‑restriction, encouraging them to make pragmatic amendments without fearing that every concession will erode their enforceable scope.

However, the decision also underscores **the importance of precise amendment drafting**. If Finesse had written “three *distinct* signals” or expressly rejected “two‑signal embodiments,” the outcome could have been dramatically different. The opinion shows that the *choice of terminology*—“separately identifiable” versus “distinct”—can be the decisive factor in later infringement litigation.

---

### Doctrinal Implications  

1. **Reaffirmation of the *Disavowal* Standard** – The Fed. Cir. continues to apply Intel’s “clear and unambiguous” test, emphasizing the need for *express* relinquishment. This is a reassuring signal to practitioners that the bar for an amendment to cut off coverage remains high.  

2. **“Separately Identifiable” Is a Flexible Term** – The ruling validates the use of softer language to achieve claim differentiation without sacrificing breadth. Future applicants may adopt similar phrasing—“separately identifiable” or “functionally distinct”—when they need to overcome prior art that challenges the number of elements.  

3. **Impact on Claim‑Construction Deference** – While claim construction is generally a matter of law reviewed de novo, the opinion illustrates how *prosecution history* can nevertheless shape the construction of ambiguous terms. The court leaned on the record to interpret “separately identifiable” as not mandating three *physically distinct* signals.  

4. **Potential for Increased Post‑Grant Challenges** – Defendants will likely lean on this decision to argue that any amendment lacking an explicit disavowal cannot be used to surrender claim scope. Expect more motions for JMOL or summary judgment that invoke the Intel standard as a defense against over‑broad assertions of infringement.  

---

### Downstream Effects  

- **Patent Drafting Strategies** – Patent counsel will be more inclined to use “separately identifiable” or “independently generated” language when a claim must reference multiple elements but the invention can be realized with fewer physical inputs.  

- **Prosecution Tactics** – When confronting an obviousness rejection, applicants may feel freer to amend element counts, provided they document the rationale without making a categorical exclusion.  

- **Litigation Forecast** – In infringement suits, defendants will have a stronger footing to challenge the *scope* of claim‑element limitations, especially where the patentee’s amendment language is ambiguous. Expect a rise in **JMOL** and **summary‑judgment** motions that focus on whether the amendment truly disavowed a particular embodiment.  

- **Appeals Landscape** – The Federal Circuit’s clear restatement of the Intel standard may reduce the variability among regional circuits on disavowal analyses, fostering a more uniform national jurisprudence.  

---

### Takeaways for Practitioners  

1. **Draft Amendments with Precision** – If you intend to *exclude* a category of devices, state it unequivocally (“the invention does not encompass embodiments that employ fewer than three independent signal sources”).  

2. **Document the Reasoning, Not the Consequence** – Emphasize why the amendment overcomes prior art; avoid language that could be read as a *limitation* on the invention’s scope.  

3. **Consider “Separately Identifiable” as a Safe Harbor** – This phrase can satisfy an examiner’s demand for distinct elements while preserving coverage for implementations that achieve distinctness through transformation (e.g., conjugation, modulus).  

4. **Prepare Robust Expert Testimony** – The Finesse case hinged on the expert’s inability to clearly map the three signals to the accused device. A well‑prepared expert should anticipate the need to explain how “separately identifiable” signals can be derived from fewer physical inputs.  

5. **Leverage the Intel Standard in Motions** – When facing a claim‑construction dispute, citing Intel and the *clear and unambiguous* requirement can be a powerful tool to preserve breadth and challenge opponent’s over‑broad interpretations.  

---

### Conclusion  

The Federal Circuit’s ruling in **Finesse Wireless** clarifies that a claim amendment requiring “three separately identifiable signals” does **not** automatically amount to a disavowal of two‑signal devices. By applying the *clear and unambiguous* test from **Cont’l Circuits v. Intel**, the court upheld a flexible, technically informed approach to claim scope. For patentees, the decision is a green light to use nuanced language to navigate prior‑art rejections without unintentionally truncating their patents’ protective reach. For litigators, it offers a fresh venue to argue that an amendment’s language—if not an explicit disclaimer—should not be read as a narrowing limitation.  

As the technology landscape continues to evolve, the balance between **claim construction** and **claim scope** will remain a pivotal battleground. The Finesse opinion reminds us that the courts are willing to uphold a patentee’s broader intent, provided the amendment language is not an unmistakable renunciation of the very embodiments the patentee wishes to enforce.



## The Perils of Mapping Patent Limitations to Complex Technical Documents: Lessons from the ’775 Multiplication Dispute  

*Why the Federal Circuit’s decision in **Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC** matters for every patent drafter who tries to shoe‑horn functional language into a sea of firmware diagrams.*

---

### The Stakes

At first glance, the dispute over U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (“the ’775 patent”) looks like a routine infringement case: a radio‑technology patent that requires “seven third‑order multiplications of three signals S1, S2, S3” versus a commercial handset that, according to the plaintiff, performs exactly that. In reality, the Federal Circuit’s reversal of the district court’s judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) on non‑infringement exposes a deeper problem that will reverberate across the entire patent ecosystem.  

If a jury can be persuaded—by an expert’s carefully constructed narrative—to find infringement when the accused device does not even produce the claimed number of distinct multiplications, then the claim language is effectively *unmoored* from the underlying technology. That creates two dangerous downstream effects: (1) an explosion of post‑grant litigation that hinges on semantic gymnastics rather than genuine inventive contribution, and (2) a chilling incentive for patentees to draft broadly worded, “separately identifiable” limitations that become impossible to enforce without resorting to dubious expert testimony.

The Federal Circuit’s opinion, authored by Chief Judge Moore, is a rare instance where the appellate court actually **policed** the claim‑construction and evidentiary standards, rather than simply deferring to the jury’s view. The court’s analysis offers a roadmap for both practitioners and drafters on how to avoid the pitfalls that led to the reversal.

---

### The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell  

**1. Claim construction was too vague.**  
The district court had construed “three signals S1, S2, S3” as “separately identifiable, but not unique.” The Federal Circuit noted that the patentee’s amendment during prosecution—adding the explicit “three signals” limitation to overcome the *Filipovic* reference—did not amount to a clear disavowal of claim scope over products that only use two unique inputs. Nonetheless, the court emphasized that any construction that leaves “separately identifiable” open to a reading that the three signals could be the same physical waveform is **suspect** when the specification expressly requires *three* distinct signals in a “seven multiplication” routine.

**2. The expert’s mapping was speculative and unsupported.**  
Dr. Jonathan Wells’ testimony attempted to map three multiplications shown in a Nokia internal document (x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, x1·|x2|·|x2|) onto the seven third‑order multiplications recited in the claim. The court found that Wells never demonstrated **how** the three listed operations could be “expanded” or “repeated” to satisfy the seven required multiplications. Moreover, the Nokia document described modulus and complex‑conjugate operations, not literal third‑order products of the raw baseband signals. The Federal Circuit described this as a *gap* in the evidentiary record: “no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed.”

**3. Extrinsic evidence cannot fill the gap.**  
The district court had leaned heavily on extrinsic evidence—inventor testimony and secondary references—to argue that “S1, S2, S3” could be interpreted functionally rather than as distinct waveforms. The Federal Circuit reminded us that extrinsic evidence can *illuminate* a claim’s meaning but **cannot* cure a deficiency in the intrinsic record when the claim language is ambiguous. The court therefore refused to let the inventor’s post‑grant statements override the lack of a concrete technical mapping.

**4. Substantial‑evidence standard not met.**  
Because the jury’s finding rested on a speculative bridge from three to seven multiplications, the appellate court concluded that “no reasonable juror could have found infringement.” Consequently, the JMOL of non‑infringement was proper, and the damages award was vacated.

---

### What This Means for Patent Drafting  

The opinion delivers a crystal‑clear warning: **functional claim language must be anchored to a concrete technical description**. When a patentee relies on a term like “separately identifiable” without spelling out the *minimum* number of distinct physical signals required, courts may interpret that term in the narrowest sense—especially when the literal claim body specifies a precise arithmetic operation count.

1. **Specify the minimum distinct inputs.** If the invention depends on three *different* signals, write “three distinct signals” rather than “three separately identifiable signals.” The former leaves no room for a “two‑signal plus a copy” reading.

2. **Correlate each claim element with a structural or algorithmic marker in the specification.** In the ’775 case, the patentee could have pointed to a flow‑chart showing all seven multiplications, each with a unique identifier (e.g., M1 through M7). Without such a roadmap, the expert is forced to *invent* a mapping, which the court rightly rejected.

3. **Avoid relying on post‑grant statements to fix ambiguities.** Inventor testimony is persuasive only if the claim language is already on a solid footing. If the language is vague, later explanations are treated as *interpretive* rather than *definitional*.

4. **Consider claim‑level numerical limits carefully.** Numerical limitations (seven multiplications, two‑stage filtering, etc.) are double‑edged swords. They can sharpen enforceability when the invention truly depends on that number, but they also provide a ready target for defendants to demonstrate non‑performance of the exact count.

---

### Doctrinal Ripples  

The Federal Circuit’s willingness to overturn a jury verdict in a patent case is not unprecedented, but it is noteworthy for three reasons:

* **Reaffirmation of the “substantial evidence” standard** – The court stressed that an expert’s narrative must be *more than* plausible; it must be *substantially supported* by the record. This may raise the bar for infringement experts who rely on inferential leaps.

* **Limits on extrinsic evidence** – While the Fifth Circuit has allowed extrinsic evidence to clarify claim terms, this decision delineates a clear frontier: extrinsic material cannot *create* a missing technical correspondence that the intrinsic record lacks.

* **Potential impact on claim‑construction precedent** – The opinion could be cited in future disputes over “separately identifiable” versus “distinct” language, especially in the communications‑technology space where signal‑processing claims abound. Plaintiffs will need to anticipate that courts may favor the narrower reading unless the specification expressly commands a broader interpretation.

---

### Looking Ahead  

For practitioners, the key takeaway is that **drafting is the first line of defense against costly litigation**. A claim that reads “three signals” without further definition invites the very kind of courtroom gymnastics that plagued Finesse. By pairing precise numerical limitations with a detailed, step‑by‑step embodiment, patentees can give juries—and later appellate courts—a clear, unambiguous chain to follow.

For litigators, the decision reminds us that **expert testimony must be tightly tethered to the record**. When an expert offers a mapping that is not directly documented, the court may treat it as speculation rather than proof. In the coming months, we can expect parties to revisit prior infringement opinions that hinged on similar “functional” constructions, especially in the increasingly complex world of digital signal processing and machine‑learning‑driven radios.

In short, the Federal Circuit has drawn a line: **claims that require a specific arithmetic operation count must be supported by an equally specific technical description**. Anything less risks being treated as a house of cards—beautifully built, but too fragile to survive appellate scrutiny.

# Damages Vacated: How a Complete JMOV Reversal Forces Courts to Re‑Think Patent Damage Calculations  

## Why This Decision Matters

The Federal Circuit’s September 24, 2025 opinion in **Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC** is a rare, high‑impact example of a court overturning *both* infringement and damages in a single step. By granting a judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) of non‑infringement on every asserted claim of Finesse’s ’134 and ’775 patents, the court simultaneously vacated a $166 million verdict—more than any single patent case in the past five years. The ruling is a stark reminder that a damages award is not a dead‑end; it lives and dies with the underlying infringement finding. For litigants, investors, and practitioners, the case reshapes strategic calculations about how to protect—or challenge—large patent verdicts.

## The Court’s Reasoning in Brief

### A De Novo Review of Infringement

The Fifth Circuit, as applied by the Federal Circuit, reviews a district court’s denial of JMOL de novo. The appellate panel held that the jury’s verdict was not supported by “substantial evidence” because the plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, provided contradictory and, ultimately, insufficient testimony on the essential claim limitations.

* For the ’134 patent, the court concluded that Wells never clearly linked the “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” to the actual inputs of the accused radios. The alleged “modeled PIM path” is generated **after** the RF ADC’s sampling stage, meaning the device cannot be said to sample the claimed interfering signals. The court found the jury’s reliance on a piecemeal inference of Wells’ testimony to be legally untenable.

* For the ’775 patent, the court emphasized that the claim requires seven distinct third‑order multiplications of three *separately identifiable* signals. The Nokia documentation relied upon by Wells showed only three distinct multiplications and, at best, ten total operations—none of which were mapped to the statutory seven. The appellate panel held that no reasonable juror could have inferred infringement from this evidentiary gap.

Because the infringement findings were vacated, the damages award—grounded in the length of the patents’ remaining life—could not stand, prompting the court to vacate the $166 million judgment along with costs.

### The Abuse‑of‑Discretion Standard for New‑Trial Motions

Even though the district court had denied a new‑trial motion on damages, the appellate panel’s reversal on infringement rendered that decision moot. The Fifth Circuit’s “abuse of discretion” standard applies only when a trial court’s discretion is exercised within the bounds of procedural fairness. Here, the trial court never had a legally sound infringement finding to base any damages calculation upon; thus, the abuse‑of‑discretion analysis collapsed into a straightforward vacatur.

## Analytical Perspective: Infringement First, Damages Second

The opinion underscores a well‑established principle: **damages flow from infringement**. What makes this case noteworthy is the categorical JMOL—*all* asserted claims were found non‑infringing. Courts rarely overturn a jury’s infringement verdict on every claim, especially after a full trial and a substantial verdict. The panel’s willingness to do so signals heightened scrutiny of expert testimony that is internally inconsistent or fails to meet claim limitations.

Two take‑aways emerge for practitioners:

1. **Expert Credibility Is Paramount.** Wells’ testimony was riddled with equivocation (“the two signals on the red path…”) and failed to bridge the gap between the patent’s language and the device’s architecture. Courts will not rescue a verdict on the basis of “creative mapping” when the expert cannot articulate a clear, logical correspondence.

2. **Claim Construction Remains Critical.** The court adhered to a *de novo* claim construction, emphasizing that the plaintiff’s amendment to “three signals” did not constitute a clear disavowal of two‑signal implementations. Yet, the lack of “unique” signals and the missing seven multiplications rendered the infringement theory untenable. Patent owners must anticipate how claim language will survive scrutiny at the construction stage and during expert testimony.

## Doctrinal Implications

### Strengthening the Substantial‑Evidence Standard

The Federal Circuit’s application of the “substantial evidence” test aligns with Fifth Circuit precedent but pushes the envelope by demanding more than a “reasonable inference” from a conflicted expert. Future courts may cite this decision when evaluating whether a jury’s finding is supported when the underlying expert testimony is internally inconsistent.

### Potential Ripple Effects on Damage Calculations

While the opinion does not directly address damages methodology, its impact is indirect but profound:

* **Reliance on Per‑Patent Life Awards** – The district court’s $166 million award was predicated on a per‑patent life multiplier. When infringement is stripped away, any calculation anchored to patent term becomes moot. This may encourage plaintiffs to seek damages based on **reasonable royalty** or **lost‑profits** alternatives that are less vulnerable to total vacatur.

* **Heightened Incentive for Early JMOL Motions** – Defendants will be more aggressive in filing JMOL motions before trial, recognizing that a successful motion can eliminate the need to battle a massive damages award later. Conversely, plaintiffs will invest more heavily in pre‑trial expert vetting to avoid exposing contradictions that could doom the case.

### Strategic Shifts for Litigants

* **For Plaintiffs** – The decision suggests that plaintiffs should prioritize *clear, unambiguous* claim language and ensure that expert testimony can unambiguously map patent limitations to product functionality. Investing in *multiple* experts or corroborating experiments may mitigate the risk of an expert’s credibility being impeached.

* **For Defendants** – The case underscores the potency of attacking the *technical* underpinnings of infringement. By exposing contradictions in the plaintiff’s expert narrative, a defendant can force a court to view the jury’s verdict as lacking substantial evidence, regardless of the size of the verdict.

* **For Investors and Licensees** – When evaluating the risk of large patent verdicts, stakeholders must now consider the *fragility* of the infringement finding itself. A $166 million award can evaporate if the infringement question is not airtight.

## Downstream Effects and What to Watch

1. **Appeals in the Fifth Circuit** – Given the appellate court’s willingness to overturn full verdicts, we may see a surge in JMOL appeals from district courts within the Fifth Circuit, particularly in high‑stakes wireless and communications patents.

2. **Expert Disclosure Practices** – Courts may impose stricter requirements on expert disclosures, insisting on detailed, *chronologically consistent* explanations of how each claim limitation is satisfied. Failure to do so could be fatal at a JMOL hearing.

3. **Damages Methodology Evolution** – Patent litigators might pivot toward *baseline damages* that survive even if infringement is later questioned, such as using *reasonable‑royalty* benchmarks that can be recalibrated if a later appeal narrows the scope of infringement.

4. **Policy Debate on Jury Verdicts** – Some scholars may argue that allowing appellate courts to overturn entire verdicts undermines the jury system. Others will contend that protecting defendants from baseless large awards is a necessary check. The tension will likely surface in law‑review articles and perhaps even in future Supreme Court considerations of patent‑damages jurisprudence.

## Conclusion

The Federal Circuit’s reversal in **Finesse Wireless v. AT&T** does more than vacate a $166 million judgment; it reaffirms that *infringement is the gatekeeper to damages*. The decision tightens the substantive evidentiary standards for expert testimony, reinforces the de novo nature of claim construction, and reminds practitioners that a massive verdict can evaporate if the underlying infringement narrative is shaky. For anyone navigating the high‑stakes world of patent litigation, the lesson is clear: win the infringement battle first, and the damages follow; lose that battle, and even a six‑figure verdict disappears in a single appellate opinion.
