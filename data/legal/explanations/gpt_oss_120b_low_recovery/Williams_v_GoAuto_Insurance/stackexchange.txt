Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does Louisiana law require a physical signature on the premium finance company’s “certification” of cancellation?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance the Fifth Circuit says that the statute only requires a “statement certifying” certain facts and finds no authority that a signature is required. I’m confused because some Louisiana cases (e.g., Benitez v. Elsayed) criticize the lack of a signed employee name. How should a lower court determine whether a signature is mandatory under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)? Does “certifying” imply a signed statement, or is an unsigned electronic notice sufficient?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under **La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)** a premium‑finance company must “send … a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a *statement certifying* …” the four statutory predicates. The Fifth Circuit in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance**, 2025 WL …, held that the word *certifying* does **not** create a statutory requirement that the statement be physically signed by an individual. Consequently, an unsigned electronic notice that contains the required certification satisfies the statute; a later signature stamp is merely a documentary convenience and is not condition precedent to a valid cancellation.

---

### Why the statute does not demand a signature

1. **Statutory language** – The statute speaks of a “statement certifying” certain facts. It does *not* mention a “signature,” “signed statement,” or any other indication of personal authentication. The Fifth Circuit therefore concluded that the legislature did not intend to add a signature requirement beyond the text.

2. **Court’s construction of “certifying”** – The opinion notes that Louisiana courts have treated “certifying” as a *statement of fact* made by the premium‑finance company, not as a *signed* oath. The court cites **KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood**, 115 So. 3d 34, 37 (La. Ct. App. 2013) (“a *statement certifying* compliance with the statute”) and **Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.**, 546 So. 2d 898, 901‑02 (La. Ct. App. 1989) (“receipt of … statement from the premium finance company”) to show that Louisiana precedent interprets “statement” rather than “signed statement.”

3. **Absence of contrary authority** – The opinion points out that *no* Louisiana decision holding a signature mandatory has been issued. The appellate decision **Benitez v. Elsayed**, 285 So. 3d 572, 579 (La. Ct. App. 5th Cir. 2019) criticized a notice that lacked an employee’s name *and* a signature, but the court expressly **did not** hold that the lack of a signature alone rendered the cancellation invalid. The Fifth Circuit therefore finds no controlling authority that would require a signature.

4. **Policy purpose** – The statute’s two stated goals are (i) to fix the exact time and date of cancellation, and (ii) to give the insured a cure period. Requiring a signature would add nothing to those purposes; the electronic transmission already provides a timestamp and a verifiable record.

---

### How a lower court should apply the rule

- **Step 1 – Verify the four predicates** – The notice must state that (1) a valid power of attorney exists, (2) the finance agreement is in default and uncured, (3) a ten‑day cancellation notice was sent to the insured, and (4) copies were sent to all interested parties. If the notice includes these affirmations, it satisfies the “statement certifying” requirement.

- **Step 2 – Check the mode of delivery** – The statute permits “depositing in the mail, with a private carrier, or via electronic mail.” An email that reaches the insurer’s computer system (or a web‑service listener) constitutes receipt, as the Fifth Circuit accepted the electronic delivery as sufficient.

- **Step 3 – Determine whether a signature is present** – The presence or absence of a physical signature **does not affect** statutory compliance. A later signature stamp, as used by APAC after the automated cancellation, is merely a filing formality and cannot invalidate the cancellation.

- **Step 4 – Conclude** – If the electronic notice contains the required certification and is received by the insurer (or its system), the cancellation is valid under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The court should not impose a signature requirement absent clear legislative or binding case authority.

---

### Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance** is controlling for federal‑court review of Louisiana law: **“certifying” does not imply a signed statement**. An unsigned electronic notice that enumerates the four statutory facts satisfies La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Lower courts should therefore focus on whether the certification statements are present and whether the insurer actually received the notice, not on whether an employee’s signature appears on the document.

### How is the term “receipt” in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) interpreted for modern electronic communications?
Question:
The court relied on Black’s Law Dictionary to say receipt can be satisfied by electronic delivery to GoAuto’s computer system, but the statute was drafted before widespread use of web services. Is there any Louisiana precedent defining “receipt” for electronic mail or web‑service calls, and does the insurer need physical possession of a paper document for the cancellation to be valid?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Louisiana courts have not given a separate definition of “receipt” for e‑mail or web‑service calls. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit accepted the ordinary dictionary meaning of “receipt” – the act of taking possession of something – and held that an insurer’s computer system “receiving” an electronic notice satisfies La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Consequently, the insurer does **not** need to have a physical paper document in its hands before it can lawfully cancel a financed policy.

---

### 1.  What does “receipt” mean in the statute?

The statute itself contains no definition of “receipt.” The Louisiana Supreme Court has never addressed the term in the context of electronic communications, and the appellate courts that have discussed § 9:3550(G)(3) (e.g., *Delatte v. Lemotte*, *Eaglin v. Champion*, *Britten v. Reavis*) focus on the timing and content requirements, not on the medium of delivery.

In *Williams v. GoAuto*, the Fifth Circuit therefore looked to the ordinary meaning of the word. The court quoted Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024):

* “Receipt – the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession.”  
* “Receive – to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.”

The court applied these definitions to the modern facts and concluded that “receipt” includes **electronic receipt**—i.e., the point at which the insurer’s computer system actually accepts the electronic message and is able to act on it.

### 2.  Is there any Louisiana precedent that limits “receipt” to paper?

No. The opinion notes expressly that “the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined ‘receipt’ as used in the statute, and the statute itself has no definition.” The Fifth Circuit rejected the argument that “receipt” requires physical possession, holding that the statute’s purpose is to fix a **determinable moment** when the insurer may act, not to prescribe a particular form of delivery.

The only related authority the district court cited was an attempt to treat the APAC employee who delivered the printed notice as a “private carrier.” That analogy was deemed unnecessary once the court accepted the electronic notice as satisfying the statutory requirement.

### 3.  Does the insurer need a signed paper document?

The opinion makes it clear that the statute requires a **statement certifying** compliance with the statutory prerequisites; it does **not** require a signature by a particular employee. Several Louisiana cases (*KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood*, *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*) treat the certification as a formal statement, not as a signed document. The Fifth Circuit therefore held that GoAuto’s cancellation was proper even though the electronic notice was not signed, and the later stamping of printed documents was merely an administrative step.

### 4.  Practical implication

For insurers and premium‑finance companies in Louisiana:

*Sending the cancellation notice by e‑mail or via a web‑service call, where the insurer’s system records receipt of the message, satisfies the “receipt” requirement of § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).*  

A physical paper copy or an employee‑signed document is not required for the cancellation to be valid, provided the electronic transmission is reliably delivered to the insurer’s system and the required certification statements are included.

---

**Bottom line:** Louisiana law, as applied by the Fifth Circuit in *Williams v. GoAuto*, treats electronic delivery as sufficient “receipt.” No Louisiana precedent forces insurers to obtain physical possession of a paper document before cancelling a financed policy.

### What is the legal significance of a “private carrier” under the cancellation statute, and can e‑mail be treated as a private carrier?
Question:
The district court held that an APAC employee delivering the notice via e‑mail qualifies as a private carrier, citing Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience. I’m unsure how the private‑carrier concept applies to electronic transmission. Does Louisiana case law support treating e‑mail or a web‑service call as a private carrier for purposes of the cancellation notice?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under Louisiana’s cancellation‑notice statute a “private carrier” is simply one of the few non‑governmental means authorized for delivering the required notice. The Fifth Circuit has already held that the statutory language does **not** require the carrier to be a traditional, physical‑transport entity; it only needs to be a party that “carries” the notice from the finance company to the insurer. Consequently, an electronic transmission such as e‑mail (or a web‑service call that delivers the same information to the insurer’s computer) can satisfy the “private‑carrier” prong so long as the notice is actually received by the insurer’s system. Louisiana case law does not provide a contrary definition, and the appellate court in *Williams v. GoAuto* expressly declined to require a physical carrier, citing *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* for a broader “carrier” analysis.

**Why the statute talks about “private carriers”**

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)‑(iv) tells a premium‑finance company that, to request cancellation, it may send the notice

1. **by mail,**
2. **by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier,** or
3. **via electronic mail.**

The legislature therefore gave the parties three alternative delivery methods. The first two are the classic “postal” and “courier” options; the third acknowledges that modern business practice now uses electronic communications. The statute’s purpose, as the Fifth Circuit notes, is to fix a **clear, identifiable moment** when the insurer “has receipt” of the cancellation request, which in turn starts the statutory cure period. Whether that moment occurs because a courier hands a paper envelope to the insurer or because the insurer’s server receives an e‑mail is immaterial so long as the delivery method is one of the three authorized.

**What “private carrier” means in Louisiana**

Louisiana courts have long used “private carrier” in the context of common‑carrier regulation (e.g., railroads, trucking companies). The term has never been defined in the context of insurance‑cancellation statutes. In *Williams v. GoAuto* the Fifth Circuit acknowledged this gap:

> “The district court recognized an absence of Louisiana case law interpreting ‘private carrier’ and found analogous a panel of this court’s explanation of when a railroad was a private carrier. See *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*, 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s definition of ‘private carrier’ was both imaginative and unconventional.”

The court did **not** hold that a private carrier must be a person who physically transports paper. Instead, it treated the “carrier” simply as the conduit that moves the notice from the finance company to the insurer. Because the statute already lists “electronic mail” as a separate permissible method, the court was comfortable concluding that an e‑mail sent by an APAC employee satisfies the requirement, even though the employee’s later physical handling of printed documents was merely a bookkeeping step.

**Can e‑mail be a private carrier?**

The Fifth Circuit opinion makes it clear that **e‑mail is not treated as a “private carrier”**; it is its own statutory category. However, the opinion also makes clear that **the same statutory requirement—“receipt” of the notice—is satisfied once the insurer’s computer system receives the e‑mail**. The district court’s inventive label of the APAP employee who “delivers” the notice as a “private carrier” was not essential to the decision; the Fifth Circuit affirmed the judgment on the basis that the electronic transmission itself meets the statutory deadline.

Thus, for purposes of the cancellation statute:

* The private‑carrier clause is a fallback to the traditional, non‑electronic delivery method.
* An e‑mail (or any other electronic transmission that reaches the insurer’s system) fulfills the statutory notice requirement without needing to be classified as a private carrier.
* Louisiana case law does not conflict with this view; the only relevant precedent (*Williams v. GoAuto*) treats the carrier concept loosely and focuses on **receipt**, not on the physical nature of the carrier.

**Practical takeaway for practitioners**

When advising a premium‑finance company or an insurer:

1. **Use the method called out in the statute**—mail, private carrier, or e‑mail.  
2. If you choose e‑mail, ensure the insurer’s system logs the receipt time (e.g., server timestamp) because that timestamp is the statutory “date of sending/receipt.”  
3. You do **not** need a human courier or a signature on the electronic notice; the statutory “statement certifying” can be a plain electronic declaration that the four required facts are true.  
4. The “private‑carrier” label is only relevant if you elect the non‑electronic route; any party that physically hands the paper to the insurer (or to a designated receiving office) can be deemed a private carrier for that purpose.

In short, Louisiana law treats “private carrier” as a generic, non‑governmental delivery option, and it allows modern electronic means to satisfy the same statutory purpose. The *Williams* opinion confirms that an e‑mail delivering the notice is sufficient; the private‑carrier analysis is peripheral and does not preclude electronic delivery.

### When can a federal appellate court certify a question of Louisiana law to the state Supreme Court?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit denied the plaintiffs’ request for certification, referencing Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n. What standards does a federal court apply when deciding whether to certify a question of state law, especially when the issue (e.g., “receipt”) has not been decided by the Louisiana Supreme Court?
Answer:
**Answer**

A federal court may certify a question of state law to that state’s highest court only when the “absence of controlling authority … makes it impossible for the federal court to decide the case without guidance” and the issue is “of a kind that the state Supreme Court is likely to find worthy of review.” 5th Cir. *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n*, 106 F.3d at 1247‑48.  The Fifth Circuit has repeatedly emphasized two thresholds:

1. **No controlling precedent** – The question must be one that the state’s highest court has not already answered.  A mere “gap” or an unsettled area is not enough; the federal court must show that without the state court’s clarification it cannot resolve the case.  *Jefferson* holds that “that a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court ‘is not sufficient to warrant certification.’”

2. **Compelling need for the state’s interpretation** – The issue must be “necessary to the resolution of the case” and the state court must have a “compelling reason” to intervene.  The federal court must demonstrate that the state law is central to the claim or defense and that an erroneous construction would affect the outcome.

The Fifth Circuit applies these standards in a **“chary”** manner.  In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, 5th Cir. No. 24‑30646 (Oct. 3 2025), the plaintiffs asked the court to certify the meaning of the word **“receipt”** in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).  The court rejected the request because:

* The Louisiana Supreme Court had **not** defined “receipt,” but that alone did not satisfy the first prong.  The lack of a prior decision “is not sufficient to warrant certification” (*Jefferson*).  
* The district court had already reached a definitive conclusion that GoAuto’s electronic notice satisfied the statutory “receipt” requirement, and the Fifth Circuit agreed.  Because the federal court could resolve the dispute using its own construction of the term (supported by dictionary definitions and the statute’s text), the “compelling need” prong was not met.

Thus, under Fifth Circuit precedent, a federal appellate court will **not** certify a question of Louisiana law unless:

* The state Supreme Court **has no controlling authority** on the precise issue, **and**  
* The federal court **cannot otherwise resolve the case** without that authority, showing a “compelling reason” for certification.

If either prong fails—as it did in *Williams*—the court will deny certification and resolve the case on its own interpretation of the state law.

### Does the timing of the APAC employee’s signature stamp affect the validity of the cancellation under Louisiana law?
Question:
In the opinion, the signature stamp is affixed after the policy is automatically cancelled by GoAuto’s computer system. If the statute required the certification to occur before cancellation, would the timing of the stamp render the cancellation ineffective, or does the electronic certification suffice regardless of when a human signs?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the timing of the APAC employee’s signature stamp does **not** affect the validity of the cancellation. The statute requires only that the premium‑finance company “certify” certain facts in its notice and that the insurer receive that notice before it may cancel. Nothing in the statute—or any Louisiana precedent cited in the opinion—makes a human signature a prerequisite, nor does it require the insurer to have physical possession of a signed document before the cancellation takes effect. Therefore, an electronic certification that is received by GoAuto’s computer system satisfies the statutory requirements even though the employee’s stamp is affixed only after the policy has been cancelled.

**Why the timing does not matter**

1. **Statutory language focuses on “certifying,” not on a signed document.**  
   La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) obligates the finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement *certifying*” four specified items. The opinion stresses that the statute “makes no explicit reference to a signature” and that “the word ‘certifying’ would have to create such a requirement” if a signature were required. The court finds no authority that the certification must be signed by a particular employee.  

2. **Louisiana case law does not impose a signature requirement.**  
   The Fifth Circuit reviews several Louisiana decisions (e.g., *Delatte v. Lemotte*, *Eaglin v. Champion*, *Britten v. Reavis*) that demand strict compliance with the notice‑and‑certification scheme, but none of those cases require a signature. The opinion notes that the only case mentioning a missing signature (*Benitez v. Elsayed*) did **not** hold the absence of a signature fatal if the other statutory elements were satisfied.  

3. **“Receipt” is satisfied by electronic delivery.**  
   The statute’s second clause, § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), permits cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” The court adopts the Black’s Law Dictionary definitions of “receipt” and “receive,” concluding that taking “possession, delivery, or custody” includes electronic receipt. GoAuto’s computer system receives the e‑mail, processes the request, and automatically cancels the policy. The later manual stamping is merely an administrative step and does not prejudice the earlier electronic receipt.  

4. **Policy cancellation occurs at the moment the insurer’s system acts, not when a human signs.**  
   The opinion explicitly states that “once the e‑mailed notice is received in early‑morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system, that computer system cancels the policy.” The subsequent stamping “does not affect the statutory compliance because the required receipt and certification have already occurred.”  

5. **No contrary precedent or statutory text.**  
   The Fifth Circuit finds no Louisiana precedent that interprets the statute to require a pre‑cancellation human signature, and it declines to read such a requirement into the text. Accordingly, the court affirms the district court’s determination that GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute.

**Conclusion**

Because the statutory scheme mandates only (i) a certification statement (which may be electronic) and (ii) receipt of that notice by the insurer, the later affixation of an employee’s signature stamp does not render the cancellation ineffective. The electronic certification and the insurer’s automated receipt satisfy Louisiana law, so the timing of the stamp is immaterial to the validity of the cancellation.

### How does Louisiana’s “strict adherence” requirement interact with technological automation in insurance cancellations?
Question:
The court emphasizes strict adherence but also accepts fully automated e‑mail and web‑service processes. Are there any Louisiana cases that address whether a fully automated cancellation process meets the “strict adherence” mandate, or is this a novel interpretation by the Fifth Circuit?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No Louisiana decision before *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* squarely holds that a wholly automated, e‑mail‑ or web‑service‑based cancellation satisfies the “strict adherence” requirement of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)‑(b). The Fifth Circuit’s analysis therefore restates the law rather than apply a precedent that directly addresses modern, fully‑automated processes. The opinion itself notes that Louisiana courts have never confronted the question of whether a signature—or a human‑hand‑delivered notice—is required, and the only cases that discuss “receipt” or “certifying” do so in the context of paper notices. Consequently, the Fifth Circuit’s holding is, at present, the first appellate construction of the statute that embraces electronic transmission and computer‑to‑computer “receipt” as compliant with the statutory mandate of strict adherence.

---

### Why the opinion reaches that conclusion

1. **Statutory text demands “strict adherence” but says nothing about signatures or manual delivery.**  
   The statute lists four certification items (power of attorney, default, ten‑day notice to the insured, and copies to interested parties) and then requires the insurer to act “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.”  The term *certifying* is used, not *signing*; the Court points out that Louisiana jurisprudence has treated “certifying” as a statement of fact, not as a requirement that a particular employee sign the document.  (See *Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d at 689; *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, 558 So. 2d at 287.)

2. **Louisiana precedent does not define the need for a human signature.**  
   The opinion reviews the limited cases that mention certification—*KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood*, 115 So. 3d at 37; *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*, 546 So. 2d at 901‑02—and finds they only require “a statement certifying compliance,” not a signed statement.  The only case that notes an absent signature, *Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So. 3d at 579, held that the lack of a signature **did not by itself** invalidate the cancellation where other statutory elements were present.  No case has held that a signature is indispensable.

3. **“Receipt” has never been interpreted in the electronic context.**  
   The Court acknowledges that Louisiana courts have not defined “receipt” for the modern communications environment.  It therefore turns to Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) to give the ordinary meaning—taking physical possession or obtaining the item.  The Fifth Circuit treats an e‑mail that lands in GoAuto’s automated inbox and triggers a web‑service call as “receipt” because the insurer’s computer system gains possession of the notice and acts on it.  This is an application of the plain‑meaning rule, not a new holding that creates a distinct “electronic receipt” doctrine.

4. **No prior Louisiana appellate decision addresses fully automated cancellations.**  
   The opinion explicitly states: *“With an exception we will discuss, no Louisiana judicial opinion considering insurance‑premium finance companies’ procedures under the statute has addressed the need for a signature.”*  It also notes that the district court’s inventive definition of a “private carrier” was necessary precisely because “there is an absence of Louisiana case law interpreting ‘private carrier’” (citing *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*, 452 F. App’x at 457).  The same gap exists for the question of electronic notice.

5. **Therefore, the Fifth Circuit’s ruling is the first to treat a fully automated process as compliant.**  
   By concluding that an e‑mail and a web‑service call satisfy both the certification and receipt requirements, the Fifth Circuit is extending the strict‑adherence doctrine to modern technology for the first time on a published appellate opinion.  The Court does not claim to create new Louisiana law; rather, it reads the existing statute in a way that “does not add to the procedures,” as required by *Delatte*.

---

### Practical implication for Louisiana practitioners

- **Current state of the law:**  
  - The statutory “strict adherence” rule remains unchanged.  
  - No Louisiana appellate decision has held that a lack of a human signature or a manual delivery invalidates a cancellation.  
  - *Williams* is the controlling authority on whether an automated, electronic notice satisfies the statute.

- **What to argue in Louisiana courts today:**  
  - Emphasize the plain language of the statute (“certifying” does not require a signature).  
  - Cite the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on dictionary definitions of “receipt” to show that electronic transmission falls within the statutory meaning.  
  - Distinguish any contrary lower‑court decisions by noting they never addressed the electronic context.

- **Future outlook:**  
  - Until the Louisiana Supreme Court decides otherwise, the *Williams* analysis will likely be followed by both state and federal courts sitting in Louisiana.  
  - If a party wishes to contest an automated cancellation, the viable strategy will be to show a defect in one of the four certification statements, not the use of automation per se.

In short, there is **no Louisiana case** that directly answers the question; the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in *Williams* is the first appellate construction that treats a fully automated cancellation process as meeting the “strict adherence” mandate.

### What is the role of the “minimum time to cure” function in the cancellation statute, and did GoAuto satisfy it?
Question:
The statute aims to provide a clear cancellation date and a minimum cure period. The opinion mentions a ten‑day notice to the insured. Did the court consider whether GoAuto’s automated system properly accounted for the statutory cure period, and how is that period measured when the notice is sent electronically?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Louisiana statute that governs the cancellation of a financed insurance policy serves two statutory purposes, **(1) to fix a single, unambiguous cancellation date and **(2) to give the insured a “minimum time” in which to cure the default.  The Fifth Circuit opinion explains both purposes and applies them to the facts.

### The “minimum time to cure” in the statute  

Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium‑finance company, before it can request cancellation, to send the insured a **ten‑day notice of cancellation**.  The notice must state that the policy “will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days.”  The Court cites *Britten v. Reavis*, 503 So. 2d at 1154, for the proposition that the ten‑day period is the **minimum cure period** mandated by the legislature.  The purpose is to give the debtor a fair chance to cure the default before the insurer is permitted to treat the contract as terminated.

The statute does **not** require the insurer to wait any longer than the ten‑day window; once the ten days have passed without cure, the finance company may certify that the default “has not been timely cured” and send the cancellation request to the insurer.

### How the Court measures the cure period when notice is electronic  

The opinion treats an electronic‑mail notice exactly the same as a mailed or private‑carrier notice for purposes of the statutory timeline.  The statute’s language—“by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, **or via electronic mail**”—places electronic communication on equal footing with traditional delivery.  Consequently, the “date of sending” is the date the electronic‑mail is transmitted, and the ten‑day cure period runs from that transmission date.

In the record, APAC’s system automatically **e‑mailed** the insured a “Ten Day Notice of Cancellation” the moment the premium became past‑due.  The Court notes that the notice “specifies the date of sending by the premium finance company to the insured,” satisfying the statutory requirement that the cancellation request include that date.  Because the ten‑day period is measured from that e‑mail timestamp, the cure period is complete at midnight on the tenth calendar day after the e‑mail was sent.

### Did GoAuto satisfy the minimum‑time‑to‑cure requirement?  

Yes.  The Fifth Circuit finds that GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statutory cure period:

1. **Notice was sent electronically** – APAC’s automated system sent the ten‑day notice by e‑mail, which the statute expressly authorizes.  
2. **The ten‑day period ran from the e‑mail transmission** – The Court treats the electronic timestamp as the “date of sending,” so the cure period began when the e‑mail left APAC’s system.  
3. **No payment was received by the end of the ten‑day window** – The record shows that the insured failed to remit payment before midnight on the tenth day.  
4. **APAC then certified the uncured default** – After the ten‑day deadline passed, APAC’s system automatically generated a cancellation request (including the required certification statements) and e‑mailed it to GoAuto.  

Because GoAuto’s computer system received that e‑mail and cancelled the policy **after** the ten‑day cure period had elapsed, the Court concludes that the statutory minimum‑time‑to‑cure requirement was satisfied.  The opinion specifically states that “once the e‑mailed notice is received … that computer system cancels the policy,” and that this satisfies the statute’s “receipt” requirement.  The Court therefore affirms the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling that GoAuto’s procedures were lawful.

### Summary  

The “minimum time to cure” function of § 9:3550(G) is to provide a ten‑day grace period after the insured receives a cancellation notice.  The Fifth Circuit treats electronic notice as valid and measures the ten days from the moment the e‑mail is sent.  In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* the court held that GoAuto’s automated, electronic process complied with that statutory timeline, and thus the minimum cure period was met.
