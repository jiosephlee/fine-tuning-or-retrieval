Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does Louisiana law require a physical signature on the premium finance company’s “certification” of cancellation?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance the Fifth Circuit says that the statute only requires a “statement certifying” certain facts and finds no authority that a signature is required. I’m confused because some Louisiana cases (e.g., Benitez v. Elsayed) criticize the lack of a signed employee name. How should a lower court determine whether a signature is mandatory under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)? Does “certifying” imply a signed statement, or is an unsigned electronic notice sufficient?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under **La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)** a premium‑finance company must “send … a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a *statement certifying* …” the four statutory predicates. The Fifth Circuit in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance**, 2025 WL …, held that the word *certifying* does **not** create a statutory requirement that the statement be physically signed by an individual. Consequently, an unsigned electronic notice that contains the required certification satisfies the statute; a later signature stamp is merely a documentary convenience and is not condition precedent to a valid cancellation.

---

### Why the statute does not demand a signature

1. **Statutory language** – The statute speaks of a “statement certifying” certain facts. It does *not* mention a “signature,” “signed statement,” or any other indication of personal authentication. The Fifth Circuit therefore concluded that the legislature did not intend to add a signature requirement beyond the text.

2. **Court’s construction of “certifying”** – The opinion notes that Louisiana courts have treated “certifying” as a *statement of fact* made by the premium‑finance company, not as a *signed* oath. The court cites **KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood**, 115 So. 3d 34, 37 (La. Ct. App. 2013) (“a *statement certifying* compliance with the statute”) and **Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.**, 546 So. 2d 898, 901‑02 (La. Ct. App. 1989) (“receipt of … statement from the premium finance company”) to show that Louisiana precedent interprets “statement” rather than “signed statement.”

3. **Absence of contrary authority** – The opinion points out that *no* Louisiana decision holding a signature mandatory has been issued. The appellate decision **Benitez v. Elsayed**, 285 So. 3d 572, 579 (La. Ct. App. 5th Cir. 2019) criticized a notice that lacked an employee’s name *and* a signature, but the court expressly **did not** hold that the lack of a signature alone rendered the cancellation invalid. The Fifth Circuit therefore finds no controlling authority that would require a signature.

4. **Policy purpose** – The statute’s two stated goals are (i) to fix the exact time and date of cancellation, and (ii) to give the insured a cure period. Requiring a signature would add nothing to those purposes; the electronic transmission already provides a timestamp and a verifiable record.

---

### How a lower court should apply the rule

- **Step 1 – Verify the four predicates** – The notice must state that (1) a valid power of attorney exists, (2) the finance agreement is in default and uncured, (3) a ten‑day cancellation notice was sent to the insured, and (4) copies were sent to all interested parties. If the notice includes these affirmations, it satisfies the “statement certifying” requirement.

- **Step 2 – Check the mode of delivery** – The statute permits “depositing in the mail, with a private carrier, or via electronic mail.” An email that reaches the insurer’s computer system (or a web‑service listener) constitutes receipt, as the Fifth Circuit accepted the electronic delivery as sufficient.

- **Step 3 – Determine whether a signature is present** – The presence or absence of a physical signature **does not affect** statutory compliance. A later signature stamp, as used by APAC after the automated cancellation, is merely a filing formality and cannot invalidate the cancellation.

- **Step 4 – Conclude** – If the electronic notice contains the required certification and is received by the insurer (or its system), the cancellation is valid under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The court should not impose a signature requirement absent clear legislative or binding case authority.

---

### Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance** is controlling for federal‑court review of Louisiana law: **“certifying” does not imply a signed statement**. An unsigned electronic notice that enumerates the four statutory facts satisfies La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Lower courts should therefore focus on whether the certification statements are present and whether the insurer actually received the notice, not on whether an employee’s signature appears on the document.
