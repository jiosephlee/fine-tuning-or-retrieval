Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### What is the role of the “minimum time to cure” function in the cancellation statute, and did GoAuto satisfy it?
Question:
The statute aims to provide a clear cancellation date and a minimum cure period. The opinion mentions a ten‑day notice to the insured. Did the court consider whether GoAuto’s automated system properly accounted for the statutory cure period, and how is that period measured when the notice is sent electronically?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Louisiana statute that governs the cancellation of a financed insurance policy serves two statutory purposes, **(1) to fix a single, unambiguous cancellation date and **(2) to give the insured a “minimum time” in which to cure the default.  The Fifth Circuit opinion explains both purposes and applies them to the facts.

### The “minimum time to cure” in the statute  

Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium‑finance company, before it can request cancellation, to send the insured a **ten‑day notice of cancellation**.  The notice must state that the policy “will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days.”  The Court cites *Britten v. Reavis*, 503 So. 2d at 1154, for the proposition that the ten‑day period is the **minimum cure period** mandated by the legislature.  The purpose is to give the debtor a fair chance to cure the default before the insurer is permitted to treat the contract as terminated.

The statute does **not** require the insurer to wait any longer than the ten‑day window; once the ten days have passed without cure, the finance company may certify that the default “has not been timely cured” and send the cancellation request to the insurer.

### How the Court measures the cure period when notice is electronic  

The opinion treats an electronic‑mail notice exactly the same as a mailed or private‑carrier notice for purposes of the statutory timeline.  The statute’s language—“by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, **or via electronic mail**”—places electronic communication on equal footing with traditional delivery.  Consequently, the “date of sending” is the date the electronic‑mail is transmitted, and the ten‑day cure period runs from that transmission date.

In the record, APAC’s system automatically **e‑mailed** the insured a “Ten Day Notice of Cancellation” the moment the premium became past‑due.  The Court notes that the notice “specifies the date of sending by the premium finance company to the insured,” satisfying the statutory requirement that the cancellation request include that date.  Because the ten‑day period is measured from that e‑mail timestamp, the cure period is complete at midnight on the tenth calendar day after the e‑mail was sent.

### Did GoAuto satisfy the minimum‑time‑to‑cure requirement?  

Yes.  The Fifth Circuit finds that GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statutory cure period:

1. **Notice was sent electronically** – APAC’s automated system sent the ten‑day notice by e‑mail, which the statute expressly authorizes.  
2. **The ten‑day period ran from the e‑mail transmission** – The Court treats the electronic timestamp as the “date of sending,” so the cure period began when the e‑mail left APAC’s system.  
3. **No payment was received by the end of the ten‑day window** – The record shows that the insured failed to remit payment before midnight on the tenth day.  
4. **APAC then certified the uncured default** – After the ten‑day deadline passed, APAC’s system automatically generated a cancellation request (including the required certification statements) and e‑mailed it to GoAuto.  

Because GoAuto’s computer system received that e‑mail and cancelled the policy **after** the ten‑day cure period had elapsed, the Court concludes that the statutory minimum‑time‑to‑cure requirement was satisfied.  The opinion specifically states that “once the e‑mailed notice is received … that computer system cancels the policy,” and that this satisfies the statute’s “receipt” requirement.  The Court therefore affirms the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling that GoAuto’s procedures were lawful.

### Summary  

The “minimum time to cure” function of § 9:3550(G) is to provide a ten‑day grace period after the insured receives a cancellation notice.  The Fifth Circuit treats electronic notice as valid and measures the ten days from the moment the e‑mail is sent.  In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* the court held that GoAuto’s automated, electronic process complied with that statutory timeline, and thus the minimum cure period was met.
