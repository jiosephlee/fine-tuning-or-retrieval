Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# The Signature Question: Does Louisiana Law Require a Hand‑Signed Certification for Policy Cancellations?

## Why This Case Matters

When a driver finances an auto‑insurance premium through a third‑party lender, the lender wields a power of attorney that lets it pull the plug on the policy if the insured falls behind. Louisiana’s statutory scheme for those cancellations is painstakingly detailed, insisting on specific notices, certifications, and a “receipt” by the insurer. Yet the language is silent on one seemingly obvious requirement: a signature.  

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, 5th Cir. (Oct. 3, 2025), the Fifth Circuit held that the absence of a handwritten (or even typed) signature on the premium‑finance company’s certification does **not** violate La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The decision resolves a lingering doctrinal uncertainty that has haunted Louisiana courts since the early 1990s and sets the stage for a new wave of disputes over electronic formalities, evidentiary weight of “certifications,” and perhaps even a legislative overhaul.

## The Statutory Framework

Louisiana’s insurance‑premium financing statute requires a premium‑finance company, upon a default that is not cured, to send the insurer:

1. A copy of the cancellation notice that was mailed to the insured;  
2. A **statement certifying** (i) the existence of a valid power of attorney, (ii) that the default exists and is uncured, (iii) that the insured received the ten‑day notice, and (iv) that any interested parties were also notified.

The statute then directs the insurer to “consider that cancellation … requested” once it **receives** the notice and statement, after which it may proceed to cancel the policy. The language uses the verb “certifying” rather than “signing,” and the only explicit procedural requirement is that the notice be **sent** (by mail, private carrier, or e‑mail) and that the insurer obtain **receipt**.

## Prior Louisiana Appellate Interpretations

Louisiana courts have long emphasized *strict* compliance with the statutory formalities, treating any defect as fatal to the cancellation. In *Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d 686 (La. App. 1993) and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, 558 So. 2d 284 (La. App. 1990), the appellate courts warned that a “defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation.”

Yet those cases never spoke directly to the signature issue. The most relevant dicta appear in *Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So. 3d 572 (La. App. 5th Cir. 2019), where the panel noted that a cancellation notice “does not identify any particular employee … and is not signed by any individual,” but declined to treat the lack of a signature as fatal. The court’s analysis hinged on other deficiencies (absence of a clear certification), leaving the signature question unresolved.

Other decisions—*KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood* and *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*—simply referred to a “statement certifying compliance” without attaching any signature requirement. The statutory text itself makes no mention of a signature; the word “certifying” is a verb that can be satisfied by an electronic affirmation, a stamp, or even a firm’s corporate seal.

## The Fifth Circuit’s Reasoning

Judge Southwick, writing for the panel, parsed the statutory language and the legislative intent. He observed:

* The statute does not expressly require a signature; it requires a “statement certifying” certain facts.  
* Louisiana precedent treats the term “certifying” as a factual assertion rather than a formal execution requirement. The absence of case law mandating a signature means courts should not “add to the procedures” absent clear legislative direction.  
* The broader purpose—providing a reliable timestamp for cancellation and a cure period—can be served by electronic communications. The court underscored that the assurance of “receipt” can be satisfied by a modern web‑service call, not necessarily by a physical paper document passing hands.

On the “receipt” issue, the Fifth Circuit accepted the district court’s more expansive definition of “receipt” as encompassing electronic delivery to a computer system that automatically triggers cancellation. The panel leaned on Black’s Law Dictionary to support a “possession or custody” view, concluding that once GoAuto’s system ingested the e‑mail, the insurer had legally “received” the notice.

Crucially, the Fifth Circuit declined to certify the question for the Louisiana Supreme Court, emphasizing the lack of a compelling need for a Supreme Court pronouncement.

## Analytical Take‑aways

### 1. Formality vs. Function

The decision reflects a pragmatic turn: the courts prioritize the function—accurately recording the moment of cancellation—over the form (hand‑signature). This aligns with a broader trend in contract law where electronic signatures (UETA, ESIGN) are deemed sufficient where the parties intend to be bound. Louisiana’s statute predates the digital era, but the Fifth Circuit’s reading shows a willingness to interpret “certifying” flexibly to incorporate modern technology.

### 2. Evidentiary Weight of Electronic Certifications

By endorsing an automated e‑mail plus a later stamped paper copy as adequate, the Fifth Circuit signals that electronic certifications can meet statutory mandates, provided the content of the certification is complete and accurate. Future litigants will likely lean on system logs, timestamped API calls, and audit trails to prove compliance. Conversely, parties that rely solely on paper stamps without electronic backup may find themselves at a disadvantage.

### 3. Potential for Litigation Over “Receipt”

While the Fifth Circuit adopted a broad definition of “receipt,” the opinion leaves open the possibility of disagreement over what counts as “possession.” A future dispute could arise where an insurer claims the electronic notice never entered a human‑reviewed queue, arguing that “receipt” requires a manual handoff. The current decision, however, gives lower courts a strong basis to treat automated processes as sufficient.

### 4. Legislative Implications

The opinion shines a light on the statute’s antiquated phrasing. Louisiana lawmakers may consider amending § 9:3550(G)(3) to explicitly accommodate electronic signatures and clarify the meaning of “receipt” in the digital age. Such an amendment would remove any lingering uncertainty and preempt a wave of litigation that seeks to test the boundary between electronic and physical formalities.

## Doctrinal Ripple Effects

1. **Insurance‑Premium Finance Litigation:** Plaintiffs in future cases will need to focus less on the presence of a signature and more on whether the certification’s content is accurate and whether the insurer truly “received” the notice in a manner that triggers cancellation. The key evidentiary battles will revolve around system logs, timestamps, and the chain of custody of electronic documents.

2. **Good‑Faith and Bad‑Faith Claims:** The Fifth Circuit’s narrow ruling on certification does not shield insurers from good‑faith claims where the substantive content of the notice is false or misleading. Even absent a signature, a misrepresentation in the certification could still support a breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.

3. **Interstate Context:** Other states with similar financing statutes may look to *Williams* for persuasive authority. While the opinion is not binding beyond Louisiana, the “no‑signature‑required” rationale could influence courts in Texas, Oklahoma, or even in the Ninth Circuit’s analysis of analogous statutes.

## Looking Ahead

*Williams* resolves a narrow but vexing question about the need for a handwritten signature on a certification statement. Its broader significance lies in the court’s willingness to read statutory language through the lens of modern technology, affirming that electronic processes can satisfy longstanding formalities. 

For insurers and premium‑finance companies, the practical takeaway is clear: maintain robust electronic audit trails, ensure every cancellation request is logged and timestamped, and keep a physical backup (even a stamped copy) for the sake of tradition and potential future challenges. For plaintiffs, the battle line has shifted from “no signature” to “false certification” and “improper receipt.”

Legislators, meanwhile, have an opportunity to close the gap between 1990s legislative drafting and 2020s digital practice. A modest amendment—defining “receipt” to include electronic receipt and confirming that an electronic “certification” satisfies the statutory requirement—would codify the Fifth Circuit’s pragmatic approach and reduce litigation costs for all parties.

In short, the Fifth Circuit’s decision is less about the pen and more about the process, and it signals that Louisiana’s insurance‑cancellation regime is ready to evolve with the times.
