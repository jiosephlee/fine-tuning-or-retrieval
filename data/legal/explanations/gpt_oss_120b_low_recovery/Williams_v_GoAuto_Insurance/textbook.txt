Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# I. Procedural Posture and Factual Landscape  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance**, No. 24‑30646 (Fed. Cir. 2025), turns on a narrow but technically demanding question: whether GoAuto Insurance’s method of canceling policies that were financed through a third‑party premium‑finance company satisfied the precise statutory requirements of Louisiana’s insurance‑premium financing law.  Before the court could address that issue, the parties traversed a layered procedural history that shaped the record and framed the appellate review.  This chapter unpacks that history, focusing on the identities of the litigants, the financing arrangement that generated the dispute, the path the case followed from state‑court class action to federal‑court summary‑judgment litigation, and the specific issues that the Fifth Circuit was asked to resolve on appeal.

## The Parties: GoAuto Insurance, APAC, and the Former Insureds  

**GoAuto Insurance Company** is a Louisiana‑licensed property‑and‑casualty insurer that issued automobile liability and physical‑damage policies to consumers throughout the state.  The insurer’s contracts with its policyholders contain a clause permitting the **premium‑finance company** to act on the insurer’s behalf when the policyholder defaults on premium payments.  

**American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”)** is a non‑bank financing entity that specializes in paying insurers the full premium on a consumer’s behalf in exchange for the consumer’s agreement to make monthly installment payments to APAC.  Crucially, each financing agreement includes a **limited power of attorney** that authorizes APAC to: (1) make the premium payment to the insurer; and (2) “cancel the insurance policy” if the insured fails to cure a default within the time prescribed by the financing agreement.  The power of attorney is a statutory device recognized by Louisiana law to permit a finance company to act as an agent of the insurer for cancellation purposes.

The **plaintiffs** are former insureds of GoAuto:  
* **Kimberly Williams** and **Felita Wright**, who each financed their GoAuto automobile policies through APAC; and  
* **Nicholas Jenkins**, the driver of a vehicle that collided with a third GoAuto‑insured driver in an accident that gave rise to the underlying state‑court class action.  Jenkins joined the litigation because his own policy had also been financed through APAC, and he alleged that GoAuto failed to effect a proper cancellation of his coverage after he defaulted on his installments.

None of the plaintiffs were parties to the financing agreements; rather, they were the **consumers** whose premiums were being serviced by APAC under the power‑of‑attorney clause.

## Premium‑Financing Structure and the Power of Attorney to Cancel  

Under the financing arrangement, APAC pays the **entire premium** to GoAuto at the outset of each policy term.  The insured then owes APAC a series of **monthly installment payments**.  The financing agreement contains the statutory language required by Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) that:

1. **Grants a valid power of attorney** to APAC, expressly permitting it to act for GoAuto in matters of cancellation;  
2. **Defines “default”** as the failure to make a scheduled installment payment; and  
3. **Mandates a “Ten‑Day Notice”** to the insured, giving the consumer ten days to cure the default before cancellation is triggered.

If the insured does not cure the default within the ten‑day window, APAC is obligated—by both the financing contract and the Louisiana statute—to **certify** that (i) the power of attorney is valid, (ii) the default is uncured, (iii) the required notice was sent, and (iv) appropriate copies of the notice were distributed to all interested parties.  Upon certification, APAC must forward a **cancellation request** to GoAuto, at which point GoAuto may effect the cancellation.

The statutory scheme is designed to **fix a precise moment of cancellation** (the insurer’s receipt of the certified request) and to give the insured a **minimum cure period**.  Failure to comply with any of the certification or notice requirements renders the cancellation ineffective, potentially exposing the insurer to liability for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.

## State‑Court Class Action and Removal to Federal Court  

The dispute first surfaced in a **Louisiana state‑court class action** filed by Williams, Jenkins, and later Wright.  The complaint alleged, among other claims, that GoAuto (1) **failed to cancel** the policies in accordance with the statutory process, (2) **misrepresented** the status of the policies to the insureds, and (3) **breached** the covenant of good faith and fair dealing by allowing coverage to continue after default.  The plaintiffs sought both **contractual** and **tort** remedies, as well as **class‑wide relief** for other similarly situated insureds.

GoAuto moved to **remove** the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana, invoking **diversity jurisdiction** (the plaintiffs were individuals; GoAuto and APAC were distinct corporate entities) and asserting that the claims were essentially **federal‑question** because they required interpretation of a Louisiana statute that governed the insurer’s conduct.  The district court retained jurisdiction and consolidated the case for pre‑trial motions.

## Summary‑Judgment Motions Focused on Statutory Cancellation Requirements  

Both sides filed **summary‑judgment motions** that confined the dispute to a single, purely statutory question: *Did GoAuto’s cancellation procedures—specifically the method by which it received APAC’s certified request—satisfy the requirements of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)?*  

*The plaintiffs* argued that:  

1. The **“certification”** required by the statute implicitly demands a **signed statement** from a named APAC employee prior to cancellation; the absence of such a signature rendered the request non‑compliant.  
2. GoAuto **never actually received** the cancellation request because the physical handling of the printed notice occurred after the insurer’s automated system had already cancelled the policy.  They contended the statutory term **“receipt”** requires physical possession before any cancellation can be effected.  

*GoAuto* countered that:  

1. The statutory word **“certifying”** is satisfied by the **electronic certification statement** that APAC’s system attaches to the e‑mail; the statute does not expressly require a handwritten or stamped signature.  
2. **Receipt** includes electronic delivery; the insurer’s web‑service call to GoAuto’s system constitutes **constructive receipt** sufficient to trigger the statutory cancellation mechanism.  

Each side submitted extensive documentary evidence—e‑mail logs, system screenshots, and affidavits from APAC and GoAuto employees—showing how the electronic notice was generated, transmitted, and processed.

## District Court’s Grant of Summary Judgment and Dismissal with Prejudice  

After a hearing on the motions, the district court **granted GoAuto’s summary‑judgment motion**.  The judge concluded that the **electronic certification** satisfied the statutory “certifying” requirement and that **receipt** was met when GoAuto’s computer system **accepted the e‑mail and invoked its internal cancellation routine**.  Accordingly, the court found **no genuine issue of material fact** and entered a judgment **granting summary judgment to GoAuto and dismissing all claims against it with prejudice**.  

The dismissal with prejudice barred the plaintiffs from refiling their claims against GoAuto in any jurisdiction, effectively ending the federal portion of the litigation.  The plaintiffs, nonetheless, preserved their right to pursue claims against APAC in state court, but that avenue was orthogonal to the Fifth Circuit’s present review.

## The Appeal: Issues Presented to the Fifth Circuit  

Unhappy with the district court’s conclusion, the plaintiffs **appealed** to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit.  The **notice of appeal** and the **appellate brief** identified three specific points of error:

1. **Interpretation of “certifying.”**  Whether Louisiana law requires a **signature** on the certification statement, and whether the absence of such a signature makes the cancellation request defective.  
2. **Interpretation of “receipt.”**  Whether GoAuto’s **electronic acceptance** of the cancellation request satisfies the statutory requirement that the insurer receive the notice **before** canceling, or whether physical possession of a printed document is required.  
3. **Denial of a partial‑summary‑judgment motion**.  Whether the district court erroneously disposed of the plaintiffs’ claim that GoAuto’s post‑cancellation handling (the stamping of documents) violated the statutory process.

The appellate brief framed these questions as **pure questions of state law** embedded in a federal case, inviting the Fifth Circuit to consider whether to **certify** the issues to the Louisiana Supreme Court.  The appellate panel, however, noted that **certification is a discretionary tool** and that the issues could be resolved through **de novo** statutory construction based on the record.  

Thus, the Fifth Circuit’s review was limited to **whether the district court correctly applied Louisiana statutory language** to the facts of the case and whether its legal conclusions were **supported by the controlling precedent**.  The court’s ultimate decision—**affirming the district court’s grant of summary judgment**—rested on its determination that **electronic certification and receipt** satisfied the statutory mandates, and that **no signature requirement** was implicit in the term “certifying.”  

Understanding this procedural and factual scaffolding is essential for law students because it illustrates how **procedural posture** (class action → removal → summary judgment → appeal) and the **specific statutory text** can together shape the scope of appellate review.  The next chapters will dissect the Fifth Circuit’s statutory analysis and its application of Louisiana case law to the facts at hand.

# II. Governing Louisiana Statute and Relevant Precedent  

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* the Fifth Circuit was asked to decide whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures satisfied Louisiana’s statutory scheme for the termination of insurance policies financed through a premium‑finance company.  The court’s analysis rested on a careful reading of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b) and on a body of Louisiana appellate precedent that commands “strict adherence” to the procedural requirements set out in that section.  This chapter unpacks the statutory text, explains the individual elements that must be satisfied, and reviews the relevant case law that guides courts in interpreting the statute—particularly the meaning of “certifying,” the significance of a signature, and the scope of the term “receipt” (and, by extension, “private carrier”).

## Text of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b)  

Louisiana law treats the relationship between an insurer and a premium‑finance company as a three‑party arrangement: the insured, the insurer, and the finance company (hereafter “the finance company”).  When the insured defaults on the financing, the finance company may, under a power of attorney granted in the financing agreement, request cancellation of the underlying insurance contract.  The statute governing that request reads in relevant part:

> **(a)** *A premium‑finance company may request cancellation of an insurance policy upon an uncured default **by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail ... a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying** that:  
> (i) the premium‑finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney;  
> (ii) the premium‑finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured;  
> (iii) upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as provided in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying the date of sending; and  
> (iv) copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the premium‑finance agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur thereunder, specifying the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, holders of a security interest, or other third parties.  

> **(b)** *Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium‑finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation **has been requested** and may proceed to cancel the contract.*  

The language is purposely mechanistic: the finance company must **send** a copy of the cancellation notice *and* a **statement certifying** the four statutory predicates.  Once the insurer **receives** that combined packet, it may effect cancellation.  The statute does not enumerate a form requirement for the certification (e.g., a handwritten signature) nor does it define the term “receipt”; instead, it leaves those terms to be interpreted in the ordinary sense or by reference to existing Louisiana law.

## Key Statutory Elements  

### 1. Valid Power of Attorney  

The finance company’s authority to act on behalf of the insured hinges on a power of attorney expressly granted in the financing agreement.  The certification must attest that such a power exists and is legally effective under Louisiana contract law.  The presence of the power of attorney is a threshold prerequisite; without it, the finance company would have no authority to request cancellation.

### 2. Default and Failure to Cure  

The finance company must demonstrate that the insured has failed to make the required installment payments and that the default remains uncured after any statutory cure period.  The certification therefore functions as a factual snapshot of the financing status at the moment the cancellation request is made.

### 3. Ten‑Day Notice to the Insured  

Louisiana law requires the finance company to give the insured a minimum ten‑day period to cure the default before cancellation can proceed.  The certification must confirm that such a notice was actually dispatched and must state the date on which it was sent.  This element serves two policy goals identified in *Britten v. Reavis*: (i) it provides a precise point in time for the start of the cancellation clock, and (ii) it creates a reasonable window for the insured to remedy the default.

### 4. Notice to Interested Parties  

If the financing agreement identifies other parties with a legal interest in the risk (e.g., a secured creditor, a governmental agency), the finance company must also send them a copy of the cancellation notice.  The certification must list those parties and their addresses, ensuring that all stakeholders are aware that the coverage may be terminated.

Together, these four predicates establish a “paper trail” that the legislature deemed necessary to protect insureds and third‑party interests while still allowing finance companies to enforce their contractual rights.

## Definition of “Receipt” and “Private Carrier”  

The statute’s operative clause in § 9:3550(G)(3)(b) hinges on the insurer’s **receipt** of the cancellation packet.  Louisiana courts have not issued a definitive interpretation of “receipt” in this context, but the plain meaning, as reflected in Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), is the act of **taking physical possession** of an item.  Courts have applied that ordinary definition in analogous statutory contexts, emphasizing that receipt does not require any special form of acknowledgment so long as the party actually gains control of the item—whether through a hand‑delivery, a private carrier, or an electronic transmission that is accessible to the recipient’s system.

The term **private carrier** likewise carries its ordinary meaning of any non‑public entity that transports property for a fee.  In *Williams*, the district court treated APAC’s electronic transmission as satisfying the “private carrier” requirement, reasoning that a modern communications service functions analogously to a private messenger.  Although the Fifth Circuit noted that the district court’s analysis of “private carrier” was “imaginative,” it ultimately accepted the conclusion that the electronic method met the statutory intent.  No Fifth Circuit precedent mandates a more restrictive construction, and Louisiana case law has similarly treated electronic delivery as a valid means of satisfying statutory delivery requirements when the law does not expressly preclude it.

## Strict‑Adherence Requirement  

Louisiana appellate courts have consistently held that the statutory cancellation scheme is **mandatory**, not merely directory.  The leading cases articulate the doctrine:

* **Delatte v. Lemotte**, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. Ct. App. 1993) – The court declared that “Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.”  

* **Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.**, 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. Ct. App. 1990) – The court warned that “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.”  

* **Britten v. Reavis**, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. Ct. App. 1987) – The court explained the dual purposes of the statutory scheme (fixing the exact time/date of cancellation and granting a cure period) and thereby underscored why the legislature demanded strict compliance.

These decisions make clear that even a minor omission—such as failing to send notice to an interested party—can render the cancellation void.  The courts view the statutory language as a **safeguard** for insureds; therefore, they will not interpret the language loosely to accommodate business conveniences.

## Interpretive Approach: Statements of Certification versus Signatures  

The statute uses the term **“certifying”** rather than “signing” or “executing.”  Louisiana courts have treated “certifying” as a **statement of factual compliance** with the four statutory predicates.  The requirement is satisfied as long as the finance company declares, in writing, that each element is true.  The courts have not required that the statement be signed by a specific officer, though they have sometimes noted the lack of a signature as a **potential weakness**.

* **KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood**, 115 So. 3d 34, 37 (La. Ct. App. 2013) – The court paraphrased the statutory requirement as “a statement certifying compliance with” the statutory items, without insisting on a signature.  

* **Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.,** 546 So. 2d 898, 901‑02 (La. Ct. App. 1989) – The court held that cancellation may proceed “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company,” again focusing on receipt of the statement, not on a signed document.  

In contrast, **Benitez v. Elsayed**, 285 So. 3d 572, 579 (La. Ct. App. 2019) discussed a cancellation notice that was **not** signed and that failed to identify the employee who prepared it.  The Fifth Circuit noted that the court did **not** hold the absence of a signature alone to be fatal; rather, the court considered the overall compliance with the statutory predicates.  Hence, while a signature may bolster the credibility of a certification, the prevailing Louisiana authority does not treat it as a mandatory element.

The Fifth Circuit, in *Williams*, adopted this interpretive stance.  It observed that the statutory language “makes no explicit reference to a signature” and that “no Louisiana judicial opinion … has addressed the need for a signature.”  Consequently, the court concluded that a finance company may “certify” the required facts **without** a signed officer’s name, provided the written statement satisfies the four predicates.

## Limited Authority on the Need for a Signed Officer  

The cases cited above collectively illustrate that Louisiana precedent **has not** established a per se rule that a signed officer must accompany the certification.  The relevant authorities can be summarized as follows:

1. **Benitez** – Highlighted the absence of a signature but stopped short of declaring it fatal.  
2. **KMJ Servs.** – Treated “certifying” as a simple statement, not a signed affidavit.  
3. **Hodges** – Focused on receipt of the notice and statement, not on signature.  

Because the Supreme Court of Louisiana has not resolved the issue, lower courts have been free to adopt a pragmatic approach: if the certification is otherwise complete and the insurer receives it in the manner prescribed (mail, private carrier, or electronic mail), the lack of a signature does **not** defeat compliance.  This is the approach the Fifth Circuit affirmed in *Williams*.

---

In sum, the statutory framework in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3) imposes a tight, four‑pronged checklist that a premium‑finance company must satisfy before an insurer may lawfully cancel a financed policy.  Louisiana appellate precedent demands **strict adherence** to each checklist item, but it does not require a signed officer’s name on the certification; a written statement that accurately reflects the statutory predicates suffices.  The term “receipt” is interpreted in its ordinary sense—possession, whether physical or electronic—so that modern electronic communications can fulfill the statutory mandate.  Understanding these nuances is essential for analyzing whether a particular cancellation, such as GoAuto’s in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, complies with Louisiana law.

# III. Evaluation of GoAuto’s Cancellation Procedure  

In this chapter we dissect the procedural steps that American Premium Assistance Company (“APAC”) and GoAuto Insurance followed to cancel the financed policies at issue, and we explain how the Fifth Circuit matched each step against the requirements of Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b).  The goal is to show law‑students how a court parses a statutory scheme, applies the language to the parties’ conduct, and resolves disagreements about the meaning of “receipt” and the need for a signature in the modern, electronic environment.

## APAC’s Ten‑Day Notice to the Insured and Default Determination  

Louisiana law obligates a premium‑finance company to (i) determine that a default exists, and (ii) give the insured a ten‑day notice of cancellation before the insurer may be asked to terminate the policy.  The statute does not prescribe a particular form for the ten‑day notice; it merely requires that the notice be sent to the insured and that the date of sending be documented.  

In the record, APAC sent each insured a written “Ten Day Notice of Cancellation” after the first missed premium installment.  The notice warned that the policy would be cancelled if the overdue payment was not received by midnight on the tenth day.  The plaintiffs argued that APAC’s notice was defective because it was not signed.  The Fifth Circuit, however, emphasized that the statutory text mandates only that a notice be *sent*; it does not require a signature on the notice itself.  The court therefore concluded that APAC satisfied the first two predicates of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) – the existence of a valid power of attorney and the presence of an uncured default – simply by sending the ten‑day notice as required.

## Electronic Transmission of the Cancellation Request to GoAuto’s Computer System  

The next statutory predicate requires the premium‑finance company to send “to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail… a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” compliance with the statutory conditions.  Here the parties diverged over the meaning of “receipt” and whether an email qualifies as a sufficient method of delivery.

APAC’s automated system generates an email the moment the ten‑day deadline expires without payment.  The email contains (1) a copy of the ten‑day notice previously mailed to the insured and (2) a certification statement that APAC has met each of the four statutory conditions.  The email is addressed to a designated GoAuto web‑service endpoint that continuously listens for incoming cancellation requests.  

The Fifth Circuit accepted this electronic transmission as a permissible method of delivery.  The court noted that the statute expressly lists “via electronic mail” as an alternative to traditional mailing or private‑carrier delivery, and it found no indication that electronic delivery is inferior in effect.  The court’s analysis therefore proceeded on the premise that the email satisfied the statutory requirement that the insurer receive a copy of the notice and a certification statement.

## Content of APAC’s Certification Statement (Four Statutory Predicates)  

Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a) sets out four factual predicates that the premium‑finance company must certify:

1. **Valid Power of Attorney** – APAC’s financing agreement grants it a power of attorney to act on the insured’s behalf, including cancellation. The certification includes this fact.  

2. **Existence of Default and Lack of Timely Cure** – The email identifies that the insured failed to make the required installment and that the ten‑day cure period elapsed.  

3. **Ten‑Day Notice Sent to the Insured** – The certification recites that the ten‑day notice was mailed (or otherwise delivered) to the insured, and it references the enclosed copy of that notice.  

4. **Copies Sent to Interested Third Parties** – The statement confirms that copies of the notice were sent to all parties identified in the financing agreement as having an interest in any loss (e.g., secured‑creditors, governmental agencies).  

The plaintiffs contended that the certification was invalid because the statement was not signed by a particular individual.  The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, explaining that the term “certifying” in the statute is a verb describing a factual assertion, not a procedural requirement for a handwritten signature.  Louisiana case law, while ambiguous on the issue, does not impose a signature requirement, and the court declined to read one into the statute.  Consequently, the certification statement, as included in the email, fulfills the four predicates.

## GoAuto’s Automated Cancellation Upon Receipt of the Email  

The statute’s subsection (b) provides that, “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium‑finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation … has been requested … and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.”  The key question therefore is when “receipt” occurs.  

GoAuto’s computer system is configured to process incoming cancellation requests automatically.  As soon as the email reaches the web‑service endpoint, the system parses the message, verifies the required fields, and triggers an internal routine that changes the policy status to “Cancelled.”  This automated action occurs before any human employee physically handles the printed documents.  

The Fifth Circuit held that “receipt” does not require physical possession by a human hand; it merely requires that the insurer obtain the notice and be able to act on it.  The court turned to Black’s Law Dictionary, which defines “receipt” as the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession.  By analogy, the court treated the computer’s acceptance of the email as a form of possession sufficient for statutory purposes.  The court reasoned that the statute was enacted in an era when electronic communications were contemplated, and it would be unreasonable to require a manual step that defeats the purpose of an automated, timely cancellation process.

## Post‑Cancellation Physical Filing, Signature Stamp, and Shared Filing Cabinet  

After the automated cancellation, an APAC employee affixes a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents.  Prior to 2017, the documents were placed in a filing cabinet jointly used by APAC and GoAuto; after 2017, a GoAuto employee stamps the documents “received” and returns them to the shared cabinet.  

The plaintiffs argued that this post‑cancellation handling demonstrated a defect in the process because the signature was added only after the policy had already been terminated.  The Fifth Circuit clarified that the statutory obligations are satisfied at the moment the insurer *receives* the electronic notice and *acts* on it.  The subsequent manual filing and stamping are administrative steps that do not affect the legality of the cancellation under the statute.  Moreover, the statute does not require a physical signature on the certification; it only requires a statement of compliance, which had already been transmitted electronically.

## Court’s Interpretation of “Receipt” in the Digital Age  

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis of “receipt” reflects a pragmatic approach to statutory construction: the court reads the term in its ordinary meaning while giving effect to the legislature’s intent to provide a clear, efficient method for determining the exact moment of cancellation.  By treating electronic delivery and automated processing as “receipt,” the court avoids anachronistic constraints that would force insurers to revert to outdated, paper‑based procedures.  The decision also underscores that, absent explicit statutory language to the contrary, courts must not expand the statute by imposing additional formalities such as a handwritten signature.

## Why the Absence of a Physical Signature Does Not Defeat Compliance  

The plaintiffs’ reliance on the lack of a signature conflated two distinct concepts: (1) the statutory *certification* that certain factual conditions exist, and (2) a traditional corporate practice of signing documents to indicate personal responsibility.  The Fifth Circuit examined the statutory text and found no requirement that the certification be signed.  The court also noted that Louisiana appellate precedent has treated “certifying” as a factual assertion rather than a signature requirement.  Consequently, the electronic certification embedded in the email satisfies the statutory mandate, and the later signature stamp is merely a clerical formality.

## The District Court’s “Private Carrier” Analysis and the Fifth Circuit’s Review  

The district court had addressed the plaintiff’s claim that GoAuto never *physically* received the cancellation request.  To preserve the statutory “receipt” requirement, the district court characterized APAC’s employee who originally placed the printed documents in the shared cabinet as a “private carrier,” borrowing from an older Fifth Circuit analysis of carriers in the context of railroad law (Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience).  The Fifth Circuit found this reasoning “imaginative and unconventional,” but it was unnecessary because the statute explicitly allows electronic transmission, and the insurer’s computer system had already received the request.  The appellate court therefore affirmed the district court’s ultimate conclusion—GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute—while rejecting the reliance on a forced “private carrier” label.

---

**Key Takeaways for Students**  

1. **Statutory predicates drive the analysis.** Identify each “must” in the statute and check the record for compliance.  
2. **Read terms in their ordinary meaning unless the legislature specifies otherwise.** “Receipt” can include electronic acceptance.  
3. **Distinguish between statutory requirements and corporate customs.** A signature may be customary, but if the statute does not demand it, its absence is irrelevant.  
4. **Electronic communication is a valid method under modern statutes that expressly authorize it.** Courts will not impose additional manual steps.  
5. **Appellate review focuses on whether the lower court correctly applied the statutory text, not on the ingenuity of its analogies.** The “private carrier” label was unnecessary once the electronic receipt was accepted.

By following this analytical framework, a law student can understand how the Fifth Circuit evaluated GoAuto’s cancellation procedure and why the court affirmed the district court’s judgment.

# IV. The Court’s Legal Reasoning and Holding  

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* the Fifth Circuit was asked to decide whether GoAuto Insurance’s computerized cancellation process satisfied the detailed requirements of Louisiana’s insurance‑premium‑finance cancellation statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)‑(b).  The panel’s analysis proceeds systematically: it first identifies the precise statutory issues, then applies well‑established principles of statutory construction—particularly the “strict adherence” rule that Louisiana courts have applied to these provisions—and finally reaches a conclusion that the district court’s grant of summary judgment was correct.  The following sections walk you through each step of the reasoning, showing how the court moved from statutory text to holding.

## Issue 1 – Whether the statutory notice‑delivery requirements were met  

### The statutory language  

The statute obliges the premium‑finance company (here, APAC) to “send … a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four specific items.  The language is mandatory and enumerated, leaving little room for interpretation that would allow a deviation from any of the four certifications.

### The court’s construction  

The Fifth Circuit began by looking at the plain meaning of “certifying.”  The statute never mentions a signature, a notary seal, or any other formal attestation.  The court therefore concluded that “certifying” simply required a declarative statement within the notice that the four conditions were satisfied.  The panel examined Louisiana precedent—*Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion*—which emphasize that the statute’s purpose is to fix an exact cancellation date and to give the insured a cure period, not to impose extraneous formalities.  Because the APAC notice contained the required four statements, the court found the notice‑delivery requirement fulfilled.

### Why the plaintiffs’ “signature” argument fails  

Williams and Wright argued that a signature was implicitly required for “certification.”  The court noted that Louisiana cases interpreting the same provision have treated “statement certifying” as a textual description, not as a command to affix a hand‑signed signature.  The only case that discussed the lack of a signature (*Benitez v. Elsayed*) held that the absence of a signature **did not** by itself invalidate the cancellation when the statutory elements were otherwise present.  Absent any controlling authority that reads “certifying” to mean “signed,” the Fifth Circuit refused to add a signature requirement.

## Issue 2 – Whether GoAuto actually “received” the cancellation request  

### Statutory requirement  

Section § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) provides that “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation … has been requested … and the insurer may proceed to cancel.”  The operative term is “receipt,” which the plaintiffs urged should be interpreted to require physical possession of a paper document.

### The court’s definition of “receipt”  

The Fifth Circuit turned to Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed., 2024) for guidance, which defines “receipt” as “the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession.”  However, the court recognized that the dictionary also accommodates the broader modern sense of “receiving” something by electronic transmission when the recipient gains control over the information.  The court emphasized that the statute was enacted in an era when electronic communications were foreseeable; it therefore does not demand a paper document to be physically handed over.

### Application to the facts  

APAC’s automated system e‑mailed the cancellation notice, including the certification, to a GoAuto web‑service address.  GoAuto’s computer system logged the e‑mail, parsed the certification, and immediately executed the policy cancellation.  The court held that this electronic “delivery” satisfied the statutory notion of receipt because GoAuto obtained the notice in a manner that allowed it to act upon it.  The later manual stamping of a printed copy was a post‑cancellation administrative step and did not affect the earlier electronic receipt.

### Rejection of the “private carrier” argument  

The district court had characterized the APAC employee who placed the printed notice in a shared filing cabinet as a “private carrier” to satisfy the delivery requirement.  The Fifth Circuit found this analysis unnecessary once it accepted electronic receipt, and it declined to adopt the district court’s “imaginative” definition of private carrier.  The key point was that the statutory language does not require a private carrier at all; any method that results in the insurer’s receipt suffices.

## Issue 3 – Whether a signature is required for the certification  

The court reiterated that no Louisiana authority has read a signature requirement into the term “certifying.”  The Fifth Circuit stressed the principle that courts should not read additional procedural safeguards into a statute where the language is clear.  Because the APAC notice included the four mandated certifications, the absence of a signature did not render the notice deficient.  Accordingly, the plaintiffs’ request that the district court’s ruling be reversed on this ground was denied.

## Application of the “strict adherence” doctrine – No addition of requirements  

Louisiana courts have long applied a “strict adherence” doctrine to § 9:3550(G)(3).  The Fifth Circuit quoted *Delatte* and *Eaglin*: compliance must be exact; any deviation invalidates the cancellation.  However, “strict adherence” means **strict adherence to the statutory text**, not to judicially created formalities.  The Fifth Circuit therefore held that the plaintiffs’ arguments—signature, physical possession, private‑carrier identification—were attempts to add requirements that the statute does not contain.  By refusing to read extra steps into the law, the court upheld the district court’s interpretation that GoAuto’s process conformed to the statutory scheme.

## Use of Black’s Law Dictionary definitions to interpret “receipt”  

The court’s reliance on Black’s definitions illustrates a common tool in statutory construction: when a term is ambiguous, turn to a respected secondary source.  The dictionary’s definition of “receipt” as physical possession was not dispositive; the court noted that modern usage and the statutory context broaden the term to include electronic acquisition.  This approach allowed the Fifth Circuit to reach a pragmatic, technology‑aware reading that aligns with the statute’s purpose of providing a clear, enforceable cancellation date.

## Rejection of the plaintiffs’ arguments and affirmation of summary judgment  

Having resolved each statutory issue, the Fifth Circuit concluded that GoAuto’s computerized cancellation process satisfied every element of § 9:3550(G)(3).  Because the plaintiffs offered no evidence that any of the statutory prerequisites were missing, there was no genuine dispute of material fact.  Accordingly, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of GoAuto was proper, and the appellate court affirmed.

## Denial of certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court  

The plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify the question of whether a signature is required for “certifying.”  The Fifth Circuit applied the doctrine articulated in *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n* (1997): a federal court may certify a state law question only when the issue is “necessarily dispositive” and “unresolved” by the state’s highest court, and only when certification is essential to the resolution of the case.  The panel found that, although the question was unresolved, it was not “necessary” because the federal court could resolve it by its own construction of the statute.  Moreover, the court was wary of overusing certification, which could erode the resource for future cases.  Consequently, the certification request was denied.

---

**Bottom line:** The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court because GoAuto’s electronic notice, coupled with APAC’s certification statements, met Louisiana’s statutory requirements.  The court applied a textual, “strict adherence” approach, rejected the plaintiffs’ demand for a signature and physical receipt, accepted contemporary definitions of “receipt,” and declined to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court.  This reasoning illustrates how federal appellate courts analyze state statutory schemes, respect state procedural doctrines, and balance deference to state courts with the need to resolve cases on federal procedural grounds.

# V. Significance, Policy Implications, and Takeaways for Practitioners  

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* the Fifth Circuit affirmed a district‑court judgment that GoAuto’s electronic, automated cancellation procedures satisfied Louisiana’s statutory scheme for the termination of financed automobile‑insurance policies.  The opinion clarifies several points of law that will shape how insurers and premium‑finance companies design and implement cancellation programs.  This chapter distills those points, explains why the court reached them, and offers practical guidance for lawyers who draft notices, advise clients on compliance, or contemplate future litigation.

## Confirmation that electronic notice and automated cancellation satisfy La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)

Louisiana’s statutory text requires a premium‑finance company to “send… a copy of the notice of cancellation… together with a statement certifying” certain facts, and then permits the insurer to cancel the policy “upon receipt of such notice and statement.”  The Fifth Circuit held that the use of electronic mail (e‑mail) and a computer‑to‑computer web‑service call satisfies both the “sending” and “receipt” requirements.  The court emphasized two principles:

1. **Statutory language is technology‑neutral.**  The statute enumerates three permissible delivery methods—deposit in the mail, private carrier, or electronic mail.  By expressly authorizing e‑mail, the legislature anticipated that electronic communication could replace paper‑based processes.

2. **“Receipt” is satisfied when the insurer’s system actually obtains the notice.**  The court accepted the definition of “receipt” found in Black’s Law Dictionary—taking physical possession or gaining custody of the item.  It concluded that a computer system that parses an incoming e‑mail and triggers an automatic cancellation constitutes “receipt” because the insurer has gained control of the information and can act on it.

Thus, a well‑designed automated workflow, in which the finance company’s system e‑mailed a certified notice and then invoked the insurer’s web service, meets the statutory requirements.  Practitioners should therefore feel confident that electronic, automated processes are permissible, provided they can demonstrate that the insurer’s system actually processed the notice before the policy was terminated.

## Clarification that a signature is not required for the statutory “certification”

The statute uses the term “certifying” but says nothing about a handwritten or electronic signature.  The Fifth Circuit noted that Louisiana precedent has treated “certifying” as a *statement of compliance* rather than a formal, signed attestation.  In *Williams*, the court found no authority requiring an individual’s name or signature on the certification; the finance company’s stamped statement—identifying the four statutory elements—was sufficient.

For practitioners, the key takeaway is that a certification can be satisfied by a document that:

* Contains the required declaratory language (the four statutory statements); and  
* Is issued on the finance company’s official letterhead, bearing any form of corporate authentication (e.g., a corporate stamp, a digital hash, or a system‑generated “certified” tag).

If a client wishes to add a signature for added comfort, that is permissible, but it is **not** a statutory prerequisite.

## Guidance on satisfying the “receipt” requirement in modern commerce

Although the court accepted electronic receipt, the opinion also underscores that the *quality* of receipt matters.  The insurer must be able to show that it actually obtained the notice before it cancelled the policy.  The following practical steps help meet that burden:

| Step | Practical Action | Why it matters |
|------|------------------|----------------|
| **1. Timestamped inbound message** | Configure the insurer’s email or web‑service gateway to log the exact date‑time of receipt, IP address, and message identifier. | Provides objective evidence that the notice arrived before cancellation. |
| **2. Automated acknowledgment** | Have the insurer’s system automatically generate an acknowledgment (e‑mail or API response) to the finance company confirming receipt. | Creates a paper trail showing the insurer was aware of the notice. |
| **3. Audit log of cancellation trigger** | Capture the system event that executed the cancellation, linking it to the inbound notice (e.g., “Cancellation initiated by receipt of Notice #12345 at 08:14 a.m.”). | Demonstrates causal connection between receipt and cancellation. |
| **4. Retain original documents** | Store the original e‑mail and any printed copies in a secure, retrievable repository for the statutory preservation period. | Satisfies any later discovery or regulatory request. |
| **5. Physical “receipt” for paper notices** | If a paper notice is sent by private carrier, ensure an employee physically signs a receipt log, or use a scanned copy with a time‑stamp. | Aligns with the traditional interpretation of “receipt” when non‑electronic delivery is used. |

By building these safeguards into their cancellation workflow, insurers and finance companies can pre‑empt the type of factual disputes that arose in *Williams*.

## Limits on certification petitions to state supreme courts

The plaintiffs in *Williams* sought certification of the ambiguous statutory terms to the Louisiana Supreme Court.  The Fifth Circuit reaffirmed the narrow discretion to certify questions of law, citing *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n*.  The court reiterated two constraints:

1. **Certification is a sparing remedy.**  Federal courts will not certify merely because a state Supreme Court has not addressed an issue; the question must be both novel and of substantial importance to the state’s jurisprudence.

2. **The existence of “reasonable certainty” in the existing law defeats certification.**  The Fifth Circuit found that, despite the lack of a definitive signature rule, the statutory language and prior appellate analysis gave a clear enough framework to resolve the dispute without resort to the state’s highest court.

Practitioners should therefore assess whether a certification request is truly necessary.  If the federal court can resolve the issue using existing state authority, or if the dispute hinges on factual rather than purely legal questions, a petition for certification is unlikely to succeed.

## Best‑practice checklist for insurers and finance companies

The following checklist consolidates the doctrinal insights from *Williams* into actionable items for compliance programs:

1. **Statutory compliance audit**  
   * Verify that the financing agreement includes a valid power of attorney.  
   * Ensure the notice of cancellation is sent to the insured at least ten days before the intended cancellation date.

2. **Electronic notice protocol**  
   * Use e‑mail or a secure API that logs receipt timestamps.  
   * Include on the notice a clear “statement certifying” the four statutory elements.  
   * Place the certification on official letterhead; a corporate stamp or digital signature suffices.

3. **Receipt verification**  
   * Configure the insurer’s system to generate an automatic acknowledgment to the finance company.  
   * Maintain an immutable audit log that links the inbound notice to the cancellation trigger.

4. **Document retention**  
   * Preserve electronic and/or printed copies of all notices, certifications, acknowledgments, and audit logs for the period required by Louisiana law (typically five years).  

5. **Training and SOPs**  
   * Train staff on the distinction between “sending” (finance company) and “receipt” (insurer).  
   * Draft standard operating procedures that detail each step, from default detection to policy termination.

6. **Periodic review**  
   * Conduct annual reviews to ensure technology updates (e.g., new email security platforms) do not inadvertently break the receipt chain.

By following this checklist, parties can mitigate the risk of “defective” cancellations that could trigger litigation under the “strict adherence” doctrine.

## Potential avenues for future litigation or legislative amendment

*Williams* leaves several gaps that may invite later disputes or policy changes:

* **Signature requirement clarification.**  Although the court found no statutory need for a signature, future plaintiffs could argue that a signature is essential for due‑process fairness, prompting either a new appellate decision or a legislative amendment expressly mandating a signed certification.

* **Definition of “receipt.”**  The court relied on dictionary definitions, but a state Supreme Court could later articulate a more precise test—especially if emerging technologies (blockchain, smart contracts) alter the notion of possession.

* **Scope of “private carrier.”**  The district court’s creative classification of the APAC employee as a private carrier was not addressed on appeal.  A future case could challenge whether individuals or automated processes qualify, potentially prompting statutory clarification.

* **Consumer‑protection claims.**  The decision focused strictly on statutory compliance; however, plaintiffs may bring claims under Louisiana’s good‑faith and fair‑dealing doctrines if they can show the insurer’s reliance on an imperfect electronic process caused actual harm.

* **Legislative response.**  Louisiana legislators, responding to industry concerns, may amend § 9:3550(G) to articulate explicit electronic‑receipt standards, introduce a signature requirement, or create a safe‑harbor for automated cancellations.

Practitioners should monitor Louisiana case law and legislative activity for developments that could affect the compliance landscape.

---

**In sum**, *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* establishes that electronic, automated cancellation processes—when coupled with a statutory “certification” statement—meet Louisiana’s strict‑adherence requirements, even absent a handwritten signature.  The decision offers a clear roadmap for insurers and premium‑finance firms to design technologically modern compliance programs, while also highlighting the limited role of certification petitions and pointing to areas where future litigation or legislative reform may arise.
