Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why did the court treat prior instances of the sawed‑off rifle as “intrinsic” evidence rather than prohibited propensity evidence under Rule 404(b)?
Question:
The opinion says the district court allowed D.F. and C.F. to testify that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 before the incident, calling it intrinsic evidence relevant to proving continuous possession. I thought evidence of prior conduct is generally barred as propensity under Rule 404(b) unless it falls under a recognized exception. How does the court justify treating these prior sightings as admissible, and what case law supports this distinction in the context of a firearms possession charge?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit treats the witnesses’ testimony that the defendant had been seen with the saw‑ed‑off Marlin rifle **before** the night of the alleged offense as “intrinsic” evidence of possession, not as prohibited propensity evidence, because the prior sightings are **directly probative of an essential element of the charged crime—continuous ownership or control of the firearm**.  Under Fed. R. Evid. 404(b) “evidence of a crime, wrong‑doing, or other conduct is admissible … **only** if it is offered for a purpose **other than** proving a person’s character or a trait of character to show action in conformity therewith.”  The key inquiry is *why* the evidence is being offered.  

In **United States v. Cutbank**, 2025 WL 5623744, at 5–6, the district court (and the affirming appellate court) explained that the prior sightings were admissible because they “help establish ownership or control of the weapon” and therefore are **intrinsic** to the *possession* element of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).  The court expressly relied on **United States v. Battle**, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014) (cert. denied), where the court held that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”  Battle is cited in Cutbank as the controlling authority for treating such evidence as *relevant* rather than as impermissible character evidence.

The distinction runs as follows:

| Purpose of the evidence | Result under Rule 404(b) |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **To prove a trait of character** (i.e., “the defendant is the type of person who keeps a saw‑ed‑off rifle, so he must have had it on the night in question”) | **Barred** as propensity evidence unless an exception applies. |
| **To prove a fact that is an element of the crime** (i.e., “the defendant possessed the rifle on the night in question”) | **Admissible** as “intrinsic” evidence because it is offered for a *relevant, non‑character* purpose. |

The Court in Cutbank emphasized that the prior sightings “are directly relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident.”  Because the prosecution needed to show that the saw‑ed‑off rifle found on Cutbank’s person was *the same weapon* he had previously handled, the testimony is not merely evidence of a propensity to own firearms; it is evidence **of ownership/control**, an element of the statutory offense.

No other 404(b) exception (e.g., habit, routine practice, or a specific statutory exception) is needed.  The *intrinsic‑evidence* analysis is sufficient where the prior conduct **has a logical relationship** to an essential fact of the case.  The Eighth Circuit has applied the same reasoning in other firearms‑possession contexts, for example:

* **United States v. Battle**, 774 F.3d at 511 – prior possession of a gun was admissible to show ownership for a later possession charge.  
* **United States v. Glover**, 964 F.2d 1129, 1131 (8th Cir. 1992) – prior sightings of a defendant with a weapon were admitted to prove continuity of ownership, not character.

Thus, the court’s justification rests on the *purpose* of the evidence (to prove an element of possession) and the well‑settled precedent that prior conduct is admissible when it is **intrinsic** to a material fact, even though the same evidence would be barred if offered simply to show that the defendant is the sort of person who keeps a gun.

### What is the standard of review for a appellate court when deciding whether a district court’s failure to give a limiting instruction on hearsay violates the Confrontation Clause?
Question:
Cutbank argued that the jury should have been instructed that D.F.’s statements to police were not substantive evidence until she testified, invoking the Confrontation Clause. The appellate panel cited United States v. Bennett and held that a later instruction is sufficient. What level of deference do appellate courts give to the trial judge’s discretion on such instructions, and are there any controlling precedents that require a limiting instruction at the time of admission rather than at the close of trial?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit reviews a district‑court decision not to give a limiting instruction on hearsay under an **abuse‑of‑discretion** standard.  The standard is articulated in *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014) (cited verbatim in *Cutbank*), which holds that the trial judge’s “discretion … is reviewed for abuse of discretion.”  Under that standard the appellate court asks only whether the trial judge’s choice was *reasonable* in light of the record; it does **not** substitute its own judgment for the trial judge’s.

### Why a later instruction is sufficient

*Cutbank* explains two propositions that flow directly from *Bennett*:

1. **A limiting instruction need not be given at the moment each item of evidence is admitted.**  
   *Bennett* states, “*The court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.*”  (Id. at 899).  The appellate panel therefore treated the district court’s decision to defer the instruction until the close of trial as a permissible exercise of discretion.

2. **The Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant testifies.**  
   *Cutbank* relies on *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010), which holds that the Clause “is satisfied *when the hearsay declarants … actually appear in court and testify in person*.”  Because D.F. eventually testified, the later instruction (or the lack of an earlier one) did not violate the Clause.

### Level of deference

Under the abuse‑of‑discretion standard, appellate courts give **great deference** to the trial judge’s evidentiary rulings, including decisions about jury instructions.  The appellate court will overturn only if the instruction (or lack thereof) was *clearly unreasonable* or *unlawful* – i.e., if the trial judge “abused” discretion by ignoring a mandatory legal requirement.  The *Cutbank* opinion makes it clear that the panel found no such abuse.

### Controlling precedent on the timing of a limiting instruction

The Eighth Circuit has **no holding** that a limiting instruction must be given *at the time of admission* of hearsay.  The controlling precedent is *Bennett*, which expressly authorizes a “later instruction … at the close of trial” as sufficient.  No other Eighth Circuit case has imposed a stricter requirement.  Consequently, the district court’s approach in *Cutbank* is consistent with controlling law, and the appellate panel was correct to affirm.

**Bottom line:**  

- The appellate court applies an *abuse‑of‑discretion* review to a trial judge’s decision on limiting instructions.  
- Under *Bennett*, a limiting instruction can be given at the close of trial; it need not be contemporaneous with the admission of the evidence.  
- No Eighth Circuit precedent requires a contemporaneous limiting instruction, so the district court’s discretion was properly respected.

### How does the Eighth Circuit apply the "harmless error" doctrine to evidentiary mistakes in a firearms case?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly references United States v. DeMarce and United States v. Keys, stating that the district court’s evidentiary rulings were not reversible because the error was harmless. Can someone explain the specific test the Eighth Circuit uses to determine harmlessness of evidentiary errors, especially when the error involves the admission of potentially prejudicial testimony about a victim’s family member’s death?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit applies the “harmless error” doctrine to evidentiary mistakes by using a two‑step test that is spelled out in *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009), and reiterated in *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019).  

1. **Identify the error and its legal significance.**  
   The court first determines whether the trial court’s ruling was an error – i.e., a departure from the Federal Rules of Evidence, the Constitution, or a governing statute. In *Cutbank* the district court’s rulings involved (a) admitting testimony that the victim’s sister had been killed by a gun, (b) allowing prior‑possession evidence, and (c) refusing a limiting instruction. The opinion notes that each of those rulings “did not violate any rule of evidence or constitutional right” and therefore qualifies as a *potential* error.  

2. **Assess whether the error was “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.”**  
   After the error is identified, the appellate court asks whether the error **had a substantial and injurious effect or influence on the jury’s verdict**. The *DeMarce* language makes clear that reversal is warranted only “if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” The court then looks to the strength of the government’s remaining proof. If the prosecution’s case was “substantial” and the error “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict,” the error is deemed harmless.  

   *Keys* refines this inquiry by emphasizing the “cumulative effect” analysis. The court will not overturn a conviction “unless there is **substantial prejudice** to the defendant,” meaning that the probative value of the error, viewed in the context of all the other evidence, must be such that the error could have caused a different outcome.  

In *Cutbank* the panel applied those standards as follows:

* **Strength of the government’s evidence.**  The government offered eyewitness identification of the saw‑ed‑off rifle, DNA linking the defendant to the weapon, and the victim’s contemporaneous testimony of the assault. This “substantial” body of proof, the opinion says, overwhelms any limited prejudice that might have arisen from the admission of the victim’s sister‑death commentary.  

* **Effect on the jury.**  The court concluded that the error “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict,” citing *DeMarce* at 998 and *Keys* at 985. Because the jury already heard numerous independent pieces of proof of the defendant’s possession and use of the firearm, the appellate court found no reasonable likelihood that the mistaken admission altered the verdict.

**Therefore, the Eighth Circuit’s test for harmless error in evidentiary contexts is:**  

1. **Determine that an evidentiary ruling was erroneous;**  
2. **Then ask whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt by evaluating (a) the overall strength of the government’s case and (b) whether the error likely had a substantial, injurious effect on the verdict.**  

If the answer to (2) is negative—i.e., the error was not “substantially prejudicial”—the conviction stands, as it did in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*.

### What is the proper calculation of the sentencing guidelines range when multiple enhancements under §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and the Armed Career Criminal Act overlap?
Question:
The PSR gave Cutbank an offense level of 35, including a 4‑level enhancement for possession of a firearm in connection with another felony, plus other enhancements. The defense argues that this over‑counts because the ACCA already adds levels for similar conduct. How should a district court reconcile overlapping enhancements under the U.S.S.G. and ensure the correct “greater offense level” is applied per §4B1.4(b)?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit resolves the “over‑lap” problem by **calculating each applicable guideline provision independently and then applying the “greater‑offense‑level” rule of USSG § 4B1.4(b)**.  In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the court performed exactly that analysis and held that the district court’s calculation was correct.

### 1.  The two enhancements at issue

1. **Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) enhancement** – Because the jury found that Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions, the ACCA adds a **four‑level** increase to the base offense level (the “Armed Career Criminal” guideline).  See the opinion’s discussion of the ACCA sentencing provision.

2. **U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) firearm‑in‑connection‑with‑another‑felony enhancement** – The PSR added another **four‑level** increase for “possession of a firearm in connection with another felony” (the “firearm‑in‑connection” guideline).  The opinion notes that this enhancement “applies if a defendant used or possessed any firearm … in connection with another … felony … regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (quoting § 2K2.1 comment (n.14)(C)).

### 2.  The Court’s approach to overlapping enhancements

The opinion explains that **the guidelines do not require the court to “deduct” one enhancement because the conduct is already counted in the other**.  Instead, the court must:

1. **Add each enhancement to the base offense level** to arrive at two separate total offense levels (one with the ACCA increase, one with the § 2K2.1 increase).  
2. **Apply USSG § 4B1.4(b)**, which directs the sentencing court to “use the greater offense level” when two separate guideline calculations are available.

> “The Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced a total offense level of 34.  The Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).”  
> (*Cutbank*, 2025)

Thus, even though both enhancements are based on the same firearm conduct, the guidelines treat them as **separate, cumulative options** and then require the court to adopt the **higher of the two resulting offense levels**.

### 3.  Application to Cutbank’s numbers

* **Base offense level** (sawed‑off firearm): 26  
* **ACCA increase**: +4 → **30** (the opinion’s “Armed Career Criminal” total)  
* **§ 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) increase**: +4 → **30** (the “firearm‑in‑connection” total)  

The PSR also included other, unrelated enhancements (altered serial number +3, reckless creation of risk +2).  Adding those to the **higher** of the two totals yields:

- **Higher base+ACCA level** = 26 + 4 = 30  
- **Add other enhancements**: 30 + 3 + 2 + 4 (the PSR’s “possession‑in‑connection” enhancement) = **35**

Because the **“greater offense level” rule** instructs the court to keep the higher level, the district court correctly arrived at **offense level 35** and the **guidelines range of 292–365 months** (Criminal History Category VI).  The appellate court affirmed that calculation and rejected the defendant’s contention that the two four‑level enhancements “double‑counted” the same conduct.

### 4.  Take‑away for future cases

- **Do not subtract overlapping enhancements**; compute each guideline provision separately.  
- **Invoke § 4B1.4(b)** to select the higher total offense level.  
- The district court must document that it performed this comparison; if it does, the appellate court will consider the range correctly calculated, even when the enhancements arise from the same factual conduct.

In short, *Cutbank* confirms that when the ACCA’s mandatory enhancement and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) firearm‑in‑connection enhancement both apply, the sentencing court adds each to the base level, compares the two totals, and adopts the greater one, per USSG § 4B1.4(b).  The resulting offense level—and consequently the advisory range—are therefore proper.

### When is a district court required to provide a detailed recitation of the §3553(a) factors at sentencing?
Question:
The appellate court noted that it is sufficient that the record shows the court considered the §3553(a) factors, citing United States v. Maluoth. However, I’m unclear on what constitutes an adequate showing. Does the district court have to explicitly list each factor and its analysis, or can a brief statement that the factors were considered satisfy the requirement? Are there any examples where a lack of detailed discussion led to reversal?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit has held that a district court does **not** have to mechanically list every § 3553(a) factor or write a paragraph‑by‑paragraph analysis in the sentencing order.  What the appellate court requires is a *record‑wide* showing that the court actually **considered** the factors when it decided the sentence.  If the transcript, presentence report, or sentencing memorandum contains any discussion—however brief—of the relevant considerations, that satisfies the procedural requirement.

### Authority

The controlling language in *United States v. Cutbank* is:

> “To uphold the sentence, we do **not** require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be **clear from the record that the district court actually considered** the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.”  
> *United States v. Cutbank*, 2025 WL 5728425, at 12‑13 (8th Cir. Oct. 7 2025) (quoting *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024)).

The opinion points to *Maluoth* for the standard: the appellate court looks for “an adequate showing” that the factors were taken into account, not for a “mechanical recitation.”  In *Cutbank* the district court’s brief note that it had reviewed the present‑sense investigation report (PSR), the sentencing memoranda, and the § 3553(a) factors was deemed sufficient; the court affirmed the sentence on that basis.

### What counts as “adequate showing”?

1. **Reference to the PSR or other pre‑sentence documents** that themselves discuss the factors (e.g., the defendant’s history, the nature of the offense, the need for deterrence, etc.).  
2. **A sentencing memorandum or oral remarks** in which the judge mentions that it has considered the defendant’s criminal history, the seriousness of the offense, or any mitigating/aggravating circumstance.  
3. **The transcript of the sentencing hearing** in which the judge asks the parties about the factors or responds to their arguments.  

Any of these, taken together, creates a record that a court “considered” the factors, even if the sentencing order itself merely says, “The court considered the § 3553(a) factors and imposed a within‑range sentence.”

### When a more detailed discussion is required

The Eighth Circuit has reversed sentences when the **record is silent** on any consideration of the § 3553(a) factors.  While *Cutbank* does not cite a specific case of reversal for lack of discussion, earlier Eighth Circuit precedent makes clear that an *absence* of any indication that the court weighed the factors is a reversible error.  For example, in *United States v. Bingham*, 986 F.2d 1149, 1154 (8th Cir. 1993), the court reversed because the sentencing transcript contained no reference to any of the statutory factors, and the sentencing order contained only the advisory range and the term imposed.

Thus, the risk of reversal arises not from the *length* of the discussion but from the *complete lack* of any discussion in the record.

### Practical take‑away for trial judges

- **Spot‑check** the transcript or sentencing memorandum to ensure the judge says, at least in passing, that each of the major § 3553(a) considerations (e.g., criminal history, need for deterrence, nature of the offense, the defendant’s personal characteristics) were weighed.  
- A short sentence such as “The Court considered the § 3553(a) factors and imposed a 292‑month sentence, which falls within the advisory guidelines” is **acceptable** if the hearing record shows the judge asked the parties about those factors or referenced a PSR that does.  
- **Avoid** a sentencing order that contains no mention of the factors and where the hearing transcript is silent on them; that creates a reversible procedural defect.

In short, the Eighth Circuit requires **sufficient evidence in the record** that the court considered the § 3553(a) factors, not a line‑by‑line recitation in the sentencing order.  A brief statement of consideration, coupled with a transcript or report that discusses the factors, satisfies the requirement; failure to show any consideration at all can lead to reversal.

### Is extrinsic evidence of a witness’s prior inconsistent statement admissible for impeachment without the witness’s own testimony?
Question:
In Cutbank’s case, the court refused to let the defense read portions of a police report to impeach C.F.’s testimony because the report wasn’t authored by C.F. The opinion cites United States v. Cumbie on authentication. Does the Eighth Circuit permit the use of written statements (e.g., police reports) to impeach a witness’s credibility if the witness does not testify to the content, and what are the authentication requirements?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes. The Eighth Circuit allows a party to use a written statement (e.g., a police report) to impeach a witness’s credibility on the ground of a prior inconsistent statement **even if the witness does not testify to the contents of that statement**, but the writing must first be **authenticated** under the same standards the circuit applies in *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907 (8th Cir. 2022).  

---

### Why the writing is admissible for impeachment

*Federal Rule of Evidence 613(b)* permits the **use of extrinsic evidence** to prove a prior inconsistent statement **when the witness is present** and is given an opportunity to explain or deny it. The rule does not require that the witness have actually made the statement in court; it only requires that the statement be **relevant to the witness’s credibility** and that the evidence be **properly authenticated**.

The *Cutbank* opinion states:

> “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.”  
> *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022) (quotation omitted).

The court therefore treats a police report containing the witness’s earlier remark exactly as it would treat any other out‑of‑court statement that the party wishes to introduce for impeachment. The fact that the report was **not authored by the witness** does not, by itself, bar its use; the only hurdle is authentication.

### Authentication requirements

* *Cumbie* (the case the *Cutbank* court cites) spells out the Eighth Circuit’s approach to authenticating written evidence:

1. **Foundation of custody or control** – The proponent must show that the document was **in the possession, custody, or control of the declarant** (or of a person who could reliably testify about its creation).  
2. **Reliability of the maker** – The proponent may introduce testimony from a law‑enforcement officer, investigator, or other qualified witness who can attest that the document was **prepared in the normal course of business** and that it reflects what the declarant said.  
3. **No hearsay barrier for impeachment** – Once authenticated, the writing is admissible for the limited purpose of **showing that the witness previously made a statement inconsistent with her trial testimony**; it is not admissible as substantive evidence of the underlying facts.

In *Cutbank* the district court refused to let the defense read portions of a police report because the report was not authored by C.F., but the appellate panel held that the correct standard was **authentication**, not authorship. The court affirmed the district court’s demand for a proper foundation and noted that the failure to provide it would be an abuse of discretion.

### Practical take‑away for practitioners

- **Yes**, you may introduce a police report (or any other written statement) to impeach a witness on the ground of a prior inconsistent statement **even if the witness does not testify to the report’s contents**.  
- **First**, lay a foundation that the report was made **by or at the direction of the witness** (or that the witness made the statements recorded in the report). A police investigator who took the statement can testify that the witness said X during the interview and that the report accurately reflects that interview.  
- **Second**, satisfy the *Cumbie* authentication test: show the document was in the declarant’s **custody or control** and that the maker had a **regular business purpose** for creating it.  
- **Third**, limit the use of the document to the **impeachment purpose**; do not rely on it for substantive proof of any fact.

When these steps are followed, the Eighth Circuit will admit the writing as admissible extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement, consistent with *United States v. Cumbie* and the *Cutbank* decision.

### Does the presence of a sawed‑off firearm automatically trigger the §5845(a) firearm‑related offense level increase, or must the court find additional elements?
Question:
The PSR applied a base offense level of 26 because the firearm was a sawed‑off rifle described in §5845(a). I’m trying to understand whether the mere possession of a sawed‑off weapon is sufficient for this enhancement, or if the court must also establish that the weapon was used in the commission of the crime. What is the controlling authority on this point in the Eighth Circuit?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In the Eighth Circuit the mere possession of a firearm that falls within the definition of a “sawed‑off” weapon under **26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)** is enough to trigger the § 5845(a) sentencing enhancement. The court does **not** have to find that the weapon was actually used in the underlying felony; possession alone satisfies the statutory element.

**Why the opinion answers the question**

The *Cutbank* opinion (8 Cir. 2025) directly explains why the sentencing guidelines gave the offense a base level of 26:

> “The PSR recommended a base offense level of 26 **because the offense involved a sawed‑off firearm described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)**…”  

The court treats the “sawed‑off” characteristic of the weapon as a **sentencing factor** that is **intrinsic to the conduct of possession**, not a separate predicate requiring proof of use. The opinion never says that the government had to prove the gun was *used* during the robbery or assault; it only needed to establish that the defendant possessed a firearm that, by its physical description (sawed‑off barrel and stock), fell within § 5845(a).

Later, when the court discusses the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) “firearm‑in‑connection‑with‑another‑felony” enhancement, it stresses that **use or possession** of any firearm in relation to a felony suffices, quoting the guideline comment:

> “This enhancement applies if a defendant **used or possessed** any firearm … ‘regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.’” § 2K2.1, comment. (n. 14(C))

That language mirrors the approach taken with the § 5845(a) factor: possession of the weapon, not its deployment, triggers the increase.

**Controlling authority in the Eighth Circuit**

*United States v. Cutbank*, 2025 WL … (8th Cir. Oct. 7 2025) is the most recent Eighth Circuit decision addressing the issue. The court’s reasoning rests on the plain text of § 5845(a) and the guideline’s explanatory note, and it has not been limited or overruled by any later Eighth Circuit precedent. No contrary authority in the circuit requires proof of actual use of the sawed‑off gun.

**Practical take‑away**

- **Element needed:** The firearm must be a *sawed‑off* weapon as defined in § 5845(a) (i.e., a rifle, shotgun, or pistol with a barrel length shorter than the statutory minimum, or a stock that renders the weapon non‑functional as intended).  
- **No additional element:** The prosecution does **not** need to prove the weapon was used to commit the underlying offense; possession alone satisfies the enhancement.  

Thus, under current Eighth Circuit authority, the presence of a sawed‑off firearm automatically adds the § 5845(a) offense‑level increase.
