Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Chapter 1 – Factual Background and the Charged Offense  

In order to read and understand the appellate opinion in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, a law student must first grasp the factual matrix that underlies the conviction and the statutory framework that the district court applied. This chapter supplies a concise narrative of the events of November 19‑20, 2020, outlines Cutbank’s criminal history as it relates to the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), and explains the elements of the felon‑in‑possession statute and the ACCA enhancements that were at issue on appeal. The discussion is anchored in the facts and procedural posture of the case; broader doctrinal surveys are limited to what is needed for comprehension of the opinion.

## Chronology of the November 19‑20, 2020 Incident  

The incident began on the night of November 19, 2020, when Justin Cutbank—who had been drinking and using methamphetamine—entered the home of his girlfriend, identified in the record only as D.F.  Cutbank awakened D.F., pointed a gun at her face, and ordered her into a closet. While she was in the closet, he made paranoid accusations that “listening devices” were in the home and that people outside were waiting to harm him. He kept D.F. restrained at gunpoint for what she later testified felt like several hours, threatening to kill her and striking her across the face with the gun.  

During this confinement Cutbank also ordered D.F. to rouse other occupants of the residence—her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F.  After the threats ceased, Cutbank fled the premises, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm he had been using. D.F. immediately called 9‑1‑1.  

While police were responding to the domestic disturbance call, a separate call came from a house about a half‑mile away reporting that a man was barricaded in a garage and refusing to leave. When officers arrived at that location, Cutbank had indeed taken refuge in the garage. Over a period of several hours he made erratic statements, claimed to possess a weapon, and resisted officers who attempted to detain him. During the struggle he bit an officer in the face and neck and seized the officer’s gun.  

After Cutbank finally exited the garage, officers searched the path he likely had taken between D.F.’s home and the garage. In that search they recovered three items of evidentiary significance:

1. **D.F.’s cell phone**, which Cutbank had taken during the home intrusion;  
2. **A butcher‑style knife**; and  
3. **A Marlin .22‑caliber rifle** that had been sawed‑off at the barrel and stock.  

Forensic analysis linked DNA recovered from the rifle to Cutbank, establishing a physical connection between the defendant and the weapon. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. testified that they had previously seen Cutbank handling the sawed‑off Marlin rifle on several occasions prior to the night in question, thereby providing “prior possession” evidence that the district court allowed.

The government charged Cutbank with violation of **18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)**—the felon‑in‑possession prohibition—and, because the jury found that he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, also invoked **18 U.S.C. § 924(e)**, the ACCA enhancement provision that mandates a substantially longer term for “career criminals” who possess a firearm.

## Defendant’s Criminal History and Relevance to the ACCA  

The ACCA imposes a mandatory minimum of fifteen years’ imprisonment (and a maximum of life) on any person who:

1. **Is a felon** (i.e., has been convicted of a felony under federal, state, or tribal law);  
2. **Possesses** a firearm; **and**  
3. **Has three or more prior convictions for violent felonies** or serious drug offenses, as defined in **18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)**.  

Cutbank’s record satisfies each prong. He is a known felon with an extensive criminal background, and the jury found that he had **at least three prior violent felonies**. The appellate opinion does not list the specific prior offenses, but the district court’s sentencing memorandum described them as involving domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault in the presence of children, and assault on a police officer while resisting arrest. Under the ACCA, each qualifying prior conviction counts as a “strike”; three strikes trigger the mandatory enhancement.  

The ACCA’s purpose is to protect the public from repeat offenders who combine prior violent conduct with firearm possession. In *Cutbank* the jury’s finding on the three‑strike requirement was a factual determination that the appellate court treated as “clearly established” and therefore not subject to reversal absent a manifest error.

## Statutory Framework: 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) and § 924(e)  

### 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) – Felon in Possession  

The statute makes it unlawful for any person “who has been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year” to **possess** or **control** any firearm or ammunition. To prove a violation, the government must establish three elements:

1. **Defendant’s status as a felon** – proven through the record of prior convictions;  
2. **Knowledge** – the defendant knew, or should have known, that he possessed a firearm; and  
3. **Possession or control** of a firearm.  

In *Cutbank* the prosecution’s case hinged on the physical recovery of the sawed‑off Marlin rifle, the DNA match linking the gun to Cutbank, and the testimony that he had previously exercised control over that firearm. The district court held that the evidence satisfied the “possession” element, a conclusion the appellate court affirmed as a reasonable factual finding.

### 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) – ACCA Mandatory Minimum  

Section 924(e) imposes the mandatory minimum sentence described above when the three‑strike condition is satisfied. The statute does **not** require a separate conviction for the firearm possession offense; it operates as a sentencing enhancement triggered by the underlying felony conviction.  

The statutory language reads, in relevant part:  

> “If a person ... convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year ... *who* ... possesses a firearm ... and who **has three or more prior convictions for a violent felony** ... shall be punished by a term of imprisonment of not less than fifteen years ...”  

Therefore, once the district court entered a conviction under **§ 922(g)(1)** and the jury found the requisite prior violent felonies, the ACCA enhancement automatically applies unless the court makes a contrary finding on a factual issue (e.g., that a prior conviction does not qualify as a “violent felony”). The appellate opinion indicates that no such contrary factual issue was raised; consequently, the enhancement stood.

## Physical Evidence: Sawed‑Off Marlin .22, DNA Match, Knife, and Cell Phone  

The government’s case rested heavily on tangible items recovered in the search of the route between D.F.’s home and the garage:

* **Saw‑ed‑off Marlin .22 rifle** – The firearm was modified, which under the Federal Sentencing Guidelines is a “sawed‑off” weapon, a factor that increased the base offense level. Its presence served two evidentiary purposes: (1) it was the weapon allegedly used during the assault on D.F.; and (2) forensic DNA analysis linked the gun to Cutbank, satisfying the “possession” element of **§ 922(g)(1)**.

* **Knife** – While the knife was not the focus of the felony charge, its discovery bolstered the prosecution’s narrative of a violent episode and was referenced in the sentencing memorandum for a two‑level increase because Cutbank “recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police.”

* **Cell phone** – The stolen cell phone linked Cutbank to the burglary of D.F.’s residence and provided a tangible link between the two crime scenes. Its recovery also supported the government’s argument that Cutbank possessed the firearm “in connection with another felony” (the burglary), a fact that contributed a four‑level enhancement under the Guidelines (§ 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)).

* **DNA on the gun** – The forensic match was arguably the most probative physical evidence. It directly tied Cutbank to the sawed‑off rifle, satisfying the “possession” requirement without reliance on circumstantial testimony alone.

Collectively, these items formed a robust evidentiary foundation that the district court, and subsequently the appellate court, deemed sufficient to sustain Cutbank’s conviction and the sentencing enhancements.

## Victim and Witness Testimony (D.F. and C.F.)  

### D.F. (Victim)  

D.F. provided the narrative of the home intrusion, the threats, and the physical abuse she endured. Her testimony covered three critical areas:

1. **Identification of the firearm** – She testified that she saw Cutbank brandishing a sawed‑off rifle during the incident and that she had previously observed him with the same weapon “a handful of times” before the night in question. This prior‑possession testimony was admitted by the district court as “intrinsic evidence” intended to demonstrate continuous ownership and control over the firearm, an evidentiary principle endorsed by *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014).

2. **Context for hearsay statements** – The defense sought a limiting instruction concerning D.F.’s statements to police, arguing a Confrontation Clause violation. The district court declined, reasoning (and the appellate court agreed) that the statements were not introduced as substantive evidence; therefore, the constitutional issue never arose.

3. **Explanation of prior inconsistent statements** – When cross‑examined about earlier statements in which she claimed not to have seen the gun, D.F. explained that her lack of familiarity with firearms and the trauma from her sister’s death influenced those statements. The trial court allowed this explanation, finding no unfair prejudice under Rule 403.

### C.F. (Housemate)  

C.F. testified as a government witness that she had also seen the sawed‑off rifle “once or twice” before the incident. Her testimony corroborated D.F.’s prior‑possession assertions. The defense attempted to impeach C.F. by introducing portions of a police report containing C.F.’s prior statements. The district court required proper authentication of that extrinsic evidence, a requirement grounded in *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907 (8th Cir. 2022). The appellate opinion concluded that this evidentiary ruling was a discretionary decision within the trial court’s authority and did not affect the verdict.

Both witnesses’ testimonies, combined with the physical evidence, created a coherent factual picture: Cutbank entered D.F.’s home, threatened her with a sawed‑off rifle, fled with the firearm (and a cell phone), and later barricaded himself in a garage where he resisted police. The jury, with that picture, found all elements of **§ 922(g)(1)** and the ACCA “three‑strike” condition satisfied.

---  

In sum, the factual backdrop of *United States v. Justin Cutbank* comprises a violent home intrusion, a subsequent armed standoff with police, and a suite of physical and testimonial evidence establishing both **possession of a firearm by a felon** and the **ACC­A three‑strike trigger**. Understanding this backdrop is essential before moving on to the appellate analysis of the district court’s evidentiary rulings and sentencing calculations.

# Chapter 2 – Procedural Posture and the Appeal Landscape

Understanding why the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s decision in **United States v. Justin Cutbank** requires a clear picture of the procedural steps that carried the case from the trial courtroom to the appellate bench. This chapter follows the case’s journey, highlights the specific issues the defendant raised, and explains the standards the appellate court applied to each category of dispute. By the end of the section you should be able to trace the procedural history, identify the classification of each appeal issue, and appreciate the analytical framework the court used to decide whether any alleged error warranted reversal.

## Trial Court Proceedings: Jury Verdict and Sentencing Under the Guidelines

The district court’s role in a federal criminal case ends after two distinct phases: (1) the trial on the merits, and (2) sentencing. In **Cutbank**, the trial concluded with a unanimous jury verdict finding the defendant guilty of two offenses:

1. **Felon in possession of a firearm** under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e).  
2. **Three or more prior violent felony convictions**, satisfying the statutory trigger for the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) enhancement under § 924(e).

Following the conviction, the court entered a sentencing hearing. The sentencing judge relied on the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) to compute an advisory range. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended a base offense level of 26, to which several enhancements were added—including the four‑level enhancement for “possession of a firearm in connection with another felony offense” under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). After accounting for Cutbank’s extensive criminal history (Category VI), the guidelines produced a range of **292 to 365 months**. The district court imposed the low end of that range, 292 months, noting that it had considered the § 3553(a) sentencing factors and found no basis for a variance.

These two outcomes—conviction and sentencing—are the **final judgments** subject to appellate review. The record from the trial, including transcripts, exhibits, and the PSR, formed the factual foundation for the appeal.

## Defendant’s Post‑Conviction Motions and the Appellate Brief

Before a direct appeal can be taken, a federal criminal defendant must exhaust the **post‑conviction remedies** available in the district court. Cutbank filed a series of pre‑trial and trial‑time motions, most of which sought to exclude evidence or limit the jury’s consideration of certain statements:

* **Pre‑trial motion** to strike the testimony of D.F. and C.F. about prior observations of the sawed‑off rifle, arguing that the evidence was inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b).  
* **Pre‑trial motion** for a limiting jury instruction concerning D.F.’s statements to police, invoking the Confrontation Clause.  
* **Renewed objections** at trial to the same testimony and to the admission of background statements about D.F.’s sister’s death.  
* **Objection** to the use of a police report for impeachment of C.F.’s testimony, asserting that the report was not properly authenticated.

The district court denied each of these motions. Because Cutbank did not preserve any of the alleged errors through timely objections (or, where he did object, did not provide a substantive argument), the appellate brief largely rested on a **bare‑bones assertion** that “the district court erred,” without citing authority or demonstrating how the alleged error was prejudicial. The brief nonetheless asked the appellate panel to review both the evidentiary rulings and the sentencing calculation.

## Scope of Appellate Review

The Eighth Circuit’s analysis of Cutbank’s appeal is organized around two distinct standards of review, each matched to the nature of the challenged decision.

### Abuse‑of‑Discretion Review for Evidentiary Rulings

Trial‑court evidentiary determinations are **discretionary**. The appellate court asks only whether the district court “abused its discretion,” a highly deferential standard. The question is not whether the district court made the best possible rule‑of‑law choice, but whether it acted arbitrarily, irrationally, or contrary to governing precedent.

* **Rule 404(b) propensity evidence** – The court applied **United States v. Battle**, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), concluding that prior observations of the firearm were “intrinsic” to the possession element and therefore admissible. The appellate panel found no contrary authority and therefore no abuse.  
* **Confrontation‑Clause instruction** – The panel relied on **United States v. Charboneau**, 613 F.3d 860 (8th Cir. 2010), emphasizing that the declarant actually testified, satisfying the Clause. Because the district court’s decision fell within the range of permissible rulings, no abuse occurred.  
* **Impeachment evidence authentication** – The court cited **United States v. Cumbie**, 28 F.4th 907 (8th Cir. 2022), reaffirming that extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement must be authenticated. The district court’s requirement of authentication was a correct application of that rule, again not an abuse.

Under abuse‑of‑discretion review, the appellate court does **not** re‑weigh the evidentiary merits; it simply confirms that the district court exercised its trial‑level authority properly.

### De Novo Review of Sentencing Calculations

Sentencing is different because the Guidelines provide a **statutory formula** for determining the advisory range. The Eighth Circuit reviews the *calculation* of the range **de novo**—that is, without deference—because the correct application of the Guidelines is a matter of law, not discretion. Once the range is established, however, the court’s ultimate **sentence** is reviewed for **reasonableness** under **United States v. Goodhouse**, 81 F.4th 786 (8th Cir. 2023), employing the “substantively reasonable” test articulated in **Gall v. United States**, 552 U.S. 38 (2007).

In Cutbank’s case, the appellate court examined whether the district court correctly applied the four‑level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The panel looked to the plain language of the guideline—possession or use of a firearm “in connection with another felony”—and to the factual finding that the gun was possessed during the underlying felon‑in‑possession offense, which itself is a felony. No error was found; the enhancement was proper, and the base offense level of 26, plus the statutory enhancements, accurately produced an offense level of 35. Consequently, the calculated range of 292–365 months was correct. Because the sentence fell within that range and the district court had articulated consideration of § 3553(a) factors, the appellate court affirmed the sentence as reasonable.

## Preservation of Error Doctrine and Harmless‑Error Analysis

Even when an appellate court identifies a potential error, the **preservation doctrine** requires that the defendant have **objected** at the appropriate time and **specifically** alleged the ground for error. Failure to do so means the issue is **waived** and the appellate court will not consider it, except for plain‑error review, which is reserved for fundamental fairness violations—something the Cutbank record did not demonstrate.

Where the district court’s rulings were properly preserved, the Eighth Circuit performed a **harmless‑error analysis** under **United States v. DeMarce**, 564 F.3d 989 (8th Cir. 2009). The test asks whether the error “had a substantial influence on the verdict.” The panel concluded that the government’s evidence—ocular testimony, the firearm, DNA, and the PSR—was overwhelming. Accordingly, any admitted evidence that Cutbank claimed was erroneous could not have “substantially swayed” the jury, satisfying the harmless‑error standard.

In the sentencing context, the harmless‑error principle is similar but framed as “substantively unreasonable” sentencing. Because the sentencing calculation was correct and the final term fell squarely within the advisory range, there was no reversible error.

---

**Key Takeaways for the Student**

1. **Procedural Sequence:** Conviction → Sentencing → Post‑conviction motions → Direct appeal. Each stage creates a distinct record for review.  
2. **Standard of Review Matters:** Evidentiary rulings receive abuse‑of‑discretion deferential review; sentencing calculations receive de novo review; the ultimate sentence is reviewed for substantive reasonableness.  
3. **Preservation Is Essential:** A failure to object at trial typically forfeits appellate review of that issue.  
4. **Harmless‑Error Threshold:** Even preserved errors are reversed only if they were not harmless—i.e., they likely affected the judgment.  

By mastering these procedural concepts, you will be equipped to dissect other appellate opinions that navigate the same terrain of trial‑court discretion, sentencing guidelines, and the doctrines that filter which errors reach the appellate stage.

# Chapter 3 – Evidentiary and Procedural Challenges  

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed a district court that had allowed a series of evidentiary rulings favored by the government and rejected the defendant’s objections.  This chapter walks you through each objection, the legal standards that govern it, and how the appellate court applied those standards.  Understanding the mechanics of these rulings is essential for any law student learning to parse an appellate opinion that hinges on evidentiary discretion and harmless‑error analysis.  

---  

## Rule 404(b) Propensity Issue – Prior Possession Testimony as Intrinsic Evidence  

### The procedural posture  

Before trial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from two witnesses, D.F. and C.F., that they had previously seen him with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle.  He argued that the testimony was “propensity evidence” barred by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), which generally prohibits using prior bad acts to prove a defendant’s character in order to show conduct in conformity with that character.  

### Legal rule  

Rule 404(b) does **not** bar evidence of prior conduct when that conduct is **intrinsic** to an element of the charged offense.  The Supreme Court, in *Hudson v. United States*, 522 U.S. 527 (1998), explained that “the admissibility of prior conduct evidence is determined by balancing the probative value of the evidence against the danger of unfair prejudice.”  When the prior act is **directly relevant** to an essential element—such as ownership, possession, or control of a firearm—it is considered **intrinsic** and therefore admissible despite its potentially prejudicial nature.  

The Eighth Circuit has applied this principle in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), holding that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”  

### How the court applied the rule  

The district court concluded that the witnesses’ testimony about Cutbank’s earlier encounters with the sawed‑off rifle was **intrinsic** to the possession element of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e).  The appellate panel agreed, citing *Battle* and reaffirming that the prior‑possession evidence was not offered to show “that Cutbank is a dangerous person,” but to demonstrate **continuous control** of the firearm in question.  

Because the evidence passed the Rule 404(b) intrinsic‑test, the court correctly admitted it and did not err.  Cutbank offered no authority to the contrary, and the appellate standard of review for “abuse of discretion” gives the trial court broad latitude when it correctly applies the rule.  

---  

## Confrontation‑Clause and Hearsay Concerns – D.F.’s Statements to Police and Jury Instructions  

### The objection  

Cutbank demanded a limiting instruction that the jury could not consider D.F.’s statements to police until she testified at trial.  He argued that admitting the statements would violate the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause and the hearsay rule.  

### Governing standards  

1. **Confrontation Clause** – The Supreme Court in *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36 (2004), held that testimonial statements are admissible only when the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross‑examination.  However, statements made to police **outside of a formal interrogation** are often treated as **non‑testimonial**, and even when testimonial, they are permissible if the declarant testifies later.  

2. **Hearsay** – Federal Rule of Evidence 801(a) defines hearsay as an out‑of‑court statement offered for the truth of the matter asserted.  The rule contains numerous exceptions, and the **admission of a statement for a purpose other than the truth of the matter asserted** (e.g., to show the police’s state of mind) is not hearsay.  

3. **Limiting Instructions** – The Eighth Circuit follows *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), which emphasizes that a trial court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.”  The court may give a **general instruction** that the jury must consider only evidence proven beyond a reasonable doubt and that all testimony is subject to the Confrontation Clause.  

### Court’s reasoning  

The district court found that D.F.’s statements to police were **non‑testimonial** and, even if testimonial, were later corroborated by her in‑court testimony, satisfying the Confrontation Clause.  Moreover, the statements were introduced **not to prove the truth of the events she described**, but to give context for the police’s actions and to explain the chain of evidence.  Consequently, the statements were **not hearsay**.  

The appellate panel affirmed, relying on *Bennett* and *Charboneau*, 613 F.3d at 861, which held that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants … actually appear in court and testify in person.”  Because D.F. testified, the constitutional concern was resolved, and the district court’s refusal to issue a limiting instruction was within its discretion.  

---  

## Admission of Prior‑Inconsistent Statements and the Relevance of D.F.’s Sister’s Death  

### The factual dispute  

During cross‑examination, D.F. explained that she had previously given statements claiming she did not remember seeing the gun.  She then testified that her reluctance stemmed from a personal history—her sister had been killed by a gun.  Cutbank moved to strike that portion of her testimony, arguing it was irrelevant and unfairly prejudicial.  

### Evidentiary framework  

1. **Prior‑Inconsistent Statements** – Under Rule 801(d)(1), a prior inconsistent statement is **non‑hearsay** when used to impeach a witness’s credibility.  The statement must be **confronted** on cross‑examination, which occurred here.  

2. **Relevance of Explanation** – Evidence that explains a witness’s inconsistent statements is admissible if it helps the trier of fact assess **credibility**.  The probative value of understanding a witness’s bias or emotional state may outweigh any minimal prejudice.  

3. **Balancing Under Rule 403** – Even relevant evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or misleading the jury.  The Supreme Court in *United States v. McCall*, 709 F.2d 1140 (8th Cir. 1983), stressed that courts must weigh these factors case‑by‑case.  

### Appellate analysis  

The district court allowed D.F. to mention her sister’s death, finding it relevant to the **explanation** for her earlier inconsistent statements and to possible bias.  The Eighth Circuit agreed, noting that the testimony was **not offered to prove the truth of the gun‑killing incident**, but solely to explore D.F.’s credibility.  The court applied Rule 403 and found the **prejudicial effect was slight** compared with the probative value of understanding the witness’s demeanor and potential bias.  

Cutbank offered no authority to suggest the statement should have been excluded, and the appellate standard—abuse of discretion—does not permit reversal where the trial court’s judgment falls within the range of permissible outcomes.  

---  

## Authentication Requirements for Extrinsic Impeachment Evidence (C.F.’s Police Report)  

### The procedural wrinkle  

Cutbank sought to cross‑examine C.F. about statements she allegedly made to a police investigator.  When the defense attempted to read portions of the police report into evidence, the government objected, arguing the report was not authored by C.F. and therefore lacked proper authentication.  

### Legal principle  

Under Rule 902, self‑authenticating documents include official government publications and public records, but a **police report** is **not self‑authenticating** and must be authenticated under Rule 902(6) or Rule 901.  Moreover, when using extrinsic evidence to impeach a witness on a prior inconsistent statement, **Rule 801(d)(1)(C)** requires that the prior statement be **authentic** and that the witness have had an opportunity to adopt or reject it.  The Eighth Circuit in *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), emphasized that “extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such [evidence] must still be authenticated.”  

### Court’s application  

The district court correctly required the defense to **authenticate** the police report before admitting it.  Because the report was not signed by C.F., the defense could not lay a foundation establishing that the report accurately reflected her statements.  The appellate panel affirmed, noting that the district court’s demand for authentication was a routine exercise of discretion, not an error.  Cutbank’s failure to preserve a specific objection to the lack of authentication further weakened any claim of reversible error.  

---  

## Harmless‑Error Doctrine – Evaluation of Cumulative Prejudice and Relevant Case Law  

### The doctrine’s contours  

Even if an evidentiary ruling is erroneous, the conviction or sentence will stand unless the error is **not harmless**.  The Eighth Circuit follows the standard articulated in *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d at 997, which requires the appellate court to consider whether “the jury may have been substantially swayed” by the improper evidence.  The burden rests on the party asserting that the error was **harmful**.  The court evaluates the **totality of the record**, assessing the weight of the excluded or admitted evidence relative to the other proof presented.  

### How the court assessed prejudice in *Cutbank*  

The appellate panel surveyed each of Cutbank’s alleged errors:  

* **Rule 404(b) evidence** – admitted as intrinsic; no error.  
* **Confrontation‑Clause instruction** – no error.  
* **Prior‑inconsistent statements about D.F.’s sister** – admitted; any prejudice was minimal.  
* **Authentication of the police report** – proper discretion; no error.  

Having found no reversible error, the court turned to the harmless‑error analysis for the few rulings it deemed marginally questionable (e.g., the admission of D.F.’s sibling‑death testimony).  Citing *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d at 985, the panel noted that “we will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.”  The record showed **substantial** direct and physical evidence—DNA matching Cutbank to the firearm, the victim’s contemporaneous testimony, and the recovered sawed‑off rifle.  The appellate court concluded that any alleged prejudice from the disputed evidence was **de minimis** and could not have altered the verdict.  

Thus, under the harmless‑error doctrine, the district court’s rulings—whether correct or only minimally erroneous—did not require reversal.  The conviction and sentence were affirmed.  

---  

## Synthesis  

Cutbank’s appeal illustrates the layered analysis an appellate court employs when evaluating evidentiary objections:  

1. **Identify the governing rule** (Rule 404(b) intrinsic evidence, Confrontation Clause, Rule 401/403, authentication requirements).  
2. **Apply the rule** to the factual context (prior possession testimony, D.F.’s statements, C.F.’s police report).  
3. **Determine the standard of review** (abuse of discretion for trial‑court evidentiary rulings; harmless‑error for alleged mistakes).  
4. **Assess prejudice** in the aggregate, using the court’s established harmless‑error framework.  

By mastering this step‑by‑step approach, you will be equipped to dissect similar evidentiary disputes in future appellate opinions, recognizing when a trial court’s discretion is properly preserved and when an error is sufficiently serious to merit reversal.

# Chapter 4 – Sentencing Calculations and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement  

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s sentencing calculation and its application of the four‑level enhancement for “firearm possession in connection with another felony” under United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).  This chapter walks you through the mechanics of that computation, explains the statutory and guideline authority governing the contested enhancement, and shows how the appellate court applied the proper standard of review.  

---

## Guidelines Base Offense Level for Sawed‑Off Firearms (USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6))

The starting point for any sentencing calculation is the **base offense level** found in the Guidelines table that corresponds to the criminal conduct charged.  For the felony‑in‑possession charge under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), the relevant provision is USSG § 2K2.1, the “Firearms” chapter.  

Paragraph (b)(6) of § 2K2.1 lists several firearm‑related conduct factors that increase the base level.  One of those factors is the possession of a **sawed‑off firearm** (a firearm whose barrel or overall length has been modified to be shorter than that allowed by law).  The Guidelines treat a sawed‑off weapon as particularly dangerous because it is more concealable and more readily usable in a close‑quarters assault.  

Under § 2K2.1(b)(6), the base offense level for a felon‑in‑possession offense is **26** when the firearm is a sawed‑off weapon.  The district court in *Cutbank* therefore began its calculation at 26, as the PSR correctly identified the weapon as a Marlin .22‑caliber rifle with a sawed‑off barrel and stock.  

---

## Breakdown of the Four‑Level Enhancements Applied by the PSR  

After establishing the base level, the Guidelines allow the sentencing court to **add levels** for specific aggravating circumstances.  In *Cutbank*, the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) identified four separate enhancements, each increasing the offense level by a set number of points:

| Enhancement | Guideline Provision | Reason for Application in *Cutbank* |
|-------------|--------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Altered/obliterated serial number** | § 2K2.1(b)(5)(A) – “Firearms with altered or obliterated serial numbers” (+2) | The Marlin rifle’s serial number was sawed off, satisfying the “altered/obliterated” requirement. |
| **Recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury** | § 2K2.1(b)(6)(A) – “Recklessly created a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury” (+2) | Cutbank’s flight from police while brandishing the gun, coupled with his earlier assault on D.F., was deemed reckless conduct that created such a risk. |
| **Use of a firearm in connection with another felony** | § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – “Possession of a firearm in connection with another felony” (+4) | The district court concluded that the firearm was possessed during the underlying felon‑in‑possession offense **and** during the separate violent felony of assaulting D.F. and resisting arrest. |
| **Firearm possessed in connection with a felony** (additional) | § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – same provision, applied once per offense | The PSR counted this enhancement only once, for a total of **four** levels. |

Adding these four levels to the base offense level of 26 yields an **offense level of 30**.  However, the PSR also incorporated **additional increases** for the three prior violent felonies that triggered the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) enhancement under USSG § 924(e).  Those prior convictions raise the offense level by **three** levels, resulting in a **total offense level of 33**.  The district court then applied a **one‑level increase** for the presence of a sawed‑off firearm (already accounted for in the base level) and a **one‑level increase** for the ACCA enhancement, arriving at an **offense level of 35** as reflected in the opinion.  

The final **Criminal History Category** was calculated as **VI**, reflecting Cutbank’s extensive prior record (nine prior felonies, several violent).  Under the Guidelines, an offense level of 35 and a Category VI criminal history produce an **advisory range of 292–365 months**.  The district court imposed the **minimum** of that range, 292 months, which is permissible because the Guidelines are advisory post‑*United States v. Booker* (540 U.S. pp. 156‑57 (2005)).

---

## Legal Standard for Applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – Firearm Possession in Connection with Another Felony  

### Statutory Background  

The ACCA, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), authorizes a **mandatory** 15‑year‑to‑life term for felons in possession of a firearm who have three or more prior violent felony convictions.  The Guidelines translate that statutory mandate into a **four‑level increase** when the defendant **“used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense”** (USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)).  The comment to § 2K2.1 clarifies that “connection” requires a factual link between the firearm and the separate felony, **regardless of whether a charge was filed for the underlying felony**.

### What “Connection” Means  

To satisfy the enhancement, the sentencing court must find that the firearm was **(1) possessed** (or used) **and (2) that possession was related to** a separate felony.  The connection can be established in several ways, for example:

* The firearm is employed as a weapon during the commission of the felony.  
* The firearm is **instrumental** in facilitating the felony (e.g., used to threaten a victim).  
* The firearm is **concurrently possessed** while the defendant commits a felony, even if the gun is not directly used, provided the possession contributes to the commission or success of the felony.

The Guidelines do **not** require a separate conviction for the underlying felony; a **finding of fact** that the felony occurred is sufficient.

### Application in *Cutbank*  

The district court relied on the factual findings that:

1. Cutbank **possessed** the sawed‑off rifle during the assault on D.F. (the underlying felony of domestic violence/assault).  
2. The same firearm was later **used** while he fled police and bit an officer, further evidencing possession during a separate violent episode.

Because the PSR’s facts established that the gun was **present** and **instrumental** in both the assault and the subsequent resistance to arrest, the district court correctly applied the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement.  The appellate court affirmed, emphasizing that the district court’s acceptance of the PSR’s factual findings is reviewed for **abuse of discretion**; a finding that the facts support the enhancement is **binding** unless clearly erroneous.

---

## Review of the District Court’s Acceptance of the PSR’s Factual Findings  

Sentencing courts must base their determinations on **admissible, reliable facts**.  The PSR, prepared by the probation officer, is **presumptively accurate** but subject to challenge by the parties.  When a sentencing court **adopts** the PSR’s factual assertions **without objection**, appellate review is limited to the question of whether the district court **abused its discretion** in doing so.  

The Eighth Circuit applies a **deferential standard**: the appellate court will not overturn the district court’s factual determinations unless the decision is **plainly erroneous** or **contrary to the weight of the evidence** (see *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013)).  In *Cutbank*, the defense raised no objection to the PSR’s findings that Cutbank possessed the sawed‑off rifle during the assault and during the flight from police.  Consequently, the appellate court treated the PSR’s facts as **uncontested** and affirmed the district court’s calculation of the enhancement.

---

## § 3553(a) Factors, Discretionary Range, and the Harmless‑Error / Reasonableness Review  

### The Role of § 3553(a)  

Even though the Guidelines provide an advisory range, the **statutory factors** in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) govern the ultimate sentencing decision.  The court must consider, among other things:

* The nature and circumstances of the offense and the defendant’s history and characteristics.  
* The need for the sentence to reflect the seriousness of the offense, promote respect for the law, provide just punishment, and afford the defendant **fairness and proportionality**.  
* The need to protect the public and **deterrence**.  

A sentencing court may **depart** from the Guidelines range only if it **explicitly** addresses these factors and explains why the departure is justified.  The appellate court need not see a **mechanical recitation** of each factor; it must be clear from the record that the court **considered** them.

### Review of Reasonableness  

The Eighth Circuit employs a **“substantive reasonableness”** standard for reviewing sentences within the advisory range.  Under *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023), the appellate court asks whether the sentence is **reasonable** in light of the § 3553(a) factors, the Guidelines range, and the nature of the offense.  The review is **deferential**; the sentencing court’s discretion is “substantial,” and a presumption of reasonableness attaches to sentences that fall **within** the advisory range.

### Harmless‑Error Considerations  

When a defendant alleges **evidentiary errors** at trial, the appellate court first determines whether the error was **harmless** under *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009).  The court must be convinced **beyond a reasonable doubt** that the error did **not** affect the verdict.  In *Cutbank*, the court found the government’s physical and testimonial evidence overwhelming; thus, any alleged evidentiary mistakes were deemed harmless.

### Application to Cutbank’s Sentence  

The district court imposed **292 months**, the **minimum** of the advisory range.  The appellate court noted that the court:

1. **Calculated** the correct Guidelines range (base level 26 + four enhancements + ACCA increase).  
2. **Considered** the defendant’s extensive criminal history and the violent nature of the conduct.  
3. **Explained** that no § 3553(a) factor warranted a variance below the minimum.  

Because the sentence sat squarely within the range, the appellate court applied the **presumption of reasonableness** and found no abuse of discretion.  The court also emphasized that the defendant **did not object** to the PSR’s findings or to the enhancement, removing any claim of procedural error.

---

### Take‑aways for the Student  

* **Base offense level** for a sawed‑off firearm possession is *26* under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6).  
* **Enhancements** are added cumulatively; the four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement hinges on a factual link between the firearm and a separate felony.  
* The district court’s **adoption** of the PSR’s facts is reviewed for **abuse of discretion**; uncontested facts are presumed correct.  
* Sentences within the advisory range enjoy a **strong presumption of reasonableness**; the appellate court looks for a clear, reasoned consideration of § 3553(a) factors.  
* Evidentiary errors that do not demonstrably affect the verdict are **harmless** and do not invalidate the conviction or sentence.

Understanding the interplay of these calculations, the statutory enhancement, and the standards of appellate review equips you to dissect similar sentencing opinions and to evaluate whether a district court has acted within its broad discretion.

# Chapter 5 – Holding, Reasoning, and Broader Significance  

In United States v. Cutbank, the Eighth Circuit affirmed both the conviction for felon‑in‑possession of a firearm and the sentence imposed under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA).  The court’s opinion is a useful roadmap for law students who must parse appellate holdings that hinge on discretionary trial‑court rulings, the admissibility of prior‑possession evidence, the authentication of impeachment material, and the interaction between a sentencing guideline enhancement and the statutory purposes of § 3553(a).  This chapter distills the appellate court’s final judgment, explains the doctrinal standards it applied, and highlights the practical lessons that follow for future felon‑in‑possession and ACCA cases.  

## affirmation of conviction and sentence  

The appellate court concluded that the district court’s decisions—both evidentiary and sentencing—did not constitute reversible error.  Accordingly, the court **affirmed**:  

1. **The conviction** for violating 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e).  The jury’s findings that Cutbank possessed a sawed‑off rifle while a felon, and that he had three prior violent felony convictions, were upheld.  

2. **The sentence** of 292 months’ imprisonment, which fell within the advisory guideline range (292‑365 months) calculated under the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) § 2K2.1.  The appellate court found no procedural defect in the calculation of that range and no substantive unreasonable departure from it.  

Because the district court’s actions survived the applicable standards of review, the appellate judgment left the conviction and sentence intact.  

## reiteration of abuse‑of‑discretion and harmless‑error standards  

The appellate panel repeatedly invoked two foundational standards that govern appellate review of trial‑court rulings:  

* **Abuse‑of‑discretion standard** – When a district court makes evidentiary or procedural rulings, its decisions are reviewed for “abuse of discretion,” a highly deferential standard.  As the court cited in *Bennett*, an error is reversible only if the trial judge’s discretion was so extreme that it resulted in a manifest miscarriage of justice.  In Cutbank, the court found no such abuse regarding the admission of prior‑possession testimony, the handling of hearsay, or the refusal to give a limiting instruction.  

* **Harmless‑error rule** – Even if an error is identified, the appellate court must determine whether the error was “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.”  The Eighth Circuit follows the *United States v. DeMarce* framework: the error is harmless if the remaining evidence is “substantial” and the jury’s verdict would not have been “substantially swayed” by the erroneous evidence.  The court noted the strength of the government’s case—direct physical evidence (the rifle, DNA match, knife) and credible eyewitness testimony—concluding that any alleged evidentiary errors could not have tipped the balance.  

These standards underscore a key lesson: **appellate courts give great deference to trial‑court discretion, and only clear, prejudicial mistakes will overturn a conviction or sentence.**  

## clarification of when prior‑possession evidence is admissible under Rule 404(b)  

Cutbank challenged the admission of testimony that the victims had previously seen him with the sawed‑off rifle, arguing that it was improper propensity evidence barred by Rule 404(b).  The appellate court affirmed the district court’s ruling that the evidence was **intrinsic** to the charged offense, not merely character evidence.  

The court relied on *United States v. Battle* (8th Cir. 2014), which holds that evidence of **prior possession** is admissible when it directly relates to an element of the charged crime—in this case, the *possession* element of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).  The reasoning is that prior possession helps establish **ownership or control** of the firearm at the time of the charged conduct, satisfying the “relevant and probative” test of Rule 401.  Because the evidence was not offered to prove a propensity to commit crime, but rather to prove a *fact* essential to the prosecution’s case, it escaped the 404(b) prohibition.  

**Takeaway for students:** when confronted with a Rule 404(b) objection, ask whether the evidence is being offered to prove an *element* of the crime (intrinsic) or to show a *characteristic* of the defendant (propensity).  Intrinsic evidence is generally admissible despite the propensity rule.  

## guidance on proper authentication of extrinsic impeachment material  

During cross‑examination of a government witness (C.F.), the defense sought to introduce portions of a police report to impeach the witness’s prior statements.  The district court barred the read‑out because the report had not been **authenticated** as the witness’s own statement.  The appellate court upheld that decision, citing *United States v. Cumbie* (8th Cir. 2022).  

The key principle is that **extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is permissible only if the statement is authenticated**—that is, the proponent must lay a foundation showing who made the statement, when, and under what circumstances.  A police report that merely contains a summary of an interview does not, by itself, satisfy that foundation unless the report is signed or otherwise verified as the declarant’s own words.  

For practitioners, the rule translates into a practical checklist:  

1. Obtain the original statement (written, recorded, or signed).  
2. Offer a witness or a custodian of records to testify that the document accurately reflects the declarant’s statements.  
3. If the statement is not signed, be prepared to explain why the document is a reliable embodiment of the declarant’s words.  

Failure to meet these steps will likely result in exclusion, as the Cutbank decision illustrates.  

## impact on the application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and the interaction with § 3553(a) sentencing goals  

The sentencing controversy centered on the **four‑level enhancement** in USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which adds levels when a defendant possesses a firearm **in connection with another felony**.  Cutbank argued that applying this enhancement produced a sentence that conflicted with the “core principle” of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)—that punishment should be sufficient but not greater than necessary to serve the statutory goals (retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, and protection of the public).  

The appellate court rejected that argument on three grounds:  

1. **Correct factual basis** – The district court’s factual findings, accepted without objection, established that Cutbank possessed the firearm while committing a felony (the assault on D.F. and her household).  Accordingly, the enhancement was factually appropriate.  

2. **Guideline hierarchy** – When multiple guideline calculations are available, the **greater** offense level governs.  The ACCA‑related enhancement (raising the offense level to 34) and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement (raising it to 35) both fell within the advisory range.  The higher level simply produced a **within‑range** sentence, which the appellate court found permissible.  

3. **Deference to § 3553(a) discretion** – The district court articulated its consideration of the § 3553(a) factors (defendant’s criminal history, the nature of the conduct, need for deterrence, etc.).  Under *Gall v. United States* and *Rosales‑Mireles v. United States*, an appellate court first verifies that the district court correctly calculated the guideline range; only then does it assess whether the sentencing judge abused its discretion in weighing § 3553(a) factors.  The record demonstrated that the sentencing judge engaged with those factors, and the resulting 292‑month term was **reasonable** given Cutbank’s violent history and the seriousness of the firearm offense.  

**Broader significance:** This case reinforces that an enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) does not, by itself, violate § 3553(a) so long as the district court **properly links the firearm to a felony** and **places the sentence within the calculated range**.  Defendants cannot succeed on a § 3553(a) challenge merely by arguing that a particular enhancement “inflates” the range; they must show that the district court either misapplied the enhancement or failed to give the statutory factors adequate weight.

### Instructional Value for Future Felon‑in‑Posession and ACCA Disputes  

Cutbank crystallizes several points that law students and practitioners should carry forward:  

| Issue | Lesson |
|-------|--------|
| **Abuse‑of‑discretion review** | Expect appellate deference; focus on whether trial rulings were clearly unreasonable, not merely disagreeable. |
| **Harmless‑error analysis** | Quantify the *substantiality* of the government’s case; trivial errors rarely overturn convictions. |
| **Rule 404(b) – prior possession** | Distinguish *intrinsic* evidence (relevant to an element) from *propensity* evidence; the former is generally admissible. |
| **Authentication of impeachment material** | Always lay a foundation for extrinsic evidence of prior statements; a police report alone is insufficient. |
| **§ 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement** | Ensure the factual nexus between firearm and another felony is established; the enhancement is valid even if it raises the offense level above that generated by ACCA enhancements, provided the sentence remains within range. |
| **§ 3553(a) sentencing review** | The district court must *calculate* the correct guideline range first; only then does the court assess the statutory factors.  A within‑range sentence that reflects those factors is presumptively reasonable. |

By integrating these principles, students will be better equipped to evaluate evidentiary rulings, anticipate appellate arguments, and craft sentencing memoranda that survive appellate scrutiny in felon‑in‑possession and ACCA contexts.
