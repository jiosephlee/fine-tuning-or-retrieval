Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4 – Sentencing Calculations and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement  

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s sentencing calculation and its application of the four‑level enhancement for “firearm possession in connection with another felony” under United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).  This chapter walks you through the mechanics of that computation, explains the statutory and guideline authority governing the contested enhancement, and shows how the appellate court applied the proper standard of review.  

---

## Guidelines Base Offense Level for Sawed‑Off Firearms (USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6))

The starting point for any sentencing calculation is the **base offense level** found in the Guidelines table that corresponds to the criminal conduct charged.  For the felony‑in‑possession charge under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), the relevant provision is USSG § 2K2.1, the “Firearms” chapter.  

Paragraph (b)(6) of § 2K2.1 lists several firearm‑related conduct factors that increase the base level.  One of those factors is the possession of a **sawed‑off firearm** (a firearm whose barrel or overall length has been modified to be shorter than that allowed by law).  The Guidelines treat a sawed‑off weapon as particularly dangerous because it is more concealable and more readily usable in a close‑quarters assault.  

Under § 2K2.1(b)(6), the base offense level for a felon‑in‑possession offense is **26** when the firearm is a sawed‑off weapon.  The district court in *Cutbank* therefore began its calculation at 26, as the PSR correctly identified the weapon as a Marlin .22‑caliber rifle with a sawed‑off barrel and stock.  

---

## Breakdown of the Four‑Level Enhancements Applied by the PSR  

After establishing the base level, the Guidelines allow the sentencing court to **add levels** for specific aggravating circumstances.  In *Cutbank*, the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) identified four separate enhancements, each increasing the offense level by a set number of points:

| Enhancement | Guideline Provision | Reason for Application in *Cutbank* |
|-------------|--------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Altered/obliterated serial number** | § 2K2.1(b)(5)(A) – “Firearms with altered or obliterated serial numbers” (+2) | The Marlin rifle’s serial number was sawed off, satisfying the “altered/obliterated” requirement. |
| **Recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury** | § 2K2.1(b)(6)(A) – “Recklessly created a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury” (+2) | Cutbank’s flight from police while brandishing the gun, coupled with his earlier assault on D.F., was deemed reckless conduct that created such a risk. |
| **Use of a firearm in connection with another felony** | § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – “Possession of a firearm in connection with another felony” (+4) | The district court concluded that the firearm was possessed during the underlying felon‑in‑possession offense **and** during the separate violent felony of assaulting D.F. and resisting arrest. |
| **Firearm possessed in connection with a felony** (additional) | § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – same provision, applied once per offense | The PSR counted this enhancement only once, for a total of **four** levels. |

Adding these four levels to the base offense level of 26 yields an **offense level of 30**.  However, the PSR also incorporated **additional increases** for the three prior violent felonies that triggered the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) enhancement under USSG § 924(e).  Those prior convictions raise the offense level by **three** levels, resulting in a **total offense level of 33**.  The district court then applied a **one‑level increase** for the presence of a sawed‑off firearm (already accounted for in the base level) and a **one‑level increase** for the ACCA enhancement, arriving at an **offense level of 35** as reflected in the opinion.  

The final **Criminal History Category** was calculated as **VI**, reflecting Cutbank’s extensive prior record (nine prior felonies, several violent).  Under the Guidelines, an offense level of 35 and a Category VI criminal history produce an **advisory range of 292–365 months**.  The district court imposed the **minimum** of that range, 292 months, which is permissible because the Guidelines are advisory post‑*United States v. Booker* (540 U.S. pp. 156‑57 (2005)).

---

## Legal Standard for Applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) – Firearm Possession in Connection with Another Felony  

### Statutory Background  

The ACCA, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), authorizes a **mandatory** 15‑year‑to‑life term for felons in possession of a firearm who have three or more prior violent felony convictions.  The Guidelines translate that statutory mandate into a **four‑level increase** when the defendant **“used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another felony offense”** (USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)).  The comment to § 2K2.1 clarifies that “connection” requires a factual link between the firearm and the separate felony, **regardless of whether a charge was filed for the underlying felony**.

### What “Connection” Means  

To satisfy the enhancement, the sentencing court must find that the firearm was **(1) possessed** (or used) **and (2) that possession was related to** a separate felony.  The connection can be established in several ways, for example:

* The firearm is employed as a weapon during the commission of the felony.  
* The firearm is **instrumental** in facilitating the felony (e.g., used to threaten a victim).  
* The firearm is **concurrently possessed** while the defendant commits a felony, even if the gun is not directly used, provided the possession contributes to the commission or success of the felony.

The Guidelines do **not** require a separate conviction for the underlying felony; a **finding of fact** that the felony occurred is sufficient.

### Application in *Cutbank*  

The district court relied on the factual findings that:

1. Cutbank **possessed** the sawed‑off rifle during the assault on D.F. (the underlying felony of domestic violence/assault).  
2. The same firearm was later **used** while he fled police and bit an officer, further evidencing possession during a separate violent episode.

Because the PSR’s facts established that the gun was **present** and **instrumental** in both the assault and the subsequent resistance to arrest, the district court correctly applied the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement.  The appellate court affirmed, emphasizing that the district court’s acceptance of the PSR’s factual findings is reviewed for **abuse of discretion**; a finding that the facts support the enhancement is **binding** unless clearly erroneous.

---

## Review of the District Court’s Acceptance of the PSR’s Factual Findings  

Sentencing courts must base their determinations on **admissible, reliable facts**.  The PSR, prepared by the probation officer, is **presumptively accurate** but subject to challenge by the parties.  When a sentencing court **adopts** the PSR’s factual assertions **without objection**, appellate review is limited to the question of whether the district court **abused its discretion** in doing so.  

The Eighth Circuit applies a **deferential standard**: the appellate court will not overturn the district court’s factual determinations unless the decision is **plainly erroneous** or **contrary to the weight of the evidence** (see *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929‑30 (8th Cir. 2013)).  In *Cutbank*, the defense raised no objection to the PSR’s findings that Cutbank possessed the sawed‑off rifle during the assault and during the flight from police.  Consequently, the appellate court treated the PSR’s facts as **uncontested** and affirmed the district court’s calculation of the enhancement.

## § 3553(a) Factors, Discretionary Range, and the Harmless‑Error / Reasonableness Review  

### The Role of § 3553(a)  

Even though the Guidelines provide an advisory range, the **statutory factors** in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) govern the ultimate sentencing decision.  The court must consider, among other things:

* The nature and circumstances of the offense and the defendant’s history and characteristics.  
* The need for the sentence to reflect the seriousness of the offense, promote respect for the law, provide just punishment, and afford the defendant **fairness and proportionality**.  
* The need to protect the public and **deterrence**.  

A sentencing court may **depart** from the Guidelines range only if it **explicitly** addresses these factors and explains why the departure is justified.  The appellate court need not see a **mechanical recitation** of each factor; it must be clear from the record that the court **considered** them.

### Review of Reasonableness  

The Eighth Circuit employs a **“substantive reasonableness”** standard for reviewing sentences within the advisory range.  Under *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023), the appellate court asks whether the sentence is **reasonable** in light of the § 3553(a) factors, the Guidelines range, and the nature of the offense.  The review is **deferential**; the sentencing court’s discretion is “substantial,” and a presumption of reasonableness attaches to sentences that fall **within** the advisory range.

### Harmless‑Error Considerations  

When a defendant alleges **evidentiary errors** at trial, the appellate court first determines whether the error was **harmless** under *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009).  The court must be convinced **beyond a reasonable doubt** that the error did **not** affect the verdict.  In *Cutbank*, the court found the government’s physical and testimonial evidence overwhelming; thus, any alleged evidentiary mistakes were deemed harmless.

### Application to Cutbank’s Sentence  

The district court imposed **292 months**, the **minimum** of the advisory range.  The appellate court noted that the court:

1. **Calculated** the correct Guidelines range (base level 26 + four enhancements + ACCA increase).  
2. **Considered** the defendant’s extensive criminal history and the violent nature of the conduct.  
3. **Explained** that no § 3553(a) factor warranted a variance below the minimum.  

Because the sentence sat squarely within the range, the appellate court applied the **presumption of reasonableness** and found no abuse of discretion.  The court also emphasized that the defendant **did not object** to the PSR’s findings or to the enhancement, removing any claim of procedural error.

---

### Take‑aways for the Student  

* **Base offense level** for a sawed‑off firearm possession is *26* under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6).  
* **Enhancements** are added cumulatively; the four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement hinges on a factual link between the firearm and a separate felony.  
* The district court’s **adoption** of the PSR’s facts is reviewed for **abuse of discretion**; uncontested facts are presumed correct.  
* Sentences within the advisory range enjoy a **strong presumption of reasonableness**; the appellate court looks for a clear, reasoned consideration of § 3553(a) factors.  
* Evidentiary errors that do not demonstrably affect the verdict are **harmless** and do not invalidate the conviction or sentence.

Understanding the interplay of these calculations, the statutory enhancement, and the standards of appellate review equips you to dissect similar sentencing opinions and to evaluate whether a district court has acted within its broad discretion.
