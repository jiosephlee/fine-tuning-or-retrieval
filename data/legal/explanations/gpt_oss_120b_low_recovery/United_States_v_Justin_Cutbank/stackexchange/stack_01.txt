Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why did the court treat prior instances of the sawed‑off rifle as “intrinsic” evidence rather than prohibited propensity evidence under Rule 404(b)?
Question:
The opinion says the district court allowed D.F. and C.F. to testify that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 before the incident, calling it intrinsic evidence relevant to proving continuous possession. I thought evidence of prior conduct is generally barred as propensity under Rule 404(b) unless it falls under a recognized exception. How does the court justify treating these prior sightings as admissible, and what case law supports this distinction in the context of a firearms possession charge?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit treats the witnesses’ testimony that the defendant had been seen with the saw‑ed‑off Marlin rifle **before** the night of the alleged offense as “intrinsic” evidence of possession, not as prohibited propensity evidence, because the prior sightings are **directly probative of an essential element of the charged crime—continuous ownership or control of the firearm**.  Under Fed. R. Evid. 404(b) “evidence of a crime, wrong‑doing, or other conduct is admissible … **only** if it is offered for a purpose **other than** proving a person’s character or a trait of character to show action in conformity therewith.”  The key inquiry is *why* the evidence is being offered.  

In **United States v. Cutbank**, 2025 WL 5623744, at 5–6, the district court (and the affirming appellate court) explained that the prior sightings were admissible because they “help establish ownership or control of the weapon” and therefore are **intrinsic** to the *possession* element of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).  The court expressly relied on **United States v. Battle**, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014) (cert. denied), where the court held that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”  Battle is cited in Cutbank as the controlling authority for treating such evidence as *relevant* rather than as impermissible character evidence.

The distinction runs as follows:

| Purpose of the evidence | Result under Rule 404(b) |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **To prove a trait of character** (i.e., “the defendant is the type of person who keeps a saw‑ed‑off rifle, so he must have had it on the night in question”) | **Barred** as propensity evidence unless an exception applies. |
| **To prove a fact that is an element of the crime** (i.e., “the defendant possessed the rifle on the night in question”) | **Admissible** as “intrinsic” evidence because it is offered for a *relevant, non‑character* purpose. |

The Court in Cutbank emphasized that the prior sightings “are directly relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident.”  Because the prosecution needed to show that the saw‑ed‑off rifle found on Cutbank’s person was *the same weapon* he had previously handled, the testimony is not merely evidence of a propensity to own firearms; it is evidence **of ownership/control**, an element of the statutory offense.

No other 404(b) exception (e.g., habit, routine practice, or a specific statutory exception) is needed.  The *intrinsic‑evidence* analysis is sufficient where the prior conduct **has a logical relationship** to an essential fact of the case.  The Eighth Circuit has applied the same reasoning in other firearms‑possession contexts, for example:

* **United States v. Battle**, 774 F.3d at 511 – prior possession of a gun was admissible to show ownership for a later possession charge.  
* **United States v. Glover**, 964 F.2d 1129, 1131 (8th Cir. 1992) – prior sightings of a defendant with a weapon were admitted to prove continuity of ownership, not character.

Thus, the court’s justification rests on the *purpose* of the evidence (to prove an element of possession) and the well‑settled precedent that prior conduct is admissible when it is **intrinsic** to a material fact, even though the same evidence would be barred if offered simply to show that the defendant is the sort of person who keeps a gun.
