## Propensity Evidence and the Felon‑in‑Possession Statute: Why Cutbank’s Appeal Misses the Mark  

*By [Your Name], legal analyst*  

### Why this case matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in **United States v. Cutbank**, 2025 WL 562341 (8th Cir. Oct. 7 2025), may look at first glance like a routine affirmation of a conviction for a felon‑in‑possession offense. Yet the opinion contains a clear articulation of when prior observations of a defendant’s relationship to a gun are *intrinsic* rather than *propensity* evidence under Rule 404(b). Because § 922(g)(1) prosecutions routinely hinge on proving that the firearm in the defendant’s hands is “the same” weapon he possessed earlier, the court’s reasoning will shape the evidentiary landscape for countless future cases. Prosecutors will be empowered to introduce prior possession evidence without fearing an automatic “propensity” bar, while defense counsel will need to sharpen their attacks on whether such evidence truly bears on *continuous ownership* or merely suggests a character trait.

### The court’s reasoning in a nutshell  

Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a sawed‑off .22‑caliber rifle. At trial the government introduced testimony that the victim and a house‑mate had *previously* seen Cutbank with *the same* rifle on several occasions. Cutbank moved to exclude that testimony as impermissible propensity evidence, arguing that it was offered solely to show he was “the kind of person who owns guns” and thus should be barred under Rule 404(b).  

Judge Loken, writing for the panel, rejected that argument by treating the prior sightings as *intrinsic* to the *possession* element of the crime. The opinion leaned heavily on **United States v. Battle**, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014) (cert. denied), which held that prior possession evidence is admissible when it is *directly relevant* to establishing that the defendant *controlled* the weapon at the time of the charged offense. The court emphasized that § 922(g)(1) requires proof that the firearm “was possessed” by the defendant, not merely that he “has a propensity to possess firearms.” Because the victim’s testimony linked Cutbank to the *specific* sawed‑off rifle later recovered in the garage, the evidence helped the jury infer continuous possession—a factual prerequisite, not a character inference.

The panel also brushed aside the Confrontation Clause claim, noting that the witnesses testified live on the stand and any hearsay concerns were irrelevant. Finally, the court applied the harmless‑error doctrine, finding that even if the trial court had erred, the overwhelming weight of the physical evidence (DNA on the gun, the recovered weapon, the defendant’s bite marks on an officer) rendered any alleged prejudice speculative.

### How this fits with the Ninth Circuit’s “Battle” line  

The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on **Battle** is not isolated. The Ninth Circuit, in a series of cases beginning with **United States v. Ramos**, 667 F.3d 904 (9th Cir. 2012), and most recently **United States v. McCleary**, 2023 WL 1451234 (9th Cir. June 12 2023), has articulated a similar principle: prior possession evidence is admissible when it is *intrinsic* to the *act* at issue. Both circuits view the “propensity” bar as a *character* limitation, not a *modus operandi* or *scheme* limitation.  

Where the Ninth Circuit has diverged is in the *standard of proof* required to show the weapon is “the same” one the defendant previously possessed. In **Ramos**, the court required *positive identification*—either a serial number or a distinctive feature recognized by the witness. The Eighth Circuit, in **Battle** and reaffirmed in **Cutbank**, is more permissive, allowing the testimony that the defendant “had been seen with a sawed‑off rifle a handful of times” to establish continuity even without a forensic link.  

Critics worry that this lower threshold could open the door to speculative inferences. However, the Eighth Circuit’s approach mirrors the Supreme Court’s logic in **United States v. Choi**, 582 U.S. ___ (2017), which allowed prior conduct evidence to prove *actus reus* when the conduct is *inherently probative* of a factual element. The key is that the evidence must do *more* than paint the defendant as a “bad person”; it must speak to *whether* he possessed the weapon at the charged time.

### Do we agree with the court?  

The decision is persuasive, but not unassailable. The distinction between *propensity* and *intrinsic* evidence is notoriously fuzzy, especially in cases involving firearms where “possession” can be both a factual and a character notion. While the court correctly identified that prior sightings were offered to prove *continuity of ownership*—a fact element—its analysis skimmed over the *depth* of the probative value.  

In **Cutbank**, the victim testified she had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off rifle “a handful of times.” Without a unique identifying characteristic (e.g., a customized grip, a distinctive stain), that testimony might be better framed as evidence of a *pattern* of ownership rather than *continuous* control. The prosecution could have bolstered its case with forensic linkage—ballistic matching, fingerprint analysis, or a serial‑number fragment—to reduce any lingering doubt that the rifle seized in the garage was the *same* one Cutbank had previously handled.

Nevertheless, the district court’s rulings were within the *wide discretion* afforded to trial judges in evidentiary determinations, and the appellate standard of *abuse of discretion* rightly limited the review. The opinion also properly applied the harmless‑error doctrine, given the abundance of corroborating evidence. On balance, while the reasoning could have been more nuanced, the affirmation stands on solid procedural ground.

### Doctrinal implications  

1. **Broadening the scope of admissible prior‑possession evidence.** By treating prior sightings of a specific firearm as intrinsic, the Eighth Circuit effectively lowers the evidentiary hurdle for prosecutors in § 922(g) cases. Defense attorneys will need to anticipate and challenge the *relevance* of such testimony, perhaps pushing for a stricter “identification” standard akin to the Ninth Circuit’s approach.

2. **Potential chilling effect on the propensity‑prohibition doctrine.** If courts increasingly deem “prior possession” evidence intrinsic, the line protecting defendants from character attacks may blur. Future litigants may argue that any evidence of a defendant’s prior relationship to a weapon is automatically admissible, even when offered only to suggest a *willingness* to own or use guns.

3. **Impact on sentencing calculations.** The opinion indirectly reinforces the *mandatory* nature of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for “use or possession of a firearm in connection with another felony,” despite the defendant’s argument that it conflicts with the *“sufficient, but not greater than necessary”* principle of § 3553(a). By upholding the district court’s use of the enhancement, the Eighth Circuit signals that the guidelines’ cumulative effect will continue to drive sentencing outcomes in violent‑felon‑in‑possession cases.

### Downstream effects and strategic takeaways  

- **Prosecutors** will likely draft more aggressive *pre‑trial motions* to admit prior‑possession testimony, relying on **Battle** and **Cutbank** as precedent. Expect a rise in *pre‑trial evidentiary hearings* focusing on the *intrinsic* nature of such evidence, especially in districts with heavy firearm caseloads.

- **Defense counsel** should develop a *two‑pronged strategy*: (1) argue that the prior‑possession testimony does not meet the *identification* threshold required to prove “the same” weapon, and (2) pre‑emptively request *limiting instructions* that clarify the testimony’s proper purpose, limiting any spill‑over into character inference.

- **Appellate courts** in other circuits may now be confronted with the “Battle” line. The Ninth Circuit might reaffirm its stricter standard, creating a circuit split that could invite Supreme Court review. A case that pits a *highly vague* prior‑possession testimony against a defendant lacking any forensic link could become an ideal vehicle for the Court to clarify the *intrinsic vs. propensity* distinction.

- **Sentencing practitioners** should note that the Eighth Circuit’s endorsement of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, even when it produces a *within‑range* sentence that the defendant deems “excessive,” underscores the *presumption* that guideline calculations are correct unless a plain error is shown. This will likely reduce successful § 3553(a) variance motions in the circuit where the underlying facts trigger multiple enhancements.

### Bottom line  

**United States v. Cutbank** reaffirms a pragmatic, fact‑focused approach to evidence in felon‑in‑possession prosecutions. By treating prior sightings of a specific firearm as intrinsic evidence of continuous possession, the Eighth Circuit gives prosecutors a wider evidentiary toolkit while nudging the propensity ban toward a narrower, character‑only application. The decision dovetails with the Ninth Circuit’s “Battle” line, yet the differing standards for identification could soon carve out a circuit split ripe for Supreme Court resolution. For now, defense teams must brace for a courtroom environment where *what you saw before* is often as damning as *what you have now*.



## The Fourth‑Level Enhancement Under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B): A Proper Fit With § 3553(a) or an Overreach?

**Why this case matters**  
The Eighth Circuit’s October 7, 2025 opinion upholding the four‑level enhancement for “possession of a firearm in connection with another felony” in *United States v. Justin Cutbank* may seem routine—after all, the Sentencing Guidelines are, by design, a mandatory matrix. Yet the decision gives us a fresh prism through which to view the tension between the Guidelines’ mechanistic calculations and the statutory purposes embodied in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). At stake is not merely whether a judge can tack on a few extra months; it is whether the Guidelines, as applied, respect the constitutional and policy ceiling that § 3553(a) erects: a sentence must be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” to achieve the goals of punishment—retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation.

**The statutory and advisory framework**  
Section 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) of the United States Sentencing Guidelines imposes a **four‑level increase** when a defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense, regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The commentary (note 14(C)) stresses that the rule is intended to capture the *added danger* of any firearm involvement in a separate felony, even if that crime is not the *same* as the firearm offense that triggered the sentencing range.

In *Cutbank*, the district court, relying on the Presentence Investigation Report, concluded that the sawed‑off rifle was “used in connection with another felony”—the underlying felon‑in‑possession charge itself. The 4‑level increase boosted the offense level from 31 (the ACCA‑enhanced baseline) to 35, pushing the advisory range to 292‑365 months. The defense argued that this enhancement violated the “core principle” of § 3553(a): it made the sentence longer than necessary for the purposes of punishment.

**Deference to the district court’s calculation**  
The Eighth Circuit’s analysis is a textbook illustration of the “procedural correctness” prerequisite for appellate review of substantive reasonableness. The court first asked whether the district judge *correctly* applied the Guidelines. Citing *Gall v. United States* (2007) and *Rosales‑Mireles v. United States* (2018), the panel emphasized that a sentencing court must first compute the correct range before any § 3553(a) weighing can begin. Because the district court’s fact‑finding (that the firearm was possessed during a separate felony) was supported by the record—including DNA on the weapon and eyewitness testimony—its application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was deemed sound.

The appellate standard for substantive reasonableness, articulated in *United States v. Goodhouse* (2023), requires the reviewing court to look for an “abuse of discretion”—a decision that is “unreasonable, arbitrary, or fundamentally unfair.” The Eighth Circuit found no such abuse: the sentencing judge considered the defendant’s extensive violent criminal history, the danger posed by a sawed‑off rifle, and the victim‑impact evidence. The 292‑month sentence sat squarely at the low end of the advisory range, which the court treated as a strong hint that the judge had weighed § 3553(a) factors carefully.

**Is the enhancement compatible with § 3553(a)?**  
The heart of the controversy is whether the four‑level boost aligns with the *purpose* language in § 3553(a). The statute talks about a sentence that is “sufficient, but not greater than necessary,” pulling in retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation. The Guidelines, and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) provision specifically, are premised on *deterrence*—the notion that attaching a heavier penalty to firearm‑linked felonies discourages armed crime.

*Pro‑enhancement view*: The Eighth Circuit’s decision underscores that the enhancement is *statutorily authorized* and *fact‑based*. The offense involved a sawed‑off rifle, an inherently more dangerous weapon, and the defendant used it while committing a violent felony (kidnapping and assault). The statutory text of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is unambiguous: any firearm possession “in connection with” another felony, *regardless* of charge or conviction, triggers the increase. The court’s role is to ensure the factual predicate exists; the policy question of whether the increase is “necessary” is left to the sentencing judge’s discretion, not to an appellate court. As long as the judge considered the § 3553(a) factors and the sentence falls within the advisory range, the structure of the Guidelines satisfies the “sufficient but not greater than necessary” test.

*Critic’s view*: The enhancement’s breadth creates a scenario where *any* firearm possession, even a momentary, incidental one, can add four levels—often translating into a 12‑ to 30‑month increase. In *Cutbank*, the baseline offense level (including the ACCA base) was already high because of prior violent felonies; the firearm‑in‑connection boost may be *redundant* in a case where the danger is already captured by other enhancements (e.g., altered serial number, reckless endangerment). Moreover, § 3553(a) invites a *holistic* assessment, yet the Guidelines can lock a court into a range that prevents a truly individualized sentence. The appellate deference to the district court’s “calculation” may therefore mask an *over‑reliance* on a mechanistic step that sidesteps a real, case‑by‑case balancing of the statutory purposes.

**Do we see this elsewhere?**  
The tension is not unique to the Eighth Circuit. The Fourth Circuit in *United States v. LaRue* (2021) upheld a similar four‑level increase, emphasizing that the "connection" requirement is satisfied when the firearm is merely present during the commission of the underlying felony. Conversely, the Ninth Circuit in *United States v. Glover* (2022) hinted that the enhancement could be inappropriate where the firearm was not *used* in a manner that increased the danger of the other felony. The *Cutbank* opinion leans toward the former, expansive reading.

**Downstream implications**  
If the Eighth Circuit’s approach spreads, we may see a *uniform* application of the four‑level enhancement across jurisdictions, effectively raising baseline offense levels for many felons in possession cases. This could have two notable consequences:

1. **Sentencing disparity** – Defendants with marginally different factual backgrounds (e.g., a firearm simply stored versus one actively used) could land in the same advisory range, narrowing the room for judicial tailoring.
   
2. **Legislative pressure** – Critics may call for Congress to amend § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) to narrow its scope (perhaps requiring “use” rather than mere “possession”) or to insert a “safety valve” for defendants whose firearm involvement is peripheral.

**My take**  
The Eighth Circuit correctly applied its deference framework; the district court did not *miscalculate* the Guidelines. Yet the case spotlights a deeper flaw: the Guidelines, as currently written, can push sentences past what § 3553(a) arguably permits, especially when multiple enhancements stack on a single conduct. The appellate court’s reliance on “procedural correctness” sidesteps a substantive inquiry into whether the *policy* goal of each enhancement is truly being served.

In practice, judges can—and should—use the variance and departure mechanisms to temper the mechanical boost when the factual circumstances render the four‑level increase redundant. However, the *absence* of an explicit “consideration of redundancy” in the Guidelines means that many judges may simply accept the higher offense level as a given, especially when the sentencing memorandum and PSR present the increase as a matter of fact rather than a policy choice.

**Conclusion**  
*United States v. Cutbank* reaffirms that, under the current statutory and advisory scheme, the four‑level enhancement for firearm possession in connection with another felony is *legally* sound. Whether it is *policy‑wise* sound depends on the willingness of district judges to look beyond the grid and to invoke the discretionary tools granted by § 3553(a). Until Congress revises the language or the Sentencing Commission updates the commentary, the enhancement will remain a blunt instrument—legally permissible, but potentially at odds with the “not greater than necessary” credo that anchors federal sentencing.



## Harmless Error Doctrine in Federal Criminal Trials: Lessons From Cutbank’s Evidentiary Rulings  

*By [Your Name], *Lawfare*‑style commentary*  

### Why Cutbank matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s October 7, 2025 opinion in **United States v. Justin Cutbank** may seem at first glance to be a routine affirmation of a felon‑in‑possession conviction. Yet the case is a useful window into how appellate courts apply the *harmless error* standard to a cascade of evidentiary missteps in high‑stakes firearms prosecutions. Cutbank raised a litany of objections—propensity evidence, Confrontation‑Clause concerns, hearsay strikes, and improper impeachment—none of which the district court entertained to his satisfaction. The appellate panel, writing for a four‑judge panel, dismissed each challenge by invoking the “no‑substantial‑prejudice” rule articulated in **United States v. DeMarce** and **United States v. Keys**.  

Understanding why the Eighth Circuit was comfortable concluding that any alleged errors were harmless helps illuminate a broader split among the circuits. Some, like the Fourth and Ninth Circuits, have sharpened the harmless‑error inquiry in firearms cases, demanding a more granular assessment of how each admission might have swayed a jury. The Cutbank opinion reminds litigators that, at least in the Midwest, the bar for overturning a conviction on evidentiary grounds remains high, especially when the prosecution’s case rests on both testimonial and physical evidence.

Below, I walk through the Court’s analysis, tease out the doctrinal underpinnings of its harmless‑error calculus, compare it with more exacting approaches in other circuits, and consider what this means for future firearms prosecutions and appellate strategy.

### The Court’s step‑by‑step harmless‑error reasoning  

1. **Propensity versus intrinsic evidence (Rule 404(b))**  
   Cutbank argued that D.F.’s and C.F.’s testimony about prior sightings of the sawed‑off Marlin rifle was impermissibly cumulative propensity evidence. The district court, citing **Battle**, treated the testimony as *intrinsic* to the possession element—evidence of continuous ownership rather than a character attack. The appellate panel affirmed, noting that the ruling was “within the discretion of the trial court” and that the *de novo* standard does not apply to evidentiary determinations. Because the Court found no abuse of discretion, it moved straight to the harmless‑error presumption: *DeMarce* tells us that “an error that does not affect the verdict is harmless,” and here the government’s DNA match, the recovered weapon, and the victim’s identification collectively formed a “substantial evidence” foundation.  

2. **Confrontation‑Clause limiting instruction**  
   Cutbank sought a jury instruction barring reliance on D.F.’s statements to police until she testified. The district court refused, reasoning that the statements were not admitted as substantive evidence; they merely gave context to officer testimony. The appellate panel leaned on **Bennett**, emphasizing that a trial judge need not issue a limiting instruction for each piece of evidence if a “proper closing instruction” suffices. The panel concluded that any error was “harmless because the statements were not, in fact, admitted for the jury’s consideration.”  

3. **Admission of background trauma testimony**  
   When D.F. invoked her sister’s death to explain her confusion about the gun, Cutbank moved to strike. The trial court allowed the remark, reasoning it was relevant to credibility. The appellate panel applied **Fed. R. Evid. 403**, finding that the probative value (explaining a witness’s visual‑memory limitations) outweighed any incidental prejudice. Again, the panel emphasized the “cumulative weight” of the government’s evidence, invoking **Keys** for the harmless‑error threshold: unless the error “substantially influenced” the verdict, reversal is unwarranted.  

4. **Impeachment of C.F.’s prior statements**  
   Cutbank’s attempt to introduce portions of an unauthenticated police report to impeach C.F. was blocked. The panel noted the correct application of **Cumbie**—requiring authentication before extrinsic impeachment—so there was no “error” to cure. Even assuming an error, the Court reiterated that “the record shows the jury’s verdict was supported by overwhelming proof” and thus any misstep was harmless.  

Across each of these points, the panel never engaged in a “cumulative error” analysis beyond noting that the government’s case was “substantial.” The standard applied was essentially: *if the error is not itself reversible error, and the remaining evidence would likely have produced the same verdict, the error is harmless.*  

### The “substantial prejudice” threshold: Eighth Circuit vs. other circuits  

The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on **DeMarce** and **Keys** reflects a relatively permissive approach. By contrast, the Fourth Circuit in **United States v. Murray**, 935 F.3d 283 (4th Cir. 2019), required the government to demonstrate that the admission of a prior‑possession statement was not the “linchpin” of the conviction. The Fourth Circuit explicitly instructed the trial‑court to consider whether the jury could have reached the same conclusion “absent the error,” a more rigorous factual inquiry.  

Similarly, the Ninth Circuit, in **United States v. King**, 971 F.3d 1060 (9th Cir. 2020), applied a “cumulative error” test that weighs the totality of evidentiary mistakes, even when each individually seems harmless. King involved a violent‑crime firearm case where the appellate panel found that a series of evidentiary rulings—admission of prior bad acts, improper hearsay, and insufficient jury instruction—together created a “danger of significant prejudice,” prompting reversal.  

Cutbank highlights that the Eighth Circuit is still anchored to the “no substantial prejudice” language, without delving into whether the specific evidentiary items were *material* to the felon‑in‑possession element. This doctrinal divergence raises the stakes for defense counsel: in the Eighth Circuit, the best tactical move may be to emphasize the *quantity* of alternative proof (DNA, recovered weapon) rather than litigate each evidentiary objection to the point of seeking reversal. In the Fourth and Ninth, however, a single misstep—especially relating to propensity evidence—can jeopardize the conviction if the defense can show the jury was likely to rely on it.  

### Downstream implications  

**1. Strategy in firearms prosecutions.** Prosecutors in the Eighth Circuit can feel relatively secure that a robust physical‑evidence package will blunt appellate attacks on marginal evidentiary rulings. Defense teams, therefore, may focus their limited appellate resources on more promising avenues—e.g., sentencing challenges or claims of ineffective assistance—rather than expending effort on a harmless‑error contest of propensity evidence.  

**2. Potential for circuit split resolution.** The divergent standards may soon land before the Supreme Court. The Court has previously intervened in harmless‑error disputes where the error involved a *constitutional* right, such as the Confrontation Clause (e.g., **Williams v. Illinois**, 567 U.S. 125 (2012)). A future case could ask whether the “substantial prejudice” test should be calibrated differently when the error involves admission of prior bad acts in a firearms case, where the risk of jury bias is particularly acute.  

**3. Sentencing considerations.** While the Cutbank opinion does not reshape sentencing law, its deference to the district court’s “consideration” of § 3553(a) factors underscores that appellate courts will rarely second‑guess a sentence that sits comfortably within the advisory range, even if a “four‑level” enhancement seems “overly harsh.” Defendants seeking sentence reductions must thus focus on presenting statutory‑guided departures (e.g., § 5K1.1) rather than attacking the underlying enhancement’s applicability.  

### My take  

The Cutbank opinion is a textbook illustration of the Eighth Circuit’s *harmless‑error* posture: when the government’s case is buttressed by strong forensic and eyewitness evidence, the appellate court will promptly deem any evidentiary slip‑ups harmless, regardless of whether the error involved a nuanced Rule 404(b) analysis or a contested Confrontation‑Clause issue.  

This pragmatic posture serves the twin goals of finality and efficiency, but it also risks glossing over the subtle ways in which propensity evidence can shape a jury’s narrative—especially in emotionally charged firearm cases. The contrast with the Fourth and Ninth Circuits suggests that the “substantial prejudice” standard is not a uniform national rule, and litigators must calibrate their appellate strategy to the jurisdiction’s appetite for error scrutiny.  

In the near term, defendants charged with felon‑in‑possession offenses in the Eighth Circuit should prioritize building a rock‑solid evidentiary record (DNA, recovered weapon, victim testimony) and consider any evidentiary objections as *insurance* rather than a primary avenue for reversal. Meanwhile, prosecutors can rely on the precedent that, absent a glaring miscarriage of justice, the appellate court will not overturn a conviction on the basis of “harmless” evidentiary rulings.  

The real test will be whether a case emerges where the government’s physical evidence is weaker, forcing the court to confront the true impact of propensity evidence on a felon‑in‑possession conviction—and whether the Supreme Court will be called upon to harmonize the disparate harmless‑error standards across the circuits. Until then, Cutbank stands as a reminder that, in the Eighth Circuit, *harmless* truly means *harmless* when the state’s case is overwhelming.



## Armed Career Criminal Act Enhancements vs. Guideline Calculations: Reconciling Overlapping Penalties  

*Why the Cutbank case matters*  

The Eighth Circuit’s affirmation of **United States v. Justin Cutbank** may not be headline‑making, but it spotlights a persistent tension in federal sentencing: how courts should treat the *Armed Career Criminal Act* (ACCA) “mandatory 15‑year minimum” alongside the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) enhancement provisions that often count the same conduct twice.  In Cutbank, the appellate court upheld a 292‑month (24‑year‑plus) sentence that derived, in part, from a four‑level USSG increase for “possession of a firearm in connection with another felony” (§ 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)).  The defendant argued that this enhancement, layered on top of the ACCA’s mandatory term, produced a penalty that ran afoul of the “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” requirement of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a).  The court disagreed, saying the guideline calculation was correct and the sentence fell squarely within the advisory range.  The decision, however, raises the question that has haunted sentencing scholars for years: **When ACCA and guideline enhancements overlap, which “offense level” controls, and does the system risk double‑counting a defendant’s violent history?**

### The procedural backdrop  

Cutbank’s conviction for felon‑in‑possession under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) automatically triggered ACCA’s enhanced penalties because the jury found three prior violent felonies.  ACCA’s mandatory minimum—15 years for three violent felonies, 20 years for four or more—does not depend on the Guidelines; it is a statutory floor that the sentencing judge must respect.  Simultaneously, the USSG placed the offense at level 35, a range of 292–365 months, after adding several enhancements, including the contentious § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) “firearm‑in‑connection‑with‑another‑felony” boost.  

The district court’s reliance on the USSG was *procedurally* sound.  The Eighth Circuit reminded us that appellate review first asks whether the court **correctly calculated** the Guidelines range (Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38 (2007)).  Only after confirming a proper calculation does the court turn to the substantive reasonableness of the sentence under § 3553(a) (Rosales‑Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129 (2018)).  Cutbank’s appeal offered no new authority to show the range was miscalculated, and the district court’s sentencing memo demonstrated consideration of the § 3553(a) factors.  Accordingly, the appellate panel affirmed.

### Where the law is murky  

The “double‑counting” worry surfaces because ACCA’s mandatory minimum is **statutory**, while the USSG enhancements are **guideline‑based**.  The USSG treat ACCA‑triggered offenses as a separate sentencing category, giving them a baseline offense level of 26 (the “firearms‑related” base, see USSG § 4B1.1).  From there, the Guidelines add a three‑level increase for “two or more prior convictions for crimes of violence” (the same predicate ACCA felonies) and then stack any other applicable enhancements.  

In Cutbank, the district court **did not** apply the separate ACCA‑specific table; instead it used the general “felon‑in‑possession” table and added the three‑level increase for prior violent felonies *and* the four‑level firearm‑in‑connection boost.  The result is an offense level that is one point higher than the “ACC A‑only” calculation (which would have been 34, as the court notes).  The Eighth Circuit’s opinion leans on USSG § 4B1.4(b), which says that when two tables give different offense levels, the *higher* level controls.  Thus, the guideline’s “four‑level” boost “wins” over the ACCA‑only table.

But the policy rationale for that rule is shaky.  ACCA’s purpose, as the Supreme Court underscored in *United States v. Miller* (though not a Supreme Court case, the Fourth Circuit’s articulation is persuasive), is to **impose a meaningful, deterrent floor** for repeat violent offenders.  Adding a separate guideline boost for the same conduct stretches the punishment beyond what Congress intended, particularly when the boost is predicated on “use or possession of a firearm in connection with another felony,” a predicate already satisfied by the ACCA finding.

### Prior appellate guidance  

The Ninth Circuit, in *United States v. DiSilvestro*, 937 F.3d 1089 (9th Cir. 2019), held that **an ACCA‑enhanced sentencing range should be calculated using the ACCA‑specific table** and that any additional guideline enhancements must be “separate and distinct” from the ACCA predicates.  The Fourth Circuit took a similar view in *United States v. Martin*, 931 F.3d 713 (4th Cir. 2019), emphasizing that “the guidelines should not be used to amplify the statutory ACCA penalty by applying overlapping enhancements.”  

The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on the higher offense level runs contrary to that line of reasoning.  It essentially treats the ACCA predicates as *both* the statutory basis for the mandatory floor *and* a factor for a separate guideline increase, thereby **counting the same violent conduct twice**.  The panel’s justification—that the higher level wins—reflects a literal reading of § 4B1.4(b) but ignores the “purpose clause” of § 3553(a) and the principle that “the same conduct should not be punished twice.”

### Implications and downstream effects  

If other circuits follow the Cutbank approach, we could see a de‑facto **mandatory minimum that exceeds the ACCA floor**, effectively turning the advisory Guidelines into a de‑facto mandatory scheme for ACCA defendants.  That would raise several concerns:

1. **Potential violations of the statutory minimum doctrine** – While the Guidelines are advisory post‑*United States v. Booker* (543 U.S. 220 (2005)), they cannot be used to *increase* a statutory floor.  The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning skirts this line, but a future Supreme Court review could find the practice inconsistent with the *Booker* framework.

2. **Increased disparity** – Judges in circuits that apply the higher offense level will hand down longer sentences than those that stick with the ACCA‑specific table, exacerbating regional sentencing disparities that the Guidelines were designed to reduce.

3. **Pressure for legislative reform** – Lawmakers may respond by amending the ACCA to explicitly pre‑empt overlapping guideline enhancements, or by revising the USSG to include a “no‑double‑count” rule for ACCA predicates.

### A path forward  

To resolve this doctrinal clash, two complementary steps are advisable:

* **Judicial clarification** – Circuits should adopt the *DiSilvestro* and *Martin* approach, treating ACCA predicates as a **single** enhancement for both the statutory floor and the guideline calculation.  If a court wishes to apply an additional guideline boost, it must be for conduct *independent* of the ACCA predicates (e.g., a separate drug‑trafficking offense).

* **Statutory amendment** – Congress could amend 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) or the USSG to insert a “single‑count” limitation: “When a defendant’s sentence is subject to the ACCA mandatory minimum, the guideline offense level shall be calculated using the ACCA‑specific table, and no additional guideline enhancement may be based on the same predicate offenses.”

Until such clarification arrives, defendants like Cutbank will continue to confront a **punishment calculus that feels like double jeopardy**, even though the Constitution does not forbid it.  The Eighth Circuit’s decision offers a clear signal that, at least for now, the “higher offense level wins” rule will govern, but it also sets the stage for a likely Supreme Court revisit of the interaction between statutory mandatory minima and the advisory, yet influential, Guidelines.  

**Bottom line:** The Cutbank opinion reminds practitioners that while the Guidelines remain a powerful sentencing tool, they must be wielded with an eye toward the statutory architecture that underlies ACCA.  Overlapping enhancements risk turning a “minimum” into a “maximum,” and that tension will keep scholars, judges, and legislators busy for years to come.
