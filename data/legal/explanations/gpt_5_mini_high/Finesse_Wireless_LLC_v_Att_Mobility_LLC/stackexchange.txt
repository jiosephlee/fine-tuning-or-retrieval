Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How can the Federal Circuit reverse a jury’s infringement verdict under the substantial evidence standard based on self-contradictory expert testimony?
Question:
The panel says it reviews JMOL denials under Fifth Circuit law and looks for substantial evidence supporting the verdict. Yet it reverses because the patentee’s expert gave confusing, self-contradictory testimony about what signals met the claim limitations, citing Johns Hopkins v. Datascope. I thought weighing credibility was for the jury. Under what circumstances can the appellate court deem an expert’s testimony legally insufficient (rather than just confusing) and conclude that no reasonable jury could rely on it?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. The Federal Circuit reversed because the patentee’s case rested on an expert whose testimony was internally inconsistent, contradicted undisputed technical facts in the record, and failed to tie the accused features to essential claim limitations. Under the substantial‑evidence/JMOL standard an appellate court may conclude that such testimony is legally insufficient — not merely a credibility choice for the jury — when no reasonable juror could have relied on it. Finesse Wireless v. AT&T, No. 2024‑1039 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) explains why.

The governing standard
- The court reviews denial of JMOL de novo under Fifth Circuit law and will reverse “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” (Finesse, quoting Wi‑LAN and Apple v. Wi‑LAN, cited in the opinion.)
- A jury’s finding must be supported by substantial evidence — i.e., evidence from which a reasonable jury could find for the prevailing party.

Why the Federal Circuit reversed in Finesse
- ’134 patent: Dr. Wells initially mapped the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to the DL(TX) reference and the “modeled PIM path.” On cross, he admitted the modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC and so could not have been sampled (a factual impossibility for the claimed limitation). He then “pivoted” to saying there are two inputs (x1 and x2) but never clearly or consistently mapped x1/x2 to the claim terms. The court found the testimony “confusing” and “self‑contradictory,” and held that “this sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.” The court applied Johns Hopkins v. Datascope: when the patentee rests on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, the testimony can be legally insufficient. (Finesse op., pp. ≈10–22.)
- ’775 patent: Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document that showed only three distinct multiplications, but the claim required seven specific multiplications. He never testified how the three map to the seven, nor was duplicative performance shown at trial. The court held that because the expert failed to make the required mapping (and attempted clarifications were made only on appeal), “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.” (Finesse op., pp. ≈38–39.)

When appellate courts may deem expert testimony legally insufficient (practical rule)
An appellate court may step in where the expert testimony that is the patentee’s sole or primary proof of an essential element:
1. Is internally inconsistent on material points and the record contains no plausible reconciliation;
2. Contradicts undisputed documentary or physical facts (e.g., claims a device does something that the record shows is impossible);
3. Fails to tie the asserted facts or exhibits to the claim limitations (no clear mapping of accused structure/operation to claimed elements);
4. Attempts to repair or explain mappings only on appeal (new explanations not presented to the jury cannot supply substantial evidence);
and, as a result,
5. No reasonable juror could have credited the testimony in a way that proves the element.

Distinction from credibility calls
- Credibility is ordinarily a jury question. But the court is not performing an improper credibility contest when it asks whether the evidence, objectively read, could support a verdict. If the testimony is legally incoherent or irreconcilable with the record, it is not “substantial evidence” regardless of whether the jury purported to believe the expert.

Bottom line
Finesse shows that appellate reversal under the substantial‑evidence/JMOL standard is proper when a patentee’s proof collapses into unreconciled, materially inconsistent expert testimony (or testimony that contradicts the record) and thus cannot reasonably support each required claim element.

### For the ’134 patent, do the ‘interference generating signals’ have to be physically present at the ADC input, or can a downstream modeled PIM signal satisfy limitation 1[a]?
Question:
Claim 1[a] requires over-sampling a passband of received signals that includes both signals of interest and interference generating signals. The patentee’s expert mapped the ‘interference generating signals’ to a modeled PIM path that is created after the RF ADC in an FPGA. The panel held that cannot satisfy limitation 1[a] because you cannot sample a signal that does not yet exist. Could the ‘interference generating signals’ instead be the actual RF interference present at the receiver front-end (even if the model is generated later), and if so, what level of explicit testimony or mapping is required at trial to tie those pre-ADC signals to the claim language?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Court treated limitation 1[a] as requiring that the “interference generating signals” be part of the passband actually sampled by the RF ADC — i.e., physically present at the ADC input when sampling occurs. A modeled PIM signal that is generated later downstream in the FPGA cannot itself satisfy 1[a] because “you cannot sample a signal that does not yet exist.” Finesse Wireless v. AT&T, No. 2024‑1039, slip op. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).
- That said, actual RF interference present at the receiver front end can satisfy 1[a] — but the patentee must present clear, non‑contradictory mapping and evidence at trial tying those pre‑ADC signals to the claim language (i.e., show that the RF ADC actually sampled those signals).

Why (what the opinion says)
- The panel reversed JMOL for the ’134 claims because Finesse’s expert expressly mapped “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and the modeled PIM path was generated in the GROOT FPGA after sampling. The court held that if the modeled PIM path is the claimed “interference generating signals,” limitation 1[a] cannot be met “because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband of received signals that includes interference generating signals since those signals are not generated until after sampling occurs.” Slip op. at pp. 21–22.
- The court also stressed that the plaintiff bears the burden to prove the accused device samples a passband that includes both the signals of interest and the interference generating signals, and that ambiguous or self‑contradictory expert testimony will not carry that burden. The panel faulted the expert for failing to testify unambiguously that x1 and x2 were the pre‑ADC “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” and for repeatedly tying the claim terms to the modeled PIM rather than to concrete pre‑ADC waveforms. Slip op. at pp. 10–12, 21–22; citing Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (self‑contradictory expert testimony may be insufficient).

What you must prove at trial to avoid the same pitfall
If you want to rely on front‑end RF interference (not a post‑ADC model) to satisfy limitation 1[a], the record must clearly show three things, with consistent testimony and documentary/experimental support:

1) Explicit mapping of claim terms to physical signals
   - The expert must unambiguously state which physical waveform(s) correspond to “signals of interest” and which correspond to “interference generating signals” (e.g., “x1 = DL(TX) reference = signal of interest; x2 = RF carrier at ADC input = interference generating signal”). The Court reversed because the expert repeatedly tied the claim language to the modeled PIM path and never clearly mapped x1/x2 to the claimed terms. Slip op. at pp. 10–12, 21.

2) Proof those signals are “received signals” sampled by the RF ADC
   - Demonstrate (with schematics, vendor docs, timing diagrams, chip datasheets, oscilloscope captures, scope traces, or other measurements) that the identified interference waveform exists at the ADC input at the time of sampling and is therefore part of the sampled passband. The opinion turned on the fact the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the ADC, and so it could not be what the ADC sampled. Slip op. at p. 21.

3) Technical linkage showing the interference is capable of generating the claimed IMPs in‑band
   - Explain why the identified RF interference is an “interference generating signal” within the meaning of the claim (i.e., capable of generating intermodulation products in‑band of the signals of interest). That is part of the limitation’s substantive content and was Finesse’s burden to prove.

Practical points drawn from the opinion
- Don’t rely on a figure’s legend or a shorthand (“red path = DL(TX) reference & modeled PIM path”) without separately and clearly identifying which physical waveform in that figure is which claim element. The court faulted Finesse for letting the legend and scattered testimony create an inference rather than providing a straightforward mapping. Slip op. at pp. 10–12.
- Avoid internally inconsistent expert testimony. The panel emphasized that the plaintiff’s case failed because the expert’s testimony was confusing and self‑contradictory about what was sampled. Slip op. at pp. 10–12; see also Johns Hopkins.
- Documentary corroboration helps. The stronger the documentary or measurement evidence tying the names/labels in vendor documentation (x1, x2, DL(TX), modeled PIM) to physical nodes and sampling points, the less vulnerable the testimony is to being characterized as speculative or contradictory.

Bottom line
- A modeled PIM signal produced after the ADC cannot satisfy limitation 1[a] because it was not a “received signal” sampled by the RF ADC. Finesse, slip op. at p. 21.
- The patentee can meet 1[a] by showing the actual RF interference at the front end was sampled by the ADC — but must do so with clear, consistent expert mapping and contemporaneous documentary or measurement evidence that the ADC sampled those pre‑ADC interference waveforms. The appellate decision reversed here precisely because that clear mapping and evidence were lacking.

### Does ‘three signals S1, S2 and S3’ in the ’775 patent require three unique inputs, and how did the court treat the prosecution amendment from ‘two or three signals’?
Question:
The district court construed ‘three signals’ as ‘separately identifiable’ but not necessarily unique, relying in part on inventor testimony. On appeal, the panel said there was no clear and unambiguous disclaimer even though the patentee amended from ‘two or three signals’ to ‘three signals.’ What is the doctrinal threshold for finding clear and unmistakable prosecution disclaimer in this context, and when can ‘three signals’ be satisfied by two unique sources plus a copy or preprocessed version of one of them?
Answer:
Short answer
No — the Federal Circuit held that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in the ’775 patent does not automatically mean three physically distinct (unique) input sources. The court applied the usual prosecution‑disclaimer standard — the patentee must clearly and unmistakably surrender claim scope — and concluded the prosecution amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals” did not meet that high threshold. The court therefore read the claim to require three “separately identifiable” signals, not necessarily three unique sources. That said, even under that reading an accused product still must actually supply three separately identifiable signals and perform the seven listed multiplications; in this case Finesse failed to prove the seven multiplications were performed, so the infringement verdict could not stand. (See Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, No. 2024‑1039, slip op. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).)

Doctrinal rule applied
- Prosecution disclaimer requires a “clear and unambiguous” (i.e., clear and unmistakable) surrender of claim scope during prosecution. The Federal Circuit applied that standard here (citing Continental Circuits and the panel opinion). The mere narrowing of claim language during prosecution does not automatically equal disclaimer unless the patentee’s statements or amendment unmistakably relinquish the broader scope. (Opinion: “that does not rise to the level of ‘clear and unambiguous’ disavowal of claim scope…” J.A. 110–11.)

How the court applied the rule to the “two or three” → “three” amendment
- The examiner rejected claims as obvious over a prior art reference that taught two inputs. Finesse amended to recite “three signals” and also argued that the cited prior art addressed a different problem (active IMPs) than the claimed invention (passive IMPs). The court found those prosecution responses did not amount to a clear, unambiguous surrender of devices that use only two unique inputs. Thus the amendment “at most” imposed a requirement of three separately identifiable signals and did not require a specific number of unique input sources. (Opinion: “At most, Finesse’s amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals.” J.A. 968–69; J.A. 110–11.)

When two unique sources + a copy/preprocessed signal can satisfy “three signals”
- Under the Federal Circuit’s reading, the claim can be satisfied by three “separately identifiable” signals even if two originate from unique sources and the third is a copy or a preprocessed version of one of them — so long as (1) those three signals are separately identifiable as S1, S2 and S3 in the accused implementation, and (2) the accused device actually carries out the claim’s technical steps using those three signals (here, the seven specified third‑order multiplications). The district court had credited inventor testimony to the effect that S1–S3 “represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals” and adopted the “separately identifiable” construction. (J.A. 109–15; J.A. 115.)

Why Finesse still lost on infringement
- Even accepting that copies/preprocessed versions can serve as separately identifiable signals, the patentee still bears the evidentiary burden to show the accused device meets the other claim requirements. The Federal Circuit reversed because Finesse’s expert relied on Nokia documents that showed only three distinct multiplications (x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|) and did not demonstrate how those map to the seven claimed multiplications or that the seven were actually performed. The court therefore held there was no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications and granted JMOL of noninfringement. (J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70; see J.A. 38–39.)

Bottom line for claim drafting and proof
- Amendment from “two or three” to “three” is not itself dispositive of disclaimer; the patentee must unmistakably give up the broader scope during prosecution. If the claim is read as requiring three “separately identifiable” signals, a copy or preprocessed version of one of two physical inputs can satisfy S1–S3, but the patentee must still prove (with evidence presented to the jury) that the accused product produces three separately identifiable signals and performs all of the claimed multiplications. In Finesse the court accepted the narrower prosecution result did not clearly disclaim two‑input devices, but found the evidentiary showing of the seven multiplications insufficient. (J.A. 110–11; J.A. 968–69; J.A. 26486.)

### What exactly must be proven to meet the ’775 claim’s requirement of seven third-order multiplications, especially when the accused design uses modulus and conjugate operations?
Question:
The claim requires generating seven specific third-order products of S1, S2, and S3. The Nokia documentation showed only three distinct products and involved |x| and complex conjugate operations. The panel found no evidence of all seven multiplications or a valid mapping. I’m unsure about: 
- Do the seven listed combinations require seven distinct computed products, or can a single computed product correspond to multiple listed permutations due to commutativity/associativity? 
- Can |x| or x′ be treated as the same ‘signal’ for purposes of S1/S2/S3, or does using modulus/conjugate move the operation outside the scope of ‘digitally multiplying’ the claimed signals? 
- If the accused system computes ten polynomial terms p(n)=a0..a9, what kind of trial proof is needed to identify which seven align with the claim’s seven products?
Answer:
Short answer
- The ’775 claim requires proof that the accused system produces the seven third‑order products recited (or products that are mathematically the same as those recited). A patentee may not rely on bare labels or later appellate argument — the jury must be shown, with non‑speculative evidence, how the accused device’s computed terms correspond to each claimed product. Finesse lost because the Nokia documents only showed three distinct multiplication forms (and used |x| and x′), and the patentee never explained at trial how those three (or the ten polynomial terms in the doc) map to the seven claim items. See Finesse Wireless v. AT&T, slip op. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).

Point 1 — distinct products vs. permutations
- What the claim actually recites is seven multiplicative multisets (S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, etc.). Mathematically, ordinary multiplication is commutative, so different orderings (S1×S1×S2 vs. S1×S2×S1) are the same numeric product. In principle one computed product can therefore satisfy more than one of the enumerated permutations so long as the multiplicities of S1/S2/S3 match.
- But the Federal Circuit reversed because the patentee failed to provide the necessary evidentiary mapping at trial. The court noted the Nokia document “only lists three distinct multiplications … and there is no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed.” The district court had assumed some of those three could count for multiple claimed items, but the Fed. Cir. rejected that result where no testimony or documentary evidence tied the accused computations to the seven claim entries. Id. (discussion at J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70).  
- Takeaway: you can rely on commutativity/account for permutations, but you must prove (to the jury) — product by product — that either (a) the accused device literally computes each claimed multiset, or (b) a computed term is mathematically identical to several claimed multisets and is therefore the functional equivalent. Absent that mapping, the proof is insufficient.

Point 2 — modulus (|x|) and conjugate (x′) vs. “same” signal
- The opinion treats |x| and x′ as different signal forms that cannot be conflated with the raw transmitter signals without proof. The court explained the Nokia document “does not disclose multiplying x1 and x2, but their complex conjugate and modulus, [so] Finesse’s mapping is flawed because it requires, for example, S3 to be mapped onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2′, and its modulus |x2|.” Id.
- Practically: you cannot simply assume |x| or x′ are the “same” S1/S2/S3 the claim requires. The claim language requires “copies of transmitter signals” and the patentee must show that the particular processed versions (conjugate, modulus) are within the scope of the claimed “copies” or otherwise are equivalent for claim purposes. If the accused implementation multiplies processed versions rather than the claimed signals (or their copies), the patentee must present evidence that those processed signals are equivalent to the claimed signals or that the claim covers them. Absent that proof the court will treat them as different signals and the mapping will fail.

Point 3 — proving a mapping from a list of computed terms (e.g., p(n)=a0…a9) to the seven claimed products
- The Federal Circuit’s error analysis makes the required proof clear: you must show, at trial, which specific computed terms correspond to each claimed multiplication. The court faulted Finesse because “nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications” and no mapping from the ten p(n) terms to the claimed seven was presented to the jury. Id.
- What that means in practice: at trial the patentee should produce concrete, non‑speculative evidence for each claimed product. Typical (sufficient) trial proof would be one or more of:
  - expert testimony that walks through the accused algorithm/code and shows that a particular computed term equals S1×S1×S2 (or is the same up to commutativity), supported by the actual code, block diagrams, internal docs, or execution traces;  
  - documentary evidence (or logs) showing the device computes the listed product(s) at runtime; or  
  - mathematical derivation showing how a polynomial term p(n)=ai is algebraically identical to a claimed multiset of S1/S2/S3 and evidence tying the ai to the transmitter signals claimed.  
  The key is a clear term‑by‑term mapping (or algebraic equivalence) presented to the jury — not a post hoc mapping on appeal.
- If processed signals (|x|, x′) appear, the proof must also explain why those processed versions are the same “copies of transmitter signals” the claim requires or how they are equivalent to the claimed S1/S2/S3 products.

Additional doctrinal note (claim construction/prosecution history)
- The court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily three different (unique) signals; amendment during prosecution did not amount to a clear disavowal that would read out two‑signal systems. That said, even if duplicates are allowed, the patentee still must demonstrate that the accused system’s actual computations cover the seven enumerated multiplicative combinations. Id. (J.A. 109–15, 110–11).

Bottom line checklist for proving infringement of the ’775 seven‑product limitation (and avoiding the Finesse result)
- Identify and tie S1/S2/S3 to concrete signal(s) in the accused device.  
- For each of the seven listed products, show a corresponding computed term OR show algebraic identity explaining why a computed term covers multiple recited permutations.  
- If the accused uses |x| or x′, show those are covered by the claim (i.e., are “copies” or equivalent).  
- Present the mapping and supporting documents/expert testimony to the jury (don’t reserve it for appellate briefing).  

The Federal Circuit reversed because that item‑by‑item, non‑speculative mapping was not made at trial; the Nokia documents only showed three distinct multiplication forms (and some ten polynomial terms) but no trial evidence tying those to the seven claimed multiplications. See Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).

### What must be preserved in a Rule 50(a) motion to avoid waiver on appeal for a claim-limitation challenge like ‘three signals,’ and do claim construction or summary judgment arguments count?
Question:
The district court said defendants waived the argument that S1, S2, S3 must be unique by not raising it in Rule 50(a). The Federal Circuit reversed on a different ground (failure to prove the seven multiplications). For preservation: 
- Is it enough in Rule 50(a) to generally assert ‘no substantial evidence supports element X,’ or must the movant specify the exact construction theory (e.g., ‘unique signals’)? 
- If a party argued a construction at claim construction or summary judgment, does that preserve it for JMOL? 
- How do courts distinguish between waiving a legal construction argument and preserving a factual sufficiency challenge tied to that element?
Answer:
Short answer
- You should preserve both (a) the legal claim‑construction theory you plan to press on appeal and (b) the factual sufficiency challenge tied to that theory. Finesse shows a district court will treat a construction‑specific argument as waived if it was not raised at the Rule 50(a) stage, even if the issue was litigated earlier at Markman/SJ. Conversely, a preserved JMOL that points to lack of evidence under the court’s construction can succeed on appeal (as the Federal Circuit did where the evidence failed to prove seven multiplications).

Why (and what the opinion shows)
- The district court had earlier construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable, but not ‘unique.’” (J.A. 109–15, 115.) At the JMOL hearing the defendants argued the claim requires unique signals, but the district court ruled that the unique‑signal argument was waived because it had not been raised in the Rule 50(a) motion. (J.A. 38, 40.) That is the core preservation lesson the opinion records.
- The Federal Circuit did not rely on the waiver ruling; it reversed on independent factual‑sufficiency grounds for both patents. For the ’775 patent the court held there was no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications: the Nokia document showed only three distinct multiplications, Dr. Wells did not map those three onto the seven claimed multiplications, and there was no evidence multiplications were repeated to yield the seven required by the claim. (J.A. 14966–70; J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 26486.) For the ’134 patent the court similarly reversed because the plaintiff’s expert gave internally inconsistent testimony and the evidence was insufficient. (J.A. 10–11; citing Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope, J.A. 543 F.3d 1342.)

Concrete answers to your bullets
1) Is a general “no substantial evidence on element X” enough in Rule 50(a), or must the movant specify a construction (e.g., “unique signals”)?  
- Finesse illustrates that a generic sufficiency protest may not preserve a construction‑based legal theory. The district court treated the “unique signals” theory as waived because it was not presented in the Rule 50(a) motion (J.A. 38, 40). So if you intend to argue noninfringement based on a particular claim construction, say that construction (or expressly tie your sufficiency challenge to that construction) in the Rule 50(a) motion.

2) If a party argued a construction at claim construction or summary judgment, does that preserve it for JMOL?  
- Not necessarily. In Finesse the parties had litigated the “three signals” language at summary judgment (the court construed it “separately identifiable, not unique”), but the district court still treated the defendants’ later “unique signals” argument as waived at JMOL because it had not been raised in the Rule 50(a) motion (J.A. 109–15; J.A. 38, 40). Put another way: earlier advocacy helps, but re‑raising the precise legal theory in your Rule 50(a) motion is the safe course.

3) How do courts distinguish waiving a legal construction argument vs. preserving a factual sufficiency challenge tied to that element?  
- The opinion shows the distinction in practice: claim construction arguments are legal theories that courts expect to be presented and preserved as such; factual sufficiency challenges require the movant to show the record lacks substantial evidence that the accused product meets the limitation as construed. The district court viewed the “unique” point as a legal construction and held it waived for not being raised; the Federal Circuit resolved the sufficiency question on the record (no evidence of seven multiplications) and reversed on that factual ground (J.A. 38–39; J.A. 14966–70; J.A. 26485–86). So, if your factual sufficiency attack depends on a different construction than the court adopted, you must explicitly tie the legal construction and the sufficiency argument together in the Rule 50(a) motion.

Practical checklist (what to put in Rule 50(a))
- State the precise ground(s) for JMOL: identify the claim limitation and the legal construction you rely on (or say you challenge the verdict under the court’s construction).  
- Make the factual sufficiency point under that construction: explain why the evidence cannot support the claim element as construed (identify the missing proof—e.g., no evidence mapping 3 multiplications to the 7 required).  
- Preserve alternatives: if you seek JMOL under two different constructions, put both alternatives in the motion so the district court and the record are on notice.

Bottom line
Finesse teaches two lessons: (1) don’t assume earlier Markman or summary‑judgment argument alone preserves a different construction for JMOL — put the construction in the Rule 50(a) motion; and (2) separately and clearly press the factual‑sufficiency point you want the court to rule on under whichever construction you rely upon (the Federal Circuit will reverse where the evidence cannot reasonably support the jury’s finding, as it did here for the seven multiplications).

### Why did the court vacate (rather than reverse) the damages award after finding no infringement, and what happens to the jury’s validity findings?
Question:
The panel reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement on all asserted claims and then vacated the $166M lump-sum damages award. If there is now a judgment of noninfringement on all asserted claims, is there any scenario in which damages could be reinstated on remand? Also, the jury found the claims valid. Do those validity findings remain in the judgment with any preclusive effect, or are they effectively moot because there is no liability?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Federal Circuit vacated the damages award because the damages were entirely derivative of the jury’s infringement verdict; once the court reversed the denial of JMOL and concluded there was no legally sufficient evidence of infringement, there was no legal basis for the $166M award. The court put it plainly: “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims …, we vacate the damages award.” (Opinion, II. Damages.)
- Damages can be reinstated only if the noninfringement ruling is itself undone (for example, on rehearing/en banc or successful further appeal) or if later proceedings lead to a new, legally sufficient infringement finding. Absent that, there is no basis to award infringement damages on remand.
- The jury’s validity findings were not disturbed by this opinion and therefore remain as jury findings in the record, but the panel did not give them independent preclusive effect. Because the court eliminated liability, the validity verdict was not the basis for a continuing damages judgment; whether that prior validity finding would have collateral‑estoppel effect in later litigation depends on usual preclusion principles (i.e., whether the issue was actually litigated and was essential to a final judgment) — matters the opinion does not decide.

Short explanation of the legal logic (grounded in the opinion)
- Damages for patent infringement are consequential on a finding of liability. The court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for both patents because the evidence was legally insufficient to support the jury’s infringement verdict (see sections I.A and I.B). Given that reversal, the damages award “cannot stand,” so the appellate court vacated it rather than leaving a now‑unmoored damages figure in the judgment (the court’s conclusion in II. Damages).
- The opinion does not remand for a new damages computation or instruct the district court to re‑enter the award; it simply vacates the award because the predicate finding of infringement was removed. That is why the court acted by vacatur rather than attempting to “fix” or adjust the damages amount on appeal.

What this means practically
- On remand the district court will have to enter judgment consistent with the Federal Circuit’s reversal of infringement and the vacatur of the damages award. There is no enforceable $166M award unless the Federal Circuit’s noninfringement ruling is reversed or new infringement findings are legally adopted after further proceedings.
- The jury’s verdict that the claims were valid remains part of the trial record (the panel did not disturb those findings), but the opinion does not convert those findings into a separate, independently enforceable judgment or address their preclusive effect. If parties later litigate validity (or seek to use the earlier validity finding defensively/offensively), standard issue‑preclusion rules (actually litigated, decided, and necessary to the judgment) will control whether that earlier finding binds anyone — a question the Federal Circuit did not resolve in this opinion.

### Was it appropriate for the district court to rely on inventor testimony to support a construction that ‘three signals’ need not be unique under Phillips and related precedent?
Question:
The claim says ‘three signals S1, S2 and S3,’ and the district court relied in part on inventor testimony to conclude they are ‘separately identifiable’ but not unique. My understanding is that inventor testimony is extrinsic and generally less reliable than the intrinsic record. Did the Federal Circuit endorse this use of inventor testimony, or simply find it non-dispositive given the intrinsic record? More broadly, when is it proper to lean on inventor testimony to add a qualifier like ‘separately identifiable’ that is not in the claim text?
Answer:
Short answer
No — the Federal Circuit did not say inventor testimony can supplant the intrinsic record. Finesse notes the district court “credited inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence” (J.A. 109–14), but the Federal Circuit’s own resolution rested on the intrinsic record and prosecution history: it rejected Appellants’ surrender theory because there was no “clear and unambiguous” disavowal and concluded, “at most, Finesse’s amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals” (J.A. 110–11; J.A. 968–69). In short, the inventor testimony was corroborative and non‑dispositive, not controlling.

Why (doctrine and what the opinion shows)
- Governing principle (as applied in the opinion): claim construction is driven by the intrinsic record (claims, specification, prosecution history); extrinsic evidence such as inventor testimony may be considered to help resolve ambiguity but cannot contradict a clear intrinsic meaning. The opinion explicitly describes the district court as having “credited inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence” in construing “three signals” (J.A. 109–14).
- Prosecution disclaimer standard: the Federal Circuit refused to treat the amendment to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as a clear surrender of two‑signal systems because the prosecution history did not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal (the court cites the governing “clear and unambiguous” standard and then rejects Appellants’ surrender argument) (J.A. 110–11). That is the primary basis for the court’s statement that the claim at most requires “three separately identifiable signals” (J.A. 968–69).

When inventor testimony may properly be used to add a qualifier
- Proper use: where the intrinsic record is ambiguous or silent about a nuance, inventor testimony can be admissible to show how the inventors understood or used a term and to corroborate a reasonable construction consistent with the specification and prosecution history.
- Improper use: inventor testimony cannot be the dispositive source that contradicts a clear intrinsic definition or manufacture a a limitation absent support in the intrinsic record or a clear inventor-led disavowal during prosecution.
- How Finesse illustrates this boundary: the district court added the phrase “separately identifiable” and credited inventor testimony that S1–S3 “can be the same signal or different signals” (J.A. 109–14). The Federal Circuit did not adopt inventor testimony as the sole ground for that construction; it looked to the prosecution history and concluded there was no clear surrender, hence the court’s bottom‑line construction (three separately identifiable, not necessarily unique) was grounded in the intrinsic record (J.A. 110–11; J.A. 968–69).

Takeaway
If you are arguing claim scope, rely first on the intrinsic record. Extrinsic inventor testimony can help resolve ambiguity or corroborate a construction, but a court will treat it as non‑dispositive and will not allow it to override a clear specification or prosecution history. Finesse demonstrates exactly that: inventor testimony was considered, but the Federal Circuit’s holding turned on the intrinsic materials and the high bar for disclaimer.
