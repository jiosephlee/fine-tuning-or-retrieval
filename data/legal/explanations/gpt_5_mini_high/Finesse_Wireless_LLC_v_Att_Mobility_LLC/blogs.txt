Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When Block Diagrams Aren’t Enough: Fed. Cir. Nixes PIM Verdict Over Expert Contradictions

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. 2025) is a reminder that in highly technical patent disputes—especially ones about digital signal processing and radio hardware—jury verdicts live or die on clean, element-by-element mappings from claim language to the accused product. The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated a $166.3 million damages award because the plaintiff’s case rested on an expert whose testimony was internally inconsistent and who leaned on ambiguous vendor diagrams rather than clear, contemporaneous proof that the accused radios met the claim elements. For patentees, the decision is a caution: block diagrams, legends, and half-explained variables can’t substitute for concrete testimony that ties each claim limitation to a physical or temporal fact in the accused device. For defendants, it confirms that attacking an expert’s mappings can be a decisive path to JMOL.

## The Federal Circuit’s reasoning

The opinion addresses two patents, but the court’s treatment of the ’134 patent is the clearer doctrinal lesson. Claim 1 of the ’134 patent requires, as a threshold matter, “over-sampling . . . a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s only direct evidence of sampling came from its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who relied heavily on a Nokia vendor diagram whose legend read “Red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path.” At trial Dr. Wells repeatedly testified that the “modeled PIM path” was the claimed “interference generating signals.” Defense cross-examination, however, exposed a fatal temporal mismatch: the modeled PIM path was generated downstream in a GROOT FPGA after the RF ADC had already sampled the incoming radio-frequency signals. Put simply, a receiver cannot have “sampled” signals that did not yet exist at the time of sampling.

Faced with that problem, Dr. Wells attempted a mid‑cross pivot—pointing to generic variables in the vendor diagram (x1 and x2) and arguing those were the two signals entering the ADC. But, as the Federal Circuit stressed, he never testified clearly that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claim terms “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The court found his testimony confusing and self‑contradictory, and it rejected the district court’s inclination to infer the necessary mapping for the jury. Citing Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), the panel reiterated that when a patentee rests its case on an expert whose testimony is internally inconsistent, a reasonable jury cannot be said to have substantial evidence supporting infringement.

The court reached a parallel evidentiary conclusion on the ’775 patent. That claim requires generating intermodulation-cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals into seven specific third-order products. The Nokia materials Dr. Wells relied on listed three distinct multiplications (and also used modulus and complex‑conjugate notation), but Dr. Wells never explained at trial how those operations produced the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse’s new, more detailed mapping appeared only on appeal, and the Federal Circuit refused to cure that gap. Because the trial record lacked substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications, the court reversed on noninfringement for the ’775 claims as well.

## Why the record failed

Two related evidentiary failures stand out. First, timing and location matter in signal processing claims. Proving a receiver “samples” particular signals requires proof those signals existed on the sampled passband at the sampling instant. An expert’s assertion that a modeled or reconstructed signal (created later in an FPGA or DSP) is the same as a “received” signal will not do if the device architecture shows the model is generated after sampling. Second, variable names and diagram legends are notorious traps. A vendor legend that lists multiple things on one color or line does not, without more, show which device variable corresponds to which claim term. Finesse’s expert relied on the legend and then equivocated under cross-examination; the equivocation was fatal.

The district court’s desire to infer the mapping for the jury could not overcome the lack of clear testimony. That is the core of the Federal Circuit’s holding: the plaintiff bears the burden of proving every limitation, and when the only evidence is a confused or shifting expert narrative, an appellate court can—and will—take away the verdict.

## Doctrinal takeaways

The decision reinforces a few settled, but sometimes underappreciated, doctrines. Substantial‑evidence review of jury infringement findings does not compel appellate courts to rubber‑stamp verdicts supported only by ambiguous expert claims. Johns Hopkins remains a controlling reminder that self‑contradictory expert testimony cannot carry a patentee’s burden. At the same time, the Federal Circuit reiterated the narrowness of prosecution disclaimer: the court declined to read the ’775 prosecution amendment as a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of two‑signal embodiments. So patentees should not assume doctrinal leniency on prosecution history and should separately ensure the trial record is robust.

The opinion also signals a practical standard for what an expert must provide in DSP and radio cases: not lofty descriptions, but concrete mappings that tie claim language to specific signals, to their timing (pre‑ or post‑ADC), and to the device’s implementation (block, firmware, math operation, or measured trace).

## Practical trial tips

Practitioners should take three concrete lessons from Finesse. First, prepare and lock in a clear mapping in the expert report and direct testimony: identify each accused variable (x1, x2, etc.), the physical node or buffer where it exists, and whether it is present at the ADC input or generated later. Demonstratives should mirror that mapping, and the expert should adopt the demonstratives on direct in simple, unambiguous terms. Second, develop independent, contemporaneous evidence of the claimed operations: instrument the device, produce oscilloscope captures, firmware snippets, signal logs, or engineer testimony that shows where and when signals exist and which algebraic operations the device actually performs. Don’t rely only on vendor diagrams or legends. Third, anticipate and neutralize the temporal argument: if a modeled or estimated signal is part of the infringement theory, explain (and prove) how that model is derived from sampled inputs in a way that satisfies the claim’s “received signals” requirement, or else concede the limitation and litigate around it.

For defense teams, Finesse offers a playbook: attack timing, press for specificity on mappings, and be ready to move for JMOL if the patentee’s case collapses into equivocation. Courts will accept JMOL where the record, viewed most favorably to the verdict winner, still points overwhelmingly to noninfringement.

## Conclusion

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T is not a rulemaking moment: block diagrams and vendor legends remain admissible and useful. But the Federal Circuit made plain that they are not a substitute for precise, consistent expert proof tying each claim element to a concrete part of the accused product at the relevant time. In DSP‑heavy litigation, the technical detail matters—and so does the expert’s ability to tell a simple, unconfused story about where each signal lives and when it is sampled.



# Three Signals, Not Three Uniques: Federal Circuit Rebuffs Disavowal in ’775 Claim

## Why this case matters
The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a useful reminder that an applicant’s prosecution amendment does not automatically operate as a clear, unambiguous disavowal of claim scope unless the record shows it was meant to do so. That lesson is particularly important in algorithmic and signal‑processing patents, where claims talk about multiple “signals” and accused products commonly use copies, filtered versions, complex conjugates, or otherwise‑derived streams rather than three wholly independent physical sources. The court here refused to read “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as necessarily meaning three different physical inputs; at most the amendment required three “separately identifiable” signals, not three “unique” inputs.

It’s also important to observe the practical flip side: the Federal Circuit rejected the disavowal argument but still reversed the denial of defendant JMOL because Finesse failed to prove the accused radios performed the seven specific multiplications required by the claim. So the decision protects claim scope at the construction/prosecution‑history layer while underscoring that a broad construction does not rescue an evidentiary gap at trial.

## The panel’s bottom‑line holding
On the core claim‑construction point for the ’775 patent, the court held that Finesse’s amendment to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” during prosecution did not amount to a clear and unmistakable surrender of embodiments that use only two physical signals (with a copy or derived third). Applying the Federal Circuit’s settled Continental Circuits standard, the panel concluded the intrinsic record supported a reading that S1, S2 and S3 must be “separately identifiable” but need not be “unique” inputs from different physical transmitters. Because Finesse overcame a Filipovic rejection by distinguishing active versus passive IMPs — not by conceding the number of inputs — the prosecution history did not supply the kind of unambiguous disclaimer that would narrow “three signals” down to three unique sources.

That said, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL on infringement because, on the actual trial record, Finesse’s expert relied on a Nokia document that showed only three distinct multiplications (and did not map them to the seven multiplications recited in the claim). The panel concluded that evidence was legally insufficient to support the jury’s verdict of infringement of the ’775 claims.

## How the court reached that result — intrinsic and extrinsic evidence
The opinion anchors its analysis in the familiar two‑part inquiry: (1) what does the intrinsic record (claims, specification, prosecution history) say, and (2) do extrinsic sources (e.g., inventor testimony) change that picture? The prosecution history mattered here. The examiner’s rejection attacked a claim reciting “digitally multiplying two or three signals” as obvious over Filipovic; Finesse amended the claim language to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and argued to the examiner that Filipovic addressed active IMPs while the asserted claims addressed passive IMPs. That explanation framed the amendment as directed to a substantive difference in the type of IMPs, not as an admission that any two‑signal implementation would fall outside the claim. Under the Federal Circuit’s longstanding rule that disavowal must be “clear and unmistakable,” that kind of situational explanation does not, by itself, produce disavowal of two‑signal embodiments.

The district court had credited inventor testimony that “S1, S2 and S3” represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals; the Federal Circuit accepted that, at most, the amendment requires three “separately identifiable” signals. The panel therefore declined to read the amendment as an unequivocal narrowing to three unique, independent inputs.

## A doctrinally conservative, practically capacious result
Doctrinally, this opinion hews to precedent: prosecutions amendments are potent evidence of limitation only when the patentee has plainly and with specificity acted to disclaim subject matter. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly warned against reading ambiguous prosecution remarks as narrowing the claim, and Finesse follows that line. Practically, the decision is significant for signal‑processing and software patents because it permits a claim drafting approach where the specification and claim language can tolerate “derived” or duplicated streams counting as separate inputs, so long as the prosecution record does not clearly state otherwise.

That practical capaciousness has limits. The court did not bless any elastic reading of “three signals” divorced from the patent’s text; it said “at most” a patentee required three separately identifiable signals. And the patentee still bore the burden at trial of proving the accused product actually practiced the claimed seven specific multiplications — a showing Finesse could not make on the record. So the decision is permissive on construction but unforgiving on proof.

## Implications for drafting and prosecution of algorithmic claims
For drafters, the Finesse opinion gives clear, actionable guidance. If you want the protection that counts copies or transforms as separate inputs, put that language and examples explicitly into the specification and include dependent claims that capture those embodiments. Conversely, if you want to insist on three distinct physical inputs, say so in the claims (for example, “three distinct transmitter signals generated by three different transmitters”) and show examples in the specification that contrast distinct‑source inputs with derived copies. During prosecution, be deliberate: if you amend to overcome prior art, explain whether the amendment narrows the number of inputs or instead addresses a different issue (frequency of IMPs, passive vs active components, etc.). If you intend to preserve coverage over copies/derived streams, state that expressly in prosecution so that the record will not be read against you.

For litigators, the teachable point is that chasing prosecution history disclaimer is a plausible defense but a risky sole strategy. Where the applicant’s explanation for an amendment focuses on a technical distinction unrelated to the precise term the accused infringer wants to exclude, courts will be reluctant to treat that amendment as a disavowal.

## Drawing the line between “separately identifiable” and “the same signal in disguise”
The Federal Circuit did not announce a single bright‑line test. But the decision points to the kinds of factual showings that will matter. A signal will be easier to call “separately identifiable” if the accused system treats it as a distinct data stream (separate sampling point, distinct buffer or register, independent label in technical documentation), if it is produced at a different point in the processing chain, or if the algorithm accesses it independently. By contrast, a court is likely to treat a signal as “the same in disguise” where the putative third stream is nothing more than a bitwise copy created only for convenience with no separate sampling or independent processing, or where the only “difference” is a trivial mathematical transform that the claim language would not plausibly encompass absent clear specification language.

Put another way: litigants should litigate the engineering facts — sampling points, where the values are generated and stored, whether the purportedly distinct stream is time‑shifted or independently modulated, and how the accused implementation actually executes the multiplications. Clear documentary and expert evidence on those points will determine whether “separately identifiable” is satisfied in any particular case.

## Takeaways
Finesse is a useful precedent for patentees who want to resist arguments that a prosecution amendment to a numerical claim term (here “three signals”) automatically narrowed claim scope to exclude embodiments that use copies or derived streams. The Federal Circuit reaffirmed that disavowal requires clear and unambiguous record language and that a proffered narrowing must track the applicant’s stated reasons for amendment. But the decision also reinforces two perennial lessons: (1) careful drafting and explicit definitions in the specification and claims avoid ambiguity down the line; and (2) even with a favorable construction, a patentee must present concrete, mapped evidence at trial to show the accused device performs every claimed operation — otherwise, a favorable construction is an empty victory.

Expect litigants to press both veins now: defendants will continue to mine prosecution history for disavowal language, but will more often pair that argument with pointed technical proof that only two physical inputs exist or that the accused device lacks the claimed multiplicative operations; patentees will try to shore up claim scope in the specification and on the record during prosecution to preserve coverage of derived/duplicated streams.



# Seven Means Seven: Why Conjugates and Moduli Didn’t Prove Infringement Under the ’775 Patent

## Why this case matters
Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a reminder that in patent litigation involving digital signal processing and other mathematically precise technologies, claim language that numerically enumerates operations is not loose poetry. A $166.3 million jury verdict evaporated because the patentee never proved the specific, enumerated third‑order multiplications the ’775 patent claims require. For practitioners, the decision is a practical lesson: when claims list particular algebraic products, plaintiffs must put a clean, admissible mapping in front of the jury showing that the accused implementation actually performs those products — not something that looks like them, or something you try to explain for the first time on appeal.

## What the Federal Circuit did
The Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent. The claim at issue requires, for third‑order cancellation, generating intermodulation‑cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 in seven specific combinations (for example S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, and so on). At trial Finesse’s expert relied on a Nokia technical document to show the accused radios performed those multiplications. The Nokia material, however, showed only three distinct operations — not the seven enumerated products — and the operations it did show involved modulus and complex‑conjugate expressions (for example x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, and x1·|x2|·|x2|), rather than a literal list of the seven S1×S2×S3‑style multiplications the claim lists. The expert did not explain at trial how those three distinct expressions map onto the seven claimed multiplications, nor did he demonstrate that any of the listed multiplications were repeated so as to account for the full set of seven. Finesse’s attempt on appeal to rely on a ten‑term polynomial in Nokia’s materials — and to map terms after the fact — came too late. The Federal Circuit concluded there was not substantial evidence to support a jury finding that the accused radios performed the seven enumerated multiplications, and it reversed.

## Why conjugates and moduli fell short (and why that matters)
At the heart of the court’s analysis is a simple evidentiary point: showing some algebraic operations in a vendor document is not the same as proving the specific algebraic operations recited in the claim. The Nokia document included operations using complex conjugation and modulus notation; those are distinct mathematical manipulations with different algebraic form and semantic meaning from the plain three‑term products listed in the claim. The court did not pronounce an across‑the‑board rule that conjugates or moduli can never satisfy a multiplication claim, but it did hold that Finesse failed to prove equivalence or to map the Nokia expressions to the seven claimed products at trial. Equivalence-by-inference offered on appeal — or an expert’s unclear or internally inconsistent testimony — is not substantial evidence. The upshot: when the claim enumerates precise algebraic terms, plaintiffs must show how the accused code or hardware implements those same algebraic terms (or establish, with admissible proof, why what is implemented is plainly equivalent).

## Doctrinal takeaways
A few doctrinal points flow directly from the opinion. First, the Federal Circuit enforced the plaintiff’s burden of proof for infringement with fidelity: a jury verdict cannot stand if the only technical proof is ambiguous or logically inconsistent. Second, the court reiterated that claim limitations that enumerate specific operations or numeric outcomes are not satisfied by amorphous or partial matches; specificity in the claim carries through to the evidence required at trial. Third, the case reaffirms that an appellant may not cure trial deficiencies with new technical mappings or theories presented for the first time on appeal. Finally, on prosecution history the court declined to find a clear and unmistakable disavowal of two‑signal implementations simply because the claims were amended to recite “three signals” — the amendment did not amount to the kind of unequivocal disclaimer that would strip the claims of any coverage of two‑signal architectures. That separate point shows the court will be careful before reading prosecution amendments as dispositive disavowals, but will be uncompromising about the evidentiary proof required at trial.

## Practical takeaways for litigators and drafters
For patentees and trial counsel: if your claim lists specific multiplications or other enumerated algebraic operations, have a trial‑ready mapping from the accused implementation’s variables and operations to each claimed algebraic term. Don’t rely on a vendor manual’s terse notation without an expert demonstration of algebraic equivalence. If the accused implementation uses complex‑valued arithmetic (conjugation, magnitude, FFTs, etc.), have your expert show step‑by‑step how those operations produce the claimed products, or why they are equivalents under the claim language and the specification. Put that mapping into demonstratives and deposition/expert reports so it is squarely before the jury.

For defendants and their experts: probe whether purported mappings actually match the claim language, and highlight when a vendor document shows modulus or conjugate operations rather than the claimed raw products. Moving for JMOL with a focused record can pay off when the patentee’s case depends on inferential or post‑hoc explanations.

For prosecutors and claim drafters: if you intend your claims to cover implementations that use conjugation, magnitude, frequency‑domain manipulations, or other non‑literal algebraic forms, claim them or define them expressly in the specification. If you prefer to claim functional equivalence, provide a specification basis showing the equivalences you might later assert, and be mindful of narrowing amendments that could invite prosecution‑history estoppel if not handled carefully.

## Conclusion
Finesse v. AT&T is not a technicality; it is an evidentiary reality check. In technology cases where the heart of infringement is a set of algebraic operations, “seven” must mean seven in the evidentiary record. Conjugates and moduli are not per se disqualified from any infringement theory, but if a patentee wants them to do the heavy lifting, the mathematics and the mapping must be presented clearly and consistently to the jury. Without that, even a large jury award will not survive appellate scrutiny.



# JMOL, Substantial Evidence, and a $166M Reversal: Lessons from Finesse v. AT&T

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC is a sharp reminder that big jury verdicts can be undone on appeal when the plaintiff’s proof depends on equivocal or internally inconsistent expert mappings. The court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL on noninfringement for two asserted patents concerning PIM/PIM‑cancellation technology and vacated a $166.3 million lump‑sum damages award. Practitioners should read Finesse as a jurisprudential and practical lesson: element‑by‑element proof must be clear, timing matters in signal processing disputes, and appellate courts will not sustain verdicts founded on confused expert testimony or mappings that were never presented coherently to the jury.

## What the Federal Circuit held

The appeals turned on two independent failures of proof.

For the ’134 patent, the claim required the accused receiver to “over‑sample . . . a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s infringement case rested on expert testimony tied to a Nokia technical diagram. The Federal Circuit found that the expert, Dr. Wells, repeatedly identified the “modeled PIM path” as the interference‑generating signal, but the record showed that the modeled PIM path is produced downstream—in the GROOT FPGA—after the RF ADC has already sampled. Faced with cross‑examination, the expert attempted to pivot to labels x1 and x2 on the diagram but never clearly mapped x1 and x2 to the claimed inputs. The court concluded the testimony was “confusing and unclear,” and that a party cannot meet its burden when its case rests on self‑contradictory expert testimony. Because no reasonable jury could have found the sampling limitation satisfied on that record, JMOL of noninfringement was warranted.

For the ’775 patent, the claim required generating third‑order intermodulation cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals in seven distinct multiplications. The district court construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique,” and the jury credited Dr. Wells’ mapping of Nokia materials to the claimed multiplications. On appeal the Federal Circuit declined to find that prosecution amendment to recite “three signals” constituted a clear disavowal of two‑signal implementations. But the court nonetheless held that there was insufficient evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications. The Nokia document admitted at trial disclosed only three distinct multiplications (involving modulus and complex conjugates—e.g., x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, x1·|x2|·|x2|), and Dr. Wells did not explain how those three items corresponded to the seven claimed products. Finesse’s attempt to supply a mapping on appeal came too late. The court reversed for lack of substantial evidence.

Because the Federal Circuit concluded none of the asserted claims were supported by substantial evidence of infringement, it vacated the damages award.

## Why the court reversed: clarity, timing, and mapping

Two themes run through the opinion. First, timing matters in technical claims. The distinction between a signal that exists at the ADC input and a modeled signal generated later is not academic; it can be outcome‑determinative. Second, an expert's testimony must do the heavy lifting in a clear, element‑by‑element way. The opinion emphasizes that attorneys cannot paper over gaps by pointing to a diagram legend (the “red path” in this case) or by arguing alternate mappings on appeal. The Federal Circuit relied on a familiar principle: when the plaintiff bears the burden, an expert’s contradictory or evasive testimony can render the evidence legally insufficient. The court explicitly cited Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope for that proposition and applied it here to undo a large verdict.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Finesse reinforces several doctrinal touchstones. Substantial‑evidence review is unforgiving of fuzzy expert testimony. Claim construction and prosecution history remain central—amendments during prosecution can create pitfalls, but the bar for finding clear and unambiguous disclaimer remains high. The case also illustrates an appellate willingness to parse technical testimony and documentary exhibits closely; where mappings are not laid out in the trial record, an appellate court will decline to invent them.

Practically, this opinion may encourage more aggressive JMOL practice in cases that rest on complex technical diagrams and expert mappings, particularly in courts (like the Eastern District of Texas historically) that have hosted large damages verdicts in telecommunications and electronics cases. Defendants will push preservation and mapping arguments earlier and more forcefully; plaintiffs will respond by sharpening their element‑by‑element proof and tightening expert narratives.

## Practice pointers

First, lock down clear, element‑by‑element proofs before and during trial and preserve challenges at Rule 50(a) when appropriate. If you expect the case to turn on a mapping of accused‑device behavior to claim limitations, put that mapping into the record explicitly—on direct, in demonstratives admitted to the jury, and in jury instructions if feasible—so you are not trying to supply or defend a new mapping on appeal.

Second, avoid overreliance on block‑diagram legends or shorthand labels. Diagrams and legends are useful, but they can be ambiguous about timing and signal provenance. Experts should tie every diagram label back to physical signals, show where those signals are generated or sampled in the pipeline, and explain why those locations satisfy the claim language.

Third, make sure the jury actually receives the technical mapping you plan to defend on appeal. An appellate brief cannot substitute for trial testimony. If a mapping is contested, seal it into the record with demonstratives, clear witness testimony, and—where helpful—live demonstrations or data traces that make timing and multiplicative operations palpable to a lay jury.

Finally, be mindful in prosecution: narrowing amendments can create risk of estoppel or at least create fodder for a preservation battle later. If you must narrow a claim to overcome a reference, preserve the reasons for narrowing and the boundaries of any disclaimers so you can defend the claim scope later.

## Bottom line

Finesse is a cautionary tale for plaintiffs who rely on complex technical diagrams and expert piecing‑together to prove infringement. The Federal Circuit made plain that juries cannot be left to knit together contradictory testimony or incomplete mappings and expect those threads to survive appellate scrutiny. For trial counsel on both sides, the remedy is straightforward in concept—be precise, be consistent, and put your element‑by‑element story in the record early and clearly.
