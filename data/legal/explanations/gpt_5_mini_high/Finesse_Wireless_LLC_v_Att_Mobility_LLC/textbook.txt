Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Technology, Patents, and Accused Feature

## Intermodulation products (IMPs) and passive intermodulation (PIM): what they are and typical physical causes

Intermodulation products (IMPs) are new radio‑frequency components that appear when two or more RF signals mix nonlinearly. When signals at different frequencies encounter a nonlinearity (for example, a nonlinear circuit element or a corroded metal contact), the products of sums and differences of those frequencies can be generated; some of those products can fall into the same frequency band as an intended communication and therefore interfere with reception. Passive intermodulation (PIM) is a subset of IMPs produced by passive nonlinearities — mechanical or material defects such as loose connectors, rusted or oxidized surfaces, metal filings, poor cable joints, or other imperfections in connectors, antennas, or nearby structures. PIM can be generated either inside the radio (internal PIM) or outside it (external PIM), but in either case the result is undesired signals that appear inband of the “signals of interest” and can degrade receiver performance.

Practically, PIM cancellation systems attempt to estimate the IMPs that will appear in the receiver and subtract a synthetic cancellation signal to restore the intended signal. Those systems rely on copies or references of the transmitter signals (the sources whose mixing produces PIM) and on signal‑processing blocks that generate an estimate of the intermodulation products and adaptively remove them from the received signal.

## ’134 patent overview: isolating signals of interest and interference‑generating signals; estimating and canceling inband IMPs; closed‑loop phase/amplitude adjustment with sub‑sample phase shifts

The ’134 patent claims a method that begins with sampling (oversampling) a passband of received signals to create a digital bitstream that “includes signals of interest and interference generating signals” (limitation 1[a]). The sampled bitstream is then processed by decimating filters to isolate the “signals of interest” on the one hand and the “source signals that generate” the intermodulation products on the other (limitations 1[b]–1[c]). From those isolated source signals the method computes an estimate of each in‑band intermodulation product and cancels the estimated IMPs from the received band (limitation 1[d]–1[e]). The final claimed step is an adaptive, closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment of the IMP estimates that includes “sub‑sample phase shifts” — i.e., phase adjustments at a resolution finer than the sampling interval (limitation 1[f]).

Technically, the claim requires that the receiver’s analog‑to‑digital conversion actually sample a passband that contains both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating source signals, because the rest of the method depends on having digital access to both kinds of signals. Decimating filters refer to digital filters that reduce sampling rate while isolating specified frequency components; closed‑loop phase/amplitude adjustment means the system uses feedback (for example, the residual error after cancellation) to tune cancellation coefficients over time. The “sub‑sample phase shifts” language signals an implementation that can adjust phase continuously or at fractional‑sample precision (typically via digital interpolation or equivalent techniques), but the central factual point for the case is temporal/physical access to the claimed source signals at the ADC sampling point.

## ’775 patent overview: co‑located transmitter/receiver; generating IMP cancellation signals continuously and near real time from copies of transmitter signals; n‑th order ICS via seven specified third‑order multiplications of three signals S1, S2, S3

The ’775 patent claims a particular architecture for generating intermodulation‑cancellation signals (ICSs) in a setup where the transmitter and receiver are co‑located (so transmitter‑side PIM can couple into the receiver). The method requires generating ICSs “continuously and near real time” using copies of transmitter signals, where the passive IMPs to be canceled are generated in passive transmitter/receiver components downstream of the high power amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter. Critically, limitation 1[d] specifies how an n‑th order ICS is formed for the third‑order case by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 in seven distinct third‑order combinations — S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3.

Two points are important for understanding the dispute. First, the claim enumerates specific multiplicative combinations; it is not satisfied by merely carrying out some third‑order processing unless the claimed combinations are produced (or a jury could reasonably find they were). Second, the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” rather than demanding three distinct (unique) physical signals, so copies can count as separate identifiers under that construction. The factual battle at trial therefore concentrated on (a) whether the accused system supplied three separately identifiable signals, and (b) whether the accused system actually performed the seven required multiplications (or provided convincing evidence that it did).

## Accused functionality: Nokia PIM‑C feature in AT&T’s deployed radios

The accused feature is Nokia’s PIM‑C (PIM cancellation) functionality implemented in radios deployed by AT&T. Nokia’s internal technical documents depict a signal‑processing chain in which the radio samples received RF via an RF ADC, processes digital samples through various blocks, generates a modeled PIM path (a digitally synthesized estimate of the PIM), and uses that model to produce cancellation signals that are combined with the received signal to suppress PIM. Finesse’s infringement theory relied primarily on those Nokia documents and on expert testimony identifying elements in the documents that Finesse argued corresponded to the patent claim terms.

Two practical facts from the Nokia materials drove the litigation. First, the Nokia documents show a “modeled PIM path” being generated inside a digital processing block (the GROOT FPGA) after the RF ADC; that timing/location matters for the ’134 claim because the patent requires the ADC to sample both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals. Second, the Nokia documents show multiplicative computations involving two named inputs x1 and x2 and operations that use complex conjugates and magnitudes, producing a limited set of distinct multiplicative terms; whether those terms can be mapped to the seven specific third‑order combinations claimed in the ’775 patent was a trial focal point.

## Key signals/components in Nokia materials: DL(TX) reference, modeled PIM path, x1 and x2, RF ADC, complex conjugate (′) and modulus (|·|)

The Nokia diagrams and legend use several labeled signals and blocks that the parties disputed in mapping to claim elements. The “DL(TX) reference” is a downlink transmit reference signal that Finesse’s expert treated as a representative “signal of interest.” The “modeled PIM path” is the digitally generated estimate of the PIM leakage path used by Nokia’s cancellation algorithm; according to the Nokia files, that modeled path is formed inside the GROOT FPGA. The labels x1 and x2 in the Nokia materials are the two principal input signals used by Nokia’s PIM algorithm; they appear in the multiplicative expressions the documents list.

The RF ADC is the radio‑frequency analog‑to‑digital converter that creates the initial digital bitstream by sampling the analog passband at the radio front end. If a given signal is only created later in the digital processing chain (for example, inside the GROOT FPGA), then it was not present at the physical ADC input and therefore was not “sampled” in the sense required by the ’134 claim language.

Nokia’s documents also show multiplicative operations that involve the complex conjugate (denoted x′ or x*) and the modulus (magnitude) of a complex signal (denoted |x|). The complex conjugate flips the sign of the imaginary component of a complex baseband sample; the modulus is the signal’s magnitude (a real, nonnegative scalar). Multiplying raw complex signals S1×S2×S3 is not algebraically the same as multiplying conjugates or magnitudes (for example, mapping a claimed S3 to x2, x2′, and |x2| simultaneously is not a straightforward identity). The Nokia materials list three distinct multiplicative forms (for example x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, x1·|x2|·|x2|) and also enumerate a larger set of terms (document references show p(n) = a0 through a9), but the expert testimony at trial identified only three of those multiplicative terms as corresponding to the claim language. The gap between what the Nokia documents actually show and what the claims require — particularly the seven enumerated third‑order combinations — was central to the ’775 infringement dispute.

## Location of PIM model generation (GROOT FPGA) versus the RF ADC sampling point

A concise technical fact that underpins much of the ’134 dispute is the order and location of operations in the Nokia design. The RF ADC sits at the front end and digitizes the incoming analog passband. The Nokia “modeled PIM path,” however, is depicted and described as being generated inside the GROOT FPGA — a programmable digital logic block that processes samples after the ADC. Because the modeled PIM path is a digital construct produced downstream of the ADC, it cannot have been part of the analog passband sampled by the RF ADC. That sequencing matters because the ’134 claim requires the receiver to oversample a passband that “includes signals of interest and interference generating signals.” If the alleged “interference generating signals” are only generated later in the FPGA (i.e., after sampling), they are not “received” and sampled in the claimed sense, and they therefore cannot satisfy limitation 1[a] of the ’134 claim unless the plaintiff shows other, sampled signals in the passband correspond to the interference‑generating sources.

In the trial record the parties disputed whether Nokia’s annotated legends and the labels x1/x2 could be read as showing the RF ADC sampled the required signals, or whether the expert’s mapping instead incorrectly identified a downstream modeled path as an interference source. That factual/technical distinction — whether the accused system digitizes the claimed source signals at the ADC or only later synthesizes a PIM model in the FPGA — is the technical hinge on which the ’134 infringement question turned. Likewise, for the ’775 claim the technical hinge was whether the Nokia processing actually produced the seven specific third‑order multiplications of three signals that the claim requires, or only a smaller, different set of multiplicative operations arising from two inputs and conjugate/modulus operations.

# Procedural Posture and Standards of Review

This case reached the Federal Circuit after a jury trial in the Eastern District of Texas and a set of post-trial motions that the district court denied. Finesse Wireless sued AT&T Mobility and Nokia, asserting claims of two patents that the jury found both valid and infringed, and the jury awarded a lump-sum damages figure of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents. Defendants moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement and JMOL/new trial on damages; the district court denied those motions. The defendants timely appealed, and the Federal Circuit, exercising its statutory jurisdiction over patent appeals under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL on noninfringement for all asserted claims and vacated the damages award.

## Claims asserted

Finesse asserted relatively small, discrete sets of claims from two patents. From U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 the asserted claims were 1–3; from U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 the asserted claims were 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36. The two patents claim different technical approaches to mitigating intermodulation product interference; understanding how the asserted claim limitations were mapped to the accused products was central to the JMOL disputes that followed the verdict.

## Jury verdict

After trial the jury found all asserted claims of both patents to be valid and infringed by the accused Nokia radios used by AT&T, and it awarded a single lump-sum damages figure of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents. Nokia had intervened in the action and participated in the trial. The damages award was tied to the jury’s finding of infringement: once the jury resolved liability in Finesse’s favor, the damages phase produced the large lump-sum award the defendants later sought to overturn.

## Post-trial motions and district court rulings

After entry of judgment, defendants moved for JMOL of noninfringement on the asserted claims and moved for JMOL on damages or, alternatively, for a new trial on damages. At the Rule 50 stage the district court denied JMOL and also denied the new-trial motion on damages. The district court’s reasoning was that the plaintiff’s expert testimony and other trial evidence were sufficient for a reasonable jury to find infringement and to support the damages award. The defendants appealed those denials, raising both legal and factual sufficiency arguments.

## Appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)

The Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over appeals in patent cases from final decisions of the district courts, under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). This appeal is a straightforward example: defendants appealed the district court’s post-trial denials in a patent case, and the Federal Circuit reviewed the denials and the underlying trial record, ultimately reversing the denial of JMOL and vacating the damages award.

## JMOL standard (Fifth Circuit law) and review of jury infringement findings

The Federal Circuit applies the law of the regional circuit to review a district court’s denial of JMOL. In this case the Fifth Circuit’s standards governed. Under that law the appellate court reviews a denial of JMOL de novo, but reversal is constrained: the court will reverse “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” The Federal Circuit also discusses the separate but related standard for reviewing a jury’s specific factual findings of infringement: such findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence exists if a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.

Read together, these standards mean the appellate court asks two related questions. First, under de novo review of the district court’s denial of JMOL it determines whether, after viewing the record in the light most favorable to the verdict, the record nonetheless lacks a legally sufficient evidentiary basis such that no reasonable jurors could have found for the prevailing party. Second, when the appeal challenges particular factual findings (for example, that an accused device meets a particular claim limitation), the court asks whether those findings are supported by substantial evidence — i.e., whether reasonable jurors could have reached the verdict the factfinder did. In practice the Federal Circuit will overturn a denial of JMOL when the plaintiff’s proof is legally insufficient under these standards.

Two practical points the opinion illustrates are worth noting. First, the plaintiff carries the burden of proving infringement at trial, and if the plaintiff relies principally on expert testimony, contradictions or failures to map evidence to claim limitations can make that proof legally insufficient. Second, the appellate court will not reweigh evidence; it must view the evidence favorably to the verdict, but it will still set aside a verdict where the evidence, so viewed, cannot reasonably support it.

## Claim construction: de novo review with clear-error review of underlying factual findings

Claim construction is a question of law reviewed de novo on appeal, but any underlying subsidiary factual findings that the district court made in construing claims are reviewed for clear error. The opinion demonstrates how these tiers operate. For the ’775 patent the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as requiring three separately identifiable signals but not requiring that they be three different (unique) signals; the district court also considered prosecution history and inventor testimony in reaching its construction. On appeal, the Federal Circuit reviewed the legal meaning of the claim language de novo and reviewed any factual findings the district court relied on for clear error.

The opinion also reinforces the well-established point that prosecution history can limit claim scope — but only if the patentee’s statements or amendments during prosecution rise to a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of claim scope. In this case the Federal Circuit concluded that amending claims to recite “three signals” did not amount to the kind of unmistakable surrender that would categorically exclude devices using only two separate signals; thus the court declined to construe the claims to that narrower meaning based solely on prosecution history.

The interplay between claim construction and JMOL arose in the trial context as well: defendants argued some legal positions were waived because they were not preserved at the Rule 50(a) stage. The district court addressed waiver arguments in denying JMOL, and the Federal Circuit addressed the underlying claim-scope and evidence questions in its de novo/substantial-evidence analysis.

## Damages review: new-trial denial reviewed for abuse of discretion; effect of noninfringement on damages

The district court denied both JMOL on damages and a new trial on damages. The Federal Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of a new trial for abuse of discretion under regional-circuit law; in this case the Fifth Circuit standard governs and the standard is deferential to the district court. However, the appellate court need not resolve the abuse-of-discretion question if it reaches and decides the underlying liability issue in the defendants’ favor. That is what happened here: because the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL on noninfringement (finding the infringement verdict unsupported by substantial evidence), the damages award had no surviving legal basis and the court vacated the damages award. In short, a reversal on liability typically requires vacatur of any damages award premised on that liability; the appellate court applied that principle and remanded or vacated the damages accordingly.

## How the Federal Circuit applied these standards in this case — a short synthesis

Applying the standards outlined above, the Federal Circuit took a close look at the evidentiary record and the plaintiff’s expert testimony. For the ’134 patent the court concluded that Finesse bore the burden to prove the accused radios sampled a passband that included both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” The plaintiff’s expert repeatedly tied the claimed “interference generating signals” to a modeled PIM path that the record showed was generated downstream of the receiver’s analog-to-digital converter; because the modeled path did not exist at the time sampling occurred, it could not be the sampled “interference generating signals.” The expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent and confusing about whether the claimed signals were the modeled PIM path or the inputs x1 and x2, and the court applied precedent holding that a party whose proof rests on self-contradictory expert testimony may fail to meet its burden. Under the governing appellate standards the Federal Circuit concluded there was not substantial evidence to support the jury’s infringement verdict for the ’134 patent and reversed the denial of JMOL.

For the ’775 patent the key disputed claim limitation required generation of seven third-order multiplications from three signals. The district court had defined “three signals” as separately identifiable but not necessarily unique. On appeal the Federal Circuit held that the prosecution history did not clearly disavow two-signal implementations, but even accepting the district court’s construction, the record evidence relied upon by Finesse (principally a Nokia technical document and expert testimony) did not show the seven claimed multiplications were performed. The Nokia document listed three distinct multiplications (and used modulus and complex conjugate operations), and the expert did not explain how those entries corresponded to the seven claimed multiplications or provide evidence that the listed operations were repeated to reach the seven enumerated multiplications. Under the substantial-evidence test the court concluded the jury’s verdict of infringement for the ’775 claims was not supported, and it reversed the denial of JMOL on those claims as well.

Because the Federal Circuit disposed of liability in favor of the defendants, it vacated the damages award that had been premised on infringement.

## Key takeaways for reading similar opinions

When you read appellate opinions that reverse JMOL denials in patent cases, start by locating the standards-of-review paragraphs: they tell you whether the court is applying regional-circuit law for JMOL denials, whether factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence, and whether claim construction will be reviewed de novo (with clear-error review of factual predicates). Then track how the appellant frames insufficiency of proof: are the challenges to discrete factual findings (e.g., mapping elements to accused device functions), to claim construction, or to both? Finally, watch how the appellate court treats expert testimony — internal inconsistency or failure to tie testimony to claim elements is often decisive under the substantial-evidence and JMOL standards, and any reversal of liability ordinarily requires vacatur of damages premised on that liability.

# Infringement Analysis—’134 Patent: Failure to Prove Sampling of Both Required Signal Categories

This chapter explains why the Federal Circuit concluded that Finesse failed to prove the accused radios met claim 1[a] of the ’134 patent. The court’s reversal turned on a narrow, but dispositive, factual and temporal problem: the plaintiff’s expert gave inconsistent testimony about which signals the receiver actually samples, and cross‑examination showed the signal the expert identified as an “interference generating signal” could not have been sampled because it is created later in the device. The chapter first states the legal standard and the claim requirement, then traces the expert’s mapping, the temporal/architectural impossibility exposed at trial, the expert’s attempted pivot, the district court’s inference, and the Federal Circuit’s reasoning in reversing the denial of JMOL.

## Claim 1[a] requirement: receiver must oversample a passband that includes both signals of interest and interference-generating signals

Claim 1[a] of the ’134 patent requires “over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” That plain language places a factual predicate on infringement: the accused receiver must be sampling (via its RF analog‑to‑digital converter, the RF ADC) a passband that actually contains both categories of analog signals at the time of sampling. Because Finesse was the patent owner and the plaintiff at trial, it bore the burden to prove that those two categories of signals existed in the sampled passband and were captured by the RF ADC. In reviewing the denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the Federal Circuit applied the Fifth Circuit’s standard: it asks whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence nonetheless so overwhelmingly favors one party that reasonable jurors could not reach a contrary conclusion; the court reviews the jury’s infringement finding for substantial evidence.

## Finesse’s mapping at trial: signals of interest = DL(TX) reference; interference-generating signals = modeled PIM path; both allegedly enter the RF ADC

Finesse’s case depended almost entirely on its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who tied the patent language to the accused Nokia radios by pointing to a Nokia technical document. Dr. Wells testified that the RF ADC (the receiver) samples a “red path” in the diagram and that the two things on that red path corresponded to the claim categories: the “DL (TX) reference” was the claimed “signals of interest,” and the “modeled PIM path” was the claimed “interference generating signals.” He repeatedly described the RF ADC as the component that samples those signals and produces the bit stream required by the claim.

## Temporal/architectural flaw: modeled PIM path is generated downstream (in the FPGA) after ADC sampling; cannot be sampled before it exists

On cross‑examination, defense counsel pointed out a fundamental timing and architectural fact in the same technical record: the modeled PIM path is not an analog signal present at the RF ADC input but is a digitally generated model produced later in the pipeline by the GROOT FPGA (i.e., downstream of the ADC). Because signals cannot be sampled before they are generated, the modeled PIM path—if it is indeed generated after the ADC—cannot logically be part of the passband sampled by the RF ADC. That temporal sequencing defeats the necessary claim element: the receiver cannot have “over-sampled” a passband that included the modeled PIM path if the modeled PIM path did not yet exist when sampling occurred.

## Cross-examination pivot: expert references x1 and x2 as two inputs but does not clearly map them to the claim-recited categories; continues to identify modeled PIM path as the interference-generating signals

Faced with the timing problem, Dr. Wells attempted a pivot. He testified at one point that “there’s two signals on that red path … x1 and x2,” suggesting two inputs were present at the ADC. But crucially he never clearly or consistently mapped those x1 and x2 identifiers to the claim terms “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Instead, in multiple places he continued to identify the red path as consisting of the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. In short, he offered two different ways of describing the red path without an unambiguous, coherent explanation that tied the claim categories to signals that the RF ADC actually sampled. Finesse later attempted to stitch together disparate snippets of testimony (over many pages) to support an inference that Dr. Wells meant x1 and x2 when he said DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path, but the testimony itself remained internally inconsistent.

## District court’s JMOL ruling: infers expert meant x1/x2 as the two claim-recited signal categories; finds sufficient evidence

At JMOL the district court resolved the ambiguity in Finesse’s favor. The court concluded that Dr. Wells had testified “there are two signals entering the RF ADC, and that those are signals of interest and the interference generating signals,” and it read his “two signals” testimony as meaning he was referring to x1 and x2. On that basis the district court denied JMOL, reasoning that a reasonable jury could find both categories were sampled. The district court therefore credited the expert’s testimony and the Nokia document as supplying the requisite mapping.

## Federal Circuit reasoning: testimony remained confusing and self-contradictory; no clear, consistent mapping to both required categories; Johns Hopkins v. Datascope principle

The Federal Circuit reversed. Its core finding was evidentiary: Dr. Wells’ testimony was confusing and self‑contradictory and never supplied the clear, consistent mapping required to show the RF ADC sampled both categories. The court focused on the timing impossibility that defense cross‑examination exposed—the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the ADC—and on the fact that Dr. Wells repeatedly and expressly identified the modeled PIM path as the “interference generating signals.” His later, garbled references to x1 and x2 did not cure that contradiction because he never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claimed categories. The Federal Circuit applied the principle from Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.: when the party bearing the burden of proof rests on expert testimony that is internally contradictory, a court may conclude the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law to support the verdict. Here, because the only evidentiary route Finesse used to prove the presence of the interference signals at the ADC was internally inconsistent and temporally impossible, no reasonable jury could have found infringement on that claim element.

## Disposition on ’134 patent: reversal of denial of JMOL—no substantial evidence of infringement

Because Finesse failed to present substantial evidence that the RF ADC sampled a passband containing both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent. The decision underscores two practical lessons for litigators and experts: first, when a claim element depends on the existence of signals at a particular point in a device, expert testimony must establish both identity and temporal/architectural presence at that point; and second, inconsistent or self‑contradictory expert testimony—especially from the party with the burden—cannot be relied on to fill such gaps in proof.

# Infringement Analysis—’775 Patent: ‘Three Signals’ and the Seven Required Multiplications

## Claim 1[d] requirement: generate an n‑th order ICS via seven third‑order multiplications of three signals S1, S2, S3
Claim 1[d] of the ’775 patent requires that the accused method generate intermodulation‑product cancellation signals (ICSs) by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, “digitally multiplying and filtering” seven specifically listed third‑order products: S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; and S2×S3×S3. Read naturally, the claim demands that those seven multiplicative products be produced as part of generating an n‑th order ICS. For infringement, the patentee bears the burden of proving the accused system actually generates the claimed products (or otherwise meets the limitation).

## Claim construction: ‘S1, S2 and S3’ must be separately identifiable, not necessarily unique; district court’s waiver finding on a ‘uniqueness’ argument
On summary judgment the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that the signals be “separately identifiable” but not to require that they be three different (unique) signals. The court relied on intrinsic text and inventor testimony that the labels S1–S3 may represent the same or different signals so long as they are separately identifiable in the implementation. At the JMOL stage the district court declined to entertain Appellants’ contention that the claim requires three unique signals because Appellants had not preserved that argument at Rule 50(a) (pre‑verdict JMOL) and thus had waived it. The district court therefore accepted the jury’s finding that two distinct signals plus a copy could satisfy the court’s construction of “three signals.”

## Prosecution history/disavowal: amendment from ‘two or three’ to ‘three’ and the Continental Circuits standard
Appellants argued that, during prosecution, Finesse narrowed its claims from a broader “two or three signals” formulation to the explicit recitation of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to overcome Filipovic, and that this amendment amounted to a surrender (disavowal) of coverage of devices that use only two input signals. The Federal Circuit applied the familiar rule that prosecution amendments result in claim narrowing only where there is a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of claim scope (the Continental Circuits standard). Here the court concluded the amendment did not rise to that level: Finesse overcame the examiner’s rejection by distinguishing Filipovic on technical grounds (active versus passive IMPs), not by an unambiguous statement that the claims would exclude two‑signal implementations. Thus the amendment at most supports the district court’s construction — three separately identifiable signals — but does not establish an incontrovertible surrender of two‑signal implementations.

## Finesse’s proof: the Nokia document and the three distinct multiplications actually shown
At trial Finesse’s infringement theory rested on expert testimony tied to Nokia technical documents describing the accused radios’ PIM‑cancellation processing. The crucial page relied on by Finesse, however, lists only three multiplicative forms: x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, and x1·|x2|·|x2|. Those entries use modulus operations (| · |) and complex conjugation (x2′) rather than the plain S1, S2, S3 multiplicative terms recited in claim 1[d]. Critically, the document does not show seven separate third‑order multiplications of the form enumerated in the claim, nor does it show that any of the three listed forms are repeated in a way that would produce all seven claimed products.

## Mapping gap: absence of trial testimony connecting the three operations to the seven claimed products
The decisive evidentiary gap at trial was the absence of testimony tying each of the seven claim products to operations actually performed by the accused radios. Dr. Wells identified the three multiplicative entries in the Nokia document but did not explain how those three forms map onto the seven specific S1×S2×S3‑type products the claim requires. On appeal Finesse sought to supply a mapping, but that mapping was not presented to the jury. Importantly, accepting the mapping Finesse proposed would require treating different mathematical forms (e.g., x2, its complex conjugate x2′, and its modulus |x2|) as interchangeable instantiations of the same S‑signal or otherwise collapsing distinct terms without trial evidence to support that equivalence. The Federal Circuit refused to fill that evidentiary void: a plaintiff cannot satisfy its burden by asserting on appeal the mapping the expert failed to present at trial.

## ‘Ten multiplications’ argument (coefficients a0–a9): why it did not save the verdict
At oral argument and on appeal Finesse pointed to another part of Nokia’s documentation showing coefficient terms (a0 through a9) and argued the accused radios actually compute ten multiplicative terms, from which the seven claimed products could be selected. The court observed that nowhere at trial did Finesse or its expert identify which subset of those ten terms correspond to the seven enumerated in the claim or explain how the ten terms map to the three distinct multiplicative forms the expert relied on. Because this mapping and identification were absent from the trial record, the appellate court could not credit the after‑the‑fact argument. The jury verdict cannot stand on post‑trial theory not presented to the jury.

## Disposition on the ’775 patent and the lesson for infringement proof
The Federal Circuit concluded that, although the prosecution amendment did not clearly and unambiguously surrender two‑signal implementations and the claim construction allowed “separately identifiable” (but not necessarily unique) S‑labels, the record contained no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven specific third‑order multiplications recited in claim 1[d]. Dr. Wells’s reliance on three multiplicative forms in the Nokia document, without testimony connecting each of the seven claimed products to actual operations in the accused system (or evidence that the document’s three forms were repeated or otherwise yielded the seven products), was insufficient to support the jury’s infringement finding. The court therefore reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent.

For the law student: when a claim enumerates specific operations or products, the patentee must present trial evidence that those specific items were generated or otherwise met. Expert testimony must tie each claim limitation to concrete, admissible evidence in the record; appellate re‑mapping or new technical explanations offered for the first time on appeal cannot cure an evidentiary deficiency. Prosecution amendments can narrow claim scope, but only a clear and unambiguous prosecution disclaimer will do so; absence of such disclaimer does not, however, relieve the patentee of the obligation to prove every claimed element at trial.

# Damages, Disposition, and Practical Significance

## Consequences for damages: vacatur of the $166,303,391 lump‑sum award
Because the Federal Circuit concluded that the jury verdict of infringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents was not supported by substantial evidence, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL on noninfringement and vacated the damages award. The jury had returned a lump‑sum award of $166,303,391 for the patents’ remaining life; that award flowed directly from the infringement verdict and therefore could not stand once the liability finding was set aside. The panel did not reach or reinstate any alternative damages findings—vacatur followed as the practical and obligatory consequence of reversing on infringement.

## Final disposition: reversed (noninfringement) and vacated (damages); costs to Appellants
The formal appellate disposition is straightforward: the denial of JMOL of noninfringement was reversed, the damages award was vacated, and costs were awarded to Defendants‑Appellants. In other words, the Federal Circuit set aside the jury’s infringement verdict and nullified the $166,303,391 award. The opinion therefore leaves no surviving liability or damages from the jury verdict that can be enforced, and it directs that the prevailing appellants recover their appellate costs.

## Expert testimony lessons: maintain clear, consistent mappings from accused system elements to each claim limitation; avoid contradictions and post hoc clarifications on appeal
A central reason the court overturned the jury verdict was the plaintiff’s reliance on an expert whose mappings from the accused radios to claim terms were unclear and internally inconsistent. Dr. Wells repeatedly referred to the same diagrammatic “red path” as representing both (a) a DL(TX) reference and a modeled PIM path and (b) the two inputs x1 and x2, but he never consistently identified which concrete signals or hardware elements corresponded to each claimed term. The court emphasized that when the patentee bears the burden of proof, an expert’s self‑contradictory or equivocal testimony can be insufficient as a matter of law (citing Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope as analogous).

For practitioners: make the expert’s mapping explicit and repeatable. The expert report and direct testimony should present a one‑to‑one mapping that ties each asserted claim limitation to identifiable, contemporaneous evidence (e.g., a particular signal node, FPGA block, source line, data capture). Anticipate cross‑examination by preparing to reconcile any apparent inconsistencies on the record rather than relying on a corrective explanation on appeal; post hoc clarifications not presented to the jury will not cure insufficiency.

## Technical proof in DSP contexts: establish when and where signals exist relative to claimed operations; prove every enumerated operation (e.g., all seven multiplications), not just a subset
This case highlights two technical proof traps common in digital signal processing litigation. First, timing and locus matter: the ’134 claim required sampling a passband that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The accused radios’ “modeled PIM path” was generated downstream in the GROOT FPGA after the RF ADC sampled the passband, so it could not satisfy the claim element that the receiver sampled those interference‑generating signals. Plaintiffs must therefore establish not only that a certain mathematical object exists, but that it exists at the claimed stage of the signal chain (e.g., at the ADC input, prior to decimation, etc.).

Second, when claims enumerate specific operations—as the ’775 claim required seven particular third‑order multiplications—proof must show that the accused device performs the required set and multiplicity of operations. Relying on a vendor document that lists three distinct complex operations (involving modulus and complex conjugation) without demonstrating how those are executed, repeated, or mapped to the seven claimed multiplications was insufficient. In DSP disputes, then, the patentee should present direct evidence that each enumerated operation was implemented and, where multiplicitous language appears, that the necessary repetitions or distinct combinations actually occurred (for example, firmware/RTL, execution traces, register dumps, or validated instrumented captures).

## Claim drafting and litigation strategy: amendments adding specificity do not necessarily disavow broader scope absent clear statements; preserve arguments to avoid waiver disputes
The court also addressed claim scope that came from prosecution history. Although Finesse amended the claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” during prosecution, the Federal Circuit declined to read that amendment as a clear and unambiguous surrender of embodiments using only two unique inputs. The takeaways are twofold. First, claim amendments can be invoked in later litigation, but a court will look for explicit, unmistakable prosecution disclaimer before narrowing claim scope beyond the ordinary meaning; mere addition of specificity to overcome prior art does not automatically produce total disclaimer. Second, litigants must preserve their positions at the right times: Rule 50(a) motions and timely trial objections preserve issues for appeal, and failure to press certain arguments at trial can lead a district court to deem them waived.

Conclusion
Finesse Wireless v. AT&T illustrates how liability and remedy are tightly linked: unclear expert mappings and incomplete technical proof on where signals exist and which operations are performed can be dispositive of infringement and thus of damages. For litigators and expert witnesses in DSP cases, the decision reinforces three practical disciplines—(1) build and present a clean, documented mapping from claim limitations to concrete elements; (2) prove the timing, location, and full set of claimed operations; and (3) preserve litigation and prosecution‑history arguments timely and clearly so they can be considered on appeal.
