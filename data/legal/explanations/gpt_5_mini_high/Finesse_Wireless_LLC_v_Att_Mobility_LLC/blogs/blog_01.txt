Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When Block Diagrams Aren’t Enough: Fed. Cir. Nixes PIM Verdict Over Expert Contradictions

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. 2025) is a reminder that in highly technical patent disputes—especially ones about digital signal processing and radio hardware—jury verdicts live or die on clean, element-by-element mappings from claim language to the accused product. The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated a $166.3 million damages award because the plaintiff’s case rested on an expert whose testimony was internally inconsistent and who leaned on ambiguous vendor diagrams rather than clear, contemporaneous proof that the accused radios met the claim elements. For patentees, the decision is a caution: block diagrams, legends, and half-explained variables can’t substitute for concrete testimony that ties each claim limitation to a physical or temporal fact in the accused device. For defendants, it confirms that attacking an expert’s mappings can be a decisive path to JMOL.

## The Federal Circuit’s reasoning

The opinion addresses two patents, but the court’s treatment of the ’134 patent is the clearer doctrinal lesson. Claim 1 of the ’134 patent requires, as a threshold matter, “over-sampling . . . a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s only direct evidence of sampling came from its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who relied heavily on a Nokia vendor diagram whose legend read “Red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path.” At trial Dr. Wells repeatedly testified that the “modeled PIM path” was the claimed “interference generating signals.” Defense cross-examination, however, exposed a fatal temporal mismatch: the modeled PIM path was generated downstream in a GROOT FPGA after the RF ADC had already sampled the incoming radio-frequency signals. Put simply, a receiver cannot have “sampled” signals that did not yet exist at the time of sampling.

Faced with that problem, Dr. Wells attempted a mid‑cross pivot—pointing to generic variables in the vendor diagram (x1 and x2) and arguing those were the two signals entering the ADC. But, as the Federal Circuit stressed, he never testified clearly that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claim terms “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The court found his testimony confusing and self‑contradictory, and it rejected the district court’s inclination to infer the necessary mapping for the jury. Citing Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), the panel reiterated that when a patentee rests its case on an expert whose testimony is internally inconsistent, a reasonable jury cannot be said to have substantial evidence supporting infringement.

The court reached a parallel evidentiary conclusion on the ’775 patent. That claim requires generating intermodulation-cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals into seven specific third-order products. The Nokia materials Dr. Wells relied on listed three distinct multiplications (and also used modulus and complex‑conjugate notation), but Dr. Wells never explained at trial how those operations produced the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse’s new, more detailed mapping appeared only on appeal, and the Federal Circuit refused to cure that gap. Because the trial record lacked substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications, the court reversed on noninfringement for the ’775 claims as well.

## Why the record failed

Two related evidentiary failures stand out. First, timing and location matter in signal processing claims. Proving a receiver “samples” particular signals requires proof those signals existed on the sampled passband at the sampling instant. An expert’s assertion that a modeled or reconstructed signal (created later in an FPGA or DSP) is the same as a “received” signal will not do if the device architecture shows the model is generated after sampling. Second, variable names and diagram legends are notorious traps. A vendor legend that lists multiple things on one color or line does not, without more, show which device variable corresponds to which claim term. Finesse’s expert relied on the legend and then equivocated under cross-examination; the equivocation was fatal.

The district court’s desire to infer the mapping for the jury could not overcome the lack of clear testimony. That is the core of the Federal Circuit’s holding: the plaintiff bears the burden of proving every limitation, and when the only evidence is a confused or shifting expert narrative, an appellate court can—and will—take away the verdict.

## Doctrinal takeaways

The decision reinforces a few settled, but sometimes underappreciated, doctrines. Substantial‑evidence review of jury infringement findings does not compel appellate courts to rubber‑stamp verdicts supported only by ambiguous expert claims. Johns Hopkins remains a controlling reminder that self‑contradictory expert testimony cannot carry a patentee’s burden. At the same time, the Federal Circuit reiterated the narrowness of prosecution disclaimer: the court declined to read the ’775 prosecution amendment as a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of two‑signal embodiments. So patentees should not assume doctrinal leniency on prosecution history and should separately ensure the trial record is robust.

The opinion also signals a practical standard for what an expert must provide in DSP and radio cases: not lofty descriptions, but concrete mappings that tie claim language to specific signals, to their timing (pre‑ or post‑ADC), and to the device’s implementation (block, firmware, math operation, or measured trace).

## Practical trial tips

Practitioners should take three concrete lessons from Finesse. First, prepare and lock in a clear mapping in the expert report and direct testimony: identify each accused variable (x1, x2, etc.), the physical node or buffer where it exists, and whether it is present at the ADC input or generated later. Demonstratives should mirror that mapping, and the expert should adopt the demonstratives on direct in simple, unambiguous terms. Second, develop independent, contemporaneous evidence of the claimed operations: instrument the device, produce oscilloscope captures, firmware snippets, signal logs, or engineer testimony that shows where and when signals exist and which algebraic operations the device actually performs. Don’t rely only on vendor diagrams or legends. Third, anticipate and neutralize the temporal argument: if a modeled or estimated signal is part of the infringement theory, explain (and prove) how that model is derived from sampled inputs in a way that satisfies the claim’s “received signals” requirement, or else concede the limitation and litigate around it.

For defense teams, Finesse offers a playbook: attack timing, press for specificity on mappings, and be ready to move for JMOL if the patentee’s case collapses into equivocation. Courts will accept JMOL where the record, viewed most favorably to the verdict winner, still points overwhelmingly to noninfringement.

## Conclusion

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T is not a rulemaking moment: block diagrams and vendor legends remain admissible and useful. But the Federal Circuit made plain that they are not a substitute for precise, consistent expert proof tying each claim element to a concrete part of the accused product at the relevant time. In DSP‑heavy litigation, the technical detail matters—and so does the expert’s ability to tell a simple, unconfused story about where each signal lives and when it is sampled.
