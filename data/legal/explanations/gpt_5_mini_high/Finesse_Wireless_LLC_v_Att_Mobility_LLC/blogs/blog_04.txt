Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# JMOL, Substantial Evidence, and a $166M Reversal: Lessons from Finesse v. AT&T

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC is a sharp reminder that big jury verdicts can be undone on appeal when the plaintiff’s proof depends on equivocal or internally inconsistent expert mappings. The court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL on noninfringement for two asserted patents concerning PIM/PIM‑cancellation technology and vacated a $166.3 million lump‑sum damages award. Practitioners should read Finesse as a jurisprudential and practical lesson: element‑by‑element proof must be clear, timing matters in signal processing disputes, and appellate courts will not sustain verdicts founded on confused expert testimony or mappings that were never presented coherently to the jury.

## What the Federal Circuit held

The appeals turned on two independent failures of proof.

For the ’134 patent, the claim required the accused receiver to “over‑sample . . . a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s infringement case rested on expert testimony tied to a Nokia technical diagram. The Federal Circuit found that the expert, Dr. Wells, repeatedly identified the “modeled PIM path” as the interference‑generating signal, but the record showed that the modeled PIM path is produced downstream—in the GROOT FPGA—after the RF ADC has already sampled. Faced with cross‑examination, the expert attempted to pivot to labels x1 and x2 on the diagram but never clearly mapped x1 and x2 to the claimed inputs. The court concluded the testimony was “confusing and unclear,” and that a party cannot meet its burden when its case rests on self‑contradictory expert testimony. Because no reasonable jury could have found the sampling limitation satisfied on that record, JMOL of noninfringement was warranted.

For the ’775 patent, the claim required generating third‑order intermodulation cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals in seven distinct multiplications. The district court construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique,” and the jury credited Dr. Wells’ mapping of Nokia materials to the claimed multiplications. On appeal the Federal Circuit declined to find that prosecution amendment to recite “three signals” constituted a clear disavowal of two‑signal implementations. But the court nonetheless held that there was insufficient evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications. The Nokia document admitted at trial disclosed only three distinct multiplications (involving modulus and complex conjugates—e.g., x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, x1·|x2|·|x2|), and Dr. Wells did not explain how those three items corresponded to the seven claimed products. Finesse’s attempt to supply a mapping on appeal came too late. The court reversed for lack of substantial evidence.

Because the Federal Circuit concluded none of the asserted claims were supported by substantial evidence of infringement, it vacated the damages award.

## Why the court reversed: clarity, timing, and mapping

Two themes run through the opinion. First, timing matters in technical claims. The distinction between a signal that exists at the ADC input and a modeled signal generated later is not academic; it can be outcome‑determinative. Second, an expert's testimony must do the heavy lifting in a clear, element‑by‑element way. The opinion emphasizes that attorneys cannot paper over gaps by pointing to a diagram legend (the “red path” in this case) or by arguing alternate mappings on appeal. The Federal Circuit relied on a familiar principle: when the plaintiff bears the burden, an expert’s contradictory or evasive testimony can render the evidence legally insufficient. The court explicitly cited Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope for that proposition and applied it here to undo a large verdict.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Finesse reinforces several doctrinal touchstones. Substantial‑evidence review is unforgiving of fuzzy expert testimony. Claim construction and prosecution history remain central—amendments during prosecution can create pitfalls, but the bar for finding clear and unambiguous disclaimer remains high. The case also illustrates an appellate willingness to parse technical testimony and documentary exhibits closely; where mappings are not laid out in the trial record, an appellate court will decline to invent them.

Practically, this opinion may encourage more aggressive JMOL practice in cases that rest on complex technical diagrams and expert mappings, particularly in courts (like the Eastern District of Texas historically) that have hosted large damages verdicts in telecommunications and electronics cases. Defendants will push preservation and mapping arguments earlier and more forcefully; plaintiffs will respond by sharpening their element‑by‑element proof and tightening expert narratives.

## Practice pointers

First, lock down clear, element‑by‑element proofs before and during trial and preserve challenges at Rule 50(a) when appropriate. If you expect the case to turn on a mapping of accused‑device behavior to claim limitations, put that mapping into the record explicitly—on direct, in demonstratives admitted to the jury, and in jury instructions if feasible—so you are not trying to supply or defend a new mapping on appeal.

Second, avoid overreliance on block‑diagram legends or shorthand labels. Diagrams and legends are useful, but they can be ambiguous about timing and signal provenance. Experts should tie every diagram label back to physical signals, show where those signals are generated or sampled in the pipeline, and explain why those locations satisfy the claim language.

Third, make sure the jury actually receives the technical mapping you plan to defend on appeal. An appellate brief cannot substitute for trial testimony. If a mapping is contested, seal it into the record with demonstratives, clear witness testimony, and—where helpful—live demonstrations or data traces that make timing and multiplicative operations palpable to a lay jury.

Finally, be mindful in prosecution: narrowing amendments can create risk of estoppel or at least create fodder for a preservation battle later. If you must narrow a claim to overcome a reference, preserve the reasons for narrowing and the boundaries of any disclaimers so you can defend the claim scope later.

## Bottom line

Finesse is a cautionary tale for plaintiffs who rely on complex technical diagrams and expert piecing‑together to prove infringement. The Federal Circuit made plain that juries cannot be left to knit together contradictory testimony or incomplete mappings and expect those threads to survive appellate scrutiny. For trial counsel on both sides, the remedy is straightforward in concept—be precise, be consistent, and put your element‑by‑element story in the record early and clearly.
