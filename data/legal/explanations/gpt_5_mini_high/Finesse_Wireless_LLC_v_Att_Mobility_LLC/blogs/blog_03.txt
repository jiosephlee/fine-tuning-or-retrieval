Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Seven Means Seven: Why Conjugates and Moduli Didn’t Prove Infringement Under the ’775 Patent

## Why this case matters
Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a reminder that in patent litigation involving digital signal processing and other mathematically precise technologies, claim language that numerically enumerates operations is not loose poetry. A $166.3 million jury verdict evaporated because the patentee never proved the specific, enumerated third‑order multiplications the ’775 patent claims require. For practitioners, the decision is a practical lesson: when claims list particular algebraic products, plaintiffs must put a clean, admissible mapping in front of the jury showing that the accused implementation actually performs those products — not something that looks like them, or something you try to explain for the first time on appeal.

## What the Federal Circuit did
The Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent. The claim at issue requires, for third‑order cancellation, generating intermodulation‑cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 in seven specific combinations (for example S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, and so on). At trial Finesse’s expert relied on a Nokia technical document to show the accused radios performed those multiplications. The Nokia material, however, showed only three distinct operations — not the seven enumerated products — and the operations it did show involved modulus and complex‑conjugate expressions (for example x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, and x1·|x2|·|x2|), rather than a literal list of the seven S1×S2×S3‑style multiplications the claim lists. The expert did not explain at trial how those three distinct expressions map onto the seven claimed multiplications, nor did he demonstrate that any of the listed multiplications were repeated so as to account for the full set of seven. Finesse’s attempt on appeal to rely on a ten‑term polynomial in Nokia’s materials — and to map terms after the fact — came too late. The Federal Circuit concluded there was not substantial evidence to support a jury finding that the accused radios performed the seven enumerated multiplications, and it reversed.

## Why conjugates and moduli fell short (and why that matters)
At the heart of the court’s analysis is a simple evidentiary point: showing some algebraic operations in a vendor document is not the same as proving the specific algebraic operations recited in the claim. The Nokia document included operations using complex conjugation and modulus notation; those are distinct mathematical manipulations with different algebraic form and semantic meaning from the plain three‑term products listed in the claim. The court did not pronounce an across‑the‑board rule that conjugates or moduli can never satisfy a multiplication claim, but it did hold that Finesse failed to prove equivalence or to map the Nokia expressions to the seven claimed products at trial. Equivalence-by-inference offered on appeal — or an expert’s unclear or internally inconsistent testimony — is not substantial evidence. The upshot: when the claim enumerates precise algebraic terms, plaintiffs must show how the accused code or hardware implements those same algebraic terms (or establish, with admissible proof, why what is implemented is plainly equivalent).

## Doctrinal takeaways
A few doctrinal points flow directly from the opinion. First, the Federal Circuit enforced the plaintiff’s burden of proof for infringement with fidelity: a jury verdict cannot stand if the only technical proof is ambiguous or logically inconsistent. Second, the court reiterated that claim limitations that enumerate specific operations or numeric outcomes are not satisfied by amorphous or partial matches; specificity in the claim carries through to the evidence required at trial. Third, the case reaffirms that an appellant may not cure trial deficiencies with new technical mappings or theories presented for the first time on appeal. Finally, on prosecution history the court declined to find a clear and unmistakable disavowal of two‑signal implementations simply because the claims were amended to recite “three signals” — the amendment did not amount to the kind of unequivocal disclaimer that would strip the claims of any coverage of two‑signal architectures. That separate point shows the court will be careful before reading prosecution amendments as dispositive disavowals, but will be uncompromising about the evidentiary proof required at trial.

## Practical takeaways for litigators and drafters
For patentees and trial counsel: if your claim lists specific multiplications or other enumerated algebraic operations, have a trial‑ready mapping from the accused implementation’s variables and operations to each claimed algebraic term. Don’t rely on a vendor manual’s terse notation without an expert demonstration of algebraic equivalence. If the accused implementation uses complex‑valued arithmetic (conjugation, magnitude, FFTs, etc.), have your expert show step‑by‑step how those operations produce the claimed products, or why they are equivalents under the claim language and the specification. Put that mapping into demonstratives and deposition/expert reports so it is squarely before the jury.

For defendants and their experts: probe whether purported mappings actually match the claim language, and highlight when a vendor document shows modulus or conjugate operations rather than the claimed raw products. Moving for JMOL with a focused record can pay off when the patentee’s case depends on inferential or post‑hoc explanations.

For prosecutors and claim drafters: if you intend your claims to cover implementations that use conjugation, magnitude, frequency‑domain manipulations, or other non‑literal algebraic forms, claim them or define them expressly in the specification. If you prefer to claim functional equivalence, provide a specification basis showing the equivalences you might later assert, and be mindful of narrowing amendments that could invite prosecution‑history estoppel if not handled carefully.

## Conclusion
Finesse v. AT&T is not a technicality; it is an evidentiary reality check. In technology cases where the heart of infringement is a set of algebraic operations, “seven” must mean seven in the evidentiary record. Conjugates and moduli are not per se disqualified from any infringement theory, but if a patentee wants them to do the heavy lifting, the mathematics and the mapping must be presented clearly and consistently to the jury. Without that, even a large jury award will not survive appellate scrutiny.
