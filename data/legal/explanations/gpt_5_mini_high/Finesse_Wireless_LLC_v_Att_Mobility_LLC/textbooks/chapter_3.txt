Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 3: # Infringement Analysis—’134 Patent: Failure to Prove Sampling of Both Required Signal Categories

This chapter explains why the Federal Circuit concluded that Finesse failed to prove the accused radios met claim 1[a] of the ’134 patent. The court’s reversal turned on a narrow, but dispositive, factual and temporal problem: the plaintiff’s expert gave inconsistent testimony about which signals the receiver actually samples, and cross‑examination showed the signal the expert identified as an “interference generating signal” could not have been sampled because it is created later in the device. The chapter first states the legal standard and the claim requirement, then traces the expert’s mapping, the temporal/architectural impossibility exposed at trial, the expert’s attempted pivot, the district court’s inference, and the Federal Circuit’s reasoning in reversing the denial of JMOL.

## Claim 1[a] requirement: receiver must oversample a passband that includes both signals of interest and interference-generating signals

Claim 1[a] of the ’134 patent requires “over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” That plain language places a factual predicate on infringement: the accused receiver must be sampling (via its RF analog‑to‑digital converter, the RF ADC) a passband that actually contains both categories of analog signals at the time of sampling. Because Finesse was the patent owner and the plaintiff at trial, it bore the burden to prove that those two categories of signals existed in the sampled passband and were captured by the RF ADC. In reviewing the denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the Federal Circuit applied the Fifth Circuit’s standard: it asks whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence nonetheless so overwhelmingly favors one party that reasonable jurors could not reach a contrary conclusion; the court reviews the jury’s infringement finding for substantial evidence.

## Finesse’s mapping at trial: signals of interest = DL(TX) reference; interference-generating signals = modeled PIM path; both allegedly enter the RF ADC

Finesse’s case depended almost entirely on its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who tied the patent language to the accused Nokia radios by pointing to a Nokia technical document. Dr. Wells testified that the RF ADC (the receiver) samples a “red path” in the diagram and that the two things on that red path corresponded to the claim categories: the “DL (TX) reference” was the claimed “signals of interest,” and the “modeled PIM path” was the claimed “interference generating signals.” He repeatedly described the RF ADC as the component that samples those signals and produces the bit stream required by the claim.

## Temporal/architectural flaw: modeled PIM path is generated downstream (in the FPGA) after ADC sampling; cannot be sampled before it exists

On cross‑examination, defense counsel pointed out a fundamental timing and architectural fact in the same technical record: the modeled PIM path is not an analog signal present at the RF ADC input but is a digitally generated model produced later in the pipeline by the GROOT FPGA (i.e., downstream of the ADC). Because signals cannot be sampled before they are generated, the modeled PIM path—if it is indeed generated after the ADC—cannot logically be part of the passband sampled by the RF ADC. That temporal sequencing defeats the necessary claim element: the receiver cannot have “over-sampled” a passband that included the modeled PIM path if the modeled PIM path did not yet exist when sampling occurred.

## Cross-examination pivot: expert references x1 and x2 as two inputs but does not clearly map them to the claim-recited categories; continues to identify modeled PIM path as the interference-generating signals

Faced with the timing problem, Dr. Wells attempted a pivot. He testified at one point that “there’s two signals on that red path … x1 and x2,” suggesting two inputs were present at the ADC. But crucially he never clearly or consistently mapped those x1 and x2 identifiers to the claim terms “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Instead, in multiple places he continued to identify the red path as consisting of the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. In short, he offered two different ways of describing the red path without an unambiguous, coherent explanation that tied the claim categories to signals that the RF ADC actually sampled. Finesse later attempted to stitch together disparate snippets of testimony (over many pages) to support an inference that Dr. Wells meant x1 and x2 when he said DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path, but the testimony itself remained internally inconsistent.

## District court’s JMOL ruling: infers expert meant x1/x2 as the two claim-recited signal categories; finds sufficient evidence

At JMOL the district court resolved the ambiguity in Finesse’s favor. The court concluded that Dr. Wells had testified “there are two signals entering the RF ADC, and that those are signals of interest and the interference generating signals,” and it read his “two signals” testimony as meaning he was referring to x1 and x2. On that basis the district court denied JMOL, reasoning that a reasonable jury could find both categories were sampled. The district court therefore credited the expert’s testimony and the Nokia document as supplying the requisite mapping.

## Federal Circuit reasoning: testimony remained confusing and self-contradictory; no clear, consistent mapping to both required categories; Johns Hopkins v. Datascope principle

The Federal Circuit reversed. Its core finding was evidentiary: Dr. Wells’ testimony was confusing and self‑contradictory and never supplied the clear, consistent mapping required to show the RF ADC sampled both categories. The court focused on the timing impossibility that defense cross‑examination exposed—the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the ADC—and on the fact that Dr. Wells repeatedly and expressly identified the modeled PIM path as the “interference generating signals.” His later, garbled references to x1 and x2 did not cure that contradiction because he never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claimed categories. The Federal Circuit applied the principle from Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.: when the party bearing the burden of proof rests on expert testimony that is internally contradictory, a court may conclude the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law to support the verdict. Here, because the only evidentiary route Finesse used to prove the presence of the interference signals at the ADC was internally inconsistent and temporally impossible, no reasonable jury could have found infringement on that claim element.

## Disposition on ’134 patent: reversal of denial of JMOL—no substantial evidence of infringement

Because Finesse failed to present substantial evidence that the RF ADC sampled a passband containing both the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent. The decision underscores two practical lessons for litigators and experts: first, when a claim element depends on the existence of signals at a particular point in a device, expert testimony must establish both identity and temporal/architectural presence at that point; and second, inconsistent or self‑contradictory expert testimony—especially from the party with the burden—cannot be relied on to fill such gaps in proof.
