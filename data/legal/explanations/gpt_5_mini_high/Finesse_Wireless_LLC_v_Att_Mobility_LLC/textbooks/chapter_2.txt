Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # Procedural Posture and Standards of Review

This case reached the Federal Circuit after a jury trial in the Eastern District of Texas and a set of post-trial motions that the district court denied. Finesse Wireless sued AT&T Mobility and Nokia, asserting claims of two patents that the jury found both valid and infringed, and the jury awarded a lump-sum damages figure of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents. Defendants moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement and JMOL/new trial on damages; the district court denied those motions. The defendants timely appealed, and the Federal Circuit, exercising its statutory jurisdiction over patent appeals under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL on noninfringement for all asserted claims and vacated the damages award.

## Claims asserted

Finesse asserted relatively small, discrete sets of claims from two patents. From U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 the asserted claims were 1–3; from U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 the asserted claims were 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36. The two patents claim different technical approaches to mitigating intermodulation product interference; understanding how the asserted claim limitations were mapped to the accused products was central to the JMOL disputes that followed the verdict.

## Jury verdict

After trial the jury found all asserted claims of both patents to be valid and infringed by the accused Nokia radios used by AT&T, and it awarded a single lump-sum damages figure of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents. Nokia had intervened in the action and participated in the trial. The damages award was tied to the jury’s finding of infringement: once the jury resolved liability in Finesse’s favor, the damages phase produced the large lump-sum award the defendants later sought to overturn.

## Post-trial motions and district court rulings

After entry of judgment, defendants moved for JMOL of noninfringement on the asserted claims and moved for JMOL on damages or, alternatively, for a new trial on damages. At the Rule 50 stage the district court denied JMOL and also denied the new-trial motion on damages. The district court’s reasoning was that the plaintiff’s expert testimony and other trial evidence were sufficient for a reasonable jury to find infringement and to support the damages award. The defendants appealed those denials, raising both legal and factual sufficiency arguments.

## Appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)

The Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over appeals in patent cases from final decisions of the district courts, under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). This appeal is a straightforward example: defendants appealed the district court’s post-trial denials in a patent case, and the Federal Circuit reviewed the denials and the underlying trial record, ultimately reversing the denial of JMOL and vacating the damages award.

## JMOL standard (Fifth Circuit law) and review of jury infringement findings

The Federal Circuit applies the law of the regional circuit to review a district court’s denial of JMOL. In this case the Fifth Circuit’s standards governed. Under that law the appellate court reviews a denial of JMOL de novo, but reversal is constrained: the court will reverse “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” The Federal Circuit also discusses the separate but related standard for reviewing a jury’s specific factual findings of infringement: such findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence exists if a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.

Read together, these standards mean the appellate court asks two related questions. First, under de novo review of the district court’s denial of JMOL it determines whether, after viewing the record in the light most favorable to the verdict, the record nonetheless lacks a legally sufficient evidentiary basis such that no reasonable jurors could have found for the prevailing party. Second, when the appeal challenges particular factual findings (for example, that an accused device meets a particular claim limitation), the court asks whether those findings are supported by substantial evidence — i.e., whether reasonable jurors could have reached the verdict the factfinder did. In practice the Federal Circuit will overturn a denial of JMOL when the plaintiff’s proof is legally insufficient under these standards.

Two practical points the opinion illustrates are worth noting. First, the plaintiff carries the burden of proving infringement at trial, and if the plaintiff relies principally on expert testimony, contradictions or failures to map evidence to claim limitations can make that proof legally insufficient. Second, the appellate court will not reweigh evidence; it must view the evidence favorably to the verdict, but it will still set aside a verdict where the evidence, so viewed, cannot reasonably support it.

## Claim construction: de novo review with clear-error review of underlying factual findings

Claim construction is a question of law reviewed de novo on appeal, but any underlying subsidiary factual findings that the district court made in construing claims are reviewed for clear error. The opinion demonstrates how these tiers operate. For the ’775 patent the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as requiring three separately identifiable signals but not requiring that they be three different (unique) signals; the district court also considered prosecution history and inventor testimony in reaching its construction. On appeal, the Federal Circuit reviewed the legal meaning of the claim language de novo and reviewed any factual findings the district court relied on for clear error.

The opinion also reinforces the well-established point that prosecution history can limit claim scope — but only if the patentee’s statements or amendments during prosecution rise to a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of claim scope. In this case the Federal Circuit concluded that amending claims to recite “three signals” did not amount to the kind of unmistakable surrender that would categorically exclude devices using only two separate signals; thus the court declined to construe the claims to that narrower meaning based solely on prosecution history.

The interplay between claim construction and JMOL arose in the trial context as well: defendants argued some legal positions were waived because they were not preserved at the Rule 50(a) stage. The district court addressed waiver arguments in denying JMOL, and the Federal Circuit addressed the underlying claim-scope and evidence questions in its de novo/substantial-evidence analysis.

## Damages review: new-trial denial reviewed for abuse of discretion; effect of noninfringement on damages

The district court denied both JMOL on damages and a new trial on damages. The Federal Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of a new trial for abuse of discretion under regional-circuit law; in this case the Fifth Circuit standard governs and the standard is deferential to the district court. However, the appellate court need not resolve the abuse-of-discretion question if it reaches and decides the underlying liability issue in the defendants’ favor. That is what happened here: because the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL on noninfringement (finding the infringement verdict unsupported by substantial evidence), the damages award had no surviving legal basis and the court vacated the damages award. In short, a reversal on liability typically requires vacatur of any damages award premised on that liability; the appellate court applied that principle and remanded or vacated the damages accordingly.

## How the Federal Circuit applied these standards in this case — a short synthesis

Applying the standards outlined above, the Federal Circuit took a close look at the evidentiary record and the plaintiff’s expert testimony. For the ’134 patent the court concluded that Finesse bore the burden to prove the accused radios sampled a passband that included both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” The plaintiff’s expert repeatedly tied the claimed “interference generating signals” to a modeled PIM path that the record showed was generated downstream of the receiver’s analog-to-digital converter; because the modeled path did not exist at the time sampling occurred, it could not be the sampled “interference generating signals.” The expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent and confusing about whether the claimed signals were the modeled PIM path or the inputs x1 and x2, and the court applied precedent holding that a party whose proof rests on self-contradictory expert testimony may fail to meet its burden. Under the governing appellate standards the Federal Circuit concluded there was not substantial evidence to support the jury’s infringement verdict for the ’134 patent and reversed the denial of JMOL.

For the ’775 patent the key disputed claim limitation required generation of seven third-order multiplications from three signals. The district court had defined “three signals” as separately identifiable but not necessarily unique. On appeal the Federal Circuit held that the prosecution history did not clearly disavow two-signal implementations, but even accepting the district court’s construction, the record evidence relied upon by Finesse (principally a Nokia technical document and expert testimony) did not show the seven claimed multiplications were performed. The Nokia document listed three distinct multiplications (and used modulus and complex conjugate operations), and the expert did not explain how those entries corresponded to the seven claimed multiplications or provide evidence that the listed operations were repeated to reach the seven enumerated multiplications. Under the substantial-evidence test the court concluded the jury’s verdict of infringement for the ’775 claims was not supported, and it reversed the denial of JMOL on those claims as well.

Because the Federal Circuit disposed of liability in favor of the defendants, it vacated the damages award that had been premised on infringement.

## Key takeaways for reading similar opinions

When you read appellate opinions that reverse JMOL denials in patent cases, start by locating the standards-of-review paragraphs: they tell you whether the court is applying regional-circuit law for JMOL denials, whether factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence, and whether claim construction will be reviewed de novo (with clear-error review of factual predicates). Then track how the appellant frames insufficiency of proof: are the challenges to discrete factual findings (e.g., mapping elements to accused device functions), to claim construction, or to both? Finally, watch how the appellate court treats expert testimony — internal inconsistency or failure to tie testimony to claim elements is often decisive under the substantial-evidence and JMOL standards, and any reversal of liability ordinarily requires vacatur of damages premised on that liability.
