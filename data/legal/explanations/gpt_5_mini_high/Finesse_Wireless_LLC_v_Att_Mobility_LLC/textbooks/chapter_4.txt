Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # Infringement Analysis—’775 Patent: ‘Three Signals’ and the Seven Required Multiplications

## Claim 1[d] requirement: generate an n‑th order ICS via seven third‑order multiplications of three signals S1, S2, S3
Claim 1[d] of the ’775 patent requires that the accused method generate intermodulation‑product cancellation signals (ICSs) by, given three signals S1, S2, and S3, “digitally multiplying and filtering” seven specifically listed third‑order products: S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; and S2×S3×S3. Read naturally, the claim demands that those seven multiplicative products be produced as part of generating an n‑th order ICS. For infringement, the patentee bears the burden of proving the accused system actually generates the claimed products (or otherwise meets the limitation).

## Claim construction: ‘S1, S2 and S3’ must be separately identifiable, not necessarily unique; district court’s waiver finding on a ‘uniqueness’ argument
On summary judgment the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that the signals be “separately identifiable” but not to require that they be three different (unique) signals. The court relied on intrinsic text and inventor testimony that the labels S1–S3 may represent the same or different signals so long as they are separately identifiable in the implementation. At the JMOL stage the district court declined to entertain Appellants’ contention that the claim requires three unique signals because Appellants had not preserved that argument at Rule 50(a) (pre‑verdict JMOL) and thus had waived it. The district court therefore accepted the jury’s finding that two distinct signals plus a copy could satisfy the court’s construction of “three signals.”

## Prosecution history/disavowal: amendment from ‘two or three’ to ‘three’ and the Continental Circuits standard
Appellants argued that, during prosecution, Finesse narrowed its claims from a broader “two or three signals” formulation to the explicit recitation of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to overcome Filipovic, and that this amendment amounted to a surrender (disavowal) of coverage of devices that use only two input signals. The Federal Circuit applied the familiar rule that prosecution amendments result in claim narrowing only where there is a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of claim scope (the Continental Circuits standard). Here the court concluded the amendment did not rise to that level: Finesse overcame the examiner’s rejection by distinguishing Filipovic on technical grounds (active versus passive IMPs), not by an unambiguous statement that the claims would exclude two‑signal implementations. Thus the amendment at most supports the district court’s construction — three separately identifiable signals — but does not establish an incontrovertible surrender of two‑signal implementations.

## Finesse’s proof: the Nokia document and the three distinct multiplications actually shown
At trial Finesse’s infringement theory rested on expert testimony tied to Nokia technical documents describing the accused radios’ PIM‑cancellation processing. The crucial page relied on by Finesse, however, lists only three multiplicative forms: x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, and x1·|x2|·|x2|. Those entries use modulus operations (| · |) and complex conjugation (x2′) rather than the plain S1, S2, S3 multiplicative terms recited in claim 1[d]. Critically, the document does not show seven separate third‑order multiplications of the form enumerated in the claim, nor does it show that any of the three listed forms are repeated in a way that would produce all seven claimed products.

## Mapping gap: absence of trial testimony connecting the three operations to the seven claimed products
The decisive evidentiary gap at trial was the absence of testimony tying each of the seven claim products to operations actually performed by the accused radios. Dr. Wells identified the three multiplicative entries in the Nokia document but did not explain how those three forms map onto the seven specific S1×S2×S3‑type products the claim requires. On appeal Finesse sought to supply a mapping, but that mapping was not presented to the jury. Importantly, accepting the mapping Finesse proposed would require treating different mathematical forms (e.g., x2, its complex conjugate x2′, and its modulus |x2|) as interchangeable instantiations of the same S‑signal or otherwise collapsing distinct terms without trial evidence to support that equivalence. The Federal Circuit refused to fill that evidentiary void: a plaintiff cannot satisfy its burden by asserting on appeal the mapping the expert failed to present at trial.

## ‘Ten multiplications’ argument (coefficients a0–a9): why it did not save the verdict
At oral argument and on appeal Finesse pointed to another part of Nokia’s documentation showing coefficient terms (a0 through a9) and argued the accused radios actually compute ten multiplicative terms, from which the seven claimed products could be selected. The court observed that nowhere at trial did Finesse or its expert identify which subset of those ten terms correspond to the seven enumerated in the claim or explain how the ten terms map to the three distinct multiplicative forms the expert relied on. Because this mapping and identification were absent from the trial record, the appellate court could not credit the after‑the‑fact argument. The jury verdict cannot stand on post‑trial theory not presented to the jury.

## Disposition on the ’775 patent and the lesson for infringement proof
The Federal Circuit concluded that, although the prosecution amendment did not clearly and unambiguously surrender two‑signal implementations and the claim construction allowed “separately identifiable” (but not necessarily unique) S‑labels, the record contained no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven specific third‑order multiplications recited in claim 1[d]. Dr. Wells’s reliance on three multiplicative forms in the Nokia document, without testimony connecting each of the seven claimed products to actual operations in the accused system (or evidence that the document’s three forms were repeated or otherwise yielded the seven products), was insufficient to support the jury’s infringement finding. The court therefore reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent.

For the law student: when a claim enumerates specific operations or products, the patentee must present trial evidence that those specific items were generated or otherwise met. Expert testimony must tie each claim limitation to concrete, admissible evidence in the record; appellate re‑mapping or new technical explanations offered for the first time on appeal cannot cure an evidentiary deficiency. Prosecution amendments can narrow claim scope, but only a clear and unambiguous prosecution disclaimer will do so; absence of such disclaimer does not, however, relieve the patentee of the obligation to prove every claimed element at trial.
