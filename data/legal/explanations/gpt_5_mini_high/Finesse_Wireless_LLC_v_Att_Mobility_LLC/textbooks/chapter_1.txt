Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 1: # Technology, Patents, and Accused Feature

## Intermodulation products (IMPs) and passive intermodulation (PIM): what they are and typical physical causes

Intermodulation products (IMPs) are new radio‑frequency components that appear when two or more RF signals mix nonlinearly. When signals at different frequencies encounter a nonlinearity (for example, a nonlinear circuit element or a corroded metal contact), the products of sums and differences of those frequencies can be generated; some of those products can fall into the same frequency band as an intended communication and therefore interfere with reception. Passive intermodulation (PIM) is a subset of IMPs produced by passive nonlinearities — mechanical or material defects such as loose connectors, rusted or oxidized surfaces, metal filings, poor cable joints, or other imperfections in connectors, antennas, or nearby structures. PIM can be generated either inside the radio (internal PIM) or outside it (external PIM), but in either case the result is undesired signals that appear inband of the “signals of interest” and can degrade receiver performance.

Practically, PIM cancellation systems attempt to estimate the IMPs that will appear in the receiver and subtract a synthetic cancellation signal to restore the intended signal. Those systems rely on copies or references of the transmitter signals (the sources whose mixing produces PIM) and on signal‑processing blocks that generate an estimate of the intermodulation products and adaptively remove them from the received signal.

## ’134 patent overview: isolating signals of interest and interference‑generating signals; estimating and canceling inband IMPs; closed‑loop phase/amplitude adjustment with sub‑sample phase shifts

The ’134 patent claims a method that begins with sampling (oversampling) a passband of received signals to create a digital bitstream that “includes signals of interest and interference generating signals” (limitation 1[a]). The sampled bitstream is then processed by decimating filters to isolate the “signals of interest” on the one hand and the “source signals that generate” the intermodulation products on the other (limitations 1[b]–1[c]). From those isolated source signals the method computes an estimate of each in‑band intermodulation product and cancels the estimated IMPs from the received band (limitation 1[d]–1[e]). The final claimed step is an adaptive, closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment of the IMP estimates that includes “sub‑sample phase shifts” — i.e., phase adjustments at a resolution finer than the sampling interval (limitation 1[f]).

Technically, the claim requires that the receiver’s analog‑to‑digital conversion actually sample a passband that contains both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating source signals, because the rest of the method depends on having digital access to both kinds of signals. Decimating filters refer to digital filters that reduce sampling rate while isolating specified frequency components; closed‑loop phase/amplitude adjustment means the system uses feedback (for example, the residual error after cancellation) to tune cancellation coefficients over time. The “sub‑sample phase shifts” language signals an implementation that can adjust phase continuously or at fractional‑sample precision (typically via digital interpolation or equivalent techniques), but the central factual point for the case is temporal/physical access to the claimed source signals at the ADC sampling point.

## ’775 patent overview: co‑located transmitter/receiver; generating IMP cancellation signals continuously and near real time from copies of transmitter signals; n‑th order ICS via seven specified third‑order multiplications of three signals S1, S2, S3

The ’775 patent claims a particular architecture for generating intermodulation‑cancellation signals (ICSs) in a setup where the transmitter and receiver are co‑located (so transmitter‑side PIM can couple into the receiver). The method requires generating ICSs “continuously and near real time” using copies of transmitter signals, where the passive IMPs to be canceled are generated in passive transmitter/receiver components downstream of the high power amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter. Critically, limitation 1[d] specifies how an n‑th order ICS is formed for the third‑order case by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 in seven distinct third‑order combinations — S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3.

Two points are important for understanding the dispute. First, the claim enumerates specific multiplicative combinations; it is not satisfied by merely carrying out some third‑order processing unless the claimed combinations are produced (or a jury could reasonably find they were). Second, the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” rather than demanding three distinct (unique) physical signals, so copies can count as separate identifiers under that construction. The factual battle at trial therefore concentrated on (a) whether the accused system supplied three separately identifiable signals, and (b) whether the accused system actually performed the seven required multiplications (or provided convincing evidence that it did).

## Accused functionality: Nokia PIM‑C feature in AT&T’s deployed radios

The accused feature is Nokia’s PIM‑C (PIM cancellation) functionality implemented in radios deployed by AT&T. Nokia’s internal technical documents depict a signal‑processing chain in which the radio samples received RF via an RF ADC, processes digital samples through various blocks, generates a modeled PIM path (a digitally synthesized estimate of the PIM), and uses that model to produce cancellation signals that are combined with the received signal to suppress PIM. Finesse’s infringement theory relied primarily on those Nokia documents and on expert testimony identifying elements in the documents that Finesse argued corresponded to the patent claim terms.

Two practical facts from the Nokia materials drove the litigation. First, the Nokia documents show a “modeled PIM path” being generated inside a digital processing block (the GROOT FPGA) after the RF ADC; that timing/location matters for the ’134 claim because the patent requires the ADC to sample both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals. Second, the Nokia documents show multiplicative computations involving two named inputs x1 and x2 and operations that use complex conjugates and magnitudes, producing a limited set of distinct multiplicative terms; whether those terms can be mapped to the seven specific third‑order combinations claimed in the ’775 patent was a trial focal point.

## Key signals/components in Nokia materials: DL(TX) reference, modeled PIM path, x1 and x2, RF ADC, complex conjugate (′) and modulus (|·|)

The Nokia diagrams and legend use several labeled signals and blocks that the parties disputed in mapping to claim elements. The “DL(TX) reference” is a downlink transmit reference signal that Finesse’s expert treated as a representative “signal of interest.” The “modeled PIM path” is the digitally generated estimate of the PIM leakage path used by Nokia’s cancellation algorithm; according to the Nokia files, that modeled path is formed inside the GROOT FPGA. The labels x1 and x2 in the Nokia materials are the two principal input signals used by Nokia’s PIM algorithm; they appear in the multiplicative expressions the documents list.

The RF ADC is the radio‑frequency analog‑to‑digital converter that creates the initial digital bitstream by sampling the analog passband at the radio front end. If a given signal is only created later in the digital processing chain (for example, inside the GROOT FPGA), then it was not present at the physical ADC input and therefore was not “sampled” in the sense required by the ’134 claim language.

Nokia’s documents also show multiplicative operations that involve the complex conjugate (denoted x′ or x*) and the modulus (magnitude) of a complex signal (denoted |x|). The complex conjugate flips the sign of the imaginary component of a complex baseband sample; the modulus is the signal’s magnitude (a real, nonnegative scalar). Multiplying raw complex signals S1×S2×S3 is not algebraically the same as multiplying conjugates or magnitudes (for example, mapping a claimed S3 to x2, x2′, and |x2| simultaneously is not a straightforward identity). The Nokia materials list three distinct multiplicative forms (for example x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, x1·|x2|·|x2|) and also enumerate a larger set of terms (document references show p(n) = a0 through a9), but the expert testimony at trial identified only three of those multiplicative terms as corresponding to the claim language. The gap between what the Nokia documents actually show and what the claims require — particularly the seven enumerated third‑order combinations — was central to the ’775 infringement dispute.

## Location of PIM model generation (GROOT FPGA) versus the RF ADC sampling point

A concise technical fact that underpins much of the ’134 dispute is the order and location of operations in the Nokia design. The RF ADC sits at the front end and digitizes the incoming analog passband. The Nokia “modeled PIM path,” however, is depicted and described as being generated inside the GROOT FPGA — a programmable digital logic block that processes samples after the ADC. Because the modeled PIM path is a digital construct produced downstream of the ADC, it cannot have been part of the analog passband sampled by the RF ADC. That sequencing matters because the ’134 claim requires the receiver to oversample a passband that “includes signals of interest and interference generating signals.” If the alleged “interference generating signals” are only generated later in the FPGA (i.e., after sampling), they are not “received” and sampled in the claimed sense, and they therefore cannot satisfy limitation 1[a] of the ’134 claim unless the plaintiff shows other, sampled signals in the passband correspond to the interference‑generating sources.

In the trial record the parties disputed whether Nokia’s annotated legends and the labels x1/x2 could be read as showing the RF ADC sampled the required signals, or whether the expert’s mapping instead incorrectly identified a downstream modeled path as an interference source. That factual/technical distinction — whether the accused system digitizes the claimed source signals at the ADC or only later synthesizes a PIM model in the FPGA — is the technical hinge on which the ’134 infringement question turned. Likewise, for the ’775 claim the technical hinge was whether the Nokia processing actually produced the seven specific third‑order multiplications of three signals that the claim requires, or only a smaller, different set of multiplicative operations arising from two inputs and conjugate/modulus operations.
