Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What must be preserved in a Rule 50(a) motion to avoid waiver on appeal for a claim-limitation challenge like ‘three signals,’ and do claim construction or summary judgment arguments count?
Question:
The district court said defendants waived the argument that S1, S2, S3 must be unique by not raising it in Rule 50(a). The Federal Circuit reversed on a different ground (failure to prove the seven multiplications). For preservation: 
- Is it enough in Rule 50(a) to generally assert ‘no substantial evidence supports element X,’ or must the movant specify the exact construction theory (e.g., ‘unique signals’)? 
- If a party argued a construction at claim construction or summary judgment, does that preserve it for JMOL? 
- How do courts distinguish between waiving a legal construction argument and preserving a factual sufficiency challenge tied to that element?
Answer:
Short answer
- You should preserve both (a) the legal claim‑construction theory you plan to press on appeal and (b) the factual sufficiency challenge tied to that theory. Finesse shows a district court will treat a construction‑specific argument as waived if it was not raised at the Rule 50(a) stage, even if the issue was litigated earlier at Markman/SJ. Conversely, a preserved JMOL that points to lack of evidence under the court’s construction can succeed on appeal (as the Federal Circuit did where the evidence failed to prove seven multiplications).

Why (and what the opinion shows)
- The district court had earlier construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable, but not ‘unique.’” (J.A. 109–15, 115.) At the JMOL hearing the defendants argued the claim requires unique signals, but the district court ruled that the unique‑signal argument was waived because it had not been raised in the Rule 50(a) motion. (J.A. 38, 40.) That is the core preservation lesson the opinion records.
- The Federal Circuit did not rely on the waiver ruling; it reversed on independent factual‑sufficiency grounds for both patents. For the ’775 patent the court held there was no substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications: the Nokia document showed only three distinct multiplications, Dr. Wells did not map those three onto the seven claimed multiplications, and there was no evidence multiplications were repeated to yield the seven required by the claim. (J.A. 14966–70; J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 26486.) For the ’134 patent the court similarly reversed because the plaintiff’s expert gave internally inconsistent testimony and the evidence was insufficient. (J.A. 10–11; citing Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope, J.A. 543 F.3d 1342.)

Concrete answers to your bullets
1) Is a general “no substantial evidence on element X” enough in Rule 50(a), or must the movant specify a construction (e.g., “unique signals”)?  
- Finesse illustrates that a generic sufficiency protest may not preserve a construction‑based legal theory. The district court treated the “unique signals” theory as waived because it was not presented in the Rule 50(a) motion (J.A. 38, 40). So if you intend to argue noninfringement based on a particular claim construction, say that construction (or expressly tie your sufficiency challenge to that construction) in the Rule 50(a) motion.

2) If a party argued a construction at claim construction or summary judgment, does that preserve it for JMOL?  
- Not necessarily. In Finesse the parties had litigated the “three signals” language at summary judgment (the court construed it “separately identifiable, not unique”), but the district court still treated the defendants’ later “unique signals” argument as waived at JMOL because it had not been raised in the Rule 50(a) motion (J.A. 109–15; J.A. 38, 40). Put another way: earlier advocacy helps, but re‑raising the precise legal theory in your Rule 50(a) motion is the safe course.

3) How do courts distinguish waiving a legal construction argument vs. preserving a factual sufficiency challenge tied to that element?  
- The opinion shows the distinction in practice: claim construction arguments are legal theories that courts expect to be presented and preserved as such; factual sufficiency challenges require the movant to show the record lacks substantial evidence that the accused product meets the limitation as construed. The district court viewed the “unique” point as a legal construction and held it waived for not being raised; the Federal Circuit resolved the sufficiency question on the record (no evidence of seven multiplications) and reversed on that factual ground (J.A. 38–39; J.A. 14966–70; J.A. 26485–86). So, if your factual sufficiency attack depends on a different construction than the court adopted, you must explicitly tie the legal construction and the sufficiency argument together in the Rule 50(a) motion.

Practical checklist (what to put in Rule 50(a))
- State the precise ground(s) for JMOL: identify the claim limitation and the legal construction you rely on (or say you challenge the verdict under the court’s construction).  
- Make the factual sufficiency point under that construction: explain why the evidence cannot support the claim element as construed (identify the missing proof—e.g., no evidence mapping 3 multiplications to the 7 required).  
- Preserve alternatives: if you seek JMOL under two different constructions, put both alternatives in the motion so the district court and the record are on notice.

Bottom line
Finesse teaches two lessons: (1) don’t assume earlier Markman or summary‑judgment argument alone preserves a different construction for JMOL — put the construction in the Rule 50(a) motion; and (2) separately and clearly press the factual‑sufficiency point you want the court to rule on under whichever construction you rely upon (the Federal Circuit will reverse where the evidence cannot reasonably support the jury’s finding, as it did here for the seven multiplications).
