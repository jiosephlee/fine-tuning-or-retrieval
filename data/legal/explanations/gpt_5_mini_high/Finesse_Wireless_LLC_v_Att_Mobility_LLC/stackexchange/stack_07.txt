Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Was it appropriate for the district court to rely on inventor testimony to support a construction that ‘three signals’ need not be unique under Phillips and related precedent?
Question:
The claim says ‘three signals S1, S2 and S3,’ and the district court relied in part on inventor testimony to conclude they are ‘separately identifiable’ but not unique. My understanding is that inventor testimony is extrinsic and generally less reliable than the intrinsic record. Did the Federal Circuit endorse this use of inventor testimony, or simply find it non-dispositive given the intrinsic record? More broadly, when is it proper to lean on inventor testimony to add a qualifier like ‘separately identifiable’ that is not in the claim text?
Answer:
Short answer
No — the Federal Circuit did not say inventor testimony can supplant the intrinsic record. Finesse notes the district court “credited inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence” (J.A. 109–14), but the Federal Circuit’s own resolution rested on the intrinsic record and prosecution history: it rejected Appellants’ surrender theory because there was no “clear and unambiguous” disavowal and concluded, “at most, Finesse’s amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals” (J.A. 110–11; J.A. 968–69). In short, the inventor testimony was corroborative and non‑dispositive, not controlling.

Why (doctrine and what the opinion shows)
- Governing principle (as applied in the opinion): claim construction is driven by the intrinsic record (claims, specification, prosecution history); extrinsic evidence such as inventor testimony may be considered to help resolve ambiguity but cannot contradict a clear intrinsic meaning. The opinion explicitly describes the district court as having “credited inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence” in construing “three signals” (J.A. 109–14).
- Prosecution disclaimer standard: the Federal Circuit refused to treat the amendment to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as a clear surrender of two‑signal systems because the prosecution history did not amount to a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal (the court cites the governing “clear and unambiguous” standard and then rejects Appellants’ surrender argument) (J.A. 110–11). That is the primary basis for the court’s statement that the claim at most requires “three separately identifiable signals” (J.A. 968–69).

When inventor testimony may properly be used to add a qualifier
- Proper use: where the intrinsic record is ambiguous or silent about a nuance, inventor testimony can be admissible to show how the inventors understood or used a term and to corroborate a reasonable construction consistent with the specification and prosecution history.
- Improper use: inventor testimony cannot be the dispositive source that contradicts a clear intrinsic definition or manufacture a a limitation absent support in the intrinsic record or a clear inventor-led disavowal during prosecution.
- How Finesse illustrates this boundary: the district court added the phrase “separately identifiable” and credited inventor testimony that S1–S3 “can be the same signal or different signals” (J.A. 109–14). The Federal Circuit did not adopt inventor testimony as the sole ground for that construction; it looked to the prosecution history and concluded there was no clear surrender, hence the court’s bottom‑line construction (three separately identifiable, not necessarily unique) was grounded in the intrinsic record (J.A. 110–11; J.A. 968–69).

Takeaway
If you are arguing claim scope, rely first on the intrinsic record. Extrinsic inventor testimony can help resolve ambiguity or corroborate a construction, but a court will treat it as non‑dispositive and will not allow it to override a clear specification or prosecution history. Finesse demonstrates exactly that: inventor testimony was considered, but the Federal Circuit’s holding turned on the intrinsic materials and the high bar for disclaimer.
