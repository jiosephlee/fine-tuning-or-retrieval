Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How can the Federal Circuit reverse a jury’s infringement verdict under the substantial evidence standard based on self-contradictory expert testimony?
Question:
The panel says it reviews JMOL denials under Fifth Circuit law and looks for substantial evidence supporting the verdict. Yet it reverses because the patentee’s expert gave confusing, self-contradictory testimony about what signals met the claim limitations, citing Johns Hopkins v. Datascope. I thought weighing credibility was for the jury. Under what circumstances can the appellate court deem an expert’s testimony legally insufficient (rather than just confusing) and conclude that no reasonable jury could rely on it?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. The Federal Circuit reversed because the patentee’s case rested on an expert whose testimony was internally inconsistent, contradicted undisputed technical facts in the record, and failed to tie the accused features to essential claim limitations. Under the substantial‑evidence/JMOL standard an appellate court may conclude that such testimony is legally insufficient — not merely a credibility choice for the jury — when no reasonable juror could have relied on it. Finesse Wireless v. AT&T, No. 2024‑1039 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) explains why.

The governing standard
- The court reviews denial of JMOL de novo under Fifth Circuit law and will reverse “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” (Finesse, quoting Wi‑LAN and Apple v. Wi‑LAN, cited in the opinion.)
- A jury’s finding must be supported by substantial evidence — i.e., evidence from which a reasonable jury could find for the prevailing party.

Why the Federal Circuit reversed in Finesse
- ’134 patent: Dr. Wells initially mapped the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to the DL(TX) reference and the “modeled PIM path.” On cross, he admitted the modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC and so could not have been sampled (a factual impossibility for the claimed limitation). He then “pivoted” to saying there are two inputs (x1 and x2) but never clearly or consistently mapped x1/x2 to the claim terms. The court found the testimony “confusing” and “self‑contradictory,” and held that “this sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.” The court applied Johns Hopkins v. Datascope: when the patentee rests on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, the testimony can be legally insufficient. (Finesse op., pp. ≈10–22.)
- ’775 patent: Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document that showed only three distinct multiplications, but the claim required seven specific multiplications. He never testified how the three map to the seven, nor was duplicative performance shown at trial. The court held that because the expert failed to make the required mapping (and attempted clarifications were made only on appeal), “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.” (Finesse op., pp. ≈38–39.)

When appellate courts may deem expert testimony legally insufficient (practical rule)
An appellate court may step in where the expert testimony that is the patentee’s sole or primary proof of an essential element:
1. Is internally inconsistent on material points and the record contains no plausible reconciliation;
2. Contradicts undisputed documentary or physical facts (e.g., claims a device does something that the record shows is impossible);
3. Fails to tie the asserted facts or exhibits to the claim limitations (no clear mapping of accused structure/operation to claimed elements);
4. Attempts to repair or explain mappings only on appeal (new explanations not presented to the jury cannot supply substantial evidence);
and, as a result,
5. No reasonable juror could have credited the testimony in a way that proves the element.

Distinction from credibility calls
- Credibility is ordinarily a jury question. But the court is not performing an improper credibility contest when it asks whether the evidence, objectively read, could support a verdict. If the testimony is legally incoherent or irreconcilable with the record, it is not “substantial evidence” regardless of whether the jury purported to believe the expert.

Bottom line
Finesse shows that appellate reversal under the substantial‑evidence/JMOL standard is proper when a patentee’s proof collapses into unreconciled, materially inconsistent expert testimony (or testimony that contradicts the record) and thus cannot reasonably support each required claim element.
