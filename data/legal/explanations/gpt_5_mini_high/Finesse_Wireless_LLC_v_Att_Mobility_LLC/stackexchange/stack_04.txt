Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What exactly must be proven to meet the ’775 claim’s requirement of seven third-order multiplications, especially when the accused design uses modulus and conjugate operations?
Question:
The claim requires generating seven specific third-order products of S1, S2, and S3. The Nokia documentation showed only three distinct products and involved |x| and complex conjugate operations. The panel found no evidence of all seven multiplications or a valid mapping. I’m unsure about: 
- Do the seven listed combinations require seven distinct computed products, or can a single computed product correspond to multiple listed permutations due to commutativity/associativity? 
- Can |x| or x′ be treated as the same ‘signal’ for purposes of S1/S2/S3, or does using modulus/conjugate move the operation outside the scope of ‘digitally multiplying’ the claimed signals? 
- If the accused system computes ten polynomial terms p(n)=a0..a9, what kind of trial proof is needed to identify which seven align with the claim’s seven products?
Answer:
Short answer
- The ’775 claim requires proof that the accused system produces the seven third‑order products recited (or products that are mathematically the same as those recited). A patentee may not rely on bare labels or later appellate argument — the jury must be shown, with non‑speculative evidence, how the accused device’s computed terms correspond to each claimed product. Finesse lost because the Nokia documents only showed three distinct multiplication forms (and used |x| and x′), and the patentee never explained at trial how those three (or the ten polynomial terms in the doc) map to the seven claim items. See Finesse Wireless v. AT&T, slip op. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).

Point 1 — distinct products vs. permutations
- What the claim actually recites is seven multiplicative multisets (S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, etc.). Mathematically, ordinary multiplication is commutative, so different orderings (S1×S1×S2 vs. S1×S2×S1) are the same numeric product. In principle one computed product can therefore satisfy more than one of the enumerated permutations so long as the multiplicities of S1/S2/S3 match.
- But the Federal Circuit reversed because the patentee failed to provide the necessary evidentiary mapping at trial. The court noted the Nokia document “only lists three distinct multiplications … and there is no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed.” The district court had assumed some of those three could count for multiple claimed items, but the Fed. Cir. rejected that result where no testimony or documentary evidence tied the accused computations to the seven claim entries. Id. (discussion at J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70).  
- Takeaway: you can rely on commutativity/account for permutations, but you must prove (to the jury) — product by product — that either (a) the accused device literally computes each claimed multiset, or (b) a computed term is mathematically identical to several claimed multisets and is therefore the functional equivalent. Absent that mapping, the proof is insufficient.

Point 2 — modulus (|x|) and conjugate (x′) vs. “same” signal
- The opinion treats |x| and x′ as different signal forms that cannot be conflated with the raw transmitter signals without proof. The court explained the Nokia document “does not disclose multiplying x1 and x2, but their complex conjugate and modulus, [so] Finesse’s mapping is flawed because it requires, for example, S3 to be mapped onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2′, and its modulus |x2|.” Id.
- Practically: you cannot simply assume |x| or x′ are the “same” S1/S2/S3 the claim requires. The claim language requires “copies of transmitter signals” and the patentee must show that the particular processed versions (conjugate, modulus) are within the scope of the claimed “copies” or otherwise are equivalent for claim purposes. If the accused implementation multiplies processed versions rather than the claimed signals (or their copies), the patentee must present evidence that those processed signals are equivalent to the claimed signals or that the claim covers them. Absent that proof the court will treat them as different signals and the mapping will fail.

Point 3 — proving a mapping from a list of computed terms (e.g., p(n)=a0…a9) to the seven claimed products
- The Federal Circuit’s error analysis makes the required proof clear: you must show, at trial, which specific computed terms correspond to each claimed multiplication. The court faulted Finesse because “nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications” and no mapping from the ten p(n) terms to the claimed seven was presented to the jury. Id.
- What that means in practice: at trial the patentee should produce concrete, non‑speculative evidence for each claimed product. Typical (sufficient) trial proof would be one or more of:
  - expert testimony that walks through the accused algorithm/code and shows that a particular computed term equals S1×S1×S2 (or is the same up to commutativity), supported by the actual code, block diagrams, internal docs, or execution traces;  
  - documentary evidence (or logs) showing the device computes the listed product(s) at runtime; or  
  - mathematical derivation showing how a polynomial term p(n)=ai is algebraically identical to a claimed multiset of S1/S2/S3 and evidence tying the ai to the transmitter signals claimed.  
  The key is a clear term‑by‑term mapping (or algebraic equivalence) presented to the jury — not a post hoc mapping on appeal.
- If processed signals (|x|, x′) appear, the proof must also explain why those processed versions are the same “copies of transmitter signals” the claim requires or how they are equivalent to the claimed S1/S2/S3 products.

Additional doctrinal note (claim construction/prosecution history)
- The court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily three different (unique) signals; amendment during prosecution did not amount to a clear disavowal that would read out two‑signal systems. That said, even if duplicates are allowed, the patentee still must demonstrate that the accused system’s actual computations cover the seven enumerated multiplicative combinations. Id. (J.A. 109–15, 110–11).

Bottom line checklist for proving infringement of the ’775 seven‑product limitation (and avoiding the Finesse result)
- Identify and tie S1/S2/S3 to concrete signal(s) in the accused device.  
- For each of the seven listed products, show a corresponding computed term OR show algebraic identity explaining why a computed term covers multiple recited permutations.  
- If the accused uses |x| or x′, show those are covered by the claim (i.e., are “copies” or equivalent).  
- Present the mapping and supporting documents/expert testimony to the jury (don’t reserve it for appellate briefing).  

The Federal Circuit reversed because that item‑by‑item, non‑speculative mapping was not made at trial; the Nokia documents only showed three distinct multiplication forms (and some ten polynomial terms) but no trial evidence tying those to the seven claimed multiplications. See Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).
