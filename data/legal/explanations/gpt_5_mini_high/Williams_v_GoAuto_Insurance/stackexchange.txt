Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does “certifying” under La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) require a human signature, or can a company certify via an unsigned, automated notice?
Question:
The panel emphasizes that the statute "makes no explicit reference to a signature" and concludes there’s "no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified." It distinguishes Benitez v. Elsayed, which flagged the lack of a signature but didn’t hold that alone was fatal. Under Louisiana law, what does “certify” in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) actually entail? Does certification imply a sworn/attested statement by a specific individual, or can a corporate entity effectively "certify" through an unsigned form email generated by its system?
Answer:
Short answer
No — under the Fifth Circuit’s reading of Louisiana law in Williams v. GoAuto, “certifying” in La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) does not itself import a requirement of a human signature or a named individual’s sworn attestation. A premium finance company may “certify” by sending an unsigned, automated notice so long as the notice actually contains the statutorily required certification statements and the insurer receives it.

Explanation (why)
- Statutory text and purpose: § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four specific items (power of attorney, default, that the ten‑day notice was sent with date, and notices to interested persons). Williams emphasizes the statute “makes no explicit reference to a signature,” and the statute refers to “the insurance premium finance company” as the actor who “certifies” the information. The court refused to read a signature requirement into that language. (Williams v. GoAuto Ins., No. 24‑30646, 5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025.)

- Precedent and limits: Louisiana courts demand “strict adherence” to the statute’s procedural requirements so that cancellation dates and cure periods are fixed (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten are cited in Williams). But the Fifth Circuit stressed that strict adherence to the statutory text is not a license for courts to add extra formalities (like a signature) that the legislature did not require. The court noted Benitez v. Elsayed had flagged the absence of a signature as a shortcoming, but that Benitez did not hold that lack of signature alone is fatal; Williams distinguished Benitez because, unlike Benitez, the APAC notices in Williams expressly “certif[ied]” the statutory items. (Williams, discussing Benitez.)

- The facts that mattered in Williams: APAC’s automated e‑mail expressly prefaced the four certification items with “APAC certifies that: … (1) … (2) … (3) … (4) …” and GoAuto’s systems received that e‑mail (its web service immediately cancelled the policy). The later physical application of a signature stamp by an employee occurred after the automated cancellation. The Fifth Circuit held that the automated, unsigned certification satisfied the statute and that electronic receipt by the insurer’s systems was sufficient “receipt.” (Williams.)

Practical bottom line
To satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) under Williams, the controlling considerations are (1) that the notice actually contain the four statutorily required certification statements and (2) that the insurer receive the notice (receipt can occur via an automated system). Formalized human signatures or naming a particular employee are not required by the statute as interpreted in Williams — though courts will still enforce strict compliance with the statutory content and timing requirements.

### What constitutes “receipt” by the insurer when cancellation notices arrive by email and are processed automatically?
Question:
Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) allows the insurer to cancel "[u]pon receipt of such notice" from the premium finance company. Here, APAC’s system emails the notice overnight, a GoAuto web service "listens" and auto-cancels before any human sees the notice, and only later does someone stamp documents. The court relies on Black’s definitions of "receipt/receive" and says the computer’s intake suffices. Is there Louisiana authority on whether server-level or automated-system intake qualifies as "receipt" or "physical possession"? At what point is an emailed cancellation request deemed "received" for purposes of this statute—upon arrival in the insurer’s mail server, successful API call, or human review?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Williams v. GoAuto (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), an insurer “receives” a premium-finance cancellation request when the insurer’s systems take custody of it — i.e., when the notice is accepted/processed by the insurer’s computer (whether by an e‑mail landing in the insurer’s system or by a successful API/web‑service call). The court held that automated, server‑level intake satisfies La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i); later human review or a stamped signature is not required.

Why (doctrine and the opinion)
- The statute allows cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice” from the finance company. The Louisiana Supreme Court had not defined “receipt,” so the Fifth Circuit looked to Black’s Law Dictionary and adopted definitions equating “receipt” with taking “physical possession,” delivery, or custody. Williams states: “We accept these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” (Williams v. GoAuto, No. 24‑30646, Oct. 3, 2025.)
- The facts the court relied on: APAC’s system automatically sends GoAuto an e‑mail (and APAC’s process can make a call to a GoAuto web service); GoAuto’s web service is a “listener” that, when called, performs the cancellation; no human is involved until later when documents are printed and stamped. The court concluded: “once the e‑mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system, that computer system cancels the policy” and that method “satisfies the statute.”
- The court refused to add extra requirements (e.g., a human signature or human receipt) not found in the statute. It emphasized that modern corporate computers do tasks formerly done by people and that the insurer’s automated acceptance counts as taking possession.

What Louisiana authority exists
- The Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined “receipt” in this statute. State appellate decisions cited in Williams were largely unhelpful on the server/automation issue (some discuss certification or signatures; Benitez noted lack of signature but did not hold signature fatal).
- Williams is the most direct appellate ruling on the exact fact pattern: a server/web‑service accepting an electronic cancellation request suffices as “receipt” under Louisiana law as interpreted by the Fifth Circuit. The Fifth Circuit declined to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

Practical takeaways
- For § 9:3550(G)(3): focus on whether the insurer’s system actually accepted/took custody of the incoming notice. If the notice reached the insurer’s mail system or successfully invoked the insurer’s web service/API such that the insurer’s system processed it, Williams treats that moment as “receipt.”
- A later human review, signature stamp, or placement in a shared cabinet is not required to satisfy the statutory “receipt” requirement under Williams — but note that state courts could reach a different view in the future.

Bottom line
Williams holds that “receipt” occurs when the insurer’s automated systems take possession (e‑mail arrival or successful API/web‑service call), not only upon subsequent human review or stamping. There is no Louisiana Supreme Court decision contradicting that view, and prior Louisiana appellate opinions did not resolve the server‑level question.

### Can an APAC employee acting as a courier qualify as a “private carrier” under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)?
Question:
The district court alternatively reasoned that the APAC employee who physically delivered/stamped notices "fits the definition of private carrier," analogizing to a federal case about railroads. The Fifth Circuit called that "imaginative and unconventional" and didn’t reach it. Under Louisiana law, does the statutory phrase "by depositing...with a private carrier" contemplate commercial carriers (e.g., FedEx/UPS) only, or can an agent/employee of the sender be a "private carrier"? Are there any Louisiana cases or statutory definitions on point for insurance-premium finance cancellations?
Answer:
Short answer
No—Williams v. GoAuto does not hold that an APAC employee who physically delivers/stamps notices qualifies as a “private carrier” under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The Fifth Circuit expressly declined to adopt the district court’s “private carrier” rationale (calling it “imaginative and unconventional”) and did not decide whether a sender’s agent/employee can satisfy the statutory phrase. The opinion also notes there is no controlling Louisiana authority that defines “private carrier” in this statute.

Why (brief explanation grounded in the opinion)
- The statute requires the premium finance company to send the insurer a cancellation notice “by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail.” Williams v. GoAuto Ins., No. 24‑30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025) (opinion).
- The Fifth Circuit resolved the case on other grounds: it held that APAC’s automated e‑mail to GoAuto’s system satisfied the statutory “receipt” requirement (relying on definitions of “receipt” and accepting electronic/computer possession as sufficient). Because the e‑mail satisfied the statute, the court “will not consider the validity” of the district court’s private‑carrier ruling. Id.
- The opinion records that the district court had concluded the APAC employee “fits the definition of private carrier” by analogy to a railroad decision, but the Fifth Circuit labeled that definition “imaginative and unconventional” and declined to adopt it. Id. The court further noted an absence of Louisiana case law interpreting “private carrier.” Id.
- The opinion also cites Louisiana appellate decisions emphasizing strict adherence to the cancellation statute (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten) but those authorities, as described in Williams, do not define “private carrier” for § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Williams cites other Louisiana appellate opinions addressing related certification/signature issues (KMJ Servs., Hodges, Benitez, Lewis) but indicates none resolve the private‑carrier question.

Net takeaway for your question
- Under current law as stated in Williams, the question whether a sender’s agent or employee acting as a courier can qualify as a “private carrier” under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) remains unsettled in Louisiana—Williams explicitly avoids deciding it and records that Louisiana courts have not defined the term in this context.
- Williams does make clear two things that are practically important: (1) Louisiana courts demand strict compliance with the statutory cancellation procedure, and (2) electronic transmission that results in the insurer’s possession/receipt (here, an APAC e‑mail received by GoAuto’s system) can satisfy the statute. Id.

If you need to press this issue in litigation, Williams is the controlling federal appellate statement: the Fifth Circuit declined to adopt the district court’s private‑carrier theory and declined to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

### How does Louisiana’s “strict adherence” doctrine apply—does § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv) require listing names/addresses of interested parties or is a general certification enough?
Question:
Strict adherence cases (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten) say any defect renders cancellation ineffective. The statute requires the finance company’s statement to specify "the names and addresses" of interested parties and the date notice was sent to the insured. The sample APAC language quoted here states only that copies were sent to "all persons shown" to have an interest and that proof of mailing is available, without listing names/addresses in the text. Did attachments to the email supply those specifics, and if so, is it enough that they are in attachments rather than in the body of the certification? Under the strict-adherence doctrine, do courts require literal inclusion of each enumerated item (names/addresses, date sent) in the certification itself?
Answer:
Short answer
Williams v. GoAuto holds that the statutory information required by La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv) need not be literally typed into the one‑paragraph “certification” itself so long as the certification and the materials actually sent to and received by the insurer, taken together, supply the statutorily required information. The Fifth Circuit accepted APAC’s e‑mail that certified copies were enclosed (and in fact transmitted copies) as satisfying the statute; it also refused to graft extra formalities (e.g., a signed, named certifier) onto the statute.

Why (doctrine and the opinion)
Louisiana courts demand “strict adherence” to the statute governing premium‑finance cancellations (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten), because the statute’s purpose is to fix the cancellation date and protect the insured’s cure period. The statute itself, quoted in Williams, requires a premium‑finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” several things, including that “Copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest … specifying the names and addresses …” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) (quoted in Williams).

In Williams the finance company’s electronic request said (as the opinion recites): the Ten Day Notice was sent, “Copies of the notices are enclosed and the affidavit proof of mailing is available upon request,” and “Copies … were sent to all persons shown by the Premium Finance Agreement to have an interest.” Those copies were in fact transmitted by e‑mail to GoAuto and the GoAuto system cancelled the policy on receipt. The Fifth Circuit held: (1) the statute does not require a handwritten or identified signature on the certification, and (2) electronic receipt by the insurer’s system satisfies the statute’s “receipt” requirement. The court rejected imposing additional formalities, saying “Strict adherence … is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.” On those facts the court affirmed that GoAuto’s procedures complied with § 9:3550.

What that means for your question
- The statute still requires the listed items (names/addresses, date sent, etc.). Williams does not dispense with those statutory requirements.  
- But Williams treats the certification and the accompanying materials that were actually sent and received as the operative “statement certifying.” In other words, the required specifics can be supplied by the materials enclosed/transmitted with the certification (attachments/copies/proof of mailing), not necessarily by verbatim recitation of each name/address inside the single‑paragraph certification.  
- Courts applying the strict‑adherence rule will insist the statutorily enumerated information actually be present and the insurer actually receive it; they will not, however, impose extra formalities (e.g., a signature or a requirement that every item appear within the one‑sentence certification) that the statute itself does not require.

Practical takeaway
To satisfy strict adherence post‑Williams, a finance company should send to the insurer a certification that (1) references and encloses the notices/proofs of mailing, (2) ensures the enclosed materials contain the names/addresses and the date(s) required by § 9:3550, and (3) document that the insurer actually received those transmitted materials. Williams shows attachments/enclosed copies that were sent and received will be treated as part of the certification package; but if the attachments lack the statutorily required details or are not actually received, the strict‑adherence doctrine can still defeat a cancellation.

### When should the Fifth Circuit certify unsettled questions of Louisiana insurance law to the state supreme court?
Question:
The panel declined to certify despite no Louisiana Supreme Court authority on the meaning of “certifying” or “receipt” here, citing Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n and warning against overuse of certification. What is the Fifth Circuit’s standard for certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court in Erie cases? Do novelty and statewide importance of insurance-cancellation procedures suffice, or must there be additional factors (e.g., conflicting intermediate appellate decisions, indeterminacy)? Did the court appropriately apply that standard in refusing certification on these statutory-interpretation issues?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit’s practice is cautious: it will certify to the Louisiana Supreme Court only for a “compelling reason.” Mere novelty or statewide importance of an unsettled statutory term is not enough. Williams v. GoAuto applies that rule: the panel refused certification because it believed the statute could be resolved with ordinary tools (text, existing appellate decisions, and dictionary guidance) and therefore no compelling reason to certify existed.

What the Williams opinion says about the standard
- The court quotes Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n for the governing posture: “As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Williams v. GoAuto, slip op. at §II (quoting Jefferson).
- The panel also reiterates that “That a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court ‘is not sufficient to warrant certification.’” Id.

How the court applied the standard in Williams
- The plaintiffs asked for certification over two statutory-interpretation questions (whether a signature is required on the premium-finance company’s “certifying” statement, and what counts as “receipt” by the insurer).
- Rather than certify, the panel (1) examined the statutory text and appellate precedent, (2) considered Black’s definitions for “receipt,” and (3) concluded it could confidently resolve the issues. It held that Louisiana law does not require a signed name on the certification and that electronic receipt/web‑service interaction satisfied “receipt.” Because the court could reach a firm answer using ordinary interpretive tools, it found no “compelling reason” to certify and therefore declined. Williams, §§I–II.

Do novelty and statewide importance suffice?
- No. Williams expressly rejects the idea that a lack of Louisiana Supreme Court precedent by itself warrants certification. The plaintiffs’ argument about the statewide importance of insurance-cancellation procedures did not overcome the Fifth Circuit’s reluctance to certify when the federal court can reach a reasoned prediction of state law. See Williams, §II.

When certification is likely appropriate (as implied by Williams)
- Williams does not supply a detailed checklist; it rests on the Jefferson principle of restraint. But the practical implication of the court’s analysis is clear: certification is appropriate when the federal court cannot confidently predict how the state supreme court would rule — for example, when the statutory text is genuinely ambiguous and ordinary tools (text, precedent, dictionary meaning, appellate guidance) leave no principled forecast, or where there are conflicting intermediate appellate decisions that make prediction speculative. By contrast, where the federal court can resolve the issue by applying statutory text and existing guidance (as the Williams panel did), certification is unnecessary.

Did the Williams panel apply the standard correctly?
- Yes, on the record in the opinion. The panel demonstrated the type of effort Jefferson contemplates: it tried to resolve the state-law questions by (a) parsing the statute, (b) reviewing relevant Louisiana appellate decisions, and (c) using recognized definitions to interpret “receipt.” Because those tools produced a clear answer for the claims before it, the court reasonably concluded there was no compelling reason to invoke certification and therefore properly declined to certify.

Bottom line for practitioners
- If you want the Fifth Circuit to certify a state-law question, you need to show more than novelty or statewide importance: demonstrate that the state high court’s resolution is genuinely unknowable from the text and existing authority (or that lower-court conflict makes prediction futile). Williams is an illustration of the Fifth Circuit declining to certify where it believed the issue could be decided confidently without asking the Louisiana Supreme Court.

### What standing or cause of action does a third-party accident victim have to challenge policy cancellation and allege insurer bad faith?
Question:
Plaintiff Jenkins was a third-party tort claimant (not an insured) but joined claims against GoAuto and APAC for misrepresentation and breach of good faith/fair dealing based on allegedly ineffective cancellations. The court affirmed dismissal after finding statutory compliance. Under Louisiana law (e.g., the Direct Action Statute, La. R.S. 22:1269, and La. R.S. 22:1973), do duties concerning cancellation notices and representations run to third-party claimants? If the cancellation was effective and coverage lapsed, can a third-party still pursue misrepresentation or bad-faith claims independent of the coverage determination?
Answer:
Short answer
- Williams does not hold that Louisiana’s Direct Action/extra‑contractual statutes (La. R.S. 22:1269 or La. R.S. 22:1973) create a free‑standing, insured‑style duty to all third‑party tort claimants. The Fifth Circuit decided this appeal on the premium‑finance cancellation statute (La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550) and on whether the cancellation was effective.  
- Practically, a third‑party claimant can join misrepresentation and bad‑faith claims challenging a cancellation (as Jenkins did), but Williams shows those claims will fail if the insurer/finance company strictly complied with the statutory cancellation procedures. Williams does not decide whether a third party can recover on independent misrepresentation or bad‑faith theories that do not depend on an ineffective cancellation.

What Williams says (and what it does not)
- The opinion treats Jenkins — a third‑party tort claimant — as a proper plaintiff who may assert misrepresentation and breach of good‑faith/fair‑dealing claims; the court affirmed dismissal, however, because it found the cancellation procedure satisfied La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550. (Williams v. GoAuto Ins., No. 24‑30646, 5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025.)  
- The Fifth Circuit’s analysis is tightly focused on § 9:3550 (the premium‑finance cancellation scheme). The court emphasized Louisiana’s requirement of “strict adherence” to that statute and reiterated that defects in the prescribed process can render a cancellation ineffective. It then concluded the finance company’s e‑mail/web‑service process satisfied the statute (no signature required; electronic “receipt” sufficed). The upshot: because the statutory procedures were met, the plaintiffs’ claims premised on an ineffective cancellation failed.
- The opinion quotes the statute’s narrowly circumscribed duty to notify “all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur thereunder” — i.e., the statute expressly protects persons identified by the finance agreement. Williams therefore recognizes that the statute creates a notification duty to that defined class, but it does not expand that duty to all third‑party tort claimants generally.

Implications for the two questions you asked
1) Do duties concerning cancellation notices and representations run to third‑party claimants under La. R.S. 22:1269 or La. R.S. 22:1973?  
- Williams does not answer that question. The court resolved the case under § 9:3550 and explicitly declined to certify other statutory questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. It does not interpret or apply La. R.S. 22:1269 or 22:1973, and it does not hold that those statutes create a general duty to third‑party tort claimants.

2) If the cancellation was effective and coverage lapsed, can a third‑party still pursue misrepresentation or bad‑faith claims independent of the coverage determination?  
- Williams tells you what happens to claims that are premised on an ineffective cancellation: they fail if the cancellation complied with § 9:3550. The court dismissed Jenkins’s misrepresentation/bad‑faith claims because the cancellation was lawful.  
- Williams does not hold that all bad‑faith or misrepresentation theories are foreclosed once coverage lapses; it simply does not reach theories that are independent of the cancellation/statutory compliance question. In other words, a third party might still attempt to pursue an independent tort (fraud, intentional misrepresentation, or other extra‑contractual conduct) that caused them loss and does not rest solely on the assertion that the policy was improperly cancelled — but Williams neither endorses nor rejects that pathway.

Takeaway for practitioners / law students
- If you represent a third‑party claimant challenging a cancellation, the critical front is statutory compliance with § 9:3550: show a defect in the finance company’s certification/notice/receipt process (because Louisiana treats such defects as rendering cancellations ineffective). Williams is insurer‑friendly only where the statute was properly followed.  
- If the insurer/finance company can show strict compliance (as GoAuto did), claims tied to an “ineffective cancellation” will likely be dismissed. To survive, a third‑party should either (a) identify a statutory noncompliance under § 9:3550 (or show the claimant was one of the persons entitled to specific notice under that statute), or (b) plead and prove an independent tort or extra‑contractual theory that does not depend on proving that the policy remained in force. Williams does not resolve the viability of claims under La. R.S. 22:1269 or 22:1973 to third parties; that remains a question for other precedent or the Louisiana courts.

### Does “receipt” by an affiliate or management company count as receipt by the insurer under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)?
Question:
Before 2017, printed requests were stored in a filing room belonging to GoAuto Management Services (an affiliate), not GoAuto. Post-2017, a document is taken to a GoAuto employee for a "received" stamp. If email receipt were ignored, would custody by an affiliate constitute the insurer’s "receipt" under the statute? What agency or corporate-law principles (actual/apparent authority, alter ego, common enterprise) would control whether an affiliate’s possession is imputed to the insurer for purposes of statutory "receipt"?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Williams v. GoAuto, “receipt” under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) means the insurer itself must take possession/delivery/custody of the cancellation request — and the Fifth Circuit held that GoAuto’s automated systems receiving APAC’s e‑mail satisfied that test. The court did not hold that mere physical custody by a separate affiliate automatically counts as insurer “receipt,” nor did it resolve or adopt any agency/alter‑ego theory to impute an affiliate’s possession to the insurer.

Why (the court’s reasoning)
- The Fifth Circuit looked to common definitions and concluded receipt requires the insurer to “take[] possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” The opinion expressly adopts Black’s Law Dictionary definitions and says, “GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.”
- Applying that standard to the record, the court emphasized that APAC’s e‑mail went to GoAuto’s systems, which triggered an automatic cancellation via a GoAuto web service (“a web service is like a listener … when it hears its name … it performs the activity”). Because GoAuto’s own system received and acted on the electronic notice, the statute was satisfied.
- The panel therefore affirmed summary judgment for GoAuto on the ground that the insurer had “receipt” in the statutory sense. The court explicitly refused to add requirements (e.g., a signer identified on the paper notice) and declined to adopt the district court’s alternative “private carrier” rationale because the e‑mail route made that inquiry unnecessary.

What Williams leaves open about affiliates and which doctrines would govern
- Williams does not hold that an affiliate’s mere custody equals insurer “receipt.” The opinion notes the pre‑2017 filing cabinet belonged to a different GoAuto affiliate, and that an APAC employee later stamped documents, but the Fifth Circuit did not rely on any theory of imputing the affiliate’s possession to GoAuto because GoAuto’s own systems had already received the notice.
- If the insurer’s systems never received the request and the only locus of possession is an affiliate, courts would ordinarily look to ordinary agency and corporate‑law principles to decide whether the affiliate’s possession is attributable to the insurer. Those are the doctrinal vehicles you would expect to see pressed and litigated:
  - Actual authority/agency: whether the insurer expressly authorized the affiliate to receive or accept cancellation notices on its behalf.
  - Apparent authority (agency by holding out): whether the insurer’s words or conduct caused third parties reasonably to believe the affiliate was authorized to receive notices for the insurer.
  - Alter ego / single enterprise / veil‑piercing: whether the corporate separateness should be disregarded because of such factors as unity of ownership/control, commingling of operations, or other indicia that the affiliate and insurer function as one enterprise.
  - (As the district court tried) characterization of the delivery mechanism (e.g., as a “private carrier”) might also be raised, but the Fifth Circuit did not adopt that theory.
- Williams does not analyze or decide these theories; it simply shows the court will treat electronic receipt by the insurer’s own systems as sufficient and will not infer additional formalities (like a handwritten signature) that the statute does not require.

Practical takeaway for litigation or pleadings
- Under Williams the strongest, simplest path is to show the insurer itself received the cancellation (logs, e‑mail/web‑service records, testimony that the insurer’s system processed the request). The Fifth Circuit accepted precisely that proof.
- If you cannot show insurer receipt and must rely on imputing an affiliate’s custody, you will need to plead and prove one of the agency/corporate doctrines above; Williams does not supply a precedent that imputes custody automatically and leaves that question open for further development on the facts.

In short: Williams treats “receipt” as possession by the insurer (including by automated systems) and upholds cancellations where the insurer’s own systems received and acted on the finance company’s e‑mail. It does not endorse a categorical rule that an affiliate’s mere custody equals insurer receipt; whether to impute that custody would turn on agency/alter‑ego/common‑enterprise proof, a question Williams avoided.

### If a signature were required, would Louisiana’s UETA or the federal E-SIGN Act make an electronic or stamped signature sufficient—and must it predate cancellation?
Question:
Although the court held no signature is required, APAC stamps a signature after the automated cancellation occurs. Louisiana has adopted the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (La. R.S. 9:2601 et seq.). If a signature were deemed necessary to "certify" under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), would an electronic signature or a post-hoc stamped signature satisfy that requirement? Does § 9:3550 demand that any required signature exist before the insurer may cancel, or could a later signature/ratification cure the timing issue under Louisiana law’s strict-adherence approach?
Answer:
Short answer
- Williams v. GoAuto holds that Louisiana’s premium‑finance cancellation statute does not require a hand‑signed, named signature on the finance company’s “certifying” statement, and that an insurer’s electronic receipt of the finance company’s notice is enough to trigger cancellation. Because the Fifth Circuit resolved the case on those grounds, it did not decide whether Louisiana’s UETA or the federal E‑SIGN Act would independently validate an electronic signature or a post‑hoc stamped signature.  
- The opinion makes clear that what must exist before cancellation is the insurer’s “receipt” of the notice; the court therefore treats the timing of receipt (not a later stamp) as dispositive. If, contrary to Williams, a signature were required by statute or controlling state authority, Williams’ strict‑adherence framework suggests that the required form would need to be present at the time the insurer has receipt — a post‑hoc stamp would be a risky cure under Louisiana’s strict‑adherence approach.

Why (grounded in the opinion)
- Strict‑adherence plus non‑expansion: The Fifth Circuit repeated Louisiana’s rule that courts must enforce the statute’s procedures strictly, but also may not add requirements that the statute does not contain. The court said: “Strict adherence to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.” (Op. ¶¶ discussing strict adherence.)
- No signature required: Confronting the plaintiffs’ argument, the court concluded “that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer,” and explicitly rejected the idea that “certifying” necessarily requires identifying or signing by a particular employee. (Op. ¶¶ discussing “certifying” and Benitez.)
- Electronic receipt is sufficient and controls timing: The court adopted the ordinary meaning of “receipt” and held the insurer may cancel once it “takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” It accepted that GoAuto’s computer receiving the emailed notice satisfied receipt and that the system’s automatic cancellation therefore occurred upon that receipt. The later application of a signature stamp by an APAC employee was after the cancellation and did not invalidate it because the statute was satisfied by the electronic transmission/receipt. (Op. ¶¶ on “receipt” and the computer/web service interaction.)

What the opinion does not decide
- Williams never analyzes Louisiana’s UETA or the federal E‑SIGN Act. The court resolved the dispute by construing § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) itself — concluding (1) the statute does not require a signature and (2) an electronic transmission to the insurer’s system satisfies the statute’s “receipt” requirement. It therefore does not hold whether, if the statute did require a signature, UETA/E‑SIGN would make an otherwise deficient paper or stamped signature sufficient.

Practical implication under Williams
- In the posture this court faced, an unsigned electronic certification that was actually received by the insurer’s system was enough; a later human signature stamp did not need to predate cancellation.  
- Conversely, because Louisiana law demands “strict adherence,” if a controlling Louisiana decision or statutory amendment ever required a contemporaneous signature as part of the certification, Williams’ logic points toward treating failure to have that signature at the time of insurer receipt as a fatal defect — a belated signature or ratification would be unlikely to be treated as a safe cure.

If you need a next step for research: look for Louisiana decisions applying UETA/E‑SIGN to insurance‑cancellation statutes or any Louisiana Supreme Court guidance on what “certifying” means under § 9:3550; Williams expressly declined to certify the question to the state high court, so state‑law development would be determinative.
