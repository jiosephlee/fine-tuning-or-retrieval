Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and roles
This case involves four principal actors. Plaintiffs Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright are former insureds of GoAuto Insurance Company. Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins is a tort claimant who was involved in an accident with a third GoAuto-insured driver (that driver is not a party here); Williams and Jenkins initially filed the underlying suit and Wright joined as an additional plaintiff by amended complaint. Defendant GoAuto is the insurer whose auto policies are at issue. American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (APAC) is the premium‑finance company that paid GoAuto the full premiums up front and then collected monthly installment payments from the insureds.

## Premium finance agreements and powers of attorney
The insureds’ relationships with APAC were governed by premium‑finance agreements: APAC paid GoAuto the entire premium and the insureds repaid APAC in installments. Each financing agreement expressly granted APAC a power of attorney authorizing APAC to cancel the insurance policy on the insured’s behalf if the insured defaulted on the financing. Louisiana law sets out detailed requirements for how a premium‑finance company must effect cancellation by exercising that power of attorney—most centrally, the finance company must send the insurer a “notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” specified information about the power of attorney, the default, the sending of a ten‑day notice to the insured, and copies sent to other interested parties. Those statutory certification requirements are the procedural hook for the dispute.

## Defaults, ten‑day cure notices, and resulting policy cancellations
When an insured failed to make a timely payment to APAC, APAC’s practice was to send the insured a ten‑day notice stating that the policy would be cancelled unless payment was made within ten days. If no payment arrived by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically generated and sent an e‑mail to GoAuto that attached a copy of the ten‑day notice and a request for cancellation on APAC letterhead. That request included a certification stating that (1) the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, (2) the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured, (3) a ten‑day notice of cancellation was sent to the insured (with copies enclosed and proof of mailing available), and (4) copies of the ten‑day notice were sent to any persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest in losses under the policy. GoAuto’s computer system was configured to cancel the policy upon receiving that e‑mail early in the morning; only later that morning did an APAC employee affix a signature stamp to printed cancellation documents. Before 2017 APAC employees placed those signed prints in a filing cabinet shared by GoAuto and APAC; since 2017 the signed document has been taken to a GoAuto employee who stamps it “received” and files it in the shared cabinet. The policies at issue were cancelled as a result of these nonpayments and the procedures just described.

## State‑court class action allegations
Williams and Jenkins filed a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC. Their operative complaint alleged that GoAuto had not effectively cancelled the financed policies and that the defendants’ conduct involved misrepresentations and breaches of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. Wright was added as a plaintiff in the amended pleading. The litigation thus challenged the legal effectiveness of the cancellations APAC had initiated under its power of attorney and alleged related statutory and common‑law injuries.

## Removal to the Middle District of Louisiana; cross‑motions for summary judgment
GoAuto removed the state‑court action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Once in federal court, the parties focused their dispute narrowly: they filed cross‑motions for summary judgment limited solely to whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures—APAC’s notices, the certification language, the timing of signature stamps, and GoAuto’s electronic cancellation processes—complied with Louisiana statutory requirements for cancellations effectuated by a premium‑finance company. The parties did not, at the summary‑judgment stage, litigate other factual or substantive aspects of the broader class claims.

## District court’s ruling granting GoAuto summary judgment and dismissing with prejudice
The district court granted GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment, concluding that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures satisfied Louisiana law. Because the court resolved the determinative statutory‑compliance issue in GoAuto’s favor, it dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. A dismissal with prejudice is final on the merits and bars refiling of those claims in state or federal court.

## Issues on appeal
The plaintiffs appealed, pressing three principal errors. First, they argued the district court erred in concluding the statutory procedures for delivery of the cancellation notice were followed—chiefly asserting that the statute’s requirement that APAC “certif[y]” certain facts implies a signature or similar attestation that must exist before cancellation. Second, they contended GoAuto did not properly “receive” the cancellation requests before cancelling the policies—so the automated cancellations based on APAC’s e‑mail did not satisfy the statute’s “upon receipt” language. Third, they argued the court should have granted their partial summary judgment. All three arguments turn on statutory interpretation and on the undisputed record about how APAC’s systems and GoAuto’s web service interacted and about the timing of printed signatures and filing.

## Appellate standard of review and scope
Because the district court’s disposition rested on cross‑motions for summary judgment, the appellate court reviewed the legal questions de novo—meaning it independently reassesses whether there are genuine disputes of material fact and whether the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Here, that de novo review is focused narrowly on whether, under Louisiana law and on the undisputed factual record developed in the district court, GoAuto’s cancellation procedures satisfied the statutory certification and receipt requirements that govern cancellations initiated by premium‑finance companies.

# Louisiana Framework for Cancelling Financed Insurance Policies

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is a useful, recent statement of how Louisiana’s statutory scheme for finance-company-initiated cancellations works and how courts apply the state’s “strict adherence” rule to that scheme. The Fifth Circuit’s analysis centers on two statutory duties: what the premium finance company must certify when it requests a cancellation, and what the insurer must do when it receives that request. The opinion also shows how modern electronic communications and automated insurer systems fit into the statutory framework. This chapter explains the statutory text interpreted in Williams, the established strict-adherence caselaw that shapes judicial review, and how the statute allocates roles and consequences among the finance company, insurer, and insured.

## Statutory authority: La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)

Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) a premium finance company may request cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default by sending to the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement “certifying” certain specified matters. The statutory mechanism permits the finance company—often armed with a power of attorney in the finance agreement—to effect cancellation on behalf of the insured by complying with the statutory notice-and-certification procedure. Williams revolves around the precise content and timing of that certification and whether the insurer actually “received” the finance company’s request before cancelling.

## Required finance-company statement certifying four items

The statute requires the premium finance company’s statement to certify four discrete points: (i) that the premium finance agreement contains the valid power of attorney required by the statute; (ii) that the premium finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; (iii) that, upon default, a notice of cancellation (the ten-day notice) was sent to the insured and the statement must specify the date it was sent; and (iv) that copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss under the policy, with specification of names and addresses for governmental agencies, lienholders, or third parties. The statute thus requires a discrete, written certification from the finance company that the prerequisites for exercising the power of attorney have been satisfied and that required notices have been sent to the insured and interested third parties.

## Permissible transmission methods: mail, private carrier, electronic mail

The statute explicitly contemplates several transmission methods: depositing the notice with the U.S. mail, delivering it via a private carrier, or sending it via electronic mail. Williams emphasizes that electronic mail is an authorized method. The case also records that lower courts have sometimes wrestled with the meaning of “private carrier,” and the district court in Williams advanced an unconventional reading (treating certain employee delivery as a “private carrier” act). The Fifth Circuit did not adopt or reject that district-court theory because the e-mail transmission alone satisfied the statute. The practical point from Williams is that the statute itself authorizes electronic transmission, and courts will look first to the statutory list rather than judicially add other required steps.

## Insurer’s authority upon receipt under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)

Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) provides that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” Williams highlights two interpretive questions: what constitutes “receipt,” and when must the certification be in the insurer’s possession relative to the effective cancellation. Faced with statutory silence on “receipt,” the Fifth Circuit consulted standard dictionary definitions and concluded that “receipt” means the act of receiving or coming into possession. Applying that understanding to modern information systems, the court held that an insurer’s automated web service that receives an e-mail request and immediately executes a cancellation satisfies the receipt requirement. The opinion illustrates that “receipt” can be electronic and instantaneous: the insurer need only take possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request (even by a computer system that then triggers cancellation), rather than await later, human handling of printed documents.

## Strict adherence requirement and consequences of defects

Louisiana appellate decisions require strict adherence to the statute when a premium finance company exercises a power of attorney to cancel a policy. Williams cites Delatte v. Lemotte for the proposition that courts enforce the statutory sequence strictly, and Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co. for the principle that any defect in the statutory process renders the cancellation ineffective. That line of cases reflects Louisiana courts’ protective stance: because cancellations remove coverage, the statutory steps must be followed precisely so that insureds are given the required notice and cure opportunity. Williams shows how strict adherence operates in practice: the court enforces the statute’s text strictly, but it will not judicially add requirements (for example, imposing a signature requirement where the statute is silent).

## Purpose of strict compliance: fixing the cancellation time and preserving the cure window

Britten v. Reavis, quoted in Williams, articulates the twin objectives that justify strict compliance: first, the statutory procedure fixes the exact date and time of cancellation so that coverage status is clear; second, the procedure guarantees a minimum cure period during which the insured can avert cancellation. Those functions explain why courts treat the statutory steps as mandatory: precise timing protects third parties and insurers from uncertainty, and the cure period protects insureds from losing coverage before reasonable opportunity to cure.

## How the statutory scheme allocates roles among finance company, insurer, and insured

The statutory scheme divides tasks and risks. The premium finance company must monitor payment, send the ten‑day cancellation notice to the insured (providing the required opportunity to cure), and then send the insurer a written certification that the power of attorney exists, the account is in default and uncured, the ten‑day notice was sent (with date), and copies were sent to interested parties. The insurer’s role is ministerial upon “receipt” of that certification: once it takes possession of the finance company’s notice, it may cancel the policy. The insured’s role is to receive the ten‑day notice and to cure the default within the statutory window if possible; failure to cure allows the finance company to pursue cancellation via its certified statement. Williams illustrates these allocations: APAC (the finance company) sent ten‑day notices and then an e‑mailed certification and copies to GoAuto; GoAuto’s systems received the e‑mail and canceled automatically; later human stamping of paper copies occurred, but the court treated those steps as subsequent ministerial acts, not prerequisites.

Conclusion

Read together, Williams and the Louisiana authorities it cites show a twofold lesson. First, the statutory content requirements are mandatory and require strict adherence because they fix cancellation timing and assure the cure opportunity. Second, strict adherence is satisfied by complying with the statute’s terms—not by adding judicial requirements not found in the text; under Williams that includes using electronic transmission and automated insurer systems to effect “receipt,” and it does not require a handwritten signature on the finance company’s certification where the statute does not demand one.

# APAC–GoAuto Cancellation Workflow and the Disputed Procedural Steps

This portion of the opinion walks through the exact sequence APAC and GoAuto used to cancel financed policies, then isolates the moments that gave rise to the Plaintiffs’ statutory arguments. Reading this section is an exercise in tracing time, transmission, and formalities: the statute prescribes what must be certified and how notice may be sent; the contested facts are whether those certifications actually occurred before cancellation and whether GoAuto “received” the cancellation request in the statutory sense. The court’s resolution turns on the concrete mechanics that occurred—automatic electronic transmission to a GoAuto system followed by later human stamping and filing—and on the court’s view of what the statute requires.

## APAC’s ten-day notice to insured after missed payment and opportunity to cure

The factual chain begins with APAC’s role as the premium finance company: when an insured misses a payment APAC sends the insured a “ten-day notice” that informs the insured they have ten days to cure the default. That ten‑day period is the cure window the statute and Louisiana precedent insist upon; the prior cases stress that the statutory procedures exist to fix a clear time and give a minimum opportunity to cure. In the case record, if the insured has not tendered payment by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s systems move to the next step in the cancellation workflow.

## Automatic email from APAC to GoAuto at the end of the cure period with copy of notice and certification language

At the end of the cure period APAC’s computer system automatically sends an electronic transmission to GoAuto: an e‑mail (and a back‑end call to a GoAuto web service) carrying a copy of the ten‑day notice plus a request for cancellation. The statute specifically contemplates delivery “by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail,” and the parties’ agreed facts show APAC used electronic means. On the record, that automatic transmission occurs overnight and is received by GoAuto’s systems in the early morning hours; the timing of that transmission is critical because it precedes any later human action on the printed documents.

## Certification content used by APAC: statements that the agreement has a valid power of attorney, is in default not cured, ten-day notice sent to insured, and notice sent to interested parties

The cancellation request that APAC transmitted included a four‑part certification matching the statutory checklist. On APAC letterhead it “certif[ied]” (1) that the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; (2) that the finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; (3) that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured (copies of the notices were enclosed and affidavit proof of mailing was available on request); and (4) that copies of the ten‑day notice were sent to any persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest in potential losses. These items track the statutory subparts the statute requires a finance company to certify to the insurer before the insurer may proceed with cancellation.

## GoAuto’s automated receipt and cancellation via a listening web service without human intervention at that moment

GoAuto’s system was configured to act immediately on the electronic request. Evidence on the record explained that APAC’s process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation,” and that the web service is “like a listener” that executes the cancellation when it receives and understands the request. Practically, that meant that upon receipt by GoAuto’s automated system the policy was canceled without any human reviewing or stamping the documents at that moment. The central legal question about the timing of “receipt” thus becomes whether receipt by GoAuto’s automated listener constitutes the insurer’s statutory “receipt” such that the insurer may consider cancellation requested and proceed.

## Later administrative steps: APAC employee applies signature stamp; physical document handling

After the automatic cancellation has occurred, APAC personnel perform later administrative steps on paper. Later that same morning an APAC employee printed the emailed materials and applied a signature stamp to the printed certification. That stamped paper, along with copies of the notices, was then placed into physical custody in the shared filing arrangement described below. The critical fact is temporal: the stamped, physical paperwork is created and handled after—rather than before—the electronic cancellation was effected.

## Pre-2017 shared filing cabinet; post-2017 hand-delivery to a GoAuto employee for received stamp and filing

How the paper was handled changed over time. Prior to 2017 APAC employees physically took the printed cancellation documents to a filing cabinet that was shared by GoAuto and APAC but belonged to GoAuto Management Services (a related entity). Since 2017 APAC’s practice was to hand the signed and printed documents to a GoAuto employee, who would apply a “received” stamp and place the papers in the shared filing cabinet. Plaintiffs relied on these post‑cancellation handling steps to argue that GoAuto did not meaningfully “receive” the cancellation request at the time its automated system canceled the policy.

## Plaintiffs’ challenges: absence of a contemporaneous signature; lack of identified APAC employee; claim that GoAuto never received the request within the meaning of the statute

Plaintiffs focused their statutory challenge on three connected complaints about the mechanics just described. First, they argued that the statutory term “certifying” requires a contemporaneous signature or an identified individual certifier before the insurer may cancel; because the only signature stamp was applied after the automatic cancellation and the notices did not identify a particular APAC employee, Plaintiffs said the required certification did not exist at the time of cancellation. Second, they pointed to Benitez and other cases noting shortcomings where notices lacked a signature or an identified preparer, arguing those features were fatal here. Third, Plaintiffs argued that the insurer never “received” the cancellation in the statutory sense before cancelling, because the physical stamp and delivery to GoAuto (or placement in a filing cabinet belonging to a different corporate entity) happened only later.

The court rejected these contentions for two related reasons grounded in the record and statutory text. On the signature issue the court observed the statute does not explicitly require a handwritten or printed employee signature and that Louisiana precedent has not imposed such a formality; the statute speaks of the “insurance premium finance company” certifying certain facts, and a company can do that by signed letterhead or other corporate statement without identifying an individual signer. On the “receipt” issue the court looked to ordinary dictionary definitions (“receipt” as taking physical possession; “receive” as coming into possession) and the undisputed operational evidence showing GoAuto’s systems took custody of the APAC transmission when the web service call/e‑mail was received and the system canceled the policy. The court emphasized that the statute contemplates electronic mail as a delivery method and that modern company computer systems perform reception and custodial acts that previously might have been done by people; accordingly the electronic transmission and the web service’s enactment of cancellation satisfied the statutory notion of receipt even though human stamping and filing took place later.

## District court’s alternative rationale referencing private carrier and Huntley analogy (not reached on appeal)

The district court had also provided an alternative ground for concluding the statutory delivery requirement was met: it treated the APAC employee who physically delivered the printed notices to the shared space as fitting the statutory category of a “private carrier,” relying on a Fifth Circuit analogy (Huntley) about when an entity counts as a private carrier. The Fifth Circuit described that alternative reasoning as “imaginative and unconventional” but declined to reach it because the court resolved the case on the electronic‑receipt theory. In short, the appellate court affirmed on the grounds that APAC’s e‑mail/web service transmission and GoAuto’s automated receipt satisfied the statute, so it did not endorse or reject the district court’s private‑carrier rationale.

Conclusion: tracing the workflow reveals the exact points Plaintiffs attacked—(1) lack of a contemporaneous, named signee; (2) post‑cancellation stamping and filing; and (3) argued absence of insurer “receipt.” The Fifth Circuit held that the statutorily required certifications were present in APAC’s corporate transmission and that GoAuto’s systems took receipt when the electronic request reached the insurer’s web service, so the automated cancellation complied with the statutory procedure. The lesson for reading opinions is to track both (a) the temporal order of communications and physical acts and (b) how the court interprets statutorily prescribed formalisms in light of the actual mechanics used by the parties.

# The Fifth Circuit’s Statutory Analysis and Holdings

## No signature requirement
The plaintiffs argued that the statute’s requirement that the premium finance company “certif[y]” certain facts (La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)) necessarily requires a handwritten or otherwise individual signature on the certification before an insurer may cancel. The Fifth Circuit rejected that argument. The panel read the statute’s text literally: it requires the insurance premium finance company to certify certain matters, but it does not mention a signature or identify any particular person who must sign. The court therefore concluded that a corporate statement of certification — here APAC’s electronic notice that recited the four required items — can satisfy the statutory “certifying” requirement even if no named individual actually signed the document before the insurer’s computer system effected cancellation.

## Comparison to Louisiana appellate cases
The court compared the statute to existing Louisiana appellate decisions and found those decisions unhelpful on the signature point. Several Louisiana courts have described the statutory requirement as a “statement certifying compliance” (KMJ Servs., Inc.; Hodges) or have noted other defects in cancellation notices (Benitez) without holding that the absence of a signature, standing alone, invalidated a cancellation. Benitez did list the lack of a signature among deficiencies in a particular notice, but it did not say a signature would have cured other problems or that signature is an independently dispositive requirement. The Fifth Circuit treated those precedents as consistent with the view that the statute demands a certification in substance — the statement of the specified facts — rather than a formalized handwritten or printed signature.

## Strict adherence does not authorize courts to add extra‑statutory prerequisites
Louisiana law requires “strict adherence” to the statutory cancellation process, because the statute’s procedural steps fix the cancellation time and guarantee a minimum cure period. But the Fifth Circuit emphasized that strict adherence limits courts to enforcing the statute as written; it does not authorize courts to add requirements the legislature did not adopt. The panel therefore refused to treat absence of a pre‑cancellation signature as a fatal defect where the certification language required by the statute was present and the notice was otherwise adequate.

## Meaning of “receipt”
Because the Louisiana Supreme Court had not defined the word “receipt” as used in the cancellation statute and the statute itself was silent on the point, the Fifth Circuit looked to dictionary definitions. It adopted Black’s Law Dictionary’s definitions — “receipt” as the act of receiving, especially by taking physical possession, and “receive” as to take something offered, given, or sent — as a sensible gloss on the statutory term. Practically, the court read “receipt” to require that the insurer take possession, delivery, or custody of the premium finance company’s notice and accompanying certification.

## Applying “receipt” to modern systems
The court then applied that definition to the facts: APAC’s system automatically e‑mailed GoAuto a copy of the ten‑day notice plus a certification request, and GoAuto’s web service (a continuously listening computer process) accepted the electronic submission and immediately executed cancellation. The Fifth Circuit treated electronic acceptance by a company’s computer system as the functional equivalent of taking possession. The panel observed that the statute is a modern one and that companies now use automated systems to perform tasks that humans previously performed; the law should accommodate that reality when the substance of “receipt” — coming into the insurer’s custody or control — is achieved electronically.

## GoAuto had “receipt” when its system accepted the email
Under this analysis, GoAuto had “receipt” of APAC’s notice at the moment its web service received the emailed certification, and the insurer’s automated cancellation that followed was therefore proper under the statute. The later affixing of a signature stamp to printed documents by an APAC employee (and a subsequent “received” stamp by a GoAuto employee after 2017) was immaterial to statutory receipt because the insurer had already taken custody through its electronic system. The plaintiffs’ contention that physical possession by a human was required before cancellation therefore failed.

## No need to reach the district court’s private‑carrier rationale
The district court had alternatively reasoned that an APAC employee who delivered printed notices counted as a “private carrier,” supplying another basis for cancellation. The Fifth Circuit declined to resolve that novel and fact‑bound question because it was unnecessary: electronic receipt by GoAuto’s systems already satisfied the statute. The panel expressly avoided endorsing the district court’s “imaginative and unconventional” private‑carrier analysis because the electronic‑receipt rationale disposed of the appeal.

## Consequences for the plaintiffs’ claims
Because the panel found that GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute’s certification and receipt requirements, the foundational factual premise for the plaintiffs’ claims — that the policies were not effectively cancelled under Louisiana law — vanished. Claims grounded on misrepresentations and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing that depended on statutory noncompliance therefore could not survive once cancellation was held effective.

## Disposition of the motions
The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to GoAuto and the denial of the plaintiffs’ partial summary judgment. The appeals court also declined the plaintiffs’ request to certify the statutory questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, explaining that mere absence of a state‑court ruling on a term is not a sufficient reason to invoke certification. For students, the takeaway is methodological: the panel relied first on the statute’s text, consulted available state precedent, used a dictionary definition where the state’s highest court had been silent, and adapted legal terms like “certify” and “receipt” to the realities of electronic communications rather than imposing extra‑textual formalities such as a required pre‑cancellation signature.

# Certification Request and Practical Significance

## Plaintiffs’ request to certify unresolved statutory-interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court

In Williams v. GoAuto the plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify two statutory-interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court: whether the statute’s requirement that the premium finance company “certif[y]” compliance requires a signature on the certification, and whether the insurer must actually take physical possession of a cancellation request before it may cancel a policy (i.e., what “receipt” means). The plaintiffs framed these questions as important to Louisiana’s insurance industry and contended that no definitive pronouncement from the Louisiana Supreme Court existed. The practical stakes were immediate: if the finance company’s procedures were legally deficient, the policy cancellations would be ineffective and the insureds’ claims could proceed.

## Governing approach to certification: Jefferson v. Lead Industries; novelty alone does not justify certification; reserved for compelling reasons

The Fifth Circuit refused the plaintiffs’ request under its conservative practice for state‑court certification summarized in Jefferson v. Lead Industries, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Jefferson instructs that federal courts should be “chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so,” because the availability of certification is a valuable resource that should not be squandered. Put simply, novelty or the mere absence of a state‑supreme‑court decision is not, by itself, a sufficient basis for certification. Certification is most appropriate when a controlling issue of state law is genuinely unsettled, the state court’s answer is outcome‑determinative, and there is no satisfactory state precedent to guide the federal court. The Fifth Circuit therefore treats certification as a narrow tool to be used only when necessary to avoid an intractable or outcome‑determinative question of state law.

## Denial of certification and decision to resolve by applying statutory text and existing Louisiana appellate guidance

Applying Jefferson, the court declined to seek the Louisiana Supreme Court’s guidance because it concluded the disputed terms could be resolved by the statute’s text and existing Louisiana appellate decisions. On the signature question, the court emphasized that the statute requires that “the insurance premium finance company” send a notice “together with a statement certifying” several listed items (La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv)), and that nothing in the statute expressly demands a signature. The panel reviewed Louisiana appellate decisions (including Benitez, KMJ Servs., Hodges, and others) and observed that although one appellate opinion criticized an unsigned notice, none established that a signature alone is a statutory prerequisite. The Fifth Circuit therefore concluded that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the certification that the finance company sends to the insurer.

On the “receipt” question the court likewise relied on ordinary dictionary definitions (Black’s) and the statute’s text. The Fifth Circuit accepted that “receipt” means taking possession or coming into custody, and it applied that meaning in a modern setting: an insurer’s computer system that receives an e‑mailed notice or a web‑service call takes possession for purposes of the statute. Because GoAuto’s system automatically canceled policies when its web service or mailbox received the APAC transmission, the court found the statutory receipt requirement satisfied. Because the statutory questions were resolvable on that basis, the court saw no compelling reason to certify.

The court declined to address the district court’s alternative (and more novel) ground — that the APAC employee who delivered paper copies qualified as a “private carrier” — because it did not need to reach that issue once it concluded electronic receipt and the content of the notices satisfied the statute.

## Operational takeaways: include all four statutory certifications in the notice; maintain proof-of-mailing records; preserve reliable logs of electronic transmittal and receipt; automated cancellations upon electronic receipt are permissible

The opinion yields several concrete operational lessons for premium finance companies and insurers who handle financed policies. First, the finance company’s cancellation notice should explicitly and unambiguously contain the four statutorily required certifications (valid power of attorney; existence and uncured status of the default; that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured, with the date specified; and that copies were sent to all persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest, with names and addresses where applicable). Louisiana courts require strict adherence to the statutory content; omitting or ambiguously phrasing any of these elements invites a challenge that can render the cancellation ineffective.

Second, keep contemporaneous proof‑of‑mailing and service records. Affidavits of mailing, certified‑mail receipts, return receipts, or equivalent proofs for notices to insureds and third parties materially strengthen a defense of statutory compliance. In the opinion the availability (or absence) of mailing proof was a focal point in earlier appellate cases cited by the panel.

Third, treat electronic transmittal and receipt as first‑class evidence and preserve technical logs. Because the Fifth Circuit accepted that an insurer’s IT system can “receive” a cancellation notice, parties should preserve e‑mail headers, SMTP logs, server timestamps, web‑service access logs, message IDs, and any application logs showing when a web service was called and when it acted. Maintain retention policies and defensible procedures for collecting and authenticating these records; produce them in discovery to allow the court to decide statutory compliance on the paper record.

Fourth, automated cancellations triggered by electronic receipt are permissible so long as the record shows the insurer’s system actually took possession of the notice before or at the point of cancellation. The court accepted GoAuto’s web‑service architecture — a listener that performs cancellation when it receives a call — as satisfying receipt. Practically, companies should ensure their automated flows leave a verifiable audit trail showing the sequence: transmission by the finance company → receipt by the insurer’s system → cancellation action.

Finally, although the Fifth Circuit held a signature is not required by the statute, including a signature (or an auditable electronic signature), a clear corporate attestation on letterhead, and contemporaneous affidavits can still reduce friction in litigation. Likewise, if paper delivery or human handoff remains part of a process (for example, shared filing rooms or employees physically transferring documents), create explicit chain‑of‑custody practices (stamps, acknowledged receipts) so that “receipt” cannot be contested as a matter of fact.

## Doctrinal takeaway: strict compliance is exacting but does not allow courts to add requirements absent from the statute; statutory terms like receipt encompass modern electronic workflows

Doctrinally, Williams v. GoAuto illustrates two complementary principles of Louisiana cancellation law. On one hand, Louisiana courts demand strict adherence to the statutory cancellation procedure because that strictness fixes the exact date and time of cancellation and protects an insured’s opportunity to cure. On the other hand, strict adherence does not license courts to read into the statute additional formalities that the legislature did not include. The Fifth Circuit refused to treat the absence of a named signatory as fatal where the statute speaks of the finance company’s certification and where appellate precedent did not impose a signature requirement. At the same time, the court acknowledged that the statute operates in a modern technological environment; statutory terms such as “receipt” must be interpreted to encompass electronic workflows when the insurer’s systems take possession automatically. The result is a doctrinal stance that is exacting about content and timing but realistic about the means by which those ends may be achieved.

## Litigation implications: with robust electronic records, statutory-compliance disputes are amenable to summary judgment

Practically for litigators, this opinion demonstrates that statutory‑compliance disputes of the sort raised here are often resolvable on summary judgment when the parties have an adequate electronic and documentary record. Where the content of the finance company’s notice is undisputed and server/e‑mail logs show transmission and insurer reception, the statutory questions become legal issues or uncontested facts that courts can decide without a trial — exactly what the Fifth Circuit did in affirming summary judgment for GoAuto. Conversely, when records are incomplete, ambiguous, or missing, plaintiffs can create genuine disputes of material fact (about whether a certification was sent, whether the insurer received it, or whether the cure period ran), making summary judgment inappropriate.

For counsel, the practical consequences are clear: if you represent a finance company or insurer, develop and preserve a clear, auditable record of notices, mailings, and electronic transmissions; if you represent plaintiffs, challenge the completeness and authenticity of those records in discovery and be prepared to show any gaps that would prevent a court from finding strict statutory compliance. The Williams panel’s refusal to certify the questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court means that, at least in the Fifth Circuit, careful documentary proof of compliance — including electronic logs — can be decisive.
