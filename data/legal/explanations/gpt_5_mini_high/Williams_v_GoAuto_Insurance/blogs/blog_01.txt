Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Certifying Without a Signature: The Fifth Circuit Clarifies Louisiana’s Premium-Finance Cancellation Protocol

## Why this case matters

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is a straight‑forward dispute with outsized consequences. Insurance cancellations tied to premium‑finance agreements are routine but high‑stakes: an invalid cancellation can mean a coverage gap at the moment of an accident, while an effective cancellation can deny an insured the opportunity to cure a default. Louisiana’s statute governing cancellations by premium‑finance companies (La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)) requires a finance company to “certify” several discrete facts to an insurer before a policy may be cancelled on that company’s initiative. The Fifth Circuit’s October 3, 2025 opinion answers two practical questions left open by Louisiana caselaw: does “certifying” require a named or handwritten signature, and what counts as the insurer’s “receipt” of the finance company’s certification in an automated environment? The court’s answers—no signature required; automated server‑to‑server communications can suffice as receipt—bring the statute into contemporary technology but leave litigable space for contesting the substance of a certification.

## What the Fifth Circuit held

Affirming the district court, the Fifth Circuit concluded that Louisiana law does not require a signature on a finance‑company “certification” and that GoAuto’s automated receipt of APAC’s e‑mailed cancellation request satisfied the statute’s “receipt” requirement. The panel relied on the statute’s text and Louisiana precedent’s emphasis on strict adherence, but it stressed that “strict adherence” means complying with the statute as written—not adding extra, judicially invented formalities such as a signature requirement. Because APAC’s automated e‑mail (and the API call to a GoAuto web service that immediately cancelled the policy) contained the statutorily required statements and GoAuto’s systems took possession of that request, the Fifth Circuit held the cancellation procedure complied with La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3).

## How the court reasoned — signature, certification, and precedent

A central strain of Louisiana authority requires “strict adherence” to the statutory cancellation process so that the exact time of cancellation is fixed and the insured has a minimum cure window. But the statute itself does not say a human signature is necessary; it speaks of the “insurance premium finance company” certifying four items (valid power of attorney; uncured default; that a ten‑day cancellation notice was sent to the insured specifying the date; and that copies were sent to interested parties). The Fifth Circuit refused to read an implicit signature requirement into that text.

The court surveyed Louisiana appellate decisions, including Benitez v. Elsayed — which had noted the absence of a signature among several defects — and concluded that Benitez did not hold that a signature alone would be dispositive. Because the notices here explicitly “certif[ied]” the required facts, the panel saw no textual basis for requiring individual attribution or a handwritten signature. In short, the company itself may “certify” through its processes and communications.

On “receipt,” the panel adopted a common‑sense, dictionary‑grounded approach: “receipt” means taking possession or custody, and that can occur when an insurer’s systems accept an incoming machine‑generated request. GoAuto’s web service acted as a listener that immediately performed the cancellation when called; the court held that electronic receipt by the insurer’s systems satisfied the statutory trigger for cancellation.

## Analytical perspective — what the decision preserves and what it leaves open

Williams is in many ways a pragmatic, textualist decision. It modernizes statutory application by treating corporate acts and machine communications as capable of performing the developer‑named functions of the law: fixing a definite cancellation time and preserving a cure period. At the same time, it does not gut the statute’s protective function. The court’s holding is narrow: Louisiana simply does not demand a handwritten or individually attributed signature in this statute, and an insurer’s automated acceptance of a cancellation request can constitute “receipt.”

That narrowness matters. The statute’s protective aim survives in two ways. First, the four statutory statements still must be made and, crucially, must be proven. The plaintiffs here lost on the form of the certification and the timing of receipt, not on the truth of the statutory statements themselves. An insured or third party can still defeat a cancellation by showing that APAC did not actually send the ten‑day notice to the insured, did not specify the date of sending, or failed to send copies to interested parties. Second, liberal acceptance of server‑to‑server communication depends on good recordkeeping: if a finance company or insurer cannot show the content and timestamps of the e‑mail/API call and the attachments (the ten‑day notice, proof of mailing, list of interested parties), a court may find the statutory steps unsatisfied.

The opinion also leaves unresolved issues for future cases. The district court’s alternative theory that some human delivery via a “private carrier” occurred was left untested by the Fifth Circuit because it resolved the case on the e‑mail/receipt ground. Nor did the panel consider whether other statutory contexts that use “certify” might require different formalities (for instance, statutory language that explicitly references a signature or notarization). Finally, the Fifth Circuit declined to certify the statutory interpretation question to the Louisiana Supreme Court, so the panel’s reading will bind courts in the Fifth Circuit absent contrary state law guidance.

## Doctrinal and downstream effects

Practically, Williams will be invoked in future litigation about premium‑finance cancellations in Louisiana federal courts and will be persuasive in state courts. It endorses a technology‑friendly reading of statutes that require “certification” and “receipt,” a posture courts elsewhere may find attractive as more business processes are automated.

At the doctrinal level the decision is notable for how it reconciles two principles that can feel in tension: strict adherence to procedural safeguards and reluctance to judicially create formalities absent statutory text. The Fifth Circuit’s approach will constrain litigants who ask courts to impose bright‑line, common‑law formalities (like signatures) not present in the statute, while preserving room for litigants to challenge the substance, truth, and delivery of the required statements.

Legislatures may respond. If Louisiana’s legislature (or regulators) wishes to impose explicit authentication or attribution rules—electronic signature requirements, validated digital certificates, prescribed metadata, or mandatory preservation standards—they remain free to do so. Absent that, courts will look to parties’ written records and system logs to determine compliance.

## Practical takeaways for insurers and premium‑finance companies

Williams is an invitation to operationalize defensibility. Firms that rely on automated workflows should assume courts will accept corporate‑level certifications and server‑to‑server communications so long as those communications are accompanied by contemporaneous, trustworthy records establishing (1) that the ten‑day notice was sent to the insured and specifying the date; (2) that the finance agreement was in default and uncured; (3) that copies were sent to all interested parties (with names and addresses); and (4) that the finance company had authority to certify (valid power of attorney). Preserve e‑mail headers and attachments, API call logs, web service request and response logs (with timestamps and IP addresses), proof‑of‑mailing affidavits, and any contemporaneous system prints or PDFs. Consider creating an express corporate certification block in the outgoing message that states the corporation’s authorization to certify and the process by which the corporate act is performed. Even where a signature is not required, a documented audit trail will often be dispositive.

For insureds and plaintiff lawyers, Williams shifts attention away from signature formality to the documentary truth of the statutory statements and the integrity of system logs. Challenges will need to focus on whether the ten‑day notice was actually sent, whether the copies required by the statute were dispatched, or whether the automated transmission ever reached the insurer’s system.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto is a modest but practically significant ruling: Louisiana’s premium‑finance cancellation statute does not demand a handwritten or individually attributed signature, and an insurer’s automated receipt of a finance company’s electronic notice may suffice to trigger cancellation, provided the statute’s substantive prerequisites are met and can be proven. The decision harmonizes Louisiana’s long‑standing “strict adherence” line with present‑day business practices, but it also places a premium on defensible, contemporaneous records. In the world of automated cancellations, the battle over coverage often will be won or lost in the logs.
