Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# The ‘Private Carrier’ Detour: Why Creative Analogies Can Misread Modern Insurance Statutes

## Why Williams v. GoAuto matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in Williams v. GoAuto provides a useful, pragmatic reading of Louisiana’s cancellation statute for financed insurance policies—and an implicit warning about stretching familiar phrases to fit awkward facts. At stake was a familiar Louisiana rule: when a premium-finance company exercises a power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy for nonpayment, the statutory delivery-and-certification regime must be followed precisely; failure means the cancellation is ineffective. That strict-compliance rule can turn tiny procedural disputes into coverage fights and bad-faith claims. The Fifth Circuit affirmed dismissal of the insureds’ claims because GoAuto’s automated e-mail/API process satisfied the statute, but it also flagged—and declined to adopt—the district court’s unconventional theory that an APAC employee who physically handed or stamped documents could be a statutory “private carrier.” The court’s treatment tells practitioners which routes are safest and where litigation is likely to land.

## What the Fifth Circuit actually held

The statute at issue authorizes a premium-finance company to request cancellation “by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail” a notice of cancellation together with a certification addressing, among other things, the existence of a valid power of attorney, the uncured default, and the date the insured was notified. Louisiana appellate precedent requires “strict adherence” to those requirements because the statute’s aims are twofold: fix the exact time and date of cancellation and ensure a minimum cure period. The plaintiffs argued two things: (1) APAC’s cancellation requests were not “certified” until someone physically stamped documents later in the morning, so the statute required a signature before cancellation; and (2) GoAuto never actually “received” the cancellation before its system cancelled the policy—so the statutorily required receipt never occurred.

The Fifth Circuit rejected both contentions. It concluded that the statute’s requirement that the premium finance company “certif[y]” certain facts does not, by its terms or existing Louisiana precedent, demand a handwritten signature or an identified employee’s name. The statute contemplates a certification by the “insurance premium finance company” itself; a corporate statement on letterhead or an explicit electronic statement can perform that function. And on “receipt,” the court accepted ordinary dictionary definitions (from Black’s) and concluded receipt can mean taking possession, delivery, or custody—concepts that a web service or e-mail server can satisfy. Because APAC’s system automatically sent an e-mail/API request that GoAuto’s listener/web service accepted and that action triggered the automated cancellation, the statutory requirement of insurer receipt was met. Because the e-mail/API route satisfied the statute, the Fifth Circuit did not need to validate the district court’s alternative rationale that a delivering APAC employee functioned as a “private carrier,” describing that theory as “imaginative and unconventional.”

## Why the “private carrier” detour is risky

The district court’s suggestion—that an APAC employee or an affiliated runner who deposited paper notices into a shared filing cabinet could fit the statutory phrase “private carrier”—is a tempting shortcut when the facts are messy, but it is legally and practically dangerous.

Textually, the statute groups “depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail.” Under familiar interpretive canons such as noscitur a sociis, a word’s meaning is informed by its companions. “Mail” invokes the U.S. Postal Service; placed next to it, “private carrier” naturally calls to mind private, paid delivery services that occupy the same functional niche—entities like UPS, FedEx, or other common carriers that handle receipts, tracking, and proof of delivery for third parties. Reading “private carrier” to include a sender’s own employee risks collapsing a distinction the statute is trying to preserve: when an independent carrier is used, the delivery itself produces an evidentiary record and an objective third-party signpost of when notice was transferred. That is precisely what Louisiana’s strict-compliance cases emphasize—the need to fix timing and afford a reliable cure window.

Functionally, treating an interested party’s courier as a “private carrier” undermines those statutory purposes. A finance company’s employee who drops notices in a shared cabinet, or stamps documents after a computer has already executed a cancellation, does not provide the neutral verification that the statutory scheme contemplates. It invites disputes over when possession actually changed hands and elevates formalistic battles over where paper was stored or whose filing room it was. The Williams court sensibly avoided that shoal by resolving the case on the cleaner, contemporaneous e-mail/API record.

Finally, the district-court analogy to prior caselaw that labeled a railroad a “private carrier” (an analogy the district court leaned on) is an awkward fit. A railroad’s status as a carrier in tort or shipping law reflects an established role moving third-party goods; it does not readily transpose to an affiliated employee delivering in-house paperwork. Labels that make sense in one doctrinal context can mislead when pressed into another.

## Doctrinal implications and likely downstream effects

Two doctrinal points emerge from Williams. First, courts will be receptive to modern, electronic means for satisfying statutory notice-and-certification requirements—so long as the electronic pathway produces reliable proof of sending and receipt. The Fifth Circuit explicitly acknowledged “modern” corporate practices and accepted server/API evidence of receipt. Second, the opinion narrows one obvious battleground but leaves another open. By rejecting a signature requirement, the court lowers a formalistic hurdle for finance companies, but because it declined to resolve the “private carrier” question (and refused to certify it to the Louisiana Supreme Court), that term remains a live issue in cases where parties rely on physical delivery or mixed processes.

Practically, Williams will push the industry toward the cleanest compliance mode: electronic notices with explicit, contemporaneous certifications and durable logs. When physical delivery is used, the ruling highlights the evidentiary peril of using affiliated delivery mechanisms: to avoid fights, finance companies and insurers should treat “private carrier” the way the statute implies—use independent carriers and secure independent proof of delivery, or else obtain contemporaneous, verifiable receipts from the insurer itself.

For litigants, Williams changes where disputes will focus. Plaintiffs who want to challenge cancellations will press on whether the certification language was actually present and whether electronic logs are complete and unaltered. Defendants will emphasize objective server logs, API call records, and unambiguous corporate certifications embedded in the transmission. And because Louisiana’s strict-compliance rule remains unforgiving, small recordkeeping failures will still generate significant coverage and bad-faith exposure.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto is an important signal that appellate courts will not demand archaic paper formalities when contemporary electronic processes produce clear proof of sending and receipt. At the same time, the case is a cautionary tale about the dangers of analogical reasoning that stretches statutory language to fit convenient facts. If counsel, companies, or regulators want fewer lawsuits over the mechanics of cancellation, the answer is simple: use the cleanest available means (email/API with express certification and robust logs), or when using paper, employ independent carriers and obtain unimpeachable proof of delivery. The statute’s purpose—fixing the time and preserving the cure window—will be best served when delivery and receipt can be proved without creative metaphors or contested memory of who put a paper in which cabinet.
