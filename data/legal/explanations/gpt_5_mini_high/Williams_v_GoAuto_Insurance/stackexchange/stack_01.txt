Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does “certifying” under La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) require a human signature, or can a company certify via an unsigned, automated notice?
Question:
The panel emphasizes that the statute "makes no explicit reference to a signature" and concludes there’s "no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified." It distinguishes Benitez v. Elsayed, which flagged the lack of a signature but didn’t hold that alone was fatal. Under Louisiana law, what does “certify” in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) actually entail? Does certification imply a sworn/attested statement by a specific individual, or can a corporate entity effectively "certify" through an unsigned form email generated by its system?
Answer:
Short answer
No — under the Fifth Circuit’s reading of Louisiana law in Williams v. GoAuto, “certifying” in La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) does not itself import a requirement of a human signature or a named individual’s sworn attestation. A premium finance company may “certify” by sending an unsigned, automated notice so long as the notice actually contains the statutorily required certification statements and the insurer receives it.

Explanation (why)
- Statutory text and purpose: § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four specific items (power of attorney, default, that the ten‑day notice was sent with date, and notices to interested persons). Williams emphasizes the statute “makes no explicit reference to a signature,” and the statute refers to “the insurance premium finance company” as the actor who “certifies” the information. The court refused to read a signature requirement into that language. (Williams v. GoAuto Ins., No. 24‑30646, 5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025.)

- Precedent and limits: Louisiana courts demand “strict adherence” to the statute’s procedural requirements so that cancellation dates and cure periods are fixed (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten are cited in Williams). But the Fifth Circuit stressed that strict adherence to the statutory text is not a license for courts to add extra formalities (like a signature) that the legislature did not require. The court noted Benitez v. Elsayed had flagged the absence of a signature as a shortcoming, but that Benitez did not hold that lack of signature alone is fatal; Williams distinguished Benitez because, unlike Benitez, the APAC notices in Williams expressly “certif[ied]” the statutory items. (Williams, discussing Benitez.)

- The facts that mattered in Williams: APAC’s automated e‑mail expressly prefaced the four certification items with “APAC certifies that: … (1) … (2) … (3) … (4) …” and GoAuto’s systems received that e‑mail (its web service immediately cancelled the policy). The later physical application of a signature stamp by an employee occurred after the automated cancellation. The Fifth Circuit held that the automated, unsigned certification satisfied the statute and that electronic receipt by the insurer’s systems was sufficient “receipt.” (Williams.)

Practical bottom line
To satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) under Williams, the controlling considerations are (1) that the notice actually contain the four statutorily required certification statements and (2) that the insurer receive the notice (receipt can occur via an automated system). Formalized human signatures or naming a particular employee are not required by the statute as interpreted in Williams — though courts will still enforce strict compliance with the statutory content and timing requirements.
