Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# An Unusual Stay: Appellate Court Stays Most Relief but Orders Reinstatement of Refugee Contracts

## Why this matters

Last week’s Ninth Circuit interim order in Pacito v. Trump is notable not just for what the court paused, but for what it preserved. The panel stayed most of a district court’s preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of President Trump’s January 2025 Executive Order that suspended refugee admissions (Exec. Order No. 14163). At the same time, however, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements that fund reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted to the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. That split—broad deference to the President’s entry suspension but an affirmative mandate to restore contracts benefiting those inside our borders—sharpens an important fault line: the difference between authority to exclude and the government’s statutory obligations to people who have already entered. The order is an emphatic example of an appellate court using stay powers to carve a narrow remedial path that both respects executive deference on national-security/immigration judgments and protects statutory programs and vulnerable beneficiaries while the merits are decided.

## What the panel did

The panel (with a partial concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee) vacated earlier motions-panel stays and issued a new stay pending appeal of the district court’s February 28 and March 24, 2025 injunctions. Applying the four-factor stay framework from Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), the court concluded the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the EO’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—relying on the deference articulated in Trump v. Hawaii—and that irreparable and public‑interest considerations support a stay of the district court’s relief blocking the entry suspension. At the same time, the court said the government is “not likely to prevail” on at least one of the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenges concerning the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements that funded reception and placement services required by § 1522. Given the government’s admitted uncertainty about having replacement providers in place and the statutory framework for reception-and-placement services, the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the § 1522 services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States—limited, the panel emphasized, to the usual and customary services those agreements supplied.

## How the panel justified this unusual split

Two legal currents run through the order. First, on the presidential authority side, the panel leaned heavily on Supreme Court precedent recognizing the special deference owed to the President’s findings about the national interest when it comes to entry and exclusion—most notably Trump v. Hawaii—reasoning that § 1182(f)’s text “exudes deference” and supplies a "single prerequisite" (the President’s finding) that the court will not second‑guess in a searching way on a stay motion.

Second, on the administrative‑law side, the panel identified a plausible APA claim tied to § 1522’s structure and the practical consequences of terminating cooperative agreements midstream. Section 1522 sets out the statutory architecture for domestic resettlement and provides for reception-and-placement services, repeatedly contemplates agency action that is tied to appropriations, and contains provisions—such as obligations regarding unaccompanied minors and development of alternative projects—that the court viewed as supporting plaintiffs’ claim that the executive branch cannot summarily halt the statutory scheme without adequate justification. The panel’s concern was practical as much as textual: the government told the court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” for a new resettlement agency, but at argument conceded uncertainty about whether any progress had been made. Faced with that uncertainty and a plausible APA claim, the panel tailored relief to preserve the statutory program for those already within the United States.

## An unusual remedial posture — stays that require affirmative, status‑preserving action

Appellate stays ordinarily suspend a lower‑court order; they are defensive. What makes the Ninth Circuit’s order striking is that, in staying the district court’s injunctions, the panel nevertheless required the government to take affirmative steps: reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to deliver § 1522 services to admitted refugees. That combination can be understood, however, as a classic equitable and status‑quo approach—the court fashioned a tailored condition on its stay to prevent the disruption of a statutory scheme and irreparable harm to beneficiaries while the merits proceed. In other words, the panel did not order the government to create a new program or to spend money beyond appropriations; it ordered the government to restore the operative contracts and deliver the “usual and customary” services that implemented the statutory scheme before the administration pulled the plug.

That sort of conditional or tailored stay is not without precedent in federal practice: courts routinely condition stays or injunctions to protect third‑party interests or to maintain the practical status quo. But it does sit at an uncomfortable intersection of judicial supervision and executive contracting discretion—and it is the sort of remedial posture that is likely to be contested further because it edges into decisions administrators typically make as part of policy realignment.

## Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent — the statutory text fight

Judge Lee’s partial dissent sharpens the doctrinal debate the court will have to resolve on the merits: is § 1522 an authorization that leaves the executive free to fund or not fund programs, or does it impose enforceable duties? Lee emphasizes the title of § 1522—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the repeated use of the phrase “is authorized,” reading the statutory scheme as permissive. He treats the use of “shall” in § 1522(a) as conditional—“in providing assistance under this section . . . the Director shall”—arguing that the “shall” only governs the manner in which programs must be run if funding is provided, not a freestanding command that compels the government to fund programs when it chooses not to. From that vantage, a full stay of the district court’s injunctions, including the requirement to reinstate agreements, would be appropriate.

The panel majority, by contrast, read some parts of § 1522 and related provisions as carrying mandatory obligations that the agencies cannot shrug off without reasoned administrative action. That textual battle—authorization versus mandate, conditioned by appropriations—will be central to the forthcoming merits opinion.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several doctrinal and practical consequences flow from this interim order.

First, on the law of presidential power and immigration, the Ninth Circuit’s approach underscores a robust distinction between entry authority and post‑entry statutory entitlements. Courts, in other words, may give broad deference to a President’s decision to stop admissions under § 1182(f) while scrutinizing agency steps that impair statutory programs for people already present in the United States.

Second, for administrative law, the order signals that agencies cannot rely on broad policy directives to summarily terminate programmatic infrastructure that implements a statute without confronting APA constraints and reliance interests. Even when an executive branch enjoys deference on a national‑security or foreign‑policy judgment, that deference does not automatically immunize simultaneous curtailments of programs that Congress has created to serve beneficiaries within the country.

Third, for contracting and grant practice, the decision should sharpen agency caution. Terminating cooperative agreements that implement statutory programs during a policy sweep invites quick litigation and—if the court finds a plausible statutory right—can lead to judicially ordered reinstatement. Agencies that want to change course will need to document reasoned decisionmaking, account for reliance interests, and plan transitional steps for beneficiaries.

Fourth, for appropriations law and separation‑of‑powers concerns, the order raises tricky questions. The Ninth Circuit limited its directive to reinstating agreements necessary to provide services “to the extent of available appropriations,” and the opinion notes the FY 2025 appropriations and partial rescission. But any eventual judicial command that effectively compels expenditure of funds that Congress has rescinded or withheld would strain separation‑of‑powers principles and likely precipitate high‑stakes litigation.

Finally, appellate management itself may take cues from this decision. The panel’s surgical remedy shows how an appellate court can thread the needle between protecting executive prerogatives and preserving statutory programs for vulnerable groups—by tailoring a stay that both defers to presidential exclusion authority and requires the maintenance of services necessary for those already inside the country.

## What to watch next

The Ninth Circuit has promised a full opinion on the merits. That forthcoming decision will need to resolve the core statutory question Judge Lee framed: whether § 1522 imposes mandatory duties enforceable against the executive, and how “to the extent of available appropriations” affects those duties. How the court treats the government’s procedural and substantive justification for terminating cooperative agreements also will matter: will the panel require a searching administrative‑record explanation or conclude the termination was arbitrary and capricious?

If the Ninth Circuit reaffirms the interim order, expect the government to weigh immediate further emergency relief, possibly to the Supreme Court, on either separation‑of‑powers or stay‑condition grounds. Either way, agencies and NGOs should take note: abrupt contract terminations that accompany broad policy shifts can be reversed quickly in the courts, at least where statutory frameworks and beneficiaries’ reliance interests are involved.

In short, Pacito’s interim order is a compact lesson in judicial triage—respecting the broad headwind of presidential entry power while using equitable tools to preserve statutory programs that serve people who are already on U.S. soil. The full merits opinion, whenever it issues, will tell us whether that balance endures.
