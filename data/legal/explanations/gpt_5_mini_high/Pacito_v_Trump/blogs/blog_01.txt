Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Hawaii, Again: Ninth Circuit Leans on § 1182(f) to Sustain Refugee Entry Suspension Pending Appeal

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump is a short, consequential reprise of the doctrinal posture the Supreme Court established in Trump v. Hawaii: when the President invokes 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend entry, courts give the executive very broad deference and will not undertake a searching inquiry into the President’s findings. That posture matters because it shapes where future challenges to refugee policy can realistically succeed. This stay preserves the President’s suspension of refugee admissions while carving out protection for resettlement services for refugees already physically in the United States — a split result that both fortifies presidential control over who may enter and preserves a path for statutory- and APA-based relief for those inside the country.

## What the Ninth Circuit did

On September 12 the Ninth Circuit vacated its earlier administrative stays and issued a stay pending appeal of the district court’s preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of large parts of Executive Order 14163 (the Trump administration’s suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program) and had ordered the reinstatement of State Department cooperative agreements for refugee processing and domestic reception-and-placement services. The panel concluded that the government is likely to prevail on its challenge to the legality of the EO’s suspension of entries under § 1182(f) and that the balance of Nken factors favors a stay. At the same time, the court said the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA-based claim arising from 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and therefore directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part, arguing that § 1522 is best read as an authorization rather than a mandatory funding duty.

The panel emphasized that a full merits opinion will follow; the order functions as an interlocutory balancing act that preserves the suspension of admissions pending appeal while protecting certain domestic statutory interests for refugees already admitted.

## How the panel applied Nken and leaned on Hawaii

The court applied the familiar four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder, but — consistent with Nken — treated the first two factors (likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury) as "the most critical." On the likelihood-of-success axis the panel leaned heavily on the Supreme Court’s treatment of § 1182(f) in Trump v. Hawaii, quoting that the statute “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power to impose entry restrictions,” and reiterating that the “sole prerequisite” in § 1182(f) is that the President have “find[ed]” the entry would be detrimental. In language lifted from Hawaii the panel said it “cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications,’” and concluded the President had “undoubtedly fulfilled” the § 1182(f) finding here.

The court then folded the other Nken factors into a traditional separation-of-powers and national-security posture: disrupting a presidential determination about who may enter implicates foreign policy and border control interests and counseled in favor of a stay. Those considerations, plus the panel’s view that the government has made a strong showing on § 1182(f), carried the day as to the suspension-of-entry claim.

## The statutory tug-of-war: § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act (§ 1522)

Pacito illustrates a common pattern: a broad presidential-entry statute (§ 1182(f)) colliding with a more detailed statutory regime established by Congress for refugee admissions and post-arrival services (the Refugee Act, implemented in part through 8 U.S.C. § 1522). The panel’s stay does not yet resolve that conflict on the merits, but it signals how a court guided by Hawaii will treat arguments that the Refugee Act cabin presidential power.

The panel stopped short of holding that § 1182(f) “trumps” the Refugee Act; instead, it concluded at the stay stage that the government is likely to prevail on its § 1182(f) defense to the challenge to the suspension of entries. That approach is doctrinally defensible under Hawaii: if § 1182(f)’s textual command — the President may “suspend the entry of . . . any class of aliens” — is read at face value and coupled with substantial deference to the President’s findings, a court will be reluctant to second-guess an entry suspension even in the face of a congressionally created admissions program.

But Pacito also demonstrates the limits of that deference. The panel recognized that the statutory framework governing reception and placement services — § 1522 — potentially imposes obligations on the executive that are subject to APA review. Thus even where the President’s proclamation may withstand direct challenge under § 1182(f), the administration cannot necessarily terminate the domestic machinery for resettlement without running into independent statutory and APA constraints.

## The Lee concurrence/dissent: is § 1522 an authorization or a mandate?

Judge Lee’s partial dissent crystallizes the text-based counterargument that will matter on the merits: § 1522’s caption reads “Authorization for programs,” many subsections use the verb “is authorized,” and several provisions are subject to the qualifier “to the extent of available appropriations.” From that vantage, § 1522 looks more like a permissive framework that lets the executive create and fund reception-and-placement programs when appropriations allow, rather than a hard command that the government must keep every past cooperative agreement in place regardless of policy choices.

The majority’s interim remedy — directing reinstatement of the cooperative agreements needed to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted — reflects a different reading. It treats parts of § 1522 as carrying binding obligations (or at least as creating reviewable expectations under the APA) when the government abruptly withdraws services that Congress anticipated as part of the resettlement regime. The textual indicators Judge Lee invokes (titles, “authorized,” and “to the extent of appropriations”) are real interpretive hooks, but they compete with subsections that use “shall” and mandate certain programmatic elements if funding is provided. The merits opinion the panel promised will need to reconcile those competing signals; the immediate effect, however, is pragmatic: the court will protect services for those already on U.S. soil while allowing the suspension of new admissions to stand for now.

## Doctrinal throughline from Hawaii — and what Pacito leaves open

Pacito is, in many respects, “Hawaii, again.” The central doctrinal lesson of the Supreme Court’s travel-ban decision — that § 1182(f) channels courts toward highly deferential review of presidential findings about entry — is the engine driving the panel’s stay. That throughline matters for litigants and policymakers. If Hawaii’s approach is applied routinely, courts will be reluctant to enjoin suspensions of particular classes of entrants so long as the President can plausibly make the statutorily required “finding.”

But Pacito also shows the contours of judicial pushback. Courts remain willing to police the executive’s domestic statutory obligations under the APA and statutory law. Plaintiffs who cannot prevail on a direct challenge to an entry suspension may still obtain meaningful relief by pressing statutory claims tied to resettlement services, funding, and procedures that govern people already present in the United States. In short, there is a two-track universe: (1) entry policy challenges face an uphill climb because of § 1182(f) and associated deference; (2) statutory and administrative-law claims about the government’s internal obligations and duties — particularly those affecting individuals already physically present — are more promising.

Finally, Pacito keeps open the possibility of a high-stakes merits decision. The panel’s stay tells us which way the wind is blowing, but the full opinion to come will have to address the hard question the stay order skirts: how, if at all, does a broad presidential entry power coexist with a detailed congressional scheme for refugee admission and post-arrival services? That question could, depending on the panel’s eventual reasoning, present a vehicle for further appellate review — and perhaps more Supreme Court attention.

## Downstream consequences

Practically speaking, the order buys the government the policy outcome it sought for the near term: refugee admissions remain suspended while the appeals court considers the merits. Operationally it also forces a partial course correction: the administration must reconstitute at least those cooperative agreements necessary to deliver reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. That will relieve some immediate humanitarian and programmatic strain on nonprofits and local resettlement networks, but it leaves thousands of conditioned approvals or applicants outside the United States in limbo.

For litigators, Pacito suggests a strategic pivot: if Hawaii-era precedent dooms direct statutory attacks on entry suspensions, counsel should focus on APA and statutory duty claims connected to services, benefits, and the administration of programs for people already in the U.S. For Congress, the case is a reminder that ambiguous statutory drafting invites executive latitude that can be decisive in periods of political change; legislators who want to cabin presidential authority over admissions will need clearer statutory language or appropriations constraints.

## Bottom line

Pacito is a forceful — if interim — application of the deference doctrine the Supreme Court articulated in Trump v. Hawaii: when the President invokes § 1182(f), courts will be reluctant to probe the substance of the President’s finding that entry would be “detrimental.” But the Ninth Circuit also made plain that deference to a proclamation does not give the executive a free pass to dismantle statutory programs that Congress established for people already admitted to the United States. That bifurcation is the practical and doctrinal takeaway: broad presidential authority at the border, paired with meaningful judicial policing of domestic statutory promises. The panel’s forthcoming full opinion will be the place to watch for how durable that balancing act proves to be.
