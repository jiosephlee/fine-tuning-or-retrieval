Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# APA as Backstop: Agency Defunding Falters Even as the President’s Entry Bar Stands

## Why this matters

The Ninth Circuit’s short interim order in Pacito v. Trump crystallizes an important doctrinal split: courts will treat presidential control over who may enter the country with unusually high deference, but they will not allow executive branch maneuvering — through procurement or funding changes — to evade statutory obligations that protect people already in the United States. Practically, that means a president can plausibly suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and courts will be reluctant to second‑guess that judgment, but agencies remain subject to the Administrative Procedure Act when they cut off services that Congress has authorized and funded for refugees already admitted.

The panel’s action is also an object lesson for administrative lawyers. An executive order may change broad policy; it does not, by itself, erase binding statutory duties or immunize implementing agencies from APA review. Where Congress has appropriated money and paired it with programmatic duties, abrupt terminations of cooperative agreements and a failure to ensure continuity of services invite judicial intervention.

## What the Ninth did

On an expedited record, the Ninth Circuit vacated its earlier, broader administrative stays and issued a stay pending appeal of the district court’s February and March preliminary injunctions — except for the district court’s order that the government reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The panel applied the four Nken stay factors, emphasizing that the first two (likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm) are the most critical.

The panel concluded the government was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f), relying on the strong deference articulated in Trump v. Hawaii: the statute requires only a presidential “find[ing]” that entry would be “detrimental,” and the court will not engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. But the court also signaled that the government was unlikely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA challenges concerning the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements and the Department of State’s suspension of USRAP funding. For that reason the panel preserved, as an interim matter, the district court’s order directing reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to carry out the reception and placement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522 for refugees already admitted.

## How the court framed the split

The order draws a clear doctrinal line. Under § 1182(f), the President’s authority to suspend classes of entrants is textually capacious and has been read to “exude[] deference” to the President. That part of the case is essentially over, at least for stay purposes: the panel found the Government has “undoubtedly fulfilled” the statute’s prerequisite “finding,” and the stay factors about irreparable harm and public interest favored the Government on that question.

By contrast, the panel treated the agencies’ termination of cooperative agreements — a procurement and funding decision — as squarely reviewable under the APA. The court anchored that review in the Refugee Resettlement statutes, particularly § 1522, which the panel read as imposing substantive reception‑and‑placement duties on federal agencies (subject, of course, to appropriations). The panel noted Congress had appropriated substantial funds for migration and refugee assistance for FY2025 (the opinion cites $3.178 billion, reduced by an $800 million rescission), and it emphasized that the government had terminated basically all cooperative agreements even while representing to the district court that it was “actively preparing” replacement arrangements but at oral argument admitting uncertainty as to any progress. Against that background, the panel found a serious risk that the agencies’ actions could be “contrary to law” or arbitrary and capricious under the APA, and it preserved relief that would ensure the statutorily described services continue for refugees already in the country.

## Judge Lee’s partial dissent — the counterreading

Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part. He agreed with the majority’s treatment of presidential power under § 1182(f) — he would have stayed the injunctions that interfere with the President’s moratorium on admissions — but he read § 1522 differently. Lee views § 1522 as an authorization rather than an unconditional command: the subchapter’s title (“Authorization for programs …”) and repeated statutory phrasing that officials “are authorized” to provide programs, together with the qualification “to the extent of available appropriations,” point toward discretionary executive programs, not mandatory duties that courts should enforce. From that vantage, he would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in full because he thinks the law does not require the executive branch to operate the programs the plaintiffs identify.

Lee’s textualist argument is familiar and forceful: “authorization” language and appropriations qualifiers ordinarily signal congressional choice, not judicially enforceable obligations. The majority, however, took the presence of several “shall” provisions, reporting requirements, an established procurement practice of cooperative agreements, and evidence of available appropriations as sufficient to create a serious legal claim that the government crossed statutory bounds or failed to engage in reasoned decisionmaking.

## The analytic takeaway: APA remains the backstop

The Ninth’s interim order illustrates — in concrete, operational terms — how the APA functions as a backstop even where the President exercises broad, statutorily granted immigration powers. Two linked points matter for practitioners:

First, presidential control of entry is a separate question from agency compliance with statutory duties to beneficiaries already in the United States. Trump‑style deference to the President under § 1182(f) largely forecloses a court’s willingness to pry into the motives behind a suspension of admissions; it does not, by itself, absolve agencies of statutory responsibilities or license them to jettison longstanding cooperative arrangements without reasoned analysis.

Second, agencies must provide a reasoned record when they terminate programs or contracts that carry out statutory duties, and they must reckon with appropriations and reliance interests. The panel flagged precisely those facts: Congress had appropriated funds, the statute contains programmatic directives and reporting duties, and the government terminated cooperative arrangements while offering only vague assurances that replacements were being procured. That combination is a classic set of facts that invites APA challenges claiming action “not in accordance with law” or “arbitrary and capricious.”

## Practical counsel for agency lawyers and counsel for plaintiffs

For agency counsel: when executing a change in policy that depends on EOs, do not treat procurement or funding actions as ancillary decisions immune from APA scrutiny. Document the legal basis for any termination or suspension, explain how statutory duties will be met for current beneficiaries, and show a concrete transition plan if services will cease. If appropriations are genuinely insufficient, explain that allocation decision in the administrative record; if funds exist, be prepared to show why reallocation is permissible and reasonable.

For plaintiffs and public‑interest counsel: bringing APA claims focused on statutory duties and contractor reliance can be an effective vehicle to protect beneficiaries on the ground even where challenges to entry suspensions are unlikely to succeed. The Ninth’s order is an encouraging signal that courts will look beyond headline EOs to the nuts and bolts of how agencies implement policy.

## Broader doctrinal and downstream effects

Pacito demonstrates a durable division in modern administrative and immigration law: courts will continue to grant the political branches wide latitude to set who may enter, but they will police how agencies treat statutory programs and currently entitled beneficiaries. That split may shape future litigation strategies: challengers who cannot overcome the high bar to impugn presidential findings under § 1182(f) may instead press APA claims aimed at agency implementation, procurement, and funding decisions.

The order also presses on a perennial question about the legal force of “to the extent of available appropriations.” Agencies can defend programmatic decisions on the ground of limited budgetary resources, but where Congress has made substantial appropriations and the record shows abrupt termination with no plausible transition plan, courts will scrutinize whether the agency has exceeded its authority or acted arbitrarily.

Finally, the panel’s narrow remedial choice — preserving relief only as to refugees already admitted — reflects a feature of equitable relief in APA cases: courts will often limit remedies to protect settled expectations and current beneficiaries while staying other parts of lower‑court relief that implicate broader policy judgments.

## Bottom line

Pacito’s interim order is a useful reminder of the two distinct checks on executive power that live within the federal courts: extraordinary deference to presidential findings on who may enter, and robust APA review of how agencies allocate resources and carry out statutory programs for people already here. An executive order can change admission policy; it cannot, without more, delete statutory duties or immunize agencies from the “hard look” required by the APA. The full opinion to follow will matter greatly, but for now administrative lawyers should take away the plain lesson: EOs may steer policy, but the APA remains the backstop for those the law already protects.
