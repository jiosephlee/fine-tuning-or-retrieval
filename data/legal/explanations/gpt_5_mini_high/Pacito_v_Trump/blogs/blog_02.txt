Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# When ‘Authorized’ Becomes ‘Obligated’: The Ninth Circuit Reads § 1522 as Requiring Post‑Admission Refugee Services

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s short, interim order in Pacito v. Trump crystallizes a sharply practical question at the intersection of immigration, administrative law, and appropriations: when the President lawfully pauses refugee admissions, does the executive retain discretion to cut off programs Congress wrote into law for refugees already inside the United States? The panel’s answer — at least for now — is: no. The court stayed most of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal but also expressly concluded that, under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government must continue to provide “reception and placement” services to refugees already admitted and ordered the reinstatement of the cooperative agreements that historically delivered those services.

That split result matters because it draws a bright line between two types of executive authority. On one hand, the court reaffirmed the well-established deference that Congress and courts afford presidential decisions to restrict entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), invoking Trump v. Hawaii and concluding the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the suspension of admissions. On the other hand, the panel made clear that deference to the President’s immigration prerogatives does not automatically immunize the executive from complying with statutory obligations enacted by Congress for people who are already in the country. The ruling thus preserves a route for judicial enforcement of statutory programs even when those programs collide with an Administration’s broader immigration policy.

## How the court got there — the short version of the order

The procedural posture is unusual and urgent. After President Trump issued an executive order (No. 14163) suspending refugee admissions and the State Department suspended USRAP funding and terminated cooperative agreements, refugees and resettlement organizations sued in the Western District of Washington. The district court entered preliminary injunctions; the government appealed and sought stays.

Applying the familiar Nken stay factors, the Ninth Circuit panel concluded that the government was likely to prevail on the merits of the plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of admissions under § 1182(f), because that statute “exudes deference” to the President and the only textual prerequisite is the President’s finding that entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” But the panel also concluded — and this is the pivot of the order — that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge based on 8 U.S.C. § 1522: the statute governing domestic resettlement services.

Noting the operative language of § 1522 (including subsection (a)(1)(A)’s direction that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement “shall” make available certain services “to the extent of available appropriations”), subsection (d)(2)(B)’s directives about unaccompanied minors, subsection (e)(7)(A)’s “shall develop and implement” language about early cash and medical support, the presence of implementing regulations, and recent appropriations for migration and refugee assistance, the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the usual reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The government’s concession that it lacked a concrete plan or progress toward replacing service providers seemed to clinch the point for the court.

Judge Lee concurred in part but dissented in part: he would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in their entirety, viewing § 1522 as an authorization rather than a mandate — a statutory grant of permissive authority, in his view, rather than a judicially enforceable command.

## The textual fight: ‘authorized’ vs. ‘shall,’ and the role of appropriations

At the heart of the disagreement is a basic statutory‑interpretation dispute that crops up in many contexts: which words control — the section title and repeated phrases like “is authorized to,” or the operative provisions that use “shall” and impose specific duties (even if qualified “to the extent of available appropriations”)?

Judge Lee presses the conventional intuition that a title reading like “Authorization for programs…” and widespread use of “is authorized” signal congressional non‑mandate. He reads the “shall” provisions in subsection (a) as imposing conditions on the way the executive must run the program if it elects to run the program — not as an independent command to run it in the absence of appropriations or other policy judgments. He points to qualifying language in subsections (d) and (e) — “if necessary,” “as needed” — as further indication of executive discretion.

The majority (in the panel order) emphasized the plain operative text in (a), the reporting requirement in § 1523, the long history of cooperative agreements implementing USRAP, and — importantly — the recent congressional appropriations for migration and refugee assistance. That combination supports a straightforward reading: Congress set up an ongoing statutory program and, insofar as funds are available, placed affirmative duties on the responsible officials to provide certain services once refugees are admitted. The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” is a limiting clause, not an eraser; it makes the obligations contingent on funds but does not convert an otherwise mandatory “shall” into a mere suggestion where appropriations exist.

Both readings are textually defensible. The court’s interim remedy leans on two practical anchors: statutory commands framed in mandatory terms and concrete evidence that Congress has provided money for the program. Taken together those elements make the government’s position — that it may unilaterally terminate cooperative agreements and leave admitted refugees without federally mandated services — a tougher sell.

## Doctrinal and practical consequences

The Ninth Circuit’s approach (as previewed in the order) draws an important doctrinal distinction. Decisions to admit or deny entry under § 1182(f) remain a domain in which courts defer heavily to the President. But once people are admitted, the executive’s discretion to undo statutory programs that Congress has established for those people is significantly narrower. That has three visible consequences.

First, it gives litigants a structural route to challenge administrative defunding or contractor terminations where statutes impose specific duties and Congress has appropriated money. Second, it imposes practical constraints on administrators who hope to effect broad policy changes by terminating contracts or cooperative agreements: there is real risk that courts will treat those terminations as unlawful if they frustrate statutorily mandated services. Third, the decision could ripple beyond refugee law: where Congress uses “to the extent of appropriations” language, courts will likely parse whether appropriations exist and thus whether a statutory mandate is in force.

This interim order is only the opening salvo. The government will press its reading on the merits; defenders of the district court’s injunction will press the contrary reading. The issues — text, structure, appropriations, and separation of powers — are classic candidates for further en banc or Supreme Court review, particularly given the national stakes and the tension between widely deferential immigration doctrines and ordinary principles of statutory enforcement.

## Bottom line

For now, the Ninth Circuit has split the difference: the Administration may pursue its suspension of admissions under § 1182(f), but it may not, at least preliminarily, abandon the statutory resettlement services Congress ordered for refugees already in the United States. The panel’s order is an important reminder that deference in immigration entry decisions does not give the executive carte blanche to ignore statutes that create rights or programs for people once they are within U.S. borders. The full merits opinion to come will be worth reading closely for how the panel resolves the textual tensions that underlie this practical and high‑stakes dispute.
