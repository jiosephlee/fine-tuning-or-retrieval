Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Case Background and Procedural Timeline

This chapter summarizes the factual and procedural history that produced the Ninth Circuit’s interlocutory stay order in Pacito v. Trump. The litigation pits two executive actions—the President’s suspension of refugee admissions and the State Department’s suspension of refugee resettlement funding—against statutory duties and the preliminary relief the district court granted. The Ninth Circuit’s order of September 12, 2025, vacated earlier interim orders, stayed most of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal, and directed limited reinstatement of cooperative agreements to serve refugees already admitted to the United States. The following sections set out the executive actions, the plaintiffs, the district-court injunctions, the government’s immediate operational responses, and the stay history on appeal that culminated in the panel’s interim order.

## Executive Order 14163 (Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program): suspension of refugee entry under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f), 1185(a)

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” The Order invoked 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (and § 1185(a)) and directed that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [USRAP] be suspended” pending further findings. Section 1182(f) authorizes the President, upon finding that the entry of any aliens or any class of aliens would be detrimental to the United States, to suspend the entry of those aliens or impose restrictions on entry. In its interlocutory order the Ninth Circuit relied on Trump v. Hawaii to describe § 1182(f) as a source of “broad” presidential discretion and as setting a limited prerequisite—essentially the President’s “finding” that entry would be detrimental—which courts have treated with substantial deference.

## State Department’s suspension of USRAP funding pursuant to Executive Order 14169 (Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid)

Four days after the Presidential order, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), citing a separate executive action—Executive Order No. 14169—addressing foreign assistance. That suspension specifically included domestic resettlement funding for refugees already admitted to the United States. The funding suspension is the operative factual trigger for the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenge and for the district court’s later injunction requiring funding-related relief.

## Plaintiffs: refugees (admitted and approved but abroad) and three nonprofit organizations holding cooperative agreements for overseas processing and domestic resettlement services

The plaintiffs are two categories of private individuals and three organizational plaintiffs. The individual plaintiffs include refugees who already had been admitted to the United States and refugees who had been conditionally approved for resettlement but remained abroad. The three organizational plaintiffs are nonprofit resettlement organizations that historically held cooperative agreements with the State Department and had performed overseas processing and domestic “reception and placement” services under USRAP. The plaintiffs sued in the Western District of Washington in February 2025, alleging the entry suspension violated the Refugee Act of 1980 (as incorporated into the INA) and that the funding suspensions and terminations violated the Administrative Procedure Act.

## District court preliminary injunctions: Feb. 25 oral order enjoining EO 14163 §§ 3(a)-(c), 4; Feb. 28 written order clarifying scope and barring funding suspension; Mar. 24 order requiring reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements

The district court moved quickly. On February 25 it orally enjoined enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The government then terminated cooperative agreements the next day. On February 28 the district court issued a written order defining the scope of its February 25 injunction and explicitly prohibiting suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24 the district court entered a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements that it had terminated after the February 25 ruling. The government appealed these preliminary injunctions and sought emergency relief in the appellate court.

## Government’s post-injunction termination of all reception-and-placement cooperative agreements and nearly all overseas processing agreements; representation of an RFP for new providers

Responding to the district court’s initial injunction, the government terminated all cooperative agreements for domestic reception-and-placement services on February 26 and terminated all but one cooperative agreement supporting overseas processing. In March the government informed the district court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” (RFP) to select a new resettlement agency or agencies that could provide reception-and-placement services; however, at the Ninth Circuit oral argument the government acknowledged uncertainty about whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement providers. That operational uncertainty played into the appellate court’s interim decision about what relief to preserve while the appeal proceeds.

## Ninth Circuit motions-panel stays: Mar. 25 partial stay with carveout for individuals conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025; Apr. 21 and May 9 clarifications; July 14 district court enforcement framework; July 17 emergency motion; July 18 administrative stay; Sept. 3 expedited argument

The government sought a stay of the district court’s orders pending appeal. A Ninth Circuit motions panel granted a partial stay on March 25, 2025: relying on Trump v. Hawaii, it emphasized the deference § 1182(f) affords the President and granted relief to the government except for a narrow “carveout.” The motions panel left the district court’s injunction operative with respect to individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. The panel later issued clarifications of that carveout on April 21 and May 9. The district court, seeking to implement the carveout, adopted (on July 14) an enforcement framework and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee it. The government filed an emergency motion (July 17) asking the Ninth Circuit to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay; the court entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order on July 18 and set an expedited oral argument, which the panel heard on September 3.

## Current panel action: vacatur of prior stays (Mar. 25, Apr. 21, May 9; and July 18 administrative stay), stay of the district court’s Feb. 28 and Mar. 24 preliminary injunctions except as specified, and denial of the July 17 emergency motion as moot

After full briefing and argument the Ninth Circuit panel submitted the case for decision and issued the September 12, 2025 interim order. The court vacated its prior administrative and motions-panel stay orders (the March 25 stay and its April 21 and May 9 amendments, and the July 18 administrative stay) and then issued a new stay pending appeal: it stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety except for one directed obligation. Applying the four-factor stay standard from Nken v. Holder (likelihood of success, irreparable harm, balance of harms, and public interest), the panel concluded the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f) (following the deference articulated in Trump v. Hawaii), and that the other stay factors favor the government on that issue. But the court also concluded that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims: the panel read 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as creating statutory duties respecting “reception and placement” services for refugees after admission and noted that services can be delivered through cooperative agreements. Given the government’s termination of agreements and uncertainty about replacement providers, the panel “directed” the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States; the order specifies that those services should include the usual and customary services afforded under prior agreements. The panel denied the government’s July 17 emergency motion as moot. The court indicated a full opinion explaining its reasoning will follow, but the stay order governs while the appeal proceeds.

This sequence illustrates the appellate court’s balancing act: substantial deference to executive authority over entry under § 1182(f) while preserving, at least for admitted refugees, statutory protections the court deemed likely enforceable under the APA and § 1522. The case remains interlocutory and expedited; a full Ninth Circuit opinion will elaborate the legal analysis behind these interim rulings.

# Governing Law: Entry Power, Refugee Resettlement Statutes, and the APA

This chapter explains the legal framework the Ninth Circuit applied in its expedited order in Pacito v. Trump. The panel confronted two blocs of law that pull in different directions. On one side is the President’s statutory authority to suspend or restrict the entry of aliens (invoked here through Executive Order No. 14163 and cited statutory bases 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)), a field in which the Supreme Court in Trump v. Hawaii has instructed courts to give great deference. On the other side is the statutory and regulatory scheme Congress created in the Refugee Act (principally 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and implementing regulations) establishing federal obligations for “reception and placement” services for refugees admitted to the United States. The Administrative Procedure Act (APA) supplies the judicial review mechanism for agency decisions to terminate funding and cooperative agreements under that statutory scheme. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order applied these authorities to stay most of the district court’s injunctions while preserving—because of likely APA and statutory claims—the obligation to provide domestic resettlement services for refugees already admitted.

## Presidential entry authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and § 1185(a); deference principles from Trump v. Hawaii

8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is the statutory provision the panel relied on most heavily. It authorizes the President, “whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens or to impose restrictions on entry. In Pacito the panel treated § 1182(f) as the core textual source for the President’s suspension of the refugee admissions program and echoed the Supreme Court’s description in Trump v. Hawaii that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and “vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions.” Under that line of authority, a court’s review of an executive suspension under § 1182(f) is tightly circumscribed: the court asks essentially whether the President has made the predicate “finding” and whether the action stays within the statute’s textual limits; courts do not undertake a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s policy or national‑security justifications.

Section 1185(a) was cited as an additional statutory authority invoked by the executive order. It is another provision in the immigration statutes that relates to presidential and federal control over the admission and arrival processes for noncitizens. In Pacito the panel relied on the combination of these statutory authorities and the deferential Supreme Court precedent to conclude that the government was likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ challenge to the authority to suspend refugee admissions. That conclusion drove the court’s decision to stay the district court’s injunction to the extent it sought to enjoin implementation of the admission suspension.

For students: when you read executive‑power cases in the immigration context, identify the statutory grant the executive invokes, then check whether controlling precedent calls for heightened deference. In Trump v. Hawaii the Supreme Court treated § 1182(f) as a broad grant of discretion and limited judicial inquiry accordingly; the Pacito panel applied that same approach in assessing the stay request.

## Refugee Act of 1980 domestic resettlement framework: 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and § 1523

The Refugee Act, enacted in 1980, established a domestic resettlement framework whereby the federal government provides reception and placement services to refugees after admission. The key statutory text for the Pacito dispute is 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which sets out programmatic goals and particular services Congress contemplated the federal government would provide to newly admitted refugees. Section 1522 is paired with § 1523, which imposes reporting and accountability obligations on the Secretary to Congress.

Section 1522 frames federal responsibility in programmatic terms: it specifies objectives (economic self‑sufficiency, language acquisition, non‑discouraging cash assistance, equal opportunities for women) and authorizes the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) to provide certain projects, grants, contracts, and services. The implementing regulations (45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.) translate that statute into grant and cooperative‑agreement programs and describe the federal funding mechanisms and typical services delivered under those programs.

In Pacito the panel focused on § 1522 because the government had terminated the cooperative agreements through which federal reception and placement services had historically been delivered. The organizations that had received cooperative agreements and the refugees who had already been admitted claimed the terminations violated § 1522 and the APA. The Ninth Circuit concluded that, although the government was likely to prevail on the lawfulness of the President’s suspension of future admissions under § 1182(f), the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA challenge to the termination of funding for services to refugees who had already been admitted. That division of results arises from the different purposes and text of the two statutory regimes: one grants the President broad, deferential power to deny or restrict entry; the other sets out a statutory program for services to admitted refugees that the court treated as potentially mandatory and reviewable.

## Key statutory directives: § 1522(a)(1)(A) “shall” obligations (subject to available appropriations); § 1522(d)(2)(B) responsibilities for unaccompanied minors; § 1522(e)(7)(A) interim support and services for refugees <36 months in the U.S.

A close read of § 1522 is central to understanding the legal dispute. Subsection (a)(1)(A) appears in the statute under the heading “Conditions and consideration” and uses the verb “shall.” The provision, “to the extent of available appropriations,” directs the Director of ORR to make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement, to provide English language training, to ensure cash assistance is available in a way that does not discourage economic self‑sufficiency, and to ensure that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instruction. Those categorical statements were the basis for the plaintiffs’ argument that Congress imposed concrete obligations on the executive to provide reception and placement services to admitted refugees.

Subsection (d)(2)(B) concerns unaccompanied refugee children and directs that “[d]uring any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Subsection (e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty‑six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” Two features matter here: statutory obligations are often framed with mandatory language (“shall”), but Congress tied many of those phrases to the availability of appropriations or to conditional phrases like “if necessary” and “as needed.”

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order treated the unqualified “shall” language in § 1522, together with the fact that Congress appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025, as sufficient to support a likely APA claim that the government could not simply terminate cooperative agreements and thereby frustrate the statutory scheme for admitted refugees. Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasized the opposite interpretive reading: he pointed to the statutory title (“Authorization for programs…”) and repeated use of “is authorized” elsewhere in § 1522 to argue the scheme is permissive—that Congress authorized programs but did not command the executive to operate them. That split highlights the interpretive question courts face when “shall” and permissive “authorized” language appear in the same statutory scheme and when appropriations language is included.

For students: when statutes combine “shall” with appropriations qualifiers or titles that include the word “authorization,” courts may legitimately disagree about whether Congress imposed a mandatory programmatic duty or merely authorized discretionary programs. The consequence is practical: if § 1522 imposes mandatory duties, courts can order the executive to perform them (subject to appropriations); if it merely authorizes, courts are less likely to compel further government action.

## Implementing regulations: 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (funding and services for refugees)

The Refugee Act’s statutory language is implemented by regulations administered principally by the Department of Health and Human Services and the Office of Refugee Resettlement. The panel referenced 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. as the regulatory framework that describes how federal funding is made available, the eligible activities and recipients, and the modalities (grants, contracts, cooperative agreements) through which reception and placement services are provided. Regulatory schemes serve two roles in litigation like Pacito: they operationalize statutory obligations (so that failing to follow them can show a statutory violation), and they create expectations and reliance interests for organizations that have historically performed refugee resettlement functions under cooperative agreements. Under the APA, a decision that is inconsistent with an agency’s own regulations or that fails to account for regulatory responsibilities can be set aside as arbitrary and capricious.

## Mechanisms to deliver services: contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofits under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)

Section 1522 expressly contemplates that reception and placement services may be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. Historically, the federal government has used cooperative agreements with domestic resettlement organizations to provide the initial reception and placement services that newly admitted refugees rely upon. A cooperative agreement is a funding instrument like a grant but typically contemplates substantial federal involvement in programmatic execution; grants are generally less agency‑directed; contracts are procurement instruments for procuring goods or services. In Pacito the government terminated cooperative agreements in February 2025; the Ninth Circuit expressly noted that history and the sudden termination when coupled with the government’s admitted uncertainty about securing replacement providers was central to the court’s conclusion that plaintiffs were likely to prevail on their APA challenge to the terminations for admitted refugees. The court therefore directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide the services required by § 1522 to refugees who had already been admitted.

## Appropriations context: FY 2025 migration and refugee assistance funding and partial rescission; “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier

Statutory directives to create programs are often qualified by appropriations language: “to the extent of available appropriations.” The Pacito order explicitly noted Congress’ FY 2025 appropriation for migration and refugee assistance—$3.178 billion—and the subsequent $800 million reduction enacted in the Rescissions Act of 2025. That factual and statutory context matters for two reasons. First, whether Congress has appropriated funds for the program influences whether a statutory “shall” can meaningfully impose an obligation the executive can be ordered to perform. Second, appropriations constraints limit what relief a court can grant: the APA cannot be used to force expenditure of funds beyond what Congress has appropriated. The panel’s citation to the FY 2025 figures supported its view that money for refugee assistance existed in the fiscal year and that the executive’s termination of cooperative agreements could not be justified simply by saying there would be no funding for the statutorily required reception and placement services. Judge Lee countered that even with appropriations in place, § 1522 is best read as an authorization scheme giving the executive discretion in deciding whether and how to operate programs. The split shows how appropriations facts enter statutory interpretation and equitable relief decisions.

## APA framework as relevant: review of agency terminations of funding and cooperative agreements for consistency with statute and reasoned decisionmaking

The Administrative Procedure Act provides the vehicle for reviewing agency actions like the termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of funding. Under the APA, a reviewing court may set aside agency action that is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law. The core elements of arbitrary‑and‑capricious review require an agency to examine relevant data, articulate a satisfactory explanation for its action, and consider alternatives and reliance interests when appropriate. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim—namely, that the termination of cooperative agreements (and the suspension of funding for reception and placement services) was inconsistent with the obligations imposed by § 1522 and/or was arbitrary and capricious given the government’s inability to show that replacement arrangements were in place.

Two APA issues arise repeatedly in such cases. First, is the termination a reviewable “final agency action”? Termination of cooperative agreements and withdrawal of funding typically is final and thus reviewable. Second, if reviewable, is the action “contrary to statute” because it frustrates a statutory duty? If § 1522 imposes mandatory duties and appropriations exist, termination without reasoned analysis or without lawful predicate risks being arbitrary and capricious and contrary to law. Conversely, if § 1522 is interpreted as merely authorizing programs and leaves the existence of programs to executive choice, then the termination may be lawful as an exercise of discretion not generally subject to judicial compulsion.

The Pacito interim order applied these principles to preserve the district court’s remedial reach over admitted refugees: it stayed the district court’s injunctions that would prevent the President from suspending entry, but it declined to stay the portion of relief that would require the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for refugees already in the United States. The panel reasoned that the plaintiffs were likely to succeed on their APA claim that the terminations were unlawful and that practical and humanitarian concerns (and statutory text) supported court‑ordered reinstatement pending final appellate resolution.

For students: when an agency alters long‑standing funding arrangements or terminates cooperative agreements, the APA inquiry focuses on whether the agency gave a reasoned explanation, considered reliance interests and alternatives, and acted consistently with statutory duties—and whether appropriations permit judicially ordered relief. The Pacito panel’s interim decision is a concise example of how courts balance deferential treatment of immigration and national‑security decisionmaking with ordinary APA review of domestic program administration affecting persons already within the United States.

Concluding note: Pacito illustrates a recurring tension in administrative and immigration law. The President’s broad, deferential statutory power to regulate entry under § 1182(f) can coexist uneasily with statutory programs that impose duties toward persons already admitted. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order applied deference to the entry suspension while preserving judicial oversight (via the APA and the substantive terms of the Refugee Act) over the executive’s termination of domestic resettlement programs for refugees already in the United States. The full merits opinion the court promised will resolve the interpretive questions sketched here, but the lawyering lesson is clear: when reading such opinions, parse the statutory text for mandatory verbs and appropriations qualifiers, track implementing regulations and established reliance interests, and identify where judicial deference doctrines (like those articulated in Trump v. Hawaii) circumscribe review.

# Stay Standard and Application to the Entry Suspension

## Nken v. Holder four-factor test and primacy of likelihood of success and irreparable harm

The Ninth Circuit began by applying the familiar four-factor test for a stay pending appeal set out in Nken v. Holder: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that he is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The court emphasized, as Nken does, that the first two factors—the showing of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are “the most critical.” In this case the government was the stay applicant seeking relief from a district-court preliminary injunction that barred enforcement of parts of Executive Order No. 14163 (the order suspending refugee admissions). Reading a stay order requires attention to how the court treats each Nken factor and, especially, whether the court finds the government’s likelihood-of-success and irreparable-harm showings strong enough to carry the day.

## Likelihood of success: reliance on Trump v. Hawaii; limited judicial inquiry into presidential findings under § 1182(f); clear textual fit of the EO

The panel’s most important step was its conclusion that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the plaintiffs’ challenge to the EO’s suspension of refugee admissions. That conclusion rests on statutory text and controlling precedent. Section 1182(f) empowers the President: “Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, he may ... suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens.” The court treated that statutory language as a broad grant of authority to suspend entry and as having only one “prerequisite”: a presidential finding that entry would be detrimental.

To decide how much judicial scrutiny that finding receives, the panel invoked the Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Hawaii, which the Ninth Circuit quoted for two propositions: that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and that courts should not engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Applying that principle here, the Ninth Circuit concluded that the President had made the requisite finding and that the Executive Order “does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.” In short, the EO’s suspension of refugee admissions is a clear textual fit within § 1182(f), and Trump v. Hawaii supplies a strong presumption of deference to the President’s determination—together giving the government a strong showing that it is likely to prevail on the merits.

For a student, the lesson is this: under § 1182(f) challenges, an appellate court considering a stay will first ask whether the EO fits the statute’s plain terms and then look to precedent that circumscribes judicial review of the President’s national-security and foreign-policy findings. If both answers favor the executive, the “likelihood” prong of Nken is often satisfied at the stay stage.

## Irreparable harm, balance of equities, and public interest: weight given to foreign and domestic interests and broad executive discretion

Having found a strong likelihood of success, the panel turned to the remaining Nken factors. The court accepted the government’s argument that enjoining the EO risks substantial and difficult-to-redress harms to domestic and foreign interests the President is charged with protecting. In the stay analysis the Ninth Circuit treated those interests—matters of national security, foreign relations, and resource allocation—as weighty considerations that the President is entrusted to evaluate. Citing again the “broad discretion” conferred on the President in this area, the panel concluded that the balance of equities and the public interest favor granting a stay.

The practical reading point for students is that in immigration and national‑security contexts a court’s deference to executive judgment on foreign and domestic harms often carries substantial weight in the equitable-balancing inquiry. When the court credits the executive’s asserted harms as serious and non‑speculative, the second critical Nken factor (irreparable injury) and the public‑interest comparison will frequently tip toward a stay.

## Result under Nken: district court’s injunction against EO 14163’s entry suspension is stayed pending appeal

Weighing the Nken factors together, and given the strong showing on the merits grounded in § 1182(f) and Trump v. Hawaii plus the court’s acceptance of executive‑branch harms, the Ninth Circuit ordered a stay pending appeal of the district court’s preliminary injunction as to the EO’s suspension of refugee admissions. Practically, that stay preserves the Executive Order’s suspension of refugee entry while the appeal proceeds. The panel made clear that a fuller merits opinion would follow, but for present purposes the court concluded the stay standard was met.

When you read stay rulings like this one, note that the appellate court is making a threshold prediction about the merits (not a full merits adjudication) based largely on statutory fit and binding precedent about the scope of judicial review; that prediction, combined with deference to executive judgments about foreign and domestic harms, often controls the outcome of a stay request in immigration‑national‑security cases.

# Statutory Services After Admission: The Narrow Exception and APA Likelihood

This interim Ninth Circuit order does two things that, taken together, reflect a familiar appellate balancing act: it stays the district court’s preliminary injunctions disturbing the President’s suspension of refugee admissions, but it simultaneously preserves a narrow, affirmative obligation—requiring the government to restore the cooperative agreements needed to provide certain reception-and-placement services to refugees who already have been admitted to the United States. The court reached this mixed result by applying the Nken stay factors and concluding that, while the government is likely to prevail in defending the suspension of admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), it is not likely to prevail on at least one APA-based challenge grounded in the statutory resettlement duties of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The panel announced that it will explain its full reasoning in a forthcoming opinion; this order supplies the interim remedy designed to protect statutory services to admitted refugees while the appeal proceeds.

## Preliminary merits assessment: government not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim tied to statutory obligations in § 1522

The panel framed its stay analysis with the familiar four-factor Nken test (likelihood of success on the merits; irreparable harm; balance of equities; and the public interest), noting that the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable harm—are the most critical. Applying that standard, the court split the merits inquiry by claim. Quoting Trump v. Hawaii and relying on the text of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the panel concluded the government has a strong likelihood of prevailing on plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions: Congress gave the President broad authority, and the statutory prerequisite is simply that the President “find[]” that entry of a class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Because that predicate is easily met and courts owe substantial deference in the entry-suspension context, the stay as to the suspension of admissions was appropriate.

By contrast, the court announced that, for reasons to be fleshed out later, the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA-based claim tied to § 1522. That determination is what undercuts the government’s entitlement to a complete stay of the district court’s relief: a stay applicant must show likely success on the particular legal challenge whose enforcement it seeks to stay. Because the plaintiffs’ APA challenge to the termination of cooperative agreements and the defunding of resettlement services had enough plausibility on the record to defeat the government’s stay request as to that discrete statutory obligation, the panel refused to leave admitted refugees without the reception-and-placement services that § 1522 describes.

## Statutory baseline: court’s reading that § 1522 requires provision of certain reception and placement services to admitted refugees, subject to appropriations and as outlined by statute and regulations

The statutory provisions the panel relied on are concentrated in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the order summarizes. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) directs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—“to the extent of available appropriations”—to make available resources for employment training and placement, provide access to English-language training, ensure cash assistance is structured not to discourage economic self-sufficiency, and ensure women have equal opportunities in training. Subsections cited by the court also include a provision authorizing the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children during an interim period (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and a requirement that the Secretary develop and implement projects providing interim support, medical services, and case management for recently resettled refugees (§ 1522(e)(7)(A)). The court also referenced implementing regulations (45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.) and the mechanism Congress provided—contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies (§ 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii))—by which those services are ordinarily delivered.

Two interpretive points matter for reading the panel’s order. First, many of § 1522’s duties are stated with “shall” language and are tied to appropriations; the panel treats those textual features as sufficient to give plaintiffs a plausible statutory claim that the executive cannot unilaterally eliminate the services that Congress contemplated for refugees once they have been admitted. Second, the statute contemplates delivery of services through cooperative agreements and grants; therefore the government’s termination of those cooperative agreements is the operative agency action that the plaintiffs challenged under the APA. The panel’s interim conclusion—that plaintiffs’ APA claim grounded in § 1522 has a nontrivial likelihood of success—turns on the idea that § 1522 can supply enforceable obligations protecting refugees already in the United States, even if the administration has broad authority to bar future entries.

## Record evidence: termination of cooperative agreements; government’s lack of progress in securing replacement capacity; existence of FY 2025 appropriations and statutory reporting duties under § 1523

The panel’s narrow directive rests on concrete record facts showing that the government’s actions had immediate operational consequences and that there was no evident replacement capacity. The government terminated cooperative agreements providing domestic reception-and-placement services (and most overseas processing support) in late February; the panel notes the district court was told on March 10 that the government was “actively preparing a request for proposals” for new resettlement agencies, but that at oral argument before this panel the government was unable to point to any actual progress in securing replacement capacity. Those facts undercut any claim that the statutory services could continue uninterrupted absent the prior agreements.

The court also observed that Congress appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025 ($3.178 billion, later reduced by a rescission), and that § 1523 imposes reporting obligations on the Secretary—facts the panel cited to support the proposition that Congress continues to expect an operational resettlement program and oversight. Taken together, the record showed a real risk that the admitted-refugee population would be left without the statutorily described services if cooperative agreements were not restored while the appeal proceeds.

## Operative directive: government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States; services to include the usual and customary services under prior agreements

Because the government failed to show it was likely to prevail on the APA claim tied to § 1522 and because the record indicated imminent service shortfalls for people already admitted, the panel issued a narrowly tailored directive: the government is ordered to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. The order specifies that these services “shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” The directive therefore preserves the status quo for admitted refugees with respect to the statutorily enumerated supports—employment training and placement, English-language training, appropriately structured cash assistance, services for unaccompanied children during interim placement, and short-term medical and support services for recent arrivals—until the merits of the APA claim are resolved.

Note the narrowness: the panel did not order the government to reverse the President’s suspension of admissions, nor did it require the government to reinstate processing or resettlement for refugees who have not yet entered the United States. The relief is explicitly confined to those who already have been admitted and to the reception and placement services that § 1522 describes and that cooperative agreements historically provided.

## Relationship to broader stay: all district court preliminary injunctions remain stayed except for this appellate directive; prior carveout for conditionally approved refugees is vacated

On balance, the court stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions “in their entirety, except as noted,” and it vacated earlier administrative and partial stays it had issued. That means the district court’s injunctions are not operating while the appeal proceeds, except for the single affirmative obligation the panel preserved—reinstatement of the cooperative agreements for admitted refugees. The motions-panel carveout that had earlier allowed the district-court injunctions to continue to apply to some conditionally approved refugees outside the United States was explicitly vacated; the current, narrower carveout is limited to people already admitted and to the reception-and-placement services described in § 1522.

For practitioners and students, the practical posture is important to track: this is an interlocutory, stay-of-injunction order that preserves statutory services for a limited class while the parties litigate the merits on an expedited appellate schedule. The full merits opinion, which will explain the panel’s statutory analysis in detail, remains forthcoming.

Takeaway for reading opinions: a stay pending appeal is not an all-or-nothing instrument. Courts apply Nken’s multi-factor test claim-by-claim when the government seeks to block a district court’s equitable relief, and they may carve out narrow, affirmative obligations where plaintiffs have shown a plausible statutory or APA claim and the record demonstrates imminent, concrete harm to a protected class (here, refugees already admitted). The order also foreshadows the statutory interpretive fight—reflected in Judge Lee’s partial dissent—about whether § 1522 is merely an authorization or contains enforceable duties; the majority’s interim remedy shows how that question can determine who gets relief while an appeal proceeds.

# Judge Lee’s Partial Concurrence and Dissent: Authorization vs. Mandate in § 1522

## Core disagreement: § 1522 as authorization to fund programs, not a mandate to operate them
Judge Lee agrees with the panel’s conclusion that the President has sweeping discretion under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, so he concurs in part. His dissent focuses narrowly on the district court’s conclusion—followed by the panel majority—that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 imposes a judicially enforceable duty to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees who had been admitted before Executive Order No. 14163. Lee’s position is that § 1522 is principally an authorizing statute: it permits the executive branch to establish and fund domestic resettlement programs, but it does not command that those programs be operated in the absence of appropriations or executive choice. Because he reads § 1522 as permissive, Lee would not allow a court to compel the executive to reinstate cooperative agreements or continue programs that the executive decided to suspend; on that basis he would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in their entirety.

Understanding Lee’s disagreement requires attention to how he treats statutory language of authorization statutes and to his view of the proper judicial role when Congress has left funding and program operation to executive discretion. He sees the district court’s remedy—forcing the executive to reinstate cooperative agreements and continue services—as an improper judicial command in the absence of a clear statutory duty.

## Textual cues: section title “Authorization,” repeated “is authorized” phrasing, and Black’s Law definition
Lee anchors his reading in ordinary textual signals. The statute’s heading—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—is, to him, not incidental: “Authorization” is commonly understood to confer permission rather than to impose a requirement. He supports that point with a citation to Black’s Law Dictionary: authorization means “permission to do something.” Lee then notes that the detailed service provisions in subsections (b) through (e) repeatedly use the passive permissive phrasing—e.g., the Director “is authorized” to make grants, contracts, or other arrangements—language that typically denotes discretion rather than command.

This line of reasoning illustrates a basic tool of statutory interpretation: titles, section headings, and recurring verb forms can function as interpretive aids when they align with the surrounding text. Lee treats those cues as corroborating evidence that Congress intended to authorize programs (and to provide a statutory framework and priorities if programs are funded), not to impose a stand-alone obligation that would allow courts to require the executive to run those programs.

## Reading § 1522(a): “shall” and “to the extent of available appropriations” as conditional provisions
The majority relied heavily on § 1522(a), which contains language using “shall,” to conclude that the statute imposes mandatory duties. Lee accepts that “shall” often signals a command, but he reads § 1522(a) in context. The paragraph begins with a limiting clause—“In providing assistance under this section”—and contains the familiar proviso “to the extent of available appropriations.” For Lee, those prefatory and qualifying phrases change the operative force of the “shall.” Rather than creating an independent duty to provide assistance, § 1522(a) sets out conditions and procedural requirements that must be observed if and when the executive elects to provide assistance under the discretionary programs authorized in subsections (b)–(e).

On this view, the “shall” in § 1522(a) prescribes how assistance must be administered when Congress (through appropriations or other actions) enables the programs, but it does not itself compel the executive to start or continue those programs. The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” is particularly important for Lee: it signals that any substantive obligations are bounded by funding, reinforcing that Congress contemplated contingent federal action, not an unconditional statutory command enforceable by courts.

## Subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A): “if necessary” and “as needed” preserve discretion
Lee responds to the majority’s reliance on specific subsections—most notably § 1522(d)(2)(B) (unaccompanied minors) and § 1522(e)(7)(A) (interim support for recently arrived refugees)—by emphasizing the qualifying language those provisions contain. Subsection (d)(2)(B) speaks of the Director’s duty to “assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary,” and authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions” to care for the child. Subsection (e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to “develop and implement alternative projects … as needed.” For Lee, those “if necessary” and “as needed” clauses are not mere words but textual indicators of contingent governmental responsibility: they grant authority to act when circumstances require, rather than setting an unconditional, court-enforceable command to provide services in every situation.

Lee also notes that the record does not establish that certain contingencies actually exist here—for example, he questions whether any unaccompanied refugee children are implicated—so even these permissive-turned-maybe-mandatory clauses do not meaningfully convert the statutory scheme into a universal statutory obligation applicable to the facts of this case. Thus, the disputed subsections do not overcome the broader structural signal of discretion.

## Remedy: would stay the district court’s injunctions in their entirety and not compel reinstatement of cooperative agreements
Because Lee reads § 1522 as discretionary, his remedial conclusion follows: the district court erred in ordering the executive to reinstate cooperative agreements and run reception-and-placement programs. He grounds that remedy—staying the injunctions in full—on the separation-of-powers implication of ordering the executive to operate programs that Congress authorized but did not unambiguously mandate. In his view, absent a clear statutory duty to provide and fund the services, courts are “in no position to mandate them.”

Practically, that means Lee would have denied the plaintiffs’ request for an injunction directing the reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements and would have stayed the district court’s orders that compelled the executive to provide specified services to admitted or conditionally approved refugees. His approach narrows the judicial remedy to situations where the statute creates a clear, mandatory duty—Lee’s reading is that § 1522 does not do so here.

Conclusion
Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent presents a textualist, restraint-oriented alternative to the panel majority: read § 1522 as a framework authorizing refugee resettlement programs and prescribing conditions for administering them if funded, but not as a command that courts may enforce to require the executive to run programs or expend resources. For students, his opinion is a compact example of (1) how statutory headings and recurring verb choice can be marshaled as interpretive evidence, (2) how qualifying phrases like “in providing assistance” and “to the extent of available appropriations” limit the scope of “shall” language, and (3) how statutory interpretation can decisively shape the available judicial remedies in separation-of-powers–sensitive contexts.

# Practical Effects and What to Watch on the Merits

This chapter explains what the Ninth Circuit’s September 12, 2025 stay order does now (operationally), what procedural housekeeping the court performed, and the narrow set of legal questions the panel signaled it will decide on the merits. The order is an interim, stay-stage ruling: it does not decide the merits finally, but it both (a) affords the Executive substantial breathing room to continue the refugee-admissions suspension and (b) preserves a statutory protection for people already inside the United States. Read the forthcoming merits opinion with that tension in mind.

## Immediate effect

Operationally the order does three things you must understand. First, the court stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24, 2025 preliminary injunctions pending appeal, subject to one express exception. The practical consequence is that Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions may remain in effect while the appeal proceeds: the Ninth Circuit concluded at the stay stage that the government is likely to prevail on its challenge to the validity of the President’s suspension authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and so it relieved the government from the district court’s injunctions that would have required resumed admissions.

Second, and critically, the stay is not absolute. The panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements that the State Department had terminated in February 2025 when those agreements are “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522” to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The court required reinstatement of the “usual and customary services” that had been provided under the prior cooperative agreements. In short: the Executive may continue to suspend new admissions, but it must (at least for now) restore the service infrastructure needed to carry out statutory reception-and-placement duties for refugees already inside the country.

Third, the court vacated prior, interim administrative orders (including the motions-panel stay and the administrative stay issued in July) and denied the government’s July 17 emergency motion as moot. Those earlier, piecemeal stay orders (which had contained a different carve‑out for certain conditionally approved refugees) have been replaced by this full-panel stay with the narrower, admitted‑refugee reinstatement requirement.

On the ground, the immediate effect is therefore bifurcated: resettlement agencies and the Department of State/HHS must act to restore terminated cooperative agreements for admitted refugees (subject to whatever practical transition problems exist after the February 26 terminations), while overseas processing and new admissions may remain halted under EO 14163 pending the appeal.

## Mootness and procedural housekeeping

Two procedural points to note because they affect how to read the case going forward. The government’s July 17 emergency motion (seeking enforcement, clarification, or amendment of the motions-panel stay) was denied as moot because the full panel’s order resolves the same issues in a comprehensive stay order. The court also expressly vacated the March 25 stay (and its April 21 and May 9 amendments) and the July 18 administrative stay; those earlier dispositions no longer control. Practitioners should therefore treat the September 12 order as the operative interlocutory disposition: it both stays the district court’s injunctions and, as to admitted refugees, preserves a portion of the district court’s relief by ordering reinstatement.

## Key merits issues to watch

The stay order sketches four doctrinal battlelines that the forthcoming merits opinion will decide. Each is explained below with the core legal concepts you should look for in the full opinion.

1. Scope of § 1182(f) deference (admissions power). The panel applied the stay standard by treating likelihood of success on the § 1182(f) challenge as the most important factor and relied heavily on Trump v. Hawaii’s teaching that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President. At the stay stage the court refused to engage in a searching inquiry into the President’s factual justifications and viewed the statutory text—authorizing suspension when the President “finds” entry would be detrimental—as plainly broad. On the merits you should watch whether the panel (a) repeats Trump’s high‑deference posture, (b) explains any limits on § 1182(f) when another statute (here, the Refugee Act) appears to govern, and (c) identifies circumstances in which courts can review the President’s “finding” (for example, if the finding is a pretext for an unconstitutional purpose or conflicts with clear statutory text). The degree to which the court confines itself to Trump’s deferential framework—or refines an exception where statutory or constitutional words intrude—will determine how robust courts can be in policing presidential entry suspensions going forward.

2. Whether § 1522 creates enforceable duties (authorization versus mandate). The order signals that the plaintiffs are likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge tied to 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (the statutory provision governing domestic reception and placement). The central statutory interpretation question is whether § 1522 is a permissive authorization to set up programs (as Judge Lee argued in his partial dissent, relying on the statute’s title and repeated uses of “is authorized”) or whether particular subsections impose mandatory duties—subject to the qualification “to the extent of available appropriations”—that the executive branch must satisfy for refugees already in the country. Pay attention in the merits opinion to (a) how the court treats titles and section headings (are they persuasive or merely aidful?), (b) how it reconciles “shall” language in § 1522(a) with the many instances of “is authorized” elsewhere in the subchapter, and (c) whether the qualification “to the extent of available appropriations” is construed to make the entire statutory scheme discretionary or merely to limit the government’s obligations to available appropriated funds. The outcome on this statutory question is decisive: if § 1522 is mandatory when funds are available, courts can enforce post‑entry service obligations; if not, the government’s terminations are harder to police.

3. Interaction of appropriations and statutory obligations. The panel explicitly noted Congress’s FY2025 appropriation for migration and refugee assistance and the subsequent rescission. The merits opinion must resolve whether sufficient appropriated funds exist to trigger any mandatory duties under § 1522 and, relatedly, whether the Executive may lawfully implement the admissions suspension by terminating cooperative agreements that deliver statutorily authorized (or required) services. Expect the court to address how (and when) appropriations language such as “to the extent of available appropriations” operates as a condition precedent to statutory duties, and whether a rescission or reprogramming argument permits the Executive to stop programs without satisfying APA requirements or separate congressional direction.

4. APA reviewability and remedies for terminating cooperative agreements. The plaintiffs pressed APA claims about the termination of cooperative agreements; the panel concluded at the stay stage that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of those claims and therefore ordered reinstatement of agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services to admitted refugees. On the merits, watch the court’s treatment of four linked questions: Are the cooperative‑agreement terminations “final agency action” subject to judicial review? If so, was the termination arbitrary and capricious (or procedurally defective) given the statutory framework and the administration’s stated rationales? Does national‑security/foreign‑affairs deference materially limit APA review here, and if so, how far? Finally, what is an appropriate remedial scope—will the court endorse wholesale reinstatement for admitted refugees (as the stay order preliminarily required), or will it draw narrower relief tied to particular statutory duties or classes of refugees?

Across these issues, also watch for how the panel addresses standing, causation, and redressability for the organizational plaintiffs and for admitted or conditionally approved refugees. The stay order assumes plaintiffs have a cognizable claim at the preliminary stage; the merits opinion will test those elements.

## Doctrinal significance

This case is likely to be read as a doctrinal test of how courts divide labor between (1) the President’s broad, heavily deferred power to control entry and (2) Congress’s power to create and fund post‑entry protections that apply to people physically within the United States. The interim order already draws that line: strong deferential treatment for the suspension of entries under § 1182(f), coupled with willingness to enforce statutory resettlement obligations under § 1522 for admitted refugees. The merits opinion will show whether that bifurcation survives close textual and administrative‑law scrutiny and whether the judiciary will generally allow an executive invocation of immigration or national‑security authority to be used as a tool to dismantle internally applicable statutory programs. More broadly, the case will refine the boundary between (a) areas where courts give national‑security/immigration judgments heavy deference and (b) ordinary administrative actions (like grants/agreements to deliver benefits) where reasoned decisionmaking and APA constraints normally apply.

## Next steps and what practitioners should do now

Expect a full merits opinion from the panel in due course; that opinion will resolve the statutory‑interpretation and APA questions sketched above. The immediate procedural posture allows the government to keep implementing the admission suspension but requires it to restore cooperative agreements for admitted refugees; compliance and enforcement of that reinstatement will be a live, practical issue while the appeal proceeds.

If you represent agencies or NGOs in similar disputes, the short checklist is: preserve the administrative record (all communications, rationales, and procurement steps regarding the terminations), quantify the operational disruption caused by the February terminations, and be ready to brief both statutory construction (text, structure, titles, and appropriations context) and APA standards (final agency action; reasoned explanation; alternatives considered). If you represent affected refugees, focus on showing concrete present harms from lost reception-and-placement services and on demonstrating that funds are available to carry out § 1522 duties.

For students reading the forthcoming opinion, concentrate on (1) the court’s textual parsing of § 1522 and its treatment of “shall” versus “is authorized,” (2) how the panel reconciles Trump v. Hawaii’s deference with statutory enforcement, (3) the standards the court applies under the APA to cooperative‑agreement terminations tied to a national‑security posture, and (4) the remedy analysis—how narrowly or broadly the court confines relief to admitted refugees and to particular services. That reasoning will set precedent about the limits of executive action at the intersection of immigration control and domestic statutory protections.
