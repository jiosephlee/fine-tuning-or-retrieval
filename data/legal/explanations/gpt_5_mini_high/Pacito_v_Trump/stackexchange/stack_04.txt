Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Under the APA, can a court order the Executive to "reinstate cooperative agreements," and is "usual and customary services" specific enough under Rule 65?
Question:
The court stays the district court’s injunctions but affirmatively "direct[s]" the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services, specifying the services "shall include the usual and customary services" under prior agreements.

- What APA theory supports this specific affirmative relief: § 706(1) (compel agency action unlawfully withheld) or § 706(2) (set aside arbitrary/ultra vires terminations with reinstatement as the remedy)? How does Norton v. SUWA’s "discrete, mandatory duty" requirement factor in if the agency’s duty is contested?
- Is it procedurally proper for an appellate court, in the course of granting a stay pending appeal, to impose this form of mandatory injunctive relief, or should it remand for the district court to tailor relief?
- Does ordering "usual and customary services" satisfy Rule 65(d)’s requirement that injunctions describe with reasonable detail what the parties must do, or is that language too vague?
Answer:
Short answer
- The reinstatement order in Pacito is most plausibly grounded on a §706(1) theory only if §1522 is read to impose a discrete, nondiscretionary duty to provide reception-and-placement services (the panel’s interim language treats §1522 that way). If §1522 is discretionary, the same outcome could instead be justified under §706(2) (set aside unlawful agency action) with reinstatement as equitable relief ancillary to vacatur/remand. Norton v. SUWA is the controlling constraint on any §706(1) theory: §706(1) can compel only a discrete, mandatory duty, not broad programmatic policy.  
- It is procedurally permissible for a court of appeals to condition a stay on interim affirmative relief (the panel applied the Nken stay factors and issued the direction), but courts of appeals ordinarily reserve detailed, ongoing remedial design and supervision to the district court; giving prospective, mandatory relief from an appellate bench is exceptional and justified only when the record and urgency support it. Pacito shows the Ninth Circuit believed the record justified a narrow, interim reinstatement order (and promised a fuller opinion to follow).  
- “Usual and customary services” standing alone is thin under Rule 65(d), but Pacito tethers that phrase both to (1) the statute’s enumerated services in §1522 and (2) the prior cooperative agreements. That tethering can make the injunction sufficiently definite for Rule 65(d) so long as the referenced agreements (and the statutory list) supply ascertainable duties and a feasible enforcement mechanism; otherwise the phrase risks being void for vagueness.

Explanation and support from Pacito

1) §706(1) vs. §706(2) (and Norton)
- Legal rule. Norton v. SUWA requires that relief under 5 U.S.C. §706(1) be aimed at a “discrete, nondiscretionary” agency duty; plaintiffs cannot use §706(1) to force the agency to pursue broad policy goals. By contrast §706(2) authorizes courts to “set aside” unlawful agency action (vacatur) and courts have long used equitable powers incident to vacatur to restore the status quo or order other relief on remand.
- What Pacito does. The interim order explicitly finds that “Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government must provide certain reception and placement services” and then directs that “the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” The panel also recites that Congress appropriated funds for FY2025 (noting $3.178 billion reduced by $800 million). Those passages show the panel preliminarily treated §1522 as imposing a concrete obligation for admitted refugees and therefore issued affirmative relief to secure performance of that statutory duty.
- How to read that. If the merits opinion holds §1522 imposes the kind of clear, non‑discretionary duty Norton requires, then ordering reinstatement fits naturally as an order compelling agency action under §706(1). If the merits opinion instead treats §1522 as largely permissive (cf. Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent arguing §1522 is an “authorization”), Norton would preclude §706(1) relief and the court would need to rely on §706(2) — i.e., find the terminations unlawful (arbitrary, ultra vires) and set them aside, with reinstatement ordered as equitable relief incident to vacatur/remand. Pacito’s interim language does not label the precise APA provision it invokes; it signals the majority’s preliminary view that a non‑discretionary obligation exists, while the concurrence shows the statutory interpretation is contested.

2) Appellate court ordering affirmative relief in a stay
- Authority and limits. Pacito follows the familiar Nken framework for stays (the panel quotes the four Nken factors). Courts of appeals routinely grant stays pending appeal and may impose conditions necessary to preserve the status quo or prevent irreparable harm. Ordering affirmative measures as part of a stay is not categorically forbidden, but it is extraordinary because it requires the appellate court to make remedial judgments the district court normally fashions and supervises.
- How Pacito fits. The Ninth Circuit exercised that stay power: after weighing the Nken factors the panel stayed the district court’s injunctions “in their entirety, except as noted below,” and then directed reinstatement limited to §1522 services for refugees already admitted. The panel justified the limited affirmative relief on the record: terminated cooperative agreements existed, the government had told the district court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” but later conceded uncertainty about replacement arrangements, and the panel had a preliminary view that §1522 imposed duties and appropriations existed. The order also noted the district court had created an enforcement framework (appointment of a magistrate) — a record the panel could use to limit and enforce its interim direction.
- Practical point. Procedurally the panel acted within appellate stay powers, but the move is atypical and contestable: remanding to the district court to fashion and supervise detailed relief is the safer, usual practice. Pacito demonstrates the line: an appellate court can impose narrowly tailored, time‑limited affirmative relief when urgency and a developed record justify doing so, but it should avoid open‑ended remedial micromanagement that belongs to the trial court.

3) “Usual and customary services” and Rule 65(d)
- Rule 65(d) principle (practical summary). An injunction must be definite enough to give the enjoined party fair notice of the required conduct and give the court and enforcers a workable standard. Vague, catch‑all commands are vulnerable.
- What Pacito says and why it matters. The order requires that the reinstated agreements provide “the reception and placement services described in §1522” and adds that “such services shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” Two features mitigate vagueness: (1) the order ties relief to an enumerated statutory list (the opinion quotes §1522’s items — employment training and placement, English training, cash and medical assistance projects, Director’s responsibilities for unaccompanied minors, etc.); and (2) the order anchors those duties to the concrete prior cooperative agreements that the government actually had in place. When the referenced agreements and the statute are on the record and specify particular services, a court can say the injunction is sufficiently definite because the required acts are ascertainable by reference.  
- The remaining risk. If the prior agreements are themselves vague, or if “usual and customary” leaves essential dimensions (scope, levels of service, funding thresholds, timelines) to the government’s residual discretion, the injunction risks failing Rule 65(d). Pacito’s interim order reduces that risk by linking the requirement to the prior agreements and the statutory descriptions and by relying on an existing enforcement framework, but challengers can plausibly argue the phrase is too amorphous without incorporation of the actual agreement text or clearer performance standards.

Bottom line for a law student
- Legally, Pacito’s reinstatement order sits at the intersection of statutory interpretation and equitable remedy doctrine. If the court concludes §1522 creates a discrete, nondiscretionary duty and appropriations are available, §706(1) supports compelled reinstatement (Norton’s “discrete, mandatory duty” test is the gatekeeper). If §1522 is discretionary, the court must proceed under §706(2) (vacatur of the terminations) and rely on equitable powers to restore the prior arrangements.  
- Procedurally, appellate courts can issue narrow, mandatory interim relief as part of a stay when the record and urgency warrant it (Pacito did so), but such relief is exceptional; ordinarily detailed remedial work is left to the district court.  
- On specificity, Pacito’s tethering of “usual and customary services” to the statute’s enumerated duties and to the prior cooperative agreements makes the phrase more defensible under Rule 65(d) than it would be standing alone, but the language remains borderline and enforceability will depend on how concrete the prior agreements and the district‑court enforcement framework prove to be in the forthcoming merits opinion.
