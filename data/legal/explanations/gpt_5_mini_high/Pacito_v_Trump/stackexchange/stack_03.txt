Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Is 8 U.S.C. § 1522 a mandate to provide services "to the extent of available appropriations," or merely permission to run programs?
Question:
The majority suggests the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one APA challenge because § 1522 requires certain post-arrival services, citing provisions like § 1522(a)(1)(A) (using "shall" and "to the extent of available appropriations"), § 1522(d)(2)(B) (Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minors "if necessary"), and § 1522(e)(7)(A) (Secretary "shall develop and implement" cash/medical projects "as needed"). Judge Lee reads § 1522 as an authorization, noting the section is titled "Authorization," and subsections (b)-(e) often say the Director "is authorized" to provide services.

- How do courts weigh a section’s title ("Authorization") against mandatory-sounding operative text that uses "shall"? Is the better reading that (a) imposes conditions only if the Executive chooses to fund programs under (b)-(e), or that (a) mandates a baseline of services if appropriations are available?
- What is the legal meaning of "to the extent of available appropriations" here? Does it create a duty to spend up to some floor if money exists, or does it preserve complete executive discretion not to operate the programs even if money is available?
- The order cites a $3.178 billion Migration and Refugee Assistance appropriation reduced by rescission. Are those "available appropriations" for the § 1522 duties (which seem to be housed in HHS/ORR), or are those State/PRM funds that may not satisfy § 1522’s "availability" language? Any case law interpreting § 1522 on this mandate-versus-authorization question?
Answer:
Short answer
- Pacito is split. The Ninth Circuit majority treated 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing at least some mandatory post‑admission duties “to the extent of available appropriations” and directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements to provide reception-and-placement services. Judge Lee disagreed, reading the same text as an authorization: §1522(b)–(e) “authoriz[e]” programs, and §1522(a) sets conditions that only bind the government if it elects to provide assistance under those programmatic subsections. The order therefore leaves the dispute live: the majority gives decisive weight to the operative “shall” language; the concurrence/dissent gives decisive weight to the section title and the repeated “is authorized” phrasing.

How the opinion treats headings vs. operative text
- The majority’s practical result rests on the operative language. The order states plainly: “Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government must provide certain reception and placement services… Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director… ‘to the extent of available appropriations’—to: (i) make available sufficient resources … (ii) provide … English language training … (iii) insure that cash assistance … (iv) insure that women have the same opportunities …” (Pacito order (9th Cir. Sept. 12, 2025)). That reading treats the “shall” in (a) as imposing duties, subject to the appropriation qualifier.
- Judge Lee, by contrast, anchors his reading in the section heading and the program subsections: he emphasizes the statutory title — “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” — and notes that subsections (b)–(e) repeatedly state the Director “is authorized” to provide services. He reads §1522(a) as “conditions and considerations” that apply only “in providing assistance under this section,” i.e., only if the Executive opts to run the authorized programs. In short: Pacito displays the two competing interpretive moves — majority gives primacy to mandatory operative text; Lee gives primacy to the authorizing label and the broader statutory structure.

What “to the extent of available appropriations” means in the order
- The panel treats the phrase as a statutory limitation, not as a nullifying escape hatch. The majority reads §1522(a)’s obligations as “to the extent of available appropriations,” and then notes that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance in FY2025 (reduced by a later rescission). On that factual footing the court concluded the government was not free simply to eliminate reception-and-placement services and therefore directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements.
- Important caveat in the order: the panel does not perform a fine‑grained appropriations‑law analysis. The order does not (a) define the legal test for whether a particular appropriation account is “available” to a particular agency component, (b) specify a dollar floor that must be spent, or (c) explain whether the Executive may reallocate funds within appropriations law limits. The practical holding is narrower: where the statute uses “shall … to the extent of available appropriations,” and Congress has recently appropriated money for migration/refugee assistance, the majority concluded the Executive could not unilaterally abandon the statutorily described reception-and-placement services and the cooperative agreements that implemented them.

Are the cited $3.178 billion appropriation dollars “available appropriations” for §1522 duties?
- The order expressly notes the FY2025 appropriation ($3.178 billion) and its $800 million rescission, but it does not decide whether those line‑item funds are legally “available” to HHS/ORR for §1522 duties or whether they reside in other accounts that would not satisfy the statute’s availability language. The opinion also records that the United States has “historically entered into cooperative agreements” (with the Department of State) to provide the services and that the government terminated those agreements; on that record the majority ordered reinstatement. But the panel did not resolve the inter‑agency appropriations/accounting question — that issue remains open for further proceedings or a fuller opinion.

Is there case law interpreting §1522 on mandate vs. authorization?
- The panel order does not cite any controlling authority that definitively resolves the mandate-versus-authorization question for §1522. The split in this panel itself is the clearest statement you have in this opinion: the majority reads §1522 as imposing duties (subject to appropriations), and Judge Lee reads it as an authorization of discretionary programs (with §1522(a) supplying conditions if the Executive chooses to fund those programs). The opinion therefore does not present a settled line of precedent on that precise point.

Practical takeaway (for a student or litigator)
- Pacito shows that §1522 is susceptible to two plausible textual readings: (1) mandatory duties qualified by appropriations (majority); or (2) a congressional authorization of programs whose substantive constraints in (a) attach only if the Executive chooses to run those authorized programs (Lee). The majority’s order indicates a court will enforce statutory post‑arrival duties where (i) the statute uses “shall,” (ii) Congress has recently appropriated funds in the relevant policy area, and (iii) there is a historical programmatic practice to implement those duties — but the order leaves open the hard appropriations‑law questions (which accounts count, how much must be obligated, and how agency discretion interacts with appropriations). The Ninth Circuit’s forthcoming full opinion (and any subsequent appeals) will be the place to watch for a fuller resolution of those finer points.
