Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the practical and legal difference between the Ninth Circuit’s prior administrative stay, its earlier partial stay, and this new "stay pending appeal"?
Question:
I'm trying to make sense of the procedural posture. The court previously issued a partial stay on March 25 (with a carveout for certain conditionally approved refugees) and later clarifications in April and May. Then, after the district court set up an enforcement framework on July 14, the Ninth Circuit entered an administrative stay on July 18. In this new order, the court vacates both the administrative stay and the earlier partial stay and issues a fresh stay pending appeal, while also denying the government's July 17 emergency motion as moot.

- What exactly distinguishes an "administrative stay" from a "stay pending appeal" in terms of purpose, standards (Nken), and duration?
- Can the merits panel freely vacate a motions panel's earlier stay orders, or does some law-of-the-case or intra-circuit practice constrain that?
- Under FRAP 8 and related authority, can the appellate court condition a stay by imposing affirmative obligations (here, directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements), or is that functionally a mandatory injunction rather than a stay?
- On the Nken factors, the order emphasizes likely success and broadly says the "remaining factors" favor the United States. Does the court need a more specific irreparable-harm finding for the government, or do asserted national interests suffice at this stage?
Answer:
Short answer
- Practically: the three orders performed different jobs. The March 25 motions‑panel stay was a limited, provisional grant (a carveout for conditionally approved refugees); the July 18 “administrative stay” was a short, stop‑gap holding pattern while the court expedited the case; the September 12 order is a formal stay pending appeal — a full, Nken‑type stay that supersedes and vacates the earlier, ad hoc orders and is meant to remain in place “pending final resolution of this appeal.” (Pacito Order ¶¶1–3, Background, Discussion.)
- Legally: the stay‑pending‑appeal is the vehicle that triggers the Nken factors and a multi‑factor equitable balancing; an administrative stay is typically temporary and may be entered without the full Nken exposition; the March 25 partial stay was a limited motions‑panel ruling under the stay‑pending‑appeal procedure that the later panel has now vacated. (Pacito Order, Discussion & Background.)
- The Ninth Circuit panel here plainly vacated its own earlier orders — the opinion does so without treating prior motions‑panel limits as unchangeable. And the court used the stay order to impose an affirmative obligation (reinstatement of cooperative agreements) — functionally compelling government action rather than merely preserving a status quo injunction. The majority characterizes that as a direction tied to its reading of 8 U.S.C. §1522; the dissent disputes that statutory reading. (Pacito Order, Discussion; Lee, J., concurring/dissenting.)

Longer explanation tied to Pacito

1) What distinguishes the three orders (purpose, standard, duration)
- March 25 partial stay (motions‑panel): a limited, provisional stay granted in response to the government’s stay‑pending‑appeal request. The panel “granted in part” and carved out individuals conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025. That order was a narrow, intermediate ruling while full briefing and calendaring proceeded. (Pacito Background: “which a motions panel of this court granted in part on March 25… ‘to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction … applies to individuals who were conditionally approved … before January 20, 2025.’”)  
- July 18 administrative stay: described in the opinion as an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and was plainly an interim measure entered while the court scheduled expedited argument. Administrative stays are typically short‑term, emergency devices to preserve matters pending fuller consideration; here it was explicitly temporary (and later vacated). (Pacito Background: “On July 18, 2025, we issued an administrative stay … We scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis.”)  
- Sept. 12 stay pending appeal (this order): the court “now vacate[s]” the administrative and March 25 orders and “issue[s] this stay pending final resolution of this appeal.” The opinion frames the stay under the Nken framework — it lists the four Nken factors, emphasizes the first two as “most critical,” and then reaches the court’s balancing conclusions. That stay is intended to govern the case until further order or final resolution. (Pacito Order ¶¶1–3; Discussion.)

2) Can a merits panel vacate a motions panel’s earlier stays?
- Yes in practice — the Pacito panel did exactly that. The order explicitly vacates the July 18 administrative stay and the March 25 stay (as amended) and supplants them with the new stay pending appeal. The opinion contains no caveat that prior stay orders are categorically insulated from revisitation; instead it demonstrates that a later panel can revisit and alter prior intermediate orders in the course of resolving the appeal. (Pacito Order ¶1; Background.)

3) Can an appellate court condition a stay by imposing affirmative obligations (reinstating cooperative agreements), or is that a mandatory injunction?
- What Pacito did: the court granted a stay pending appeal but also “directed” the government to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States,” and to provide “the usual and customary services” under prior agreements. That is an affirmative command (it compels action), not merely a passive preservation of status quo. (Pacito Discussion, last paragraph.)
- How to characterize it: functionally, that language operates like mandatory equitable relief (an affirmative injunction) rather than a pure negative stay that only bars enforcement. The majority justified the directive on its reading of §1522 (finding the government not likely to prevail on that APA challenge), while Judge Lee’s partial dissent insists §1522 is permissive and argues against judicial compulsion. So the order blurs the line: it purports to be a stay pending appeal but contains an affirmative obligation intended to protect the statutory interests the court found vulnerable. (Pacito Discussion; Lee, J., concurring/dissenting.) The opinion itself does not label the directive “a mandatory injunction,” but the effect is to require government action while the appeal proceeds.

4) Nken factors and the irreparable‑harm showing here
- The court framed the standard with Nken’s four factors and reiterated that the first two (likelihood of success and irreparable injury) are “the most critical.” Then it found the government “is likely to prevail” on the challenge to the EO under §1182(f) and concluded that “the remaining factors … favor the United States as well.” (Pacito Discussion (quoting Nken and Hawaii).)
- What the order does (and does not) do: the opinion makes a clear likely‑success finding and then treats the other factors — including irreparable‑injury and the public interest — collectively, relying on deference in immigration/foreign‑affairs matters (citing Trump v. Hawaii) and concluding those considerations favor the government. It does not set out a separate, granular irreparable‑harm finding paragraph for the government; instead the court folded that consideration into its broader conclusion that the remaining factors support a stay. (Pacito Discussion.)
- Practical takeaway: at this interlocutory, expedited stage the panel accepted national and executive‑branch interests and deference as sufficient to carry the remaining factors for purposes of a stay pending appeal. Whether a more particularized irreparable‑harm finding would be required on fuller review depends on the context and the reviewing tribunal — but Pacito itself treated the national interests + deference as an adequate basis for the “remaining factors” in its Nken balance.

Bottom line for litigation strategy and practice
- If you’re reading Pacito for practical effect, treat the March 25 and July 18 orders as provisional; the Sept. 12 stay pending appeal is the operative, Nken‑framed order for the duration of the appeal (subject to further order).  
- The Ninth Circuit here shows it will vacate earlier interim orders and can — when it believes statutory rights would be injured by doing otherwise — fold affirmative protections into a stay. That affirmative relief is controversial (note the concurrence/dissent) because it compels government action and therefore looks like a mandatory injunction even when styled as a stay.
