Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the practical and legal difference between the Ninth Circuit’s prior administrative stay, its earlier partial stay, and this new "stay pending appeal"?
Question:
I'm trying to make sense of the procedural posture. The court previously issued a partial stay on March 25 (with a carveout for certain conditionally approved refugees) and later clarifications in April and May. Then, after the district court set up an enforcement framework on July 14, the Ninth Circuit entered an administrative stay on July 18. In this new order, the court vacates both the administrative stay and the earlier partial stay and issues a fresh stay pending appeal, while also denying the government's July 17 emergency motion as moot.

- What exactly distinguishes an "administrative stay" from a "stay pending appeal" in terms of purpose, standards (Nken), and duration?
- Can the merits panel freely vacate a motions panel's earlier stay orders, or does some law-of-the-case or intra-circuit practice constrain that?
- Under FRAP 8 and related authority, can the appellate court condition a stay by imposing affirmative obligations (here, directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements), or is that functionally a mandatory injunction rather than a stay?
- On the Nken factors, the order emphasizes likely success and broadly says the "remaining factors" favor the United States. Does the court need a more specific irreparable-harm finding for the government, or do asserted national interests suffice at this stage?
Answer:
Short answer
- Practically: the three orders performed different jobs. The March 25 motions‑panel stay was a limited, provisional grant (a carveout for conditionally approved refugees); the July 18 “administrative stay” was a short, stop‑gap holding pattern while the court expedited the case; the September 12 order is a formal stay pending appeal — a full, Nken‑type stay that supersedes and vacates the earlier, ad hoc orders and is meant to remain in place “pending final resolution of this appeal.” (Pacito Order ¶¶1–3, Background, Discussion.)
- Legally: the stay‑pending‑appeal is the vehicle that triggers the Nken factors and a multi‑factor equitable balancing; an administrative stay is typically temporary and may be entered without the full Nken exposition; the March 25 partial stay was a limited motions‑panel ruling under the stay‑pending‑appeal procedure that the later panel has now vacated. (Pacito Order, Discussion & Background.)
- The Ninth Circuit panel here plainly vacated its own earlier orders — the opinion does so without treating prior motions‑panel limits as unchangeable. And the court used the stay order to impose an affirmative obligation (reinstatement of cooperative agreements) — functionally compelling government action rather than merely preserving a status quo injunction. The majority characterizes that as a direction tied to its reading of 8 U.S.C. §1522; the dissent disputes that statutory reading. (Pacito Order, Discussion; Lee, J., concurring/dissenting.)

Longer explanation tied to Pacito

1) What distinguishes the three orders (purpose, standard, duration)
- March 25 partial stay (motions‑panel): a limited, provisional stay granted in response to the government’s stay‑pending‑appeal request. The panel “granted in part” and carved out individuals conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025. That order was a narrow, intermediate ruling while full briefing and calendaring proceeded. (Pacito Background: “which a motions panel of this court granted in part on March 25… ‘to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction … applies to individuals who were conditionally approved … before January 20, 2025.’”)  
- July 18 administrative stay: described in the opinion as an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and was plainly an interim measure entered while the court scheduled expedited argument. Administrative stays are typically short‑term, emergency devices to preserve matters pending fuller consideration; here it was explicitly temporary (and later vacated). (Pacito Background: “On July 18, 2025, we issued an administrative stay … We scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis.”)  
- Sept. 12 stay pending appeal (this order): the court “now vacate[s]” the administrative and March 25 orders and “issue[s] this stay pending final resolution of this appeal.” The opinion frames the stay under the Nken framework — it lists the four Nken factors, emphasizes the first two as “most critical,” and then reaches the court’s balancing conclusions. That stay is intended to govern the case until further order or final resolution. (Pacito Order ¶¶1–3; Discussion.)

2) Can a merits panel vacate a motions panel’s earlier stays?
- Yes in practice — the Pacito panel did exactly that. The order explicitly vacates the July 18 administrative stay and the March 25 stay (as amended) and supplants them with the new stay pending appeal. The opinion contains no caveat that prior stay orders are categorically insulated from revisitation; instead it demonstrates that a later panel can revisit and alter prior intermediate orders in the course of resolving the appeal. (Pacito Order ¶1; Background.)

3) Can an appellate court condition a stay by imposing affirmative obligations (reinstating cooperative agreements), or is that a mandatory injunction?
- What Pacito did: the court granted a stay pending appeal but also “directed” the government to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States,” and to provide “the usual and customary services” under prior agreements. That is an affirmative command (it compels action), not merely a passive preservation of status quo. (Pacito Discussion, last paragraph.)
- How to characterize it: functionally, that language operates like mandatory equitable relief (an affirmative injunction) rather than a pure negative stay that only bars enforcement. The majority justified the directive on its reading of §1522 (finding the government not likely to prevail on that APA challenge), while Judge Lee’s partial dissent insists §1522 is permissive and argues against judicial compulsion. So the order blurs the line: it purports to be a stay pending appeal but contains an affirmative obligation intended to protect the statutory interests the court found vulnerable. (Pacito Discussion; Lee, J., concurring/dissenting.) The opinion itself does not label the directive “a mandatory injunction,” but the effect is to require government action while the appeal proceeds.

4) Nken factors and the irreparable‑harm showing here
- The court framed the standard with Nken’s four factors and reiterated that the first two (likelihood of success and irreparable injury) are “the most critical.” Then it found the government “is likely to prevail” on the challenge to the EO under §1182(f) and concluded that “the remaining factors … favor the United States as well.” (Pacito Discussion (quoting Nken and Hawaii).)
- What the order does (and does not) do: the opinion makes a clear likely‑success finding and then treats the other factors — including irreparable‑injury and the public interest — collectively, relying on deference in immigration/foreign‑affairs matters (citing Trump v. Hawaii) and concluding those considerations favor the government. It does not set out a separate, granular irreparable‑harm finding paragraph for the government; instead the court folded that consideration into its broader conclusion that the remaining factors support a stay. (Pacito Discussion.)
- Practical takeaway: at this interlocutory, expedited stage the panel accepted national and executive‑branch interests and deference as sufficient to carry the remaining factors for purposes of a stay pending appeal. Whether a more particularized irreparable‑harm finding would be required on fuller review depends on the context and the reviewing tribunal — but Pacito itself treated the national interests + deference as an adequate basis for the “remaining factors” in its Nken balance.

Bottom line for litigation strategy and practice
- If you’re reading Pacito for practical effect, treat the March 25 and July 18 orders as provisional; the Sept. 12 stay pending appeal is the operative, Nken‑framed order for the duration of the appeal (subject to further order).  
- The Ninth Circuit here shows it will vacate earlier interim orders and can — when it believes statutory rights would be injured by doing otherwise — fold affirmative protections into a stay. That affirmative relief is controversial (note the concurrence/dissent) because it compels government action and therefore looks like a mandatory injunction even when styled as a stay.

### Does § 1182(f) allow the President to suspend refugee admissions notwithstanding § 1157’s specific refugee admissions framework?
Question:
The panel leans on Trump v. Hawaii to conclude the President likely acted within § 1182(f), saying the court cannot undertake a "searching inquiry" and that the "sole prerequisite" (a detriment finding) is satisfied. But refugee admissions are also governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1157, which sets up an annual determination process after consultation with Congress.

- How do courts reconcile § 1182(f)'s broad suspension power with § 1157's specific, consultative scheme for refugee admissions? Does the canon that the specific governs the general apply here?
- Are there "textual limits" (as Hawaii contemplates) arising from § 1157 that would constrain § 1182(f) in the refugee context?
- Can an indefinite or open-ended suspension of refugee entry "pending further findings" effectively nullify the § 1157 process, and have courts addressed that precise interaction?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Pacito (following Trump v. Hawaii), §1182(f) is treated as a broad, independent grant of presidential power to suspend classes of aliens — including refugees — and courts will not conduct a “searching inquiry” into the President’s judgment. That means the “specific-governs-general” canon can limit §1182(f) only if a more specific statute (e.g., §1157) contains clear, unambiguous text that forecloses suspension. Pacito itself did not squarely resolve a §1182(f)/§1157 conflict; it concluded the Executive Order was likely within §1182(f) but simultaneously enforced statutory obligations to refugees already admitted (8 U.S.C. §1522), showing that suspension authority does not automatically nullify other statutory duties.

Explanation (law‑student level, tied to Pacito)

1) The governing approach in Pacito
- Pacito relies on Trump v. Hawaii and repeats that §1182(f) “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power” and that the “sole prerequisite” is the President’s finding that entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Pacito states that courts “cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications’” and that “the language of §1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.” (Pacito order, Sept. 12, 2025 (quoting Trump v. Hawaii)).
- In practice Pacito applied that principle to stay the district court injunctions against suspending refugee admissions — i.e., the panel concluded the government was likely to prevail on its §1182(f) defense.

2) How the “specific‑governs‑general” canon fits
- The canonical rule (specialia generalibus derogant) remains a basic tool of statutory interpretation, but Pacito indicates it will not be dispositive unless the specific statute contains clear language that conflicts with or limits §1182(f).
- Put plainly: courts following Pacito will first look to the text of §1157 for an unmistakable command that would be incompatible with a §1182(f) suspension. Absent such a clear textual limitation, Pacito follows the Trump line and gives §1182(f) broad effect even alongside more specific INA provisions.

3) “Textual limits” in the refugee context
- Trump v. Hawaii (as quoted in Pacito) recognized that §1182(f) might be subject to “textual limits,” but found none in that case; Pacito likewise concluded the challenged order “does not exceed any textual limit.”
- Pacito does not analyze §1157’s text; consequently it does not hold that §1157 itself is (or is not) a textual limit. The panel’s approach suggests that courts will deem §1157 a limiting statute only if its language unmistakably and directly precludes the President’s suspension power.

4) Open‑ended suspensions and nullifying §1157
- §1182(f) authorizes suspension “for such period as he shall deem necessary,” language the panel treated as giving the President temporal discretion. Pacito therefore indicates that an open‑ended “pending further findings” suspension falls within the statutory wording of §1182(f) unless a specific statute forbids that result.
- But Pacito also makes clear that a suspension of admissions is not a license to obliterate all statutory refugee protections: the panel directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services under 8 U.S.C. §1522 to refugees already admitted, and the court explicitly treated §1522 obligations as survivable against the Executive’s funding suspensions. Thus a §1182(f) suspension may affect admissions going forward but will not necessarily nullify statutory schemes that govern resettlement or benefits for refugees already in the United States.

5) Where Pacito leaves the law
- Pacito points in the direction of deference to §1182(f) for suspending refugee admissions, but it does not announce a definitive, general rule that §1182(f) can always override §1157 or other specific refugee provisions. The panel stayed the district court’s injunctions because the government was “likely to prevail” on its §1182(f) claim, but it reserved fuller analysis for the opinion to follow and separately enforced §1522 obligations. The concurrence/dissent (Judge Lee) underscores the statutory‑text dispute over whether §1522 is mandatory or merely permissive — showing that the interaction of §1182(f) and refugee statutes remains a live, contested question.

Practical takeaways
- Under Pacito, challengers to an executive suspension should press any clear, specific statutory language in §1157 (or related refugee provisions) that would make a suspension impossible or procedurally defective; mere procedural consultation rules or an annual determination mechanism will not automatically trump §1182(f) unless the text is unambiguous.
- Defendants relying on §1182(f) will emphasize Trump v. Hawaii and Pacito’s refrain that the President’s finding is the “sole prerequisite” and that courts should avoid a searching merits inquiry.
- Even if a suspension of admissions is upheld, Pacito demonstrates courts may still enforce statutory duties that apply to refugees already admitted (e.g., reception and placement under §1522), so an admission‑suspension is not a carte blanche to abandon all statutory refugee programs.

Bottom line: Pacito follows Trump v. Hawaii in reading §1182(f) broadly and will require clear, specific statutory text in §1157 to displace that power; Pacito did not itself resolve a textual conflict with §1157 and left intact enforcement of some statutory obligations to admitted refugees, so the precise limits remain an open question pending the panel’s full opinion.

### Is 8 U.S.C. § 1522 a mandate to provide services "to the extent of available appropriations," or merely permission to run programs?
Question:
The majority suggests the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one APA challenge because § 1522 requires certain post-arrival services, citing provisions like § 1522(a)(1)(A) (using "shall" and "to the extent of available appropriations"), § 1522(d)(2)(B) (Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minors "if necessary"), and § 1522(e)(7)(A) (Secretary "shall develop and implement" cash/medical projects "as needed"). Judge Lee reads § 1522 as an authorization, noting the section is titled "Authorization," and subsections (b)-(e) often say the Director "is authorized" to provide services.

- How do courts weigh a section’s title ("Authorization") against mandatory-sounding operative text that uses "shall"? Is the better reading that (a) imposes conditions only if the Executive chooses to fund programs under (b)-(e), or that (a) mandates a baseline of services if appropriations are available?
- What is the legal meaning of "to the extent of available appropriations" here? Does it create a duty to spend up to some floor if money exists, or does it preserve complete executive discretion not to operate the programs even if money is available?
- The order cites a $3.178 billion Migration and Refugee Assistance appropriation reduced by rescission. Are those "available appropriations" for the § 1522 duties (which seem to be housed in HHS/ORR), or are those State/PRM funds that may not satisfy § 1522’s "availability" language? Any case law interpreting § 1522 on this mandate-versus-authorization question?
Answer:
Short answer
- Pacito is split. The Ninth Circuit majority treated 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing at least some mandatory post‑admission duties “to the extent of available appropriations” and directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements to provide reception-and-placement services. Judge Lee disagreed, reading the same text as an authorization: §1522(b)–(e) “authoriz[e]” programs, and §1522(a) sets conditions that only bind the government if it elects to provide assistance under those programmatic subsections. The order therefore leaves the dispute live: the majority gives decisive weight to the operative “shall” language; the concurrence/dissent gives decisive weight to the section title and the repeated “is authorized” phrasing.

How the opinion treats headings vs. operative text
- The majority’s practical result rests on the operative language. The order states plainly: “Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government must provide certain reception and placement services… Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director… ‘to the extent of available appropriations’—to: (i) make available sufficient resources … (ii) provide … English language training … (iii) insure that cash assistance … (iv) insure that women have the same opportunities …” (Pacito order (9th Cir. Sept. 12, 2025)). That reading treats the “shall” in (a) as imposing duties, subject to the appropriation qualifier.
- Judge Lee, by contrast, anchors his reading in the section heading and the program subsections: he emphasizes the statutory title — “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” — and notes that subsections (b)–(e) repeatedly state the Director “is authorized” to provide services. He reads §1522(a) as “conditions and considerations” that apply only “in providing assistance under this section,” i.e., only if the Executive opts to run the authorized programs. In short: Pacito displays the two competing interpretive moves — majority gives primacy to mandatory operative text; Lee gives primacy to the authorizing label and the broader statutory structure.

What “to the extent of available appropriations” means in the order
- The panel treats the phrase as a statutory limitation, not as a nullifying escape hatch. The majority reads §1522(a)’s obligations as “to the extent of available appropriations,” and then notes that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance in FY2025 (reduced by a later rescission). On that factual footing the court concluded the government was not free simply to eliminate reception-and-placement services and therefore directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements.
- Important caveat in the order: the panel does not perform a fine‑grained appropriations‑law analysis. The order does not (a) define the legal test for whether a particular appropriation account is “available” to a particular agency component, (b) specify a dollar floor that must be spent, or (c) explain whether the Executive may reallocate funds within appropriations law limits. The practical holding is narrower: where the statute uses “shall … to the extent of available appropriations,” and Congress has recently appropriated money for migration/refugee assistance, the majority concluded the Executive could not unilaterally abandon the statutorily described reception-and-placement services and the cooperative agreements that implemented them.

Are the cited $3.178 billion appropriation dollars “available appropriations” for §1522 duties?
- The order expressly notes the FY2025 appropriation ($3.178 billion) and its $800 million rescission, but it does not decide whether those line‑item funds are legally “available” to HHS/ORR for §1522 duties or whether they reside in other accounts that would not satisfy the statute’s availability language. The opinion also records that the United States has “historically entered into cooperative agreements” (with the Department of State) to provide the services and that the government terminated those agreements; on that record the majority ordered reinstatement. But the panel did not resolve the inter‑agency appropriations/accounting question — that issue remains open for further proceedings or a fuller opinion.

Is there case law interpreting §1522 on mandate vs. authorization?
- The panel order does not cite any controlling authority that definitively resolves the mandate-versus-authorization question for §1522. The split in this panel itself is the clearest statement you have in this opinion: the majority reads §1522 as imposing duties (subject to appropriations), and Judge Lee reads it as an authorization of discretionary programs (with §1522(a) supplying conditions if the Executive chooses to fund those programs). The opinion therefore does not present a settled line of precedent on that precise point.

Practical takeaway (for a student or litigator)
- Pacito shows that §1522 is susceptible to two plausible textual readings: (1) mandatory duties qualified by appropriations (majority); or (2) a congressional authorization of programs whose substantive constraints in (a) attach only if the Executive chooses to run those authorized programs (Lee). The majority’s order indicates a court will enforce statutory post‑arrival duties where (i) the statute uses “shall,” (ii) Congress has recently appropriated funds in the relevant policy area, and (iii) there is a historical programmatic practice to implement those duties — but the order leaves open the hard appropriations‑law questions (which accounts count, how much must be obligated, and how agency discretion interacts with appropriations). The Ninth Circuit’s forthcoming full opinion (and any subsequent appeals) will be the place to watch for a fuller resolution of those finer points.

### Under the APA, can a court order the Executive to "reinstate cooperative agreements," and is "usual and customary services" specific enough under Rule 65?
Question:
The court stays the district court’s injunctions but affirmatively "direct[s]" the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services, specifying the services "shall include the usual and customary services" under prior agreements.

- What APA theory supports this specific affirmative relief: § 706(1) (compel agency action unlawfully withheld) or § 706(2) (set aside arbitrary/ultra vires terminations with reinstatement as the remedy)? How does Norton v. SUWA’s "discrete, mandatory duty" requirement factor in if the agency’s duty is contested?
- Is it procedurally proper for an appellate court, in the course of granting a stay pending appeal, to impose this form of mandatory injunctive relief, or should it remand for the district court to tailor relief?
- Does ordering "usual and customary services" satisfy Rule 65(d)’s requirement that injunctions describe with reasonable detail what the parties must do, or is that language too vague?
Answer:
Short answer
- The reinstatement order in Pacito is most plausibly grounded on a §706(1) theory only if §1522 is read to impose a discrete, nondiscretionary duty to provide reception-and-placement services (the panel’s interim language treats §1522 that way). If §1522 is discretionary, the same outcome could instead be justified under §706(2) (set aside unlawful agency action) with reinstatement as equitable relief ancillary to vacatur/remand. Norton v. SUWA is the controlling constraint on any §706(1) theory: §706(1) can compel only a discrete, mandatory duty, not broad programmatic policy.  
- It is procedurally permissible for a court of appeals to condition a stay on interim affirmative relief (the panel applied the Nken stay factors and issued the direction), but courts of appeals ordinarily reserve detailed, ongoing remedial design and supervision to the district court; giving prospective, mandatory relief from an appellate bench is exceptional and justified only when the record and urgency support it. Pacito shows the Ninth Circuit believed the record justified a narrow, interim reinstatement order (and promised a fuller opinion to follow).  
- “Usual and customary services” standing alone is thin under Rule 65(d), but Pacito tethers that phrase both to (1) the statute’s enumerated services in §1522 and (2) the prior cooperative agreements. That tethering can make the injunction sufficiently definite for Rule 65(d) so long as the referenced agreements (and the statutory list) supply ascertainable duties and a feasible enforcement mechanism; otherwise the phrase risks being void for vagueness.

Explanation and support from Pacito

1) §706(1) vs. §706(2) (and Norton)
- Legal rule. Norton v. SUWA requires that relief under 5 U.S.C. §706(1) be aimed at a “discrete, nondiscretionary” agency duty; plaintiffs cannot use §706(1) to force the agency to pursue broad policy goals. By contrast §706(2) authorizes courts to “set aside” unlawful agency action (vacatur) and courts have long used equitable powers incident to vacatur to restore the status quo or order other relief on remand.
- What Pacito does. The interim order explicitly finds that “Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government must provide certain reception and placement services” and then directs that “the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” The panel also recites that Congress appropriated funds for FY2025 (noting $3.178 billion reduced by $800 million). Those passages show the panel preliminarily treated §1522 as imposing a concrete obligation for admitted refugees and therefore issued affirmative relief to secure performance of that statutory duty.
- How to read that. If the merits opinion holds §1522 imposes the kind of clear, non‑discretionary duty Norton requires, then ordering reinstatement fits naturally as an order compelling agency action under §706(1). If the merits opinion instead treats §1522 as largely permissive (cf. Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent arguing §1522 is an “authorization”), Norton would preclude §706(1) relief and the court would need to rely on §706(2) — i.e., find the terminations unlawful (arbitrary, ultra vires) and set them aside, with reinstatement ordered as equitable relief incident to vacatur/remand. Pacito’s interim language does not label the precise APA provision it invokes; it signals the majority’s preliminary view that a non‑discretionary obligation exists, while the concurrence shows the statutory interpretation is contested.

2) Appellate court ordering affirmative relief in a stay
- Authority and limits. Pacito follows the familiar Nken framework for stays (the panel quotes the four Nken factors). Courts of appeals routinely grant stays pending appeal and may impose conditions necessary to preserve the status quo or prevent irreparable harm. Ordering affirmative measures as part of a stay is not categorically forbidden, but it is extraordinary because it requires the appellate court to make remedial judgments the district court normally fashions and supervises.
- How Pacito fits. The Ninth Circuit exercised that stay power: after weighing the Nken factors the panel stayed the district court’s injunctions “in their entirety, except as noted below,” and then directed reinstatement limited to §1522 services for refugees already admitted. The panel justified the limited affirmative relief on the record: terminated cooperative agreements existed, the government had told the district court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” but later conceded uncertainty about replacement arrangements, and the panel had a preliminary view that §1522 imposed duties and appropriations existed. The order also noted the district court had created an enforcement framework (appointment of a magistrate) — a record the panel could use to limit and enforce its interim direction.
- Practical point. Procedurally the panel acted within appellate stay powers, but the move is atypical and contestable: remanding to the district court to fashion and supervise detailed relief is the safer, usual practice. Pacito demonstrates the line: an appellate court can impose narrowly tailored, time‑limited affirmative relief when urgency and a developed record justify doing so, but it should avoid open‑ended remedial micromanagement that belongs to the trial court.

3) “Usual and customary services” and Rule 65(d)
- Rule 65(d) principle (practical summary). An injunction must be definite enough to give the enjoined party fair notice of the required conduct and give the court and enforcers a workable standard. Vague, catch‑all commands are vulnerable.
- What Pacito says and why it matters. The order requires that the reinstated agreements provide “the reception and placement services described in §1522” and adds that “such services shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” Two features mitigate vagueness: (1) the order ties relief to an enumerated statutory list (the opinion quotes §1522’s items — employment training and placement, English training, cash and medical assistance projects, Director’s responsibilities for unaccompanied minors, etc.); and (2) the order anchors those duties to the concrete prior cooperative agreements that the government actually had in place. When the referenced agreements and the statute are on the record and specify particular services, a court can say the injunction is sufficiently definite because the required acts are ascertainable by reference.  
- The remaining risk. If the prior agreements are themselves vague, or if “usual and customary” leaves essential dimensions (scope, levels of service, funding thresholds, timelines) to the government’s residual discretion, the injunction risks failing Rule 65(d). Pacito’s interim order reduces that risk by linking the requirement to the prior agreements and the statutory descriptions and by relying on an existing enforcement framework, but challengers can plausibly argue the phrase is too amorphous without incorporation of the actual agreement text or clearer performance standards.

Bottom line for a law student
- Legally, Pacito’s reinstatement order sits at the intersection of statutory interpretation and equitable remedy doctrine. If the court concludes §1522 creates a discrete, nondiscretionary duty and appropriations are available, §706(1) supports compelled reinstatement (Norton’s “discrete, mandatory duty” test is the gatekeeper). If §1522 is discretionary, the court must proceed under §706(2) (vacatur of the terminations) and rely on equitable powers to restore the prior arrangements.  
- Procedurally, appellate courts can issue narrow, mandatory interim relief as part of a stay when the record and urgency warrant it (Pacito did so), but such relief is exceptional; ordinarily detailed remedial work is left to the district court.  
- On specificity, Pacito’s tethering of “usual and customary services” to the statute’s enumerated duties and to the prior cooperative agreements makes the phrase more defensible under Rule 65(d) than it would be standing alone, but the language remains borderline and enforceability will depend on how concrete the prior agreements and the district‑court enforcement framework prove to be in the forthcoming merits opinion.

### Which agency actually owes the "reception and placement" duty the court is enforcing—HHS/ORR under § 1522 or State/PRM under the MRA account?
Question:
The opinion grounds the obligation to provide post-arrival services in § 1522 and references the "Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement" (an HHS office). Yet the factual background focuses on the State Department terminating all reception-and-placement cooperative agreements and on Migration and Refugee Assistance (a State/PRM appropriation). The court then directs "the government" to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services.

- Historically, R&P has been administered by State/PRM, whereas ORR (HHS) funds different services after arrival. If § 1522 duties fall on HHS/ORR, can the court properly order the State Department to reinstate its agreements to satisfy an HHS-centered statutory duty?
- Is there a statutory or delegated basis that links State/PRM’s R&P cooperative agreements to § 1522’s obligations, or are these parallel but distinct assistance regimes?
- Do appropriations constraints (State’s MRA vs. HHS’s Refugee and Entrant Assistance funding) affect which agency can be compelled to act under § 1522?
Answer:
Short answer
- The substantive statutory duty to provide post‑arrival “reception and placement” services is assigned to the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) in HHS under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, as the Ninth Circuit panel explains. But the court enforced that HHS/ORR duty by ordering the executive branch to restore the very cooperative‑agreement mechanism (administered historically by State/PRM) that actually delivered those §1522 services. In Pacito the court therefore directed “the government” — practically, the Department of State, which had terminated the agreements — to reinstate those agreements so §1522 services could be provided.

Why (statutory) duty belongs to HHS/ORR
- The panel explicitly grounds the substantive obligation in § 1522 and identifies the Director of ORR (an HHS office) as the statutory obligor: §1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make resources available for employment training and placement, English instruction, cash assistance rules, etc. (Pacito, slip op. ¶¶ discussing 8 U.S.C. §1522(a)(1)(A)). So, on the statute’s face the duty sits with ORR/HHS.

Why the court told State to reinstate agreements
- Section 1522 also authorizes delivery of resettlement services “through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with ‘public or private nonprofit agencies’” (8 U.S.C. §1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)), and the opinion records that the United States has “historically entered into cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations” and that the organizational plaintiffs had cooperative agreements with the Department of State. The opinion then recounts that State terminated those cooperative agreements and concludes that, because that termination threatened the government’s ability to provide the §1522 services, “the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522.” (Pacito, slip op. ¶¶ quoted above.)
- In short: the panel saw ORR’s statutory duty but saw State’s terminations as the immediate obstacle to performance. The court therefore ordered the executive branch (and practically the component that had broken the delivery mechanism) to restore the contractual vehicle by which §1522 services were delivered.

Are these two regimes (HHS §1522 and State/PRM R&P) distinct or linked?
- The opinion does not rest on a novel textual command that State must perform §1522 duties; rather it relies on §1522’s authorization to use cooperative agreements plus the historical practice that State/PRM held those agreements. Thus the link the court enforces is a practical one: cooperative agreements administered by State were the vehicle for fulfilling ORR’s statutory obligations. The statute permits use of such agreements, and past practice placed the agreements in State’s hands; Pacito treats that arrangement as an acceptable (indeed historical) means of implementing §1522.

Do appropriations constraints change who can be compelled to act?
- §1522’s obligations are expressly qualified by “to the extent of available appropriations,” and the panel cites the FY2025 Migration and Refugee Assistance (MRA) appropriation figures in the opinion. The court’s remedy — reinstatement of cooperative agreements — did not commandeer a particular fund or order a reprogramming of accounts; it restored the contractual framework so the statutory services could be provided using whatever appropriations were available.
- Put differently: appropriations are a limiting condition on §1522 (the statute itself says so, and the opinion notes the relevant appropriations), and a court cannot require expenditures beyond available appropriations. Pacito does not resolve any fine‑grained accounting question (e.g., whether particular MRA or HHS line items must be used); it simply found reinstating the agreements was necessary to allow provision of §1522 services in light of the government’s termination of those agreements.

A final practical gloss (and the dissenting view)
- The Ninth Circuit’s order therefore reflects a two‑step structure in the opinion: (1) the substantive duty resides with ORR/HHS under §1522; (2) the appropriate, practical remedy for the government’s disruption of service delivery was to require the executive branch (here, the Department of State, which had ended the agreements) to restore the cooperative agreements that historically implemented those duties. Judge Lee’s partial dissent stresses a competing statutory reading — that §1522 is an authorization rather than a mandate — so the panel’s enforcement approach is contested and may be revisited in the full opinion or on further review.

### After this order, what happens to refugees "conditionally approved" but still abroad—did the Ninth Circuit’s new stay erase the earlier carveout?
Question:
The March 25 stay order denied the stay "to the extent" it applied to individuals conditionally approved before January 20, 2025, and the district court later set up an enforcement framework (with a magistrate) for that carveout. The Ninth Circuit then issued an administrative stay of that enforcement order. Now this new order vacates the earlier stays and stays the district court’s injunctions "in their entirety" except for requiring services to admitted refugees inside the U.S.

- Does the new stay eliminate the prior carveout for conditionally approved refugees who remain overseas, meaning they receive no interim protection pending appeal?
- Was the district court permitted to implement/enforce the Ninth Circuit’s earlier carveout while the merits appeal was pending, or did the divestiture rule limit the district court’s authority?
- If the carveout is gone, is there any remaining judicial mechanism for the conditionally approved group to obtain interim relief?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes. The September 12 Ninth Circuit order removed the March 25 “carveout.” The panel vacated its earlier stays and then stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions “in their entirety, except as specified below.” The only exception the panel preserved was a requirement that the government reinstate cooperative agreements to provide reception-and‑placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. Condition‑ally approved refugees who remain abroad are not covered by that exception and therefore have no interim protection from the Ninth Circuit’s stay.
- The district court could attempt to implement its injunction, but its authority was subject to appellate supervision while the appeal was pending; the Ninth Circuit exercised that authority here (it administratively stayed the district court’s July 14 enforcement order). In short, the district court’s enforcement was vulnerable to appellate intervention once the appeal and the appellate stay questions were live.
- The practical route for the conditionally approved-but‑abroad group is to seek emergency relief from the Ninth Circuit (or ultimately the Supreme Court): an application to modify or dissolve the appellate stay. Any such request must satisfy the Nken stay factors the panel expressly adopted (with likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm being “the most critical” factors), and the panel’s opinion already signals that the government is likely to prevail on the core §1182(f) challenge—so relief will be difficult to obtain.

Explanation tied to the opinion
1) What the September 12 order did
- The panel first “vacate[d] our administrative stay issued July 18” and “vacate[d] our prior stay issued March 25, 2025.” It then issued a new stay “pending final resolution of this appeal” and stated that “The district court’s orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025 are stayed in their entirety, except as noted below.” (Order, Sept. 12, 2025.)
- The only “excepted” obligation the court preserved was a reinstatement requirement directed at reception-and‑placement cooperative agreements for refugees “who have been admitted to the United States.” The panel’s direction is limited to services for admitted refugees; it does not resurrect the March 25 carveout that had left the district‑court injunction operative for “individuals who were conditionally approved … before January 20, 2025” and who remained abroad. Because the new stay explicitly stays the district court’s orders “in their entirety” except for domestic services to admitted refugees, the earlier carveout for overseas, conditionally‑approved individuals has been erased by the new stay.

2) Did the district court have authority to implement the carveout while the appeal was pending?
- Factually, the district court issued a July 14 enforcement order to implement the carveout; the government moved on July 17; the Ninth Circuit “issued an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order” on July 18. (Order, Sept. 12, 2025.)
- That sequence shows the appellate court’s supervisory control while the appeal was pending: the district court can issue orders to enforce its injunction, but when an appeal and an appellate stay question are live the appellate court can step in and stay district‑court enforcement (which is what happened). The opinion adopts the Nken stay framework for assessing stays and demonstrates the panel’s willingness to intervene to protect the status of the appeal. The upshot: the district court’s ability to press the carveout while the Ninth Circuit was considering the appeal was limited and, practically, ended when the Ninth Circuit issued its administrative stay and later its comprehensive stay.

3) If the carveout is gone, what remedies remain for the conditionally‑approved abroad?
- The Ninth Circuit’s order itself says: “In the interim, this Order shall constitute a stay pending appeal and further order of this court.” That language makes the Ninth Circuit the proper first stop for any effort to restore the carveout now—i.e., an emergency application to the panel (or motions panel) asking the court to modify its stay.
- That application will be judged under the Nken factors the panel identified and quoted: (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) irreparable injury, (3) substantial injury to other parties, and (4) the public interest, with the first two being the most critical. The panel already signaled that the government is “likely to prevail” on the administration’s §1182(f) authority to suspend admissions—so persuading the court to reinstate a carveout for people abroad will be a steep climb.
- The only immediate, court‑ordered relief that survives the stay is the panel’s direction regarding reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees who have already been admitted to the United States; that does not help conditionally approved persons still overseas.

Bottom line
The Sept. 12 Ninth Circuit order eliminated the earlier limited carveout for conditionally approved refugees abroad. The district court’s July 14 attempt to implement that carveout was stayed by the Ninth Circuit, and any restoration of interim relief for people still overseas now requires convincing the Ninth Circuit (under the Nken framework) to modify its stay—an uphill task in light of the panel’s merits preview.

### Is the reinstatement directive an impermissible "nationwide" injunction, or is broad relief proper when enforcing a statutory duty?
Question:
The order directs reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services "to refugees who have been admitted to the United States," which seems to extend beyond the named plaintiffs and their specific clients. Given debates over "nationwide" or universal injunctions and the Supreme Court’s remedial guidance (e.g., Gill v. Whitford),

- Is this relief properly characterized as party-specific, universal, or something else (e.g., a form of APA vacatur-type effect)?
- How do courts decide the proper scope of injunctions when enforcing a statutory duty owed to a broad class of persons, especially at the appellate-stay stage?
- Does the posture (a stay order with an affirmative directive) change the analysis compared to a final merits injunction?
Answer:
Short answer
The Ninth Circuit’s reinstatement directive is best read not as an unfettered “nationwide” injunction but as a class‑targeted remedial order enforcing a statutory duty owed to a defined group — “refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” Pacito ties scope to the statute (8 U.S.C. § 1522) and to the imminence of harm, and it reached that result while expressly applying the stay‑pending‑appeal standard (Nken). In form it resembles an affirmative, vacatur‑like remedy restoring pre‑termination status for the statutory class; in substance the panel limited relief to the statutory class rather than granting an unconstrained universal injunction. The concurrence/dissent shows that the underlying statutory question (mandatory duty v. authorization) is contested and central to whether broad relief is proper on the merits.

Why that is so (doctrine and how Pacito applies it)
1) Stay standard controls the posture here.  
The panel framed its action as a stay pending appeal and applied the Nken stay factors: “we consider four factors: (1) Whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that he is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken v. Holder … Of these factors, the first two … are ‘the most critical.’” (Pacito, Sept. 12, 2025). That governs the interlocutory remedy here — not the same analysis a court would use for a final permanent injunction.

2) Relief is tied to a statutory duty and to the class the statute protects.  
The panel concluded (for the purposes of this interim order) that “Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government must provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission into the United States,” and it described specific statutory duties. Because the Government had terminated cooperative agreements and could not (at oral argument) show replacement arrangements, the court “directed” the Government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That language shows two limiting features: (a) the remedy is premised on a statutory duty; and (b) the beneficiary group is the statutory class — admitted refugees — not an unlimited universe of persons or a remedy untethered to plaintiffs’ injuries.

3) It is functionally similar to vacatur for the statutory class, but it is an affirmative, tailored order.  
The panel’s command that the Government reinstate the cooperative agreements had the practical effect of restoring the pre‑termination arrangements for the statutory class. That is vacatur‑like in effect for those agreements, but the court framed it as an affirmative reinstatement limited to agreements “necessary to provide” the § 1522 services and to refugees “who have been admitted.” So Pacito’s remedy is broad relative to the named plaintiffs (it reaches all admitted refugees), but it is not unlimited: it enforces the statute’s scope rather than imposing a programmatic rule beyond that scope.

How courts decide scope when enforcing statutory duties (and what Pacito illustrates)
- First look to the statute. The textual scope of the statutory right or duty defines who may claim relief and what relief would redress the injury. Pacito relied on § 1522’s text and on the statutory architecture to justify relief for admitted refugees.  
- Ask whether narrower relief would redress the plaintiffs’ injuries. If the plaintiffs’ injury flows from a government decision that affects a whole class and individual relief would be illusory, courts may fashion classwide or programmatic relief. Pacito found the Government’s wholesale termination of cooperative agreements made broader relief necessary to ensure the statute’s protections actually reached admitted refugees.  
- Balance equities and public interest (at the stay stage apply Nken). The panel weighed likelihood of success and irreparable harm and tailored relief to address imminent harm to the statutory class while staying other portions of the district court’s injunctions.  
- Avoid overbreadth: effective remedies must be tailored to redress the legally cognizable injury and must respect separation‑of‑powers concerns. The Pacito concurrence/dissent (Judge Lee) underscores that whether § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty or is merely permissive is decisive for how far a court may go in commanding executive action.

Does the interlocutory posture (stay with affirmative directive) change the analysis versus a final merits injunction?
Yes. Pacito shows three practical consequences of the interlocutory posture:
- Different governing test: the panel explicitly used Nken for the stay‑pending‑appeal decision. Nken’s factors (likelihood of success; irreparable injury; balance of harms; public interest) drive whether temporary relief is appropriate. A permanent injunction would require the usual merits and equitable showing on a fuller record.  
- Temporary, prophylactic tailoring: appellate courts at the stay stage may issue provisional relief narrowly designed to prevent irreparable harm pending appeal (here, reinstatement limited to admitted refugees and to the usual services under prior agreements). That relief is pragmatic and provisional; the final remedial scope will be revisited on the merits.  
- Greater sensitivity to systemic effects and practicality: because a stay affects the status quo nationwide and may impose operational burdens, an appellate court will try to confine interim relief to what is necessary to cure imminent statutory violations or harms — which is what the panel attempted by limiting relief to the admitted‑refugee class.

Bottom line for analysis and for litigation strategy
- Pacito treats the reinstatement order as a tailored, class‑oriented remedy enforcing a statutory obligation to admitted refugees — not as an open‑ended universal injunction unmoored from plaintiffs’ injuries.  
- Whether such broad relief is ultimately permissible turns on the merits (does § 1522 impose a mandatory duty?), on whether the relief is necessary to redress plaintiffs’ injuries, and on the equities/public interest under Nken at the stay stage (and under the standard for preliminary/permanent relief on final resolution).  
- The concurrence/dissent demonstrates the appealable tension: if § 1522 is discretionary rather than mandatory, the case for court‑ordered reinstatement is much weaker.

If you’re briefing or studying this opinion, quote the panel’s Nken paragraph and the reinstatement directive together: Pacito ties the stay decision and the affirmative reinstatement to the court’s view of a statutory duty and to the necessity of avoiding irreparable harm to the class the statute protects.

### What role, if any, does 8 U.S.C. § 1185(a) play here, since the panel’s analysis relies entirely on § 1182(f)?
Question:
The Executive Order invoked both §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), but the panel’s likelihood-of-success analysis rests on § 1182(f) and Trump v. Hawaii and does not meaningfully discuss § 1185(a).

- Does § 1185(a) confer any independent authority relevant to suspending refugee entry, or is it primarily about travel documents and regulation of entry/departure procedures without adding to § 1182(f)’s suspension power?
- Is there case law where § 1185(a) has been used to justify limits on refugee admissions (as opposed to general entry restrictions), or is it surplusage in this context?
Answer:
Short answer
In this Ninth Circuit stay order § 1185(a) plays no independent role. The panel acknowledged that the Executive Order invoked both 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), but the stay analysis rests entirely on § 1182(f) (and Trump v. Hawaii); the opinion neither analyzes § 1185(a) nor cites any authority treating § 1185(a) as an independent basis to suspend refugee admissions. In the court’s view §1182(f) alone was sufficient to show a likelihood of success on the merits for stay purposes, so §1185(a) is effectively unused (i.e., surplusage) in this interim order.

Why (and where the opinion shows it)
- The panel framed the stay inquiry under Nken and said the two most critical factors are likelihood of success and irreparable injury. It then concluded the government is “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions,” because the Order was issued under § 1182(f) and Trump v. Hawaii. The opinion emphasizes that “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” (Pacito order).
- Earlier the background records that the Executive Order invoked both §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), but the merits discussion quotes and relies on § 1182(f) (and Hawaii’s language that §1182(f) “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power”). There is no parallel discussion of §1185(a) in the stay analysis.

What this means for § 1185(a)
- From this order you cannot read an endorsement of §1185(a) as an independent or necessary source of authority to suspend refugee admissions—the court simply didn’t need it because it found §1182(f) dispositive for the stay analysis.
- The order also does not cite any case law using §1185(a) to justify limits on refugee admissions; the only precedent the panel relies on for entry-suspension authority is Trump v. Hawaii (as quoted). Thus Pacito does not create (or point to) precedent that §1185(a) independently supports a refugee-admissions suspension.

Bottom line
For purposes of this interim stay order §1185(a) is unused. Whether §1185(a) could independently justify such a suspension remains unaddressed here and therefore unresolved by this opinion; look to the forthcoming merits opinion (and other authorities) for any court analysis treating §1185(a) as more than redundant to §1182(f).
