Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # Governing Law: Entry Power, Refugee Resettlement Statutes, and the APA

This chapter explains the legal framework the Ninth Circuit applied in its expedited order in Pacito v. Trump. The panel confronted two blocs of law that pull in different directions. On one side is the President’s statutory authority to suspend or restrict the entry of aliens (invoked here through Executive Order No. 14163 and cited statutory bases 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a)), a field in which the Supreme Court in Trump v. Hawaii has instructed courts to give great deference. On the other side is the statutory and regulatory scheme Congress created in the Refugee Act (principally 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and implementing regulations) establishing federal obligations for “reception and placement” services for refugees admitted to the United States. The Administrative Procedure Act (APA) supplies the judicial review mechanism for agency decisions to terminate funding and cooperative agreements under that statutory scheme. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order applied these authorities to stay most of the district court’s injunctions while preserving—because of likely APA and statutory claims—the obligation to provide domestic resettlement services for refugees already admitted.

## Presidential entry authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and § 1185(a); deference principles from Trump v. Hawaii

8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is the statutory provision the panel relied on most heavily. It authorizes the President, “whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens or to impose restrictions on entry. In Pacito the panel treated § 1182(f) as the core textual source for the President’s suspension of the refugee admissions program and echoed the Supreme Court’s description in Trump v. Hawaii that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and “vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions.” Under that line of authority, a court’s review of an executive suspension under § 1182(f) is tightly circumscribed: the court asks essentially whether the President has made the predicate “finding” and whether the action stays within the statute’s textual limits; courts do not undertake a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s policy or national‑security justifications.

Section 1185(a) was cited as an additional statutory authority invoked by the executive order. It is another provision in the immigration statutes that relates to presidential and federal control over the admission and arrival processes for noncitizens. In Pacito the panel relied on the combination of these statutory authorities and the deferential Supreme Court precedent to conclude that the government was likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ challenge to the authority to suspend refugee admissions. That conclusion drove the court’s decision to stay the district court’s injunction to the extent it sought to enjoin implementation of the admission suspension.

For students: when you read executive‑power cases in the immigration context, identify the statutory grant the executive invokes, then check whether controlling precedent calls for heightened deference. In Trump v. Hawaii the Supreme Court treated § 1182(f) as a broad grant of discretion and limited judicial inquiry accordingly; the Pacito panel applied that same approach in assessing the stay request.

## Refugee Act of 1980 domestic resettlement framework: 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and § 1523

The Refugee Act, enacted in 1980, established a domestic resettlement framework whereby the federal government provides reception and placement services to refugees after admission. The key statutory text for the Pacito dispute is 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which sets out programmatic goals and particular services Congress contemplated the federal government would provide to newly admitted refugees. Section 1522 is paired with § 1523, which imposes reporting and accountability obligations on the Secretary to Congress.

Section 1522 frames federal responsibility in programmatic terms: it specifies objectives (economic self‑sufficiency, language acquisition, non‑discouraging cash assistance, equal opportunities for women) and authorizes the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) to provide certain projects, grants, contracts, and services. The implementing regulations (45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.) translate that statute into grant and cooperative‑agreement programs and describe the federal funding mechanisms and typical services delivered under those programs.

In Pacito the panel focused on § 1522 because the government had terminated the cooperative agreements through which federal reception and placement services had historically been delivered. The organizations that had received cooperative agreements and the refugees who had already been admitted claimed the terminations violated § 1522 and the APA. The Ninth Circuit concluded that, although the government was likely to prevail on the lawfulness of the President’s suspension of future admissions under § 1182(f), the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA challenge to the termination of funding for services to refugees who had already been admitted. That division of results arises from the different purposes and text of the two statutory regimes: one grants the President broad, deferential power to deny or restrict entry; the other sets out a statutory program for services to admitted refugees that the court treated as potentially mandatory and reviewable.

## Key statutory directives: § 1522(a)(1)(A) “shall” obligations (subject to available appropriations); § 1522(d)(2)(B) responsibilities for unaccompanied minors; § 1522(e)(7)(A) interim support and services for refugees <36 months in the U.S.

A close read of § 1522 is central to understanding the legal dispute. Subsection (a)(1)(A) appears in the statute under the heading “Conditions and consideration” and uses the verb “shall.” The provision, “to the extent of available appropriations,” directs the Director of ORR to make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement, to provide English language training, to ensure cash assistance is available in a way that does not discourage economic self‑sufficiency, and to ensure that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instruction. Those categorical statements were the basis for the plaintiffs’ argument that Congress imposed concrete obligations on the executive to provide reception and placement services to admitted refugees.

Subsection (d)(2)(B) concerns unaccompanied refugee children and directs that “[d]uring any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Subsection (e)(7)(A) requires the Secretary to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty‑six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” Two features matter here: statutory obligations are often framed with mandatory language (“shall”), but Congress tied many of those phrases to the availability of appropriations or to conditional phrases like “if necessary” and “as needed.”

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order treated the unqualified “shall” language in § 1522, together with the fact that Congress appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025, as sufficient to support a likely APA claim that the government could not simply terminate cooperative agreements and thereby frustrate the statutory scheme for admitted refugees. Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasized the opposite interpretive reading: he pointed to the statutory title (“Authorization for programs…”) and repeated use of “is authorized” elsewhere in § 1522 to argue the scheme is permissive—that Congress authorized programs but did not command the executive to operate them. That split highlights the interpretive question courts face when “shall” and permissive “authorized” language appear in the same statutory scheme and when appropriations language is included.

For students: when statutes combine “shall” with appropriations qualifiers or titles that include the word “authorization,” courts may legitimately disagree about whether Congress imposed a mandatory programmatic duty or merely authorized discretionary programs. The consequence is practical: if § 1522 imposes mandatory duties, courts can order the executive to perform them (subject to appropriations); if it merely authorizes, courts are less likely to compel further government action.

## Implementing regulations: 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (funding and services for refugees)

The Refugee Act’s statutory language is implemented by regulations administered principally by the Department of Health and Human Services and the Office of Refugee Resettlement. The panel referenced 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. as the regulatory framework that describes how federal funding is made available, the eligible activities and recipients, and the modalities (grants, contracts, cooperative agreements) through which reception and placement services are provided. Regulatory schemes serve two roles in litigation like Pacito: they operationalize statutory obligations (so that failing to follow them can show a statutory violation), and they create expectations and reliance interests for organizations that have historically performed refugee resettlement functions under cooperative agreements. Under the APA, a decision that is inconsistent with an agency’s own regulations or that fails to account for regulatory responsibilities can be set aside as arbitrary and capricious.

## Mechanisms to deliver services: contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofits under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)

Section 1522 expressly contemplates that reception and placement services may be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. Historically, the federal government has used cooperative agreements with domestic resettlement organizations to provide the initial reception and placement services that newly admitted refugees rely upon. A cooperative agreement is a funding instrument like a grant but typically contemplates substantial federal involvement in programmatic execution; grants are generally less agency‑directed; contracts are procurement instruments for procuring goods or services. In Pacito the government terminated cooperative agreements in February 2025; the Ninth Circuit expressly noted that history and the sudden termination when coupled with the government’s admitted uncertainty about securing replacement providers was central to the court’s conclusion that plaintiffs were likely to prevail on their APA challenge to the terminations for admitted refugees. The court therefore directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide the services required by § 1522 to refugees who had already been admitted.

## Appropriations context: FY 2025 migration and refugee assistance funding and partial rescission; “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier

Statutory directives to create programs are often qualified by appropriations language: “to the extent of available appropriations.” The Pacito order explicitly noted Congress’ FY 2025 appropriation for migration and refugee assistance—$3.178 billion—and the subsequent $800 million reduction enacted in the Rescissions Act of 2025. That factual and statutory context matters for two reasons. First, whether Congress has appropriated funds for the program influences whether a statutory “shall” can meaningfully impose an obligation the executive can be ordered to perform. Second, appropriations constraints limit what relief a court can grant: the APA cannot be used to force expenditure of funds beyond what Congress has appropriated. The panel’s citation to the FY 2025 figures supported its view that money for refugee assistance existed in the fiscal year and that the executive’s termination of cooperative agreements could not be justified simply by saying there would be no funding for the statutorily required reception and placement services. Judge Lee countered that even with appropriations in place, § 1522 is best read as an authorization scheme giving the executive discretion in deciding whether and how to operate programs. The split shows how appropriations facts enter statutory interpretation and equitable relief decisions.

## APA framework as relevant: review of agency terminations of funding and cooperative agreements for consistency with statute and reasoned decisionmaking

The Administrative Procedure Act provides the vehicle for reviewing agency actions like the termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of funding. Under the APA, a reviewing court may set aside agency action that is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law. The core elements of arbitrary‑and‑capricious review require an agency to examine relevant data, articulate a satisfactory explanation for its action, and consider alternatives and reliance interests when appropriate. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim—namely, that the termination of cooperative agreements (and the suspension of funding for reception and placement services) was inconsistent with the obligations imposed by § 1522 and/or was arbitrary and capricious given the government’s inability to show that replacement arrangements were in place.

Two APA issues arise repeatedly in such cases. First, is the termination a reviewable “final agency action”? Termination of cooperative agreements and withdrawal of funding typically is final and thus reviewable. Second, if reviewable, is the action “contrary to statute” because it frustrates a statutory duty? If § 1522 imposes mandatory duties and appropriations exist, termination without reasoned analysis or without lawful predicate risks being arbitrary and capricious and contrary to law. Conversely, if § 1522 is interpreted as merely authorizing programs and leaves the existence of programs to executive choice, then the termination may be lawful as an exercise of discretion not generally subject to judicial compulsion.

The Pacito interim order applied these principles to preserve the district court’s remedial reach over admitted refugees: it stayed the district court’s injunctions that would prevent the President from suspending entry, but it declined to stay the portion of relief that would require the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for refugees already in the United States. The panel reasoned that the plaintiffs were likely to succeed on their APA claim that the terminations were unlawful and that practical and humanitarian concerns (and statutory text) supported court‑ordered reinstatement pending final appellate resolution.

For students: when an agency alters long‑standing funding arrangements or terminates cooperative agreements, the APA inquiry focuses on whether the agency gave a reasoned explanation, considered reliance interests and alternatives, and acted consistently with statutory duties—and whether appropriations permit judicially ordered relief. The Pacito panel’s interim decision is a concise example of how courts balance deferential treatment of immigration and national‑security decisionmaking with ordinary APA review of domestic program administration affecting persons already within the United States.

Concluding note: Pacito illustrates a recurring tension in administrative and immigration law. The President’s broad, deferential statutory power to regulate entry under § 1182(f) can coexist uneasily with statutory programs that impose duties toward persons already admitted. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order applied deference to the entry suspension while preserving judicial oversight (via the APA and the substantive terms of the Refugee Act) over the executive’s termination of domestic resettlement programs for refugees already in the United States. The full merits opinion the court promised will resolve the interpretive questions sketched here, but the lawyering lesson is clear: when reading such opinions, parse the statutory text for mandatory verbs and appropriations qualifiers, track implementing regulations and established reliance interests, and identify where judicial deference doctrines (like those articulated in Trump v. Hawaii) circumscribe review.
