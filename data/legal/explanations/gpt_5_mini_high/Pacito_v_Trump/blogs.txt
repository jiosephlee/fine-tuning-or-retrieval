Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Hawaii, Again: Ninth Circuit Leans on § 1182(f) to Sustain Refugee Entry Suspension Pending Appeal

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump is a short, consequential reprise of the doctrinal posture the Supreme Court established in Trump v. Hawaii: when the President invokes 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend entry, courts give the executive very broad deference and will not undertake a searching inquiry into the President’s findings. That posture matters because it shapes where future challenges to refugee policy can realistically succeed. This stay preserves the President’s suspension of refugee admissions while carving out protection for resettlement services for refugees already physically in the United States — a split result that both fortifies presidential control over who may enter and preserves a path for statutory- and APA-based relief for those inside the country.

## What the Ninth Circuit did

On September 12 the Ninth Circuit vacated its earlier administrative stays and issued a stay pending appeal of the district court’s preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of large parts of Executive Order 14163 (the Trump administration’s suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program) and had ordered the reinstatement of State Department cooperative agreements for refugee processing and domestic reception-and-placement services. The panel concluded that the government is likely to prevail on its challenge to the legality of the EO’s suspension of entries under § 1182(f) and that the balance of Nken factors favors a stay. At the same time, the court said the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA-based claim arising from 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and therefore directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part, arguing that § 1522 is best read as an authorization rather than a mandatory funding duty.

The panel emphasized that a full merits opinion will follow; the order functions as an interlocutory balancing act that preserves the suspension of admissions pending appeal while protecting certain domestic statutory interests for refugees already admitted.

## How the panel applied Nken and leaned on Hawaii

The court applied the familiar four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder, but — consistent with Nken — treated the first two factors (likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury) as "the most critical." On the likelihood-of-success axis the panel leaned heavily on the Supreme Court’s treatment of § 1182(f) in Trump v. Hawaii, quoting that the statute “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power to impose entry restrictions,” and reiterating that the “sole prerequisite” in § 1182(f) is that the President have “find[ed]” the entry would be detrimental. In language lifted from Hawaii the panel said it “cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications,’” and concluded the President had “undoubtedly fulfilled” the § 1182(f) finding here.

The court then folded the other Nken factors into a traditional separation-of-powers and national-security posture: disrupting a presidential determination about who may enter implicates foreign policy and border control interests and counseled in favor of a stay. Those considerations, plus the panel’s view that the government has made a strong showing on § 1182(f), carried the day as to the suspension-of-entry claim.

## The statutory tug-of-war: § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act (§ 1522)

Pacito illustrates a common pattern: a broad presidential-entry statute (§ 1182(f)) colliding with a more detailed statutory regime established by Congress for refugee admissions and post-arrival services (the Refugee Act, implemented in part through 8 U.S.C. § 1522). The panel’s stay does not yet resolve that conflict on the merits, but it signals how a court guided by Hawaii will treat arguments that the Refugee Act cabin presidential power.

The panel stopped short of holding that § 1182(f) “trumps” the Refugee Act; instead, it concluded at the stay stage that the government is likely to prevail on its § 1182(f) defense to the challenge to the suspension of entries. That approach is doctrinally defensible under Hawaii: if § 1182(f)’s textual command — the President may “suspend the entry of . . . any class of aliens” — is read at face value and coupled with substantial deference to the President’s findings, a court will be reluctant to second-guess an entry suspension even in the face of a congressionally created admissions program.

But Pacito also demonstrates the limits of that deference. The panel recognized that the statutory framework governing reception and placement services — § 1522 — potentially imposes obligations on the executive that are subject to APA review. Thus even where the President’s proclamation may withstand direct challenge under § 1182(f), the administration cannot necessarily terminate the domestic machinery for resettlement without running into independent statutory and APA constraints.

## The Lee concurrence/dissent: is § 1522 an authorization or a mandate?

Judge Lee’s partial dissent crystallizes the text-based counterargument that will matter on the merits: § 1522’s caption reads “Authorization for programs,” many subsections use the verb “is authorized,” and several provisions are subject to the qualifier “to the extent of available appropriations.” From that vantage, § 1522 looks more like a permissive framework that lets the executive create and fund reception-and-placement programs when appropriations allow, rather than a hard command that the government must keep every past cooperative agreement in place regardless of policy choices.

The majority’s interim remedy — directing reinstatement of the cooperative agreements needed to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted — reflects a different reading. It treats parts of § 1522 as carrying binding obligations (or at least as creating reviewable expectations under the APA) when the government abruptly withdraws services that Congress anticipated as part of the resettlement regime. The textual indicators Judge Lee invokes (titles, “authorized,” and “to the extent of appropriations”) are real interpretive hooks, but they compete with subsections that use “shall” and mandate certain programmatic elements if funding is provided. The merits opinion the panel promised will need to reconcile those competing signals; the immediate effect, however, is pragmatic: the court will protect services for those already on U.S. soil while allowing the suspension of new admissions to stand for now.

## Doctrinal throughline from Hawaii — and what Pacito leaves open

Pacito is, in many respects, “Hawaii, again.” The central doctrinal lesson of the Supreme Court’s travel-ban decision — that § 1182(f) channels courts toward highly deferential review of presidential findings about entry — is the engine driving the panel’s stay. That throughline matters for litigants and policymakers. If Hawaii’s approach is applied routinely, courts will be reluctant to enjoin suspensions of particular classes of entrants so long as the President can plausibly make the statutorily required “finding.”

But Pacito also shows the contours of judicial pushback. Courts remain willing to police the executive’s domestic statutory obligations under the APA and statutory law. Plaintiffs who cannot prevail on a direct challenge to an entry suspension may still obtain meaningful relief by pressing statutory claims tied to resettlement services, funding, and procedures that govern people already present in the United States. In short, there is a two-track universe: (1) entry policy challenges face an uphill climb because of § 1182(f) and associated deference; (2) statutory and administrative-law claims about the government’s internal obligations and duties — particularly those affecting individuals already physically present — are more promising.

Finally, Pacito keeps open the possibility of a high-stakes merits decision. The panel’s stay tells us which way the wind is blowing, but the full opinion to come will have to address the hard question the stay order skirts: how, if at all, does a broad presidential entry power coexist with a detailed congressional scheme for refugee admission and post-arrival services? That question could, depending on the panel’s eventual reasoning, present a vehicle for further appellate review — and perhaps more Supreme Court attention.

## Downstream consequences

Practically speaking, the order buys the government the policy outcome it sought for the near term: refugee admissions remain suspended while the appeals court considers the merits. Operationally it also forces a partial course correction: the administration must reconstitute at least those cooperative agreements necessary to deliver reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. That will relieve some immediate humanitarian and programmatic strain on nonprofits and local resettlement networks, but it leaves thousands of conditioned approvals or applicants outside the United States in limbo.

For litigators, Pacito suggests a strategic pivot: if Hawaii-era precedent dooms direct statutory attacks on entry suspensions, counsel should focus on APA and statutory duty claims connected to services, benefits, and the administration of programs for people already in the U.S. For Congress, the case is a reminder that ambiguous statutory drafting invites executive latitude that can be decisive in periods of political change; legislators who want to cabin presidential authority over admissions will need clearer statutory language or appropriations constraints.

## Bottom line

Pacito is a forceful — if interim — application of the deference doctrine the Supreme Court articulated in Trump v. Hawaii: when the President invokes § 1182(f), courts will be reluctant to probe the substance of the President’s finding that entry would be “detrimental.” But the Ninth Circuit also made plain that deference to a proclamation does not give the executive a free pass to dismantle statutory programs that Congress established for people already admitted to the United States. That bifurcation is the practical and doctrinal takeaway: broad presidential authority at the border, paired with meaningful judicial policing of domestic statutory promises. The panel’s forthcoming full opinion will be the place to watch for how durable that balancing act proves to be.



# When ‘Authorized’ Becomes ‘Obligated’: The Ninth Circuit Reads § 1522 as Requiring Post‑Admission Refugee Services

## Why this case matters

The Ninth Circuit’s short, interim order in Pacito v. Trump crystallizes a sharply practical question at the intersection of immigration, administrative law, and appropriations: when the President lawfully pauses refugee admissions, does the executive retain discretion to cut off programs Congress wrote into law for refugees already inside the United States? The panel’s answer — at least for now — is: no. The court stayed most of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal but also expressly concluded that, under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government must continue to provide “reception and placement” services to refugees already admitted and ordered the reinstatement of the cooperative agreements that historically delivered those services.

That split result matters because it draws a bright line between two types of executive authority. On one hand, the court reaffirmed the well-established deference that Congress and courts afford presidential decisions to restrict entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), invoking Trump v. Hawaii and concluding the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the suspension of admissions. On the other hand, the panel made clear that deference to the President’s immigration prerogatives does not automatically immunize the executive from complying with statutory obligations enacted by Congress for people who are already in the country. The ruling thus preserves a route for judicial enforcement of statutory programs even when those programs collide with an Administration’s broader immigration policy.

## How the court got there — the short version of the order

The procedural posture is unusual and urgent. After President Trump issued an executive order (No. 14163) suspending refugee admissions and the State Department suspended USRAP funding and terminated cooperative agreements, refugees and resettlement organizations sued in the Western District of Washington. The district court entered preliminary injunctions; the government appealed and sought stays.

Applying the familiar Nken stay factors, the Ninth Circuit panel concluded that the government was likely to prevail on the merits of the plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of admissions under § 1182(f), because that statute “exudes deference” to the President and the only textual prerequisite is the President’s finding that entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” But the panel also concluded — and this is the pivot of the order — that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge based on 8 U.S.C. § 1522: the statute governing domestic resettlement services.

Noting the operative language of § 1522 (including subsection (a)(1)(A)’s direction that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement “shall” make available certain services “to the extent of available appropriations”), subsection (d)(2)(B)’s directives about unaccompanied minors, subsection (e)(7)(A)’s “shall develop and implement” language about early cash and medical support, the presence of implementing regulations, and recent appropriations for migration and refugee assistance, the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the usual reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The government’s concession that it lacked a concrete plan or progress toward replacing service providers seemed to clinch the point for the court.

Judge Lee concurred in part but dissented in part: he would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in their entirety, viewing § 1522 as an authorization rather than a mandate — a statutory grant of permissive authority, in his view, rather than a judicially enforceable command.

## The textual fight: ‘authorized’ vs. ‘shall,’ and the role of appropriations

At the heart of the disagreement is a basic statutory‑interpretation dispute that crops up in many contexts: which words control — the section title and repeated phrases like “is authorized to,” or the operative provisions that use “shall” and impose specific duties (even if qualified “to the extent of available appropriations”)?

Judge Lee presses the conventional intuition that a title reading like “Authorization for programs…” and widespread use of “is authorized” signal congressional non‑mandate. He reads the “shall” provisions in subsection (a) as imposing conditions on the way the executive must run the program if it elects to run the program — not as an independent command to run it in the absence of appropriations or other policy judgments. He points to qualifying language in subsections (d) and (e) — “if necessary,” “as needed” — as further indication of executive discretion.

The majority (in the panel order) emphasized the plain operative text in (a), the reporting requirement in § 1523, the long history of cooperative agreements implementing USRAP, and — importantly — the recent congressional appropriations for migration and refugee assistance. That combination supports a straightforward reading: Congress set up an ongoing statutory program and, insofar as funds are available, placed affirmative duties on the responsible officials to provide certain services once refugees are admitted. The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” is a limiting clause, not an eraser; it makes the obligations contingent on funds but does not convert an otherwise mandatory “shall” into a mere suggestion where appropriations exist.

Both readings are textually defensible. The court’s interim remedy leans on two practical anchors: statutory commands framed in mandatory terms and concrete evidence that Congress has provided money for the program. Taken together those elements make the government’s position — that it may unilaterally terminate cooperative agreements and leave admitted refugees without federally mandated services — a tougher sell.

## Doctrinal and practical consequences

The Ninth Circuit’s approach (as previewed in the order) draws an important doctrinal distinction. Decisions to admit or deny entry under § 1182(f) remain a domain in which courts defer heavily to the President. But once people are admitted, the executive’s discretion to undo statutory programs that Congress has established for those people is significantly narrower. That has three visible consequences.

First, it gives litigants a structural route to challenge administrative defunding or contractor terminations where statutes impose specific duties and Congress has appropriated money. Second, it imposes practical constraints on administrators who hope to effect broad policy changes by terminating contracts or cooperative agreements: there is real risk that courts will treat those terminations as unlawful if they frustrate statutorily mandated services. Third, the decision could ripple beyond refugee law: where Congress uses “to the extent of appropriations” language, courts will likely parse whether appropriations exist and thus whether a statutory mandate is in force.

This interim order is only the opening salvo. The government will press its reading on the merits; defenders of the district court’s injunction will press the contrary reading. The issues — text, structure, appropriations, and separation of powers — are classic candidates for further en banc or Supreme Court review, particularly given the national stakes and the tension between widely deferential immigration doctrines and ordinary principles of statutory enforcement.

## Bottom line

For now, the Ninth Circuit has split the difference: the Administration may pursue its suspension of admissions under § 1182(f), but it may not, at least preliminarily, abandon the statutory resettlement services Congress ordered for refugees already in the United States. The panel’s order is an important reminder that deference in immigration entry decisions does not give the executive carte blanche to ignore statutes that create rights or programs for people once they are within U.S. borders. The full merits opinion to come will be worth reading closely for how the panel resolves the textual tensions that underlie this practical and high‑stakes dispute.



# An Unusual Stay: Appellate Court Stays Most Relief but Orders Reinstatement of Refugee Contracts

## Why this matters

Last week’s Ninth Circuit interim order in Pacito v. Trump is notable not just for what the court paused, but for what it preserved. The panel stayed most of a district court’s preliminary injunctions that had blocked enforcement of President Trump’s January 2025 Executive Order that suspended refugee admissions (Exec. Order No. 14163). At the same time, however, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements that fund reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted to the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. That split—broad deference to the President’s entry suspension but an affirmative mandate to restore contracts benefiting those inside our borders—sharpens an important fault line: the difference between authority to exclude and the government’s statutory obligations to people who have already entered. The order is an emphatic example of an appellate court using stay powers to carve a narrow remedial path that both respects executive deference on national-security/immigration judgments and protects statutory programs and vulnerable beneficiaries while the merits are decided.

## What the panel did

The panel (with a partial concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee) vacated earlier motions-panel stays and issued a new stay pending appeal of the district court’s February 28 and March 24, 2025 injunctions. Applying the four-factor stay framework from Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), the court concluded the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the EO’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—relying on the deference articulated in Trump v. Hawaii—and that irreparable and public‑interest considerations support a stay of the district court’s relief blocking the entry suspension. At the same time, the court said the government is “not likely to prevail” on at least one of the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenges concerning the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements that funded reception and placement services required by § 1522. Given the government’s admitted uncertainty about having replacement providers in place and the statutory framework for reception-and-placement services, the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the § 1522 services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States—limited, the panel emphasized, to the usual and customary services those agreements supplied.

## How the panel justified this unusual split

Two legal currents run through the order. First, on the presidential authority side, the panel leaned heavily on Supreme Court precedent recognizing the special deference owed to the President’s findings about the national interest when it comes to entry and exclusion—most notably Trump v. Hawaii—reasoning that § 1182(f)’s text “exudes deference” and supplies a "single prerequisite" (the President’s finding) that the court will not second‑guess in a searching way on a stay motion.

Second, on the administrative‑law side, the panel identified a plausible APA claim tied to § 1522’s structure and the practical consequences of terminating cooperative agreements midstream. Section 1522 sets out the statutory architecture for domestic resettlement and provides for reception-and-placement services, repeatedly contemplates agency action that is tied to appropriations, and contains provisions—such as obligations regarding unaccompanied minors and development of alternative projects—that the court viewed as supporting plaintiffs’ claim that the executive branch cannot summarily halt the statutory scheme without adequate justification. The panel’s concern was practical as much as textual: the government told the court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” for a new resettlement agency, but at argument conceded uncertainty about whether any progress had been made. Faced with that uncertainty and a plausible APA claim, the panel tailored relief to preserve the statutory program for those already within the United States.

## An unusual remedial posture — stays that require affirmative, status‑preserving action

Appellate stays ordinarily suspend a lower‑court order; they are defensive. What makes the Ninth Circuit’s order striking is that, in staying the district court’s injunctions, the panel nevertheless required the government to take affirmative steps: reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to deliver § 1522 services to admitted refugees. That combination can be understood, however, as a classic equitable and status‑quo approach—the court fashioned a tailored condition on its stay to prevent the disruption of a statutory scheme and irreparable harm to beneficiaries while the merits proceed. In other words, the panel did not order the government to create a new program or to spend money beyond appropriations; it ordered the government to restore the operative contracts and deliver the “usual and customary” services that implemented the statutory scheme before the administration pulled the plug.

That sort of conditional or tailored stay is not without precedent in federal practice: courts routinely condition stays or injunctions to protect third‑party interests or to maintain the practical status quo. But it does sit at an uncomfortable intersection of judicial supervision and executive contracting discretion—and it is the sort of remedial posture that is likely to be contested further because it edges into decisions administrators typically make as part of policy realignment.

## Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent — the statutory text fight

Judge Lee’s partial dissent sharpens the doctrinal debate the court will have to resolve on the merits: is § 1522 an authorization that leaves the executive free to fund or not fund programs, or does it impose enforceable duties? Lee emphasizes the title of § 1522—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the repeated use of the phrase “is authorized,” reading the statutory scheme as permissive. He treats the use of “shall” in § 1522(a) as conditional—“in providing assistance under this section . . . the Director shall”—arguing that the “shall” only governs the manner in which programs must be run if funding is provided, not a freestanding command that compels the government to fund programs when it chooses not to. From that vantage, a full stay of the district court’s injunctions, including the requirement to reinstate agreements, would be appropriate.

The panel majority, by contrast, read some parts of § 1522 and related provisions as carrying mandatory obligations that the agencies cannot shrug off without reasoned administrative action. That textual battle—authorization versus mandate, conditioned by appropriations—will be central to the forthcoming merits opinion.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several doctrinal and practical consequences flow from this interim order.

First, on the law of presidential power and immigration, the Ninth Circuit’s approach underscores a robust distinction between entry authority and post‑entry statutory entitlements. Courts, in other words, may give broad deference to a President’s decision to stop admissions under § 1182(f) while scrutinizing agency steps that impair statutory programs for people already present in the United States.

Second, for administrative law, the order signals that agencies cannot rely on broad policy directives to summarily terminate programmatic infrastructure that implements a statute without confronting APA constraints and reliance interests. Even when an executive branch enjoys deference on a national‑security or foreign‑policy judgment, that deference does not automatically immunize simultaneous curtailments of programs that Congress has created to serve beneficiaries within the country.

Third, for contracting and grant practice, the decision should sharpen agency caution. Terminating cooperative agreements that implement statutory programs during a policy sweep invites quick litigation and—if the court finds a plausible statutory right—can lead to judicially ordered reinstatement. Agencies that want to change course will need to document reasoned decisionmaking, account for reliance interests, and plan transitional steps for beneficiaries.

Fourth, for appropriations law and separation‑of‑powers concerns, the order raises tricky questions. The Ninth Circuit limited its directive to reinstating agreements necessary to provide services “to the extent of available appropriations,” and the opinion notes the FY 2025 appropriations and partial rescission. But any eventual judicial command that effectively compels expenditure of funds that Congress has rescinded or withheld would strain separation‑of‑powers principles and likely precipitate high‑stakes litigation.

Finally, appellate management itself may take cues from this decision. The panel’s surgical remedy shows how an appellate court can thread the needle between protecting executive prerogatives and preserving statutory programs for vulnerable groups—by tailoring a stay that both defers to presidential exclusion authority and requires the maintenance of services necessary for those already inside the country.

## What to watch next

The Ninth Circuit has promised a full opinion on the merits. That forthcoming decision will need to resolve the core statutory question Judge Lee framed: whether § 1522 imposes mandatory duties enforceable against the executive, and how “to the extent of available appropriations” affects those duties. How the court treats the government’s procedural and substantive justification for terminating cooperative agreements also will matter: will the panel require a searching administrative‑record explanation or conclude the termination was arbitrary and capricious?

If the Ninth Circuit reaffirms the interim order, expect the government to weigh immediate further emergency relief, possibly to the Supreme Court, on either separation‑of‑powers or stay‑condition grounds. Either way, agencies and NGOs should take note: abrupt contract terminations that accompany broad policy shifts can be reversed quickly in the courts, at least where statutory frameworks and beneficiaries’ reliance interests are involved.

In short, Pacito’s interim order is a compact lesson in judicial triage—respecting the broad headwind of presidential entry power while using equitable tools to preserve statutory programs that serve people who are already on U.S. soil. The full merits opinion, whenever it issues, will tell us whether that balance endures.



# APA as Backstop: Agency Defunding Falters Even as the President’s Entry Bar Stands

## Why this matters

The Ninth Circuit’s short interim order in Pacito v. Trump crystallizes an important doctrinal split: courts will treat presidential control over who may enter the country with unusually high deference, but they will not allow executive branch maneuvering — through procurement or funding changes — to evade statutory obligations that protect people already in the United States. Practically, that means a president can plausibly suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and courts will be reluctant to second‑guess that judgment, but agencies remain subject to the Administrative Procedure Act when they cut off services that Congress has authorized and funded for refugees already admitted.

The panel’s action is also an object lesson for administrative lawyers. An executive order may change broad policy; it does not, by itself, erase binding statutory duties or immunize implementing agencies from APA review. Where Congress has appropriated money and paired it with programmatic duties, abrupt terminations of cooperative agreements and a failure to ensure continuity of services invite judicial intervention.

## What the Ninth did

On an expedited record, the Ninth Circuit vacated its earlier, broader administrative stays and issued a stay pending appeal of the district court’s February and March preliminary injunctions — except for the district court’s order that the government reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The panel applied the four Nken stay factors, emphasizing that the first two (likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm) are the most critical.

The panel concluded the government was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f), relying on the strong deference articulated in Trump v. Hawaii: the statute requires only a presidential “find[ing]” that entry would be “detrimental,” and the court will not engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. But the court also signaled that the government was unlikely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA challenges concerning the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements and the Department of State’s suspension of USRAP funding. For that reason the panel preserved, as an interim matter, the district court’s order directing reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to carry out the reception and placement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522 for refugees already admitted.

## How the court framed the split

The order draws a clear doctrinal line. Under § 1182(f), the President’s authority to suspend classes of entrants is textually capacious and has been read to “exude[] deference” to the President. That part of the case is essentially over, at least for stay purposes: the panel found the Government has “undoubtedly fulfilled” the statute’s prerequisite “finding,” and the stay factors about irreparable harm and public interest favored the Government on that question.

By contrast, the panel treated the agencies’ termination of cooperative agreements — a procurement and funding decision — as squarely reviewable under the APA. The court anchored that review in the Refugee Resettlement statutes, particularly § 1522, which the panel read as imposing substantive reception‑and‑placement duties on federal agencies (subject, of course, to appropriations). The panel noted Congress had appropriated substantial funds for migration and refugee assistance for FY2025 (the opinion cites $3.178 billion, reduced by an $800 million rescission), and it emphasized that the government had terminated basically all cooperative agreements even while representing to the district court that it was “actively preparing” replacement arrangements but at oral argument admitting uncertainty as to any progress. Against that background, the panel found a serious risk that the agencies’ actions could be “contrary to law” or arbitrary and capricious under the APA, and it preserved relief that would ensure the statutorily described services continue for refugees already in the country.

## Judge Lee’s partial dissent — the counterreading

Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part. He agreed with the majority’s treatment of presidential power under § 1182(f) — he would have stayed the injunctions that interfere with the President’s moratorium on admissions — but he read § 1522 differently. Lee views § 1522 as an authorization rather than an unconditional command: the subchapter’s title (“Authorization for programs …”) and repeated statutory phrasing that officials “are authorized” to provide programs, together with the qualification “to the extent of available appropriations,” point toward discretionary executive programs, not mandatory duties that courts should enforce. From that vantage, he would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in full because he thinks the law does not require the executive branch to operate the programs the plaintiffs identify.

Lee’s textualist argument is familiar and forceful: “authorization” language and appropriations qualifiers ordinarily signal congressional choice, not judicially enforceable obligations. The majority, however, took the presence of several “shall” provisions, reporting requirements, an established procurement practice of cooperative agreements, and evidence of available appropriations as sufficient to create a serious legal claim that the government crossed statutory bounds or failed to engage in reasoned decisionmaking.

## The analytic takeaway: APA remains the backstop

The Ninth’s interim order illustrates — in concrete, operational terms — how the APA functions as a backstop even where the President exercises broad, statutorily granted immigration powers. Two linked points matter for practitioners:

First, presidential control of entry is a separate question from agency compliance with statutory duties to beneficiaries already in the United States. Trump‑style deference to the President under § 1182(f) largely forecloses a court’s willingness to pry into the motives behind a suspension of admissions; it does not, by itself, absolve agencies of statutory responsibilities or license them to jettison longstanding cooperative arrangements without reasoned analysis.

Second, agencies must provide a reasoned record when they terminate programs or contracts that carry out statutory duties, and they must reckon with appropriations and reliance interests. The panel flagged precisely those facts: Congress had appropriated funds, the statute contains programmatic directives and reporting duties, and the government terminated cooperative arrangements while offering only vague assurances that replacements were being procured. That combination is a classic set of facts that invites APA challenges claiming action “not in accordance with law” or “arbitrary and capricious.”

## Practical counsel for agency lawyers and counsel for plaintiffs

For agency counsel: when executing a change in policy that depends on EOs, do not treat procurement or funding actions as ancillary decisions immune from APA scrutiny. Document the legal basis for any termination or suspension, explain how statutory duties will be met for current beneficiaries, and show a concrete transition plan if services will cease. If appropriations are genuinely insufficient, explain that allocation decision in the administrative record; if funds exist, be prepared to show why reallocation is permissible and reasonable.

For plaintiffs and public‑interest counsel: bringing APA claims focused on statutory duties and contractor reliance can be an effective vehicle to protect beneficiaries on the ground even where challenges to entry suspensions are unlikely to succeed. The Ninth’s order is an encouraging signal that courts will look beyond headline EOs to the nuts and bolts of how agencies implement policy.

## Broader doctrinal and downstream effects

Pacito demonstrates a durable division in modern administrative and immigration law: courts will continue to grant the political branches wide latitude to set who may enter, but they will police how agencies treat statutory programs and currently entitled beneficiaries. That split may shape future litigation strategies: challengers who cannot overcome the high bar to impugn presidential findings under § 1182(f) may instead press APA claims aimed at agency implementation, procurement, and funding decisions.

The order also presses on a perennial question about the legal force of “to the extent of available appropriations.” Agencies can defend programmatic decisions on the ground of limited budgetary resources, but where Congress has made substantial appropriations and the record shows abrupt termination with no plausible transition plan, courts will scrutinize whether the agency has exceeded its authority or acted arbitrarily.

Finally, the panel’s narrow remedial choice — preserving relief only as to refugees already admitted — reflects a feature of equitable relief in APA cases: courts will often limit remedies to protect settled expectations and current beneficiaries while staying other parts of lower‑court relief that implicate broader policy judgments.

## Bottom line

Pacito’s interim order is a useful reminder of the two distinct checks on executive power that live within the federal courts: extraordinary deference to presidential findings on who may enter, and robust APA review of how agencies allocate resources and carry out statutory programs for people already here. An executive order can change admission policy; it cannot, without more, delete statutory duties or immunize agencies from the “hard look” required by the APA. The full opinion to follow will matter greatly, but for now administrative lawyers should take away the plain lesson: EOs may steer policy, but the APA remains the backstop for those the law already protects.
