Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Standing, Speculation, and FOIA: The D.C. Circuit Closes the Door on Suits to Force OSC Investigations

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is an important drill-down on the boundaries of Article III standing when plaintiffs try to use the courts to compel an executive-branch enforcement body to act. At stake is a recurring strategic question in FOIA and administrative-law litigation: can a requester sue a watchdog or prosecutor (here, the Office of Special Counsel) to force an investigation into an agency’s alleged withholding of records, or must a challenger proceed directly against the agency that withheld the records?

The panel’s answer is emphatic: not here. By treating the asserted injuries as either traceable to the withholding agency (DOJ) or as too attenuated to be redressed by a court order compelling OSC to investigate, the opinion tightens both causation and redressability requirements for procedural- and informational-injury claims. That puts a practical limit on efforts to use the courts to marshal third-party enforcement agencies into action.

## What the court held

America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought DOJ emails under FOIA and separately asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged practice of not searching employee email accounts without consent. OSC declined, on the view that a separate FOIA-triggered enforcement provision (§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) had not been satisfied. AFL sued; the district court found error but ultimately remanded. On appeal the D.C. Circuit did not reach the statutory question. Instead it ordered supplemental briefing on Article III standing and concluded that AFL lacked standing. The court vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

The court rejected both of AFL’s standing theories. First, any informational injury from DOJ’s withholding was traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain by which an OSC investigation might lead to MSPB sanctions and then to DOJ compliance was too speculative to support redressability. Second, AFL’s claimed informational interest in receiving notices or investigative fruits from OSC failed because § 1216, even if it requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, creates no entitlement to information—section 1214’s notice rules do not automatically attach in § 1216 investigations and, in any event, OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 matters is discretionary. Because AFL could not show a concrete injury likely to be redressed by the relief sought, it lacked Article III standing.

## How the court reasoned — tightening causation and redressability

The panel’s analysis rests on well-known Article III building blocks: injury-in-fact, causation (traceability), and redressability. But its application of those principles is notable for how insistently it applies recent Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit precedent to reject attenuated causation theories.

On AFL’s first theory—that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused AFL’s informational injury from DOJ’s continued nondisclosure—the court emphasized that the immediate source of the alleged harm was DOJ’s withholding decision. Any remedial sequence that would transform an OSC investigation into document production required several contingent events by independent actors (OSC’s decision to investigate aggressively, the MSPB’s choice and potential sanctions, DOJ’s reaction). Citing the Supreme Court’s recent boundary-setting on speculative causation and redressability, the court treated that multi-step chain as too conjectural to sustain standing. The point is doctrinally familiar: causation cannot rest on speculation about the “unfettered choices” of actors not before the court.

On the second theory—that AFL had a standalone informational right to notices or investigation outcomes from OSC—the panel leaned on the principle that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive information. It concluded § 1216, as read here, does not create such an entitlement to OSC-produced information. Nor does § 1216 automatically import § 1214’s notice regime; the latter’s application to § 1216 investigations is discretionary under controlling precedent. Without an identifiable statutory entitlement to the investigatory information, AFL’s injury was not the kind of concrete informational injury that carries Article III standing.

Throughout, the opinion anchors itself in Summers’ warning against “procedural rights in vacuo” and recent decisions underscoring that a procedural-statute claim must be tethered to a concrete, redressable interest. In short, relaxed standing for procedural claims is not a license to sue whenever a plaintiff can allege a statutory procedural entitlement in the abstract.

## Why this ruling matters doctrinally — and what it leaves open

Doctrinally, the decision reinforces two strands of modern standing law. First, it underscores that the relaxed or “procedural” standing line remains narrow: plaintiffs must show (not just allege) that the violation of a procedural right injured some concrete interest and that litigation will likely redress that injury. Second, it reiterates that redressability is measured against how a court’s order will bind the defendant, not against how persuasive a court’s reasoning might be to third parties.

Those emphases make sense in institutional terms. They keep courts from being turned into after-the-fact managers of executive enforcement choices and preserve the core of Heckler v. Chaney—that an agency’s prosecutorial discretion is usually not subject to judicial compulsion. Standing doctrine, in this view, functions as a guardrail against federal judicial micromanagement of executive priorities.

But important questions remain open. The D.C. Circuit expressly declined to reach the underlying statutory interpretation: whether § 1216 independently imposes a duty on OSC to investigate FOIA-withholding claims regardless of the narrow FOIA-trigger set out in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The district court had thought § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not limit § 1216, but because AFL lacked standing the panel did not and did not need to decide that statutory dispute. That leaves substantive statutory questions unresolved, and it preserves a path for future litigation if a plaintiff can establish the requisite standing facts.

The ruling also leaves unsettled whether a different kind of plaintiff—a person able to show a more direct injury traceable to OSC’s nonaction, or a plaintiff seeking different relief that would bind OSC more directly—could clear the Article III bar. The panel treated as decisive the speculative nature of the chain from OSC non-investigation to eventual production; a case presenting a tighter causal link (for example, where OSC’s investigatory powers produce statutorily required notices that themselves would compel agency action) could yield a different standing result.

## Practical effects and downstream implications

For FOIA practitioners and public-interest litigants, the immediate practical takeaway is straightforward: if your goal is disclosure, your most reliable route remains litigation directly against the withholding agency under FOIA rather than suing a separate enforcement body to force it into action. Victory in a FOIA suit that produces a court order and attorney’s fees can trigger OSC’s FOIA-specific enforcement obligation under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), but absent those judicial predicates plaintiffs cannot count on using OSC as a backdoor to compel production.

More broadly, the decision likely will be cited to resist suits that seek to compel investigative action by other oversight actors—inspectors general, independent counsel offices, or similar entities—where any remedy would depend on successive decisions by other agencies or political actors. The combination of standing scrutiny and Heckler’s protection of enforcement discretion will make such suits difficult unless the plaintiff can identify a direct, legally protected interest harmed by the overseer’s refusal to act.

That institutional insulation could, however, have democratic costs. OSC’s willingness to investigate can be an important check when FOIA litigation stalls or when systemic agency practices make it difficult for individual requesters to obtain relief. If litigants cannot hold a watchdog accountable in court for declining to investigate, enforcement will be left to internal executive incentives, political oversight, or Congress. That dynamic may encourage legislative responses: Congress could clarify OSC’s investigatory duties or create a clearer entitlement to particular investigatory information to stabilize the standing analysis. Absent such changes, critics will likely argue that the decision lets an important layer of accountability escape meaningful judicial scrutiny.

## Bottom line

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer tightens the Article III constraints on attempting to use the courts to force executive enforcement action. The D.C. Circuit’s refusal to rest standing on hypothetical chains of agency action reinforces recent Supreme Court emphases on non-speculative causation and meaningful redressability, and it preserves the executive’s discretion not to prosecute or investigate. The ruling narrows one avenue that FOIA challengers have used to seek accountability, leaving litigants to pursue more direct FOIA claims against withholding agencies or to press Congress for clearer enforcement tools. What it does not do—because it did not need to—is resolve the statutory question about the scope of OSC’s investigatory duty under § 1216, so that issue remains available for another day if a litigant can clear Article III’s gate.



# Who Polices FOIA Misconduct? The Unresolved Tension Between § 1216 and FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)

## Why this case matters

At stake in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is how—and how often—government employees who allegedly flout FOIA will be investigated and disciplined. Congress created two statutory touchpoints for policing FOIA withholding: a general investigative power in 5 U.S.C. § 1216 and a FOIA‑specific disciplinary trigger in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The district court read § 1216’s command that the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings as independent of FOIA’s referral trigger. The D.C. Circuit did not reach that statutory question, however; it vacated the district court’s merits rulings and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of Article III standing. The result leaves the central statutory tension unresolved—and therefore leaves open important questions about OSC’s role, prosecutorial discretion, and the incentives around FOIA compliance.

## What the D.C. Circuit held

The D.C. Circuit’s opinion (Judge Katsas) is narrow: AFL lacks Article III standing to press its claim that OSC was required to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails for FOIA responses. The court framed its decision around the familiar triptych of standing requirements—injury in fact, causation, and redressability—and emphasized that a “procedural right in vacuo” cannot sustain jurisdiction (invoking Summers).

AFL advanced two theories of concrete, informational injury: first, that OSC’s refusal to investigate prevented DOJ from producing the FOIA records AFL sought; and second, that OSC’s refusal denied AFL the notices and information it would receive if OSC were conducting a § 1214-style investigation. The court rejected both.

On the first theory, the court found the causal chain too speculative. Any informational injury from DOJ’s nondisclosure was traceable to DOJ, not OSC; linking OSC’s non-investigation to the eventual release of records would require speculation about OSC’s enforcement choices, the independent actions of the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), and DOJ’s responses—precisely the kind of attenuated causal story Lujan and later cases disfavor.

On the second theory, the court observed that even if § 1216 requires OSC to investigate FOIA allegations, that statute does not, standing alone, entitle a complainant to receive investigatory notices. As AFL conceded (and the court noted), Carson v. OSC, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), treats OSC’s decision whether to apply § 1214’s procedural safeguards in a § 1216 inquiry as discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to information, there is no cognizable informational injury.

Because AFL could not clear even the “relaxed” hurdles that sometimes attend alleged procedural‑rights violations, the court remanded for dismissal and left the core statutory question—whether § 1216 independently mandates OSC investigations of FOIA withholding—open.

## The statutory puzzle

Read side-by-side the two provisions create a real interpretive puzzle. Section 1216(a)(3) says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” Subsection (c) goes on to allow OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), by contrast, is tailored and conditional: if a court orders production, awards attorneys’ fees, and specifically questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted.”

One plausible harmonizing reading is that Congress gave OSC a general duty and power to investigate FOIA allegations under § 1216 while also creating an additional, mandatory referral obligation for courts that have made particular findings under § 552. Alternatively, FOIA’s tailored three‑part trigger might be read as the exclusive, lex specialis mechanism that limits when OSC must act in the FOIA context; under that view, § 1216 authorizes OSC to investigate but does not impose an independent, judicially enforceable mandate to do so. The district court favored the former reading on the merits; the D.C. Circuit did not decide which view prevails.

Carson complicates the landscape further. Even if § 1216 imposes a duty to investigate, Carson indicates OSC retains discretion about whether to adopt § 1214’s procedural protections (including notice to complainants) in a § 1216 inquiry. That tension helps explain the D.C. Circuit’s skepticism that AFL had a statutory entitlement to information it could claim as injury.

## Broader doctrinal and institutional implications

Why does this matter beyond doctrinal parsing? The answer lies in enforcement design and incentives. If § 1216 truly mandates OSC investigations of FOIA complaints regardless of whether a court has made the FOIA‑specific findings of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), OSC’s docket and MSPB’s role could expand significantly. That would create stronger institutional deterrence against arbitrary withholding but also risks: greater burdens on OSC’s limited resources, increased MSPB adjudication, and the potential for politicized or frivolous complaints to create chilling effects on intra‑agency communications.

If, instead, FOIA discipline is largely channeled through § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), disciplinary scrutiny will hinge on FOIA plaintiffs obtaining the relatively rare combination of a production order, fees award, and an explicit judicial finding of arbitrariness. That approach concentrates enforcement on cases that have already been judicially validated but leaves most alleged FOIA misconduct outside the disciplinary pipeline.

There are also separation‑of‑powers and administrative law implications. Courts are generally reluctant to micromanage prosecutorial choices (Heckler v. Chaney), but statutes that impose mandatory duties can change the reviewability calculus. Whether Congress intended § 1216 to be a broadly self‑executing investigative duty or a grant of authority subject to OSC’s prosecutorial discretion matters both for judicial review and for the practical availability of mandamus or APA relief.

## What to watch next

Because the D.C. Circuit disposed of this case on standing grounds, the status quo remains: no controlling circuit decision resolves whether § 1216 independently requires OSC to investigate FOIA withholding. Practitioners who want to trigger OSC action should regard the FOIA‑specific route—obtaining the three § 552 elements in district court—as the clearest statutory lever. For litigants aiming to create a justiciable claim to compel OSC to act, the lesson of AFL is plain: plead a concrete, non‑speculative injury that is directly traceable to OSC’s conduct and likely to be redressed by a judicial order.

Congress could also eliminate the uncertainty by clarifying whether OSC’s investigatory command in § 1216 is mandatory and what procedural protections a complainant may expect. Absent legislative action, a future case that presents a plaintiff with a closer causal nexus—perhaps a FOIA litigant who already has secured the § 552(a)(4)(F) prerequisites and then sues to enforce OSC’s “shall promptly initiate” duty—would be the likeliest vehicle for courts to confront the merits.

For now, the D.C. Circuit’s jurisdictional ruling leaves the central policy choice—should OSC be a broad, proactive FOIA disciplinarian or a more limited backstop tied to judicial findings—unanswered. That lack of answer matters for the accountability architecture Congress built around FOIA, and it will keep FOIA practitioners, watchdog groups, and agencies watching for the next case or legislative move that finally forces a choice.



# Chaney’s Long Shadow: Why Private Plaintiffs Can’t Conscript Executive Enforcement

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer (Oct. 3, 2025) is a short, crisp reminder that Article III standing and the nonreviewability of enforcement choices remain powerful limits on efforts to use the federal courts to police executive-branch priorities. At stake was a familiar if consequential tactic: a private requester who sued under FOIA tried to force the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) — a statutory watchdog with authority to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings — to open an investigation. The panel did not reach the novel statutory question whether OSC’s “shall … conduct an investigation” language is self-executing. Instead, relying on standing doctrine and the logic of Heckler v. Chaney, the court concluded AFL lacked Article III standing and sent the case back for dismissal. The practical lesson is straightforward: plaintiffs cannot generally conscript the Executive’s enforcement machinery unless they can show a concrete, redressable injury traceable to the defendant.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

Judge Katsas’s opinion begins with familiar standing basics: a plaintiff must show injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability. AFL framed its injury as procedural (OSC’s alleged statutory duty to investigate) but buttressed that claim with two putative informational injuries. First, AFL said OSC’s refusal to investigate had prevented it from obtaining the FOIA records it sought from DOJ. Second, AFL said it was deprived of the notices and information that OSC would provide in a §1214-style corrective investigation.

The court disposed of both theories. The claimed FOIA-disclosure injury was (the court held) traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain from an OSC investigation to DOJ’s production was too speculative to support standing. An OSC probe might lead to corrective or disciplinary action, which might lead the MSPB to sanction employees, which might lead DOJ to produce records — a chain the court characterized as dependent on independent actors and discretionary choices. That analysis leaned on Chaney’s core insight about prosecutorial discretion and recent standing decisions cautioning against speculation about the choices of third parties.

The second informational theory failed for a different reason. Informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive information; AFL could not point to any statutory right to the notices it sought. To the contrary, AFL conceded — and the court relied on precedent — that OSC’s decision whether to apply §1214’s procedural protections in a §1216 investigation is discretionary. Absent an entitlement to information, there was no concrete informational injury that a judicial order could redress.

Because AFL could not clear the relaxed standing hurdles that apply when a procedural right is asserted, the D.C. Circuit vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

## Chaney vs. Norton v. SUWA — the doctrinal fault line

This opinion is best read as the D.C. Circuit steering between two established doctrines. Norton v. SUWA created a narrow route for compelled action suits: where a statute imposes a discrete, nondiscretionary duty, courts may compel the agency to act. Heckler v. Chaney, by contrast, announced the baseline rule that where enforcement and prosecutorial choices are involved, courts generally should not substitute judicial judgment for executive prioritization.

Here the court did not directly settle whether §1216’s “shall … conduct an investigation” is in the SUWA category of judicially enforceable, nondiscretionary obligations. Instead it closed the case on standing grounds, applying Chaney’s policy logic in the redressability and causation analysis: the relief AFL sought (records from DOJ, or investigatory notices from OSC) would depend on a sequence of discretionary choices by agencies not all before the court. That made redressability remote even if a court ordered OSC to investigate. So while SUWA remains theoretically available when a statute imposes a clear, individualized duty, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion signals caution about stretching that doctrine to convert generalized oversight commands into private entitlements.

## What this means going forward

The decision has practical and doctrinal ripple effects:

First, FOIA requesters remain anchored to FOIA litigation for immediate relief. Using an independent oversight body to force disclosure looks like an uphill climb: a plaintiff must show the denial to the oversight body caused an injury and that a judicial order would directly produce the sought-after relief.

Second, the opinion discourages a popular litigation strategy of “conscript[ing]” executive watchdogs — OSC, Inspectors General, ethics offices, and similar bodies — as proxy enforcers simply because Congress gave them investigatory authority. Unless the statute creates a plaintiff-centered entitlement or the plaintiff can demonstrate a concrete, traceable injury, Article III will bar the suit.

Third, the ruling reinforces separation-of-powers concerns. Courts are wary of turning themselves into referees of executive enforcement priorities. That restraint preserves executive discretion over resource allocation and avoids micromanaging enforcement decisions, but it also narrows private avenues for accountability when internal enforcement offices decline to act.

Finally, Congress that cares about private enforcement or accountability has levers if it wants a different outcome: it can create clear, non-discretionary duties, provide an express private right of action, or structure statutory remedies so that a plaintiff can show a concrete, individualized entitlement to information or relief. Absent that, litigants seeking to compel investigatory action will face an uphill Article III barrier.

## Bottom line

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a tidy chapter in an ongoing story: procedural statutory language that looks commanding on paper does not automatically give private parties standing to drag enforcement agencies into court. The D.C. Circuit did not decide whether OSC’s statutory “shall” is substantive and judicially enforceable; it found that, on the facts here, AFL lacked a concrete, redressable injury, and that the relief it sought would depend on discretionary choices by independent actors. For plaintiffs hoping to make enforcement offices into litigation tools, Chaney’s long shadow remains a powerful deterrent.



# After AFL v. Greer: A Practitioner’s Playbook for Prompting OSC FOIA Discipline the Right Way

## Why this case matters
The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer closes off a tempting shortcut for FOIA plaintiffs: suing the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to force it to police agency FOIA practices. Instead of resolving the statutory tug-of-war between OSC’s investigatory powers under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 and FOIA’s enforcement-trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), the court disposed of AFL’s suit on Article III grounds. For practitioners, the practical takeaway is clear: if you want OSC to take disciplinary action over a withholding, the reliable route is to use FOIA litigation to generate the statutory triggers that make OSC’s involvement mandatory — don’t expect a standalone suit against OSC to get you there.

## What the court said (in brief)
The panel (Judge Katsas) held that AFL lacked Article III standing and therefore the district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction. AFL had asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of refusing to search employee emails for responsive FOIA records. OSC declined, saying FOIA itself provides a specific enforcement trigger: if a court (1) orders production of improperly withheld records, (2) awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) specifically questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate” a proceeding under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL sued to compel OSC to investigate under § 1216(a)(3), which on its face directs OSC to “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings.

Rather than resolve whether § 1216 affords an independent private right to compel OSC to investigate, the court rejected AFL’s standing theories. AFL had tried to show concrete informational injuries: that OSC’s refusal prevented DOJ from producing the records AFL sought, and that AFL was denied the notices and information that accompany OSC investigations under § 1214. The D.C. Circuit found both theories deficient. The causal chain—from an OSC investigation to MSPB discipline to DOJ production—was too speculative and depended on independent actors’ unfettered choices. And § 1216, the court observed, does not give plaintiffs a statutory entitlement to receive investigatory notices, so AFL could not claim the kind of informational injury that supports standing. The court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

## Why the standing analysis matters for practitioners
The opinion strings together familiar standing rules in a way that should shape FOIA strategy. Standing doctrine requires an injury fairly traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. When the sought relief is to prompt an agency’s discretionary enforcement action, courts are especially demanding about causation and redressability. That is the logic behind Heckler v. Chaney and recent Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit emphases on not relying on speculation about independent actors (see the court’s reliance on All. for Hippocratic Med. and Haaland for that theme). The upshot is that plaintiffs whose injury is essentially “OSC didn’t investigate” will struggle to show an Article III case unless they can tie OSC’s refusal directly to a concrete, redressable harm that a court order against OSC would likely remedy.

That doctrinal posture favors plaintiffs who can secure concrete judicial relief against the withholding agency itself. If your goal is to produce documents and to catalyze OSC discipline, the D.C. Circuit is signaling that you should litigate to obtain a court order that simultaneously satisfies FOIA’s three statutory predicates for mandatory OSC action. Absent those predicates, a collateral suit against OSC is unlikely to survive a jurisdictional challenge.

## A practitioner’s playbook — litigating to trigger OSC discipline
If your case involves an agency policy or practice you believe systematically prevents disclosure (for example, a categorical refusal to search employee emails), build your FOIA litigation to produce three things that matter under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i): a final judicial order compelling production, an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and a judicial statement that specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding.

First, litigate the FOIA merits with that statutory endgame in mind. Preserve a record showing the agency’s policy or practice (declarations, agency guidance, internal e-mails, testimony if available) and tie it to the requested records. Seek summary judgment on the ground that the agency’s categorical practice is unlawful under FOIA. When proposing or drafting orders and proposed findings, press the court to enter a clear, final judgment rather than a remand or piecemeal production, and to use explicit language that the withholding was “arbitrary and capricious” or to otherwise “specifically question” the agency’s conduct in the statutory phrasing. The statute turns on the court’s concrete acts: words matter.

Second, litigate the fee motion. An award of fees and costs is one of the three statutory triggers for OSC. Make the prevailing-party arguments required under FOIA’s fee-shifting provision, and be prepared to brief why your litigation record justifies fees. If the court is reluctant to award fees at the merits stage, consider post-judgment motions or appeal strategies that seek both a binding judgment on production and the fee award necessary to activate OSC’s duty.

Third, for systemic practices, frame a policy-or-practice claim that seeks injunctive relief ordering the agency to alter a categorical practice (e.g., develop a search policy for employee e-mails). An injunction changing agency practice gives you direct relief on the systemic problem and, if coupled with a finding of arbitrary withholding and a fee award, will also put OSC on mandatory notice under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

Fourth, use administrative complaints as complements, not substitutes. Filing a complaint with OSC, the agency’s Inspector General, or relevant congressional oversight committees can create additional pressure and records that support the FOIA suit, but don’t rely on those filings as a jurisdictionally sufficient backstop if OSC declines to act. The D.C. Circuit’s decision shows that an OSC declination, standing alone, will often leave you without a judicially redressable injury.

Finally, when you win production, don’t assume OSC will act on its own. If you secure an order, a fee award, and an explicit arbitrariness finding, promptly notify OSC and provide the judgment and supporting record. That step puts OSC on the statutory clock to “promptly initiate” proceedings under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

## Limits, friction points, and where the law still leaves questions
A few practical complications deserve emphasis. First, winning all three statutory triggers can be hard: courts do not always enter blunt arbitrariness findings, fee awards can be contested, and plaintiffs sometimes obtain partial or negotiated productions (or remands) rather than a final adversary judgment. If the court issues a remand or an equivocal opinion, OSC’s statutory duty likely will not attach. Litigators should therefore litigate both the substance and the form of relief sought.

Second, the D.C. Circuit expressly steered clear of resolving whether § 1216 independently requires OSC to investigate FOIA complaints absent FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates. That question remains open. In some circumstances OSC may still decide to investigate under § 1216 on its own initiative, and administrative complaints can sometimes move OSC to do so. But if OSC declines and you try to sue to compel investigation, AFL v. Greer shows that Article III hurdles are substantial unless you can show a more direct injury traceable to OSC’s refusal.

Third, there are policy and equity implications. The decision concentrates enforcement pressure on FOIA litigants and on judicial engagement. That can be costly and may leave systemic, non-privileged harms unredressed if no plaintiff can assemble the three statutory triggers. Congress could, of course, recalibrate the balance — but until then, OSC is likely to remain a back-end discipline mechanism rather than a front-line remedy accessible by routine mandamus-style suits.

## Bottom line
AFL v. Greer teaches a straightforward practitioner lesson: don’t treat OSC as an easily sued shortcut to discipline an agency for FOIA practices. If your objective is production and to put OSC on the hook for discipline, litigating FOIA to obtain a final production order, an attorneys’-fee award, and an explicit judicial finding that the withholding was arbitrary or capricious is the path the statute—and now the D.C. Circuit—makes most likely to succeed. Use administrative complaints as pressure points and pursue policy-or-practice claims when systemic change is the goal, but build your case in court with an eye to securing the precise, binding relief that activates § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).
