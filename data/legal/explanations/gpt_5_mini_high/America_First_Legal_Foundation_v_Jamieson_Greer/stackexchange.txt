Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Did § 1216’s “shall … conduct an investigation” obligate OSC here, independent of FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)?
Question:
The district court said § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)’s three predicates (court order, fee award, and specific finding about arbitrariness) don’t limit OSC’s power under § 1216(a)(3), but the D.C. Circuit didn’t reach the merits. How should these provisions be read together? Does § 1216(a)(3) impose a nondiscretionary duty to investigate upon any allegation of an “arbitrary or capricious withholding” under FOIA, or is there some threshold? If the duty is nondiscretionary, what is the proper vehicle to enforce it—APA § 706(1) or mandamus?
Answer:
Short answer
The D.C. Circuit did not decide the issue. The panel vacated the district court’s merits decision for lack of Article III standing and expressly declined to reach whether §1216(a)(3) independently compels OSC to investigate. That said, the statutes read naturally as two co‑existing obligations: §1216(a)(3)’s plain text (“shall … conduct an investigation”) creates a broad, apparently mandatory duty to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, while FOIA’s §552(a)(4)(F)(i) is a narrower, separate statutory trigger that imposes an additional mandatory duty to “promptly initiate” disciplinary proceedings after a court (1) orders production, (2) awards fees, and (3) specifically questions arbitrariness. Whether a court will treat §1216’s investigatory duty as judicially enforceable (and by what remedy) was left unresolved.

Why that reading is the most natural reading of the statutes (and what the opinion says)
- The opinion reproduces the statutory language: §1216(a)(3) “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” whereas FOIA §552(a)(4)(F)(i) says that if a court (1) orders production, (2) awards fees, and (3) specifically questions arbitrariness, OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted.” The D.C. Circuit recited both texts (Op. I.A) but did not reach the merits.
- That textual contrast supports a two‑tier reading: §1216(a)(3) establishes a general, textually mandatory investigatory duty; §552(a)(4)(F)(i) supplies a distinct, narrower, post‑litigation command to initiate disciplinary proceedings when specified judicial findings are present. The district court adopted essentially that reconciliation (it held §552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit §1216), and the D.C. Circuit described that ruling—but remanded only to dismiss for lack of standing rather than resolve the statutory question. See district court opinion quoted at Op. I.A.

Limits on judicial enforcement
- Even if §1216(a)(3) is read as mandatory, the opinion highlights two practical and doctrinal limits that make enforcement difficult.
  1. Standing/redressability: the D.C. Circuit rejected AFL’s attempt to treat OSC’s refusal to investigate as a standalone procedural injury because AFL could not show the required causal chain and redressability (the court found any link between a compelled OSC investigation and AFL’s receipt of FOIA records too speculative). The opinion explains that a plaintiff must show a concrete injury and that relief would likely redress it (Op. II).
  2. Enforcement discretion/Heckler problem: the opinion invokes the general rule that courts “generally may not compel” agency enforcement decisions (citing Heckler v. Chaney language). Further, the court noted precedent (Carson) saying OSC’s choice whether to apply §1214’s procedural protections in a §1216 investigation is discretionary. Those passages point to a possible limiting principle: even if OSC must investigate, courts may be unable to commandeer prosecutorial or remedial choices that follow investigation. (Op. II; see also Op. I.A discussion of §1216(c)’s “may” language for seeking corrective/disciplinary action.)

Which plaintiffs’ vehicle—APA §706(1) or mandamus?
- The D.C. Circuit did not resolve the remedial question. The opinion, however, frames the obstacles a litigant would face:
  - If §1216(a)(3) imposes a clear, nondiscretionary, ministerial duty to investigate, traditional remedies in other contexts have included writs of mandamus (to compel a ministerial duty) and APA §706(1) actions (to compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed). The court did not hold which is correct here.
  - Regardless of the vehicle chosen, the plaintiff must overcome standing and non‑justiciability hurdles: show a concrete injury and that ordering an investigation would likely redress it, and show the duty is not committed to agency discretion (Heckler). The opinion’s standing analysis makes clear that speculative chains of events (investigation → MSPB action → DOJ production) will not suffice (Op. II).
  - Even if a court could compel the investigatory step, the opinion suggests courts will be far less willing to force OSC to take prosecutorial steps (seek disciplinary or corrective action) when the statute leaves those choices to OSC (see §1216(c)’s “may”; the opinion’s citation to Carson).

Takeaway for a litigant
- If you want to press a §1216(a)(3) claim: (1) frame and plead a concrete, particularized injury that will be directly redressed by an order requiring OSC to investigate; (2) press the statutory text showing a clear, nondiscretionary obligation to investigate; and (3) distinguish the relief you seek (compel investigation) from what courts are reluctant to order (compel prosecutorial/disciplinary decisionmaking). The D.C. Circuit’s decision here does not foreclose a merits win, but it signals (a) the panel will police standing closely and (b) the Heckler principle constrains judicial relief beyond an investigatory mandate. The panel left the central statutory/merits question open.

### Why isn’t DOJ’s failure to produce records a traceable, redressable informational injury caused by OSC’s refusal to investigate?
Question:
AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ under § 1216 ultimately kept AFL from getting FOIA records. The panel said the injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the chain through possible OSC action, MSPB sanctions, and DOJ compliance is too speculative (citing Haaland v. Brackeen and FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine). Can someone clarify how these cases limit causation/redressability when a plaintiff sues a regulator for not enforcing against a third party? Are there examples where a regulator’s inaction has been held sufficiently traceable/redressable despite intervening independent actors?
Answer:
Short answer
Because AFL’s asserted informational injury — DOJ’s withholding of FOIA records — is not causally and legally attributable to OSC’s refusal to investigate. The D.C. Circuit treated OSC’s inaction as an attenuated, speculative link in a chain that would require (1) OSC to commence enforcement, (2) the MSPB to impose sanctions, and (3) DOJ then to produce records. Under Haaland and FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine (as the panel applied them), standing cannot rest on that kind of speculation about the independent choices of actors not before the court; redressability turns on what a judicial order would actually bind the defendant to do, not on whether the order might persuade others to act. (Op. II.) 

Why the court rejected AFL’s “traceability/redressability” argument (step‑by‑step)
1. Standing basics and the “procedural right” wrinkle. Article III requires injury in fact, causation, and redressability. The panel accepted that procedural‑rights cases get only a relaxed (not eliminated) causation/redressability inquiry, but even there the plaintiff must show a concrete interest that was harmed by the procedural violation. (Op. II.)

2. The injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. AFL’s injury (not getting DOJ records) is directly traceable to DOJ’s withholding. The court said that any causal link to OSC depends on a multi‑step, hypothetical chain of events — OSC might open an investigation, might seek corrective/disciplinary action, MSPB might sanction, and DOJ might then produce. That chain leaves the injury “plainly traceable” to DOJ and only indirectly (and speculatively) to OSC. (Op. II.)

3. Courts cannot base standing on speculative choices by independent actors. The panel relied on FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine: “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” The OSC→MSPB→DOJ chain is exactly the sort of speculative sequence the Supreme Court and this circuit will not accept. (Op. II (quoting FDA).)

4. Redressability requires that a favorable judgment bind the defendant (or very likely produce the remedy). The court cited Haaland for the proposition that redressability “turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court.’” Ordering OSC to investigate would not reliably force MSPB or DOJ to act; it therefore would not “very likely spur” the steps needed to obtain the FOIA records. (Op. II (quoting Haaland and Hecate Energy).)

5. Enforcement discretion makes the chain even more speculative. OSC’s choice to pursue enforcement or to apply section 1214 procedures in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary; courts ordinarily cannot compel an agency to exercise its enforcement powers (Heckler v. Chaney). The panel relied on that principle to reject AFL’s reliance on a court order compelling OSC to investigate as a path to relief. (Op. II; citing Heckler; Carson cited on OSC discretion.)

Informational‑standing cases the opinion treats as different
The opinion explains (and distinguishes) the class of cases in which courts have found an informational injury: where a statute actually entitles the plaintiff to information and the defendant is the actor who can withhold it. It cites cases such as American Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Zivotofsky, FEC v. Akins, and Pub. Citizen as examples of statutory entitlement/informational‑standing rulings. The key point the panel draws from those precedents is that informational standing typically requires (a) a statutory entitlement to information and (b) that the defendant’s own conduct is what denies the information — not a claim that a third‑party agency’s failure to enforce caused the withholding. (Op. II.)

Are there examples where regulator inaction against a third party was held sufficient?
The panel’s opinion does not identify any precedents that validate standing based on a plaintiff suing a regulator for failing to enforce against a separate agency when redress would depend on a sequence of independent choices. Instead, the opinion treats the line of cases that support standing as those where the plaintiff is denied information to which a statute entitles them and the defendant is the source of the denial. Put differently: the opinion points to the narrow, traditional informational‑standing path (statutory entitlement + defendant‑withholder), not to successful cases premising standing on a speculative enforcement chain through independent actors. (Op. II.)

Practical takeaways for litigants
- If your injury is that X withheld information, sue X (the actor who withholds) or show a statutory entitlement that the court can vindicate directly. The court is willing to find informational standing when the statute entitles the plaintiff to the information and the defendant is the one withholding it. (Op. II.)
- Don’t rely on standing theories that require courts to assume that a third‑party regulator will take enforcement steps and that yet another agency will then change its conduct; Haaland and FDA make clear courts will not rest standing on such speculation. (Op. II.)
- Where relief would depend on an agency’s discretionary enforcement choices (and other independent actors), expect the causation/redressability inquiry to defeat standing unless you can show a more direct, non‑speculative link between the defendant’s conduct and the injury. (Op. II; Heckler cited.)

Bottom line
The panel held AFL lacked standing because its informational injury was directly traceable to DOJ, and any causal/redressive connection to OSC’s alleged refusal to investigate required a speculative chain of independent decisions (OSC → MSPB → DOJ). Haaland and FDA supply the controlling principles: standing cannot rest on speculation about independent actors, and redressability depends on what a judgment would bind the defendant to do, not on how persuasive the judgment might be to others. (Op. II.)

### Does § 1216(c)’s “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” language import § 1214’s notice rights?
Question:
AFL claimed an informational injury from not receiving the investigation notices and updates that § 1214 provides. The panel says § 1216 doesn’t itself entitle requesters to information and cites Carson v. OSC for the idea that applying § 1214 procedures in § 1216 investigations is discretionary. How do courts interpret § 1216(c)’s cross-reference—does it incorporate only the remedial avenues (corrective under § 1214 and disciplinary under § 1215), or also § 1214’s procedural notice requirements to complainants? If some § 1214 procedures do carry over, which ones and on what rationale?
Answer:
Short answer
Courts (including the D.C. Circuit in America First Legal Foundation) read 5 U.S.C. § 1216(c) as authorizing OSC to use the enforcement remedies provided in §§ 1214 and 1215 (i.e., corrective or disciplinary proceedings), but they do not treat § 1216(c) as automatically conferring § 1214’s procedural notice-and-update rights on outsiders. Application of § 1214’s procedural rules to a § 1216 investigation is treated as discretionary, not as a statutory entitlement that third parties can enforce.

Why: text, precedent, and the D.C. Circuit’s reasoning
- The statutes. Section 1214 contains both remedial mechanisms (corrective proceedings before the MSPB) and procedural rules (including requirements that complainants be notified about investigation progress—see 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)). Section 1216(c) authorizes OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That language plainly authorizes OSC to use §§ 1214/1215 enforcement avenues.
- What courts have done. The D.C. Circuit in America First Legal Foundation relied on (and cites) prior authority — notably Carson v. OSC — for the proposition that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214 procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. The panel observed that AFL “acknowledges cases holding that OSC’s ‘decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements’ in a section 1216 investigation is ‘discretionary.’” Op. at II.B. On that basis (and because AFL could not show a statutory entitlement to information), the court held that § 1216 did not give AFL a concrete informational right and therefore AFL lacked standing to sue over the absence of notices.
- Enforcement vs. entitlement. The D.C. Circuit stressed the difference between OSC’s authority to pursue remedies under § 1214/1215 and a private party’s entitlement to receive investigatory information. Even assuming § 1216 required OSC to investigate FOIA violations, the court said that § 1216 “creates no entitlement to receive information.” Op. at II.B. That distinction is why courts treat remedial carryover (the ability to use MSPB procedures) differently from automatically importing complainant-facing procedural rights.

Which §1214 procedures, if any, carry over?
- What the opinion supports: remedial mechanisms (OSC may seek corrective relief under § 1214 or disciplinary relief under § 1215) are available under § 1216(c). The opinion explicitly notes §1214’s notification provisions as part of §1214’s procedural package (citing § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)), but it does not hold that those notification rights automatically attach to §1216 investigations.
- What actually happens in practice (as the courts have treated it): if OSC elects to proceed under § 1214 in a § 1216 investigation, OSC may apply § 1214’s procedural rules (including notice provisions), but that application is an agency choice rather than a statutory right enforceable by outside requesters absent clearer statutory language.

Practical takeaway for litigants
- If you want to argue that § 1216 imports § 1214’s notice rights as a enforceable entitlement, the America First Legal Foundation opinion is unhelpful: the D.C. Circuit treated those procedural protections as discretionary and disposed of the case on standing grounds because no statutory entitlement to information had been shown. Your best path is to identify clear statutory text or binding precedent that unambiguously converts § 1216 investigations into § 1214 investigations for purposes of third‑party procedural rights; absent that, courts will generally treat procedural application as agency discretion and will not find an informational right sufficient for standing.

### What exactly is relaxed in procedural-injury standing, and why didn’t it help AFL here?
Question:
The opinion quotes D.C. Circuit precedent that imminence and redressability are relaxed for procedural injuries, but injury in fact and causation are not. Yet it finds AFL’s first theory fails on causation and redressability, and its second theory lacks a statutory entitlement (injury) and redressability. In practice, what does the relaxed standard permit (e.g., probabilistic chains)? Where is the line between acceptable probability and impermissible speculation about “independent actors not before the court”?
Answer:
Short answer
The D.C. Circuit (following its precedents quoted in the opinion) treats only imminence and redressability as “relaxed” in procedural‑injury cases; injury‑in‑fact and causation still must be shown. That limited relaxation allows plaintiffs to rely on less-than-certain, probabilistic links between a procedural wrong and the substantive interest it protects—but only so long as the causal link is non‑speculative and tied to what the court can actually order. It does not save claims that rest on a chain of discretionary choices by independent actors not before the court. That is exactly why AFL lost.

What “relaxed” means (and what still isn’t relaxed)
- The court quotes the governing formulation: when a plaintiff alleges a statutory procedural right meant to protect a concrete interest, courts “relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., quoted in the opinion. Summers v. Earth Island and related authority also warn that a “procedural right in vacuo” is insufficient.  
- Practically, the relaxation means a plaintiff need not always show that the procedural violation caused an immediate or certain substantive harm; a procedural deprivation can itself count as injury when the statute entitles the plaintiff to a process designed to protect a concrete interest. But the plaintiff still must plead a concrete, particularized injury and a plausible causal link showing that a favorable court order would likely (not merely possibly) remedy that injury.

Where probabilistic chains are permissible, and where they fail
- Permissible: short, plausible probabilistic links in which (i) the defendant’s procedural violation is the proximate cause of the plaintiff’s concrete interest being frustrated, and (ii) a judicial remedy would bind the defendant (or otherwise put in motion non‑speculative steps) that very likely lead to relief. The relaxed rules let the plaintiff avoid absolute certainty about the substantive outcome, but not speculation. (See Hecate Energy quoted in the opinion: a favorable judgment must “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress.”)  
- Impermissible: causal theories that depend on a sequence of discretionary decisions by third parties or agencies not before the court. The opinion relies on FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Med. for the proposition that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” If redress would turn on persuading or hoping independent actors (here, OSC’s enforcement choices, the MSPB’s discretionary sanctions decisions, and DOJ’s later choice to produce records) to act, the chain is too speculative.

Why the relaxation didn’t help AFL
- First theory (AFL lost FOIA records because OSC declined to investigate): the court held the informational injury is traceable to DOJ’s FOIA refusal, not OSC’s inaction. Even if a procedural theory could relax redressability, AFL’s causal chain required multiple discretionary steps: OSC might have investigated and chosen corrective/disciplinary action; MSPB might have sanctioned employees; DOJ might then have produced records. The opinion treats that chain as “speculative and attenuated,” relies on the All. for Hippocratic Med. principle about independent actors, cites Heckler v. Chaney on enforcement discretion, and applies Hecate/Haaland to show a court order against OSC would not “very likely” or directly bind the actors whose choices allegedly would produce the records. So causation and redressability fail.  
- Second theory (injury from being denied notices/information from an OSC investigation): informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive the information (Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Zivotofsky, Akins, cited). Section 1216 may require OSC to investigate, but it does not create an entitlement to the §1214 notices AFL seeks; and AFL conceded (and the opinion cites Carson) that applying §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations is discretionary. Thus AFL lacks the required concrete statutory entitlement (injury) and, for the same reasons, the court could not say a judicial order would likely redress that alleged informational injury.

Bottom line (rule of thumb)
Relaxation helps when the procedural right is designed to protect a concrete interest and a court order compelling the procedure would directly (or very likely) produce the plaintiff’s relief. It doesn’t help when standing depends on a treadmill of discretionary decisions by independent actors not before the court—those chains are legally too speculative. The America First Legal Foundation opinion applies that precise distinction.

### Could AFL have pled organizational standing (resource diversion) based on OSC’s refusal to investigate?
Question:
AFL’s supplemental briefing focused on informational/procedural injuries. Under D.C. Circuit organizational-standing doctrine, could AFL have alleged a concrete resource-diversion injury—e.g., that OSC’s non-investigation forced AFL to divert staff time and money to counteract DOJ’s email-search policy—traceable to OSC and redressable by a court order? What specific factual allegations would be needed to satisfy injury, causation, and redressability on that theory?
Answer:
Short answer
Possibly — but only if AFL had pleaded very specific, non‑speculative facts tying (1) concrete expenditure of resources to OSC’s refusal (not to DOJ’s FOIA stance), and (2) a remedy that would directly eliminate that need to spend. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion rests on the classical standing triad (injury, causation, redressability) and stresses two obstacles that sank AFL’s informational theories: the asserted injury was traceable to DOJ (not OSC), and the causal chain from an OSC investigation to relief was too speculative. Any resource‑diversion theory would have to be pleaded to avoid those exact pitfalls.

Why the opinion matters for a resource‑diversion theory
- The panel reiterated the basic Article III test (injury, causation, redressability) and that, at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element. (Op. § II.)  
- It applied Summers to hold that a mere procedural deprivation is insufficient without a concrete interest affected. (Op. § II.)  
- It rejected AFL’s FOIA‑information theory because the informational injury was traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and because redress depended on a speculative chain of independent actors (OSC → MSPB → DOJ). The court relied on the rule that causation cannot rest on speculation about independent third‑party choices and that redressability turns on what relief would bind the defendant, not what might persuade others. (Op. § II (citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.; Haaland v. Brackeen).)

What AFL would have needed to plead — element by element
Below is a practical checklist of the kinds of factual allegations that, taken together, would make a resource‑diversion theory plausible in light of the panel’s reasoning.

1. Injury (concrete, particularized organizational injury)
- Specific, quantified resource diversion. Plead which staff were diverted (titles), how many hours, over what period, and the dollar value (salaries, fees, consultant costs). Example: “Since OSC’s refusal on [date], AFL diverted two full‑time attorneys (1,800 hours cumulatively, $X) and an IT consultant ($Y) from core programs to perform targeted email searches, interview witnesses, and assemble evidentiary files.”  
- Concrete description of tasks performed that OSC would have performed (investigatory work, running searches of employee mailboxes, collecting witness statements, technical forensics), not generic litigation overhead.  
- Measurable organizational impact (programming delayed, staff reassigned from mission work, membership services reduced) showing the diversion was real and not de minimis.

Why this matters: the panel applied Summers — a procedural theory must be linked to a concrete interest. Detailed, quantified reallocation converts a procedural grievance into a tangible organizational injury.

2. Causation (traceable to OSC’s refusal, not to DOJ)
- Allegations tying AFL’s decision to divert resources directly to OSC’s refusal: contemporaneous internal communications, board minutes, budgets, or emails showing AFL expected OSC to investigate and would have stood down if OSC had done so. Example: “AFL’s litigation plan anticipated relying on OSC’s investigation; internal email dated X notes that AFL would not initiate its own IT forensics if OSC opened a section 1216 probe; after OSC declined, AFL authorized the IT hiring described above.”  
- Avoid framing the diversion as a response primarily to DOJ’s FOIA withholding. The panel rejected AFL’s informational theory because the informational injury was traceable to DOJ; to survive, AFL must show the but‑for cause of the diversion was OSC’s non‑investigation (i.e., the burden created by OSC’s refusal), not DOJ’s choice to withhold.

Why this matters: the opinion emphasizes that traceability cannot rest on an attenuated chain through independent actors (MSPB/DOJ). Plead contemporaneous reliance showing a direct causal link.

3. Redressability (a court order would likely stop the diversion)
- Specify a remedy that would bind OSC and that plausibly would eliminate AFL’s need to keep diverting resources — not a remedy that merely “might” persuade MSPB or DOJ. For example: an order compelling OSC to (a) open the statutorily mandated section 1216 investigation, (b) complete it within a fixed time, and (c) deliver to AFL the investigative findings, notices, or investigative materials that AFL alleges OSC would have produced if it had investigated. Plead how those concrete investigatory products would directly obviate AFL’s current resource expenditures.  
- Explain why OSC is capable of and likely to produce the very information or findings that would end the diversion (e.g., OSC’s investigative record would supply the documents/answers AFL else had to develop itself).

Why this matters: the panel held redressability turns on how a judgment would bind the defendant, not whether it might persuade non‑parties. An order that requires OSC to do specific, traceable things is more likely to satisfy redressability than an order whose effect depends on subsequent MSPB or DOJ action.

A short sample pleading paragraph (illustrative)
- “After OSC publicly declined to investigate DOJ’s alleged email‑search policy, AFL diverted two full‑time attorneys (1,800 hours total) and a forensic consultant ($Y) from X program to run targeted searches, interview relevant witnesses, and assemble a complete evidentiary file—tasks AFL had planned to rely on OSC to perform. AFL will cease this extraordinary allocation of staff and funds if OSC is ordered to initiate and complete a section 1216 investigation within 90 days and produce to AFL the resulting investigative findings and materials, because those investigative products would supplant AFL’s need to perform the investigative work described above.”

Practical assessment in light of the opinion
- The theory is possible in the abstract, but the D.C. Circuit’s opinion signals a tough pathway: anything that looks like an attempt to bootstrap relief against DOJ (i.e., to obtain FOIA records) by forcing OSC to act will likely founder on the court’s concern about speculative causation and about reliance on independent actors. To survive, AFL would have to tie the diversion tightly to OSC’s own conduct and seek relief that obliges OSC itself to produce the investigatory product that ends the diversion. Absent those particularized allegations, the panel’s standing analysis (traceability and speculative chain) would likely defeat the claim as it did AFL’s informational theories.

Bottom line
Yes — but only if pleaded with granular, contemporaneous facts showing (1) concrete money/time diverted; (2) that diversion was caused by OSC’s refusal (not DOJ’s withholding); and (3) a judicial remedy that would directly and predictably end the diversion by compelling OSC to take and produce specific investigatory steps. The Greer opinion makes clear that anything less risks dismissal for lack of Article III standing.

### After finding no standing, why did the D.C. Circuit vacate the merits ruling and remand with instructions to dismiss?
Question:
The district court held OSC made a legal error about its authority but still declined to order an investigation, and it remanded to OSC. The court of appeals vacated because there was no Article III standing. Is vacatur of the merits ruling automatic when jurisdiction is lacking (per Steel Co.)? Do the district court’s legal conclusions about § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) have any continuing effect, or are they null once vacated?
Answer:
Short answer
The court vacated because it concluded the district court never had Article III jurisdiction to decide the merits. Under Steel Co. the jurisdictional question comes first; once the court finds no standing it cannot leave a merits judgment in place. As the panel put it: “Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.” That vacatur removes the district court’s final merits disposition — including its legal conclusions about § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) — from the field as a binding judgment.

Why the panel did this (briefly)
- Steel Co. requires courts to resolve jurisdiction before reaching the merits. The D.C. Circuit followed that rule: it ordered supplemental briefing on standing, concluded AFL lacked Article III standing, and therefore treated the district court as having decided the merits without jurisdiction.
- A court that lacks subject‑matter jurisdiction has no authority to enter a binding merits judgment. The panel’s remedy was to vacate the district court’s judgment and instruct dismissal (the exact language quoted above).

Is vacatur “automatic”?
- The opinion does not announce a generalized new rule that vacatur is automatic in every jurisdictional reversal; it applies the familiar consequence here: because there was no Article III jurisdiction, the appellate court vacated the district court’s judgment. The D.C. Circuit invoked Steel Co. to prioritize the jurisdictional question and then corrected the record by vacatur and dismissal.

Do the district court’s legal conclusions still have effect?
- No as a binding decision. By vacating the district court’s judgment the panel removed that judgment’s legal force. The district court’s conclusions about § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) no longer stand as a final, binding adjudication of the issues in this case (the D.C. Circuit explicitly vacated the judgment and ordered dismissal).

### If standing were satisfied, could a court compel OSC to investigate under § 1216, or would Heckler v. Chaney bar that relief?
Question:
The district court suggested decisions under § 1216(c) are committed to OSC’s discretion. Chaney generally bars review of nonenforcement, while Norton v. SUWA allows courts to compel discrete, nondiscretionary agency actions. Would a § 1216(a)(3) “shall … conduct an investigation” duty be enforceable under APA § 706(1) or mandamus, and if so, would the remedy be limited to initiating an investigation (as opposed to dictating outcomes or enforcement actions)?
Answer:
Short answer
If standing were present, a court could plausibly compel OSC to take the discrete step §1216(a)(3) describes — i.e., to initiate an investigation of an allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding — because the statute uses mandatory “shall.” But Heckler v. Chaney would still bar a court from dictating how OSC prosecutes or resolves the matter (for example, ordering particular corrective or disciplinary outcomes). In practice the available remedies would be limited to compelling the nondiscretionary investigatory step (or vacating and remanding for lawful exercise of discretion), not to micromanaging enforcement choices or results.

Why (the legal doctrine and how it applies here)
- Two competing doctrines govern. Chaney holds that agency decisions not to enforce or to bring enforcement actions are presumptively unreviewable because they are quintessentially prosecutorial and discretionary. By contrast, the Supreme Court’s compulsion jurisprudence (Norton v. SUWA and its progeny) permits a court to compel an agency to perform a discrete, mandatory duty when the statute leaves no room for agency discretion. The practical line is: courts can force ministerial/mandatory acts but cannot compel the agency to make particular enforcement judgments.

- What the opinion says about §1216. The D.C. Circuit (Katsas, J.) reproduces the relevant text: §1216(a)(3) provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” while §1216(c) provides that OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The district court concluded (1) OSC’s refusal to investigate was legally erroneous (i.e., it read §1216(a)(3) as mandatory) but (2) nonetheless declined to order OSC to investigate because it viewed §1216(c) as committing the decision to OSC’s discretion and remanded for further consideration. The D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits because AFL lacked standing. (America First Legal Found. v. Jamieson Greer, slip op. §§ I.A–B, II (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025).)

- Application to the relief question. Read on its face, §1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” is mandatory language that courts could enforce under APA §706(1) or mandamus if the plaintiff shows standing and that the duty is truly nondiscretionary. But §1216(c)’s “may” language and the surrounding scheme (authorizing corrective/disciplinary proceedings under §§1214–1215) indicate that many follow-on enforcement choices remain discretionary. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion echoes these themes: it cites Chaney when observing that courts “generally may not compel” speculation about how OSC might exercise enforcement discretion, and it notes authorities holding that OSC’s decision whether to apply §1214’s procedural protections in a §1216 matter is discretionary. Id. § II & I.A.

What a court could (and could not) order
- Could order/initiate: compel OSC to perform the discrete statutory duty to investigate an allegation (or to reconsider a refusal) if the statute is read as imposing such a non‑discretionary duty and standing and other jurisdictional prerequisites are met. That is precisely the sort of relief courts grant under APA §706(1) or mandamus when a statute imposes a mandatory duty.

- Could not order: require OSC to take particular enforcement steps, to prosecute a case in a particular way, or to impose specific corrective or disciplinary outcomes. Those are enforcement decisions Chaney protects as discretionary; the opinion expressly relies on Chaney to reject redressability based on speculation about how OSC, MSPB, or DOJ would act. See slip op. § II.

Bottom line
If the jurisdictional hurdles (Article III standing, etc.) were cleared, a court could likely force OSC to stop declining outright and to take the statutory investigatory step that §1216(a)(3) appears to command. But Chaney’s principle — as the D.C. Circuit described it here — would keep the court from ordering substantive enforcement results; relief would be procedural (initiate or reconsider an investigation; vacate and remand) rather than substantive (order discipline, set remedial outcomes, or tell OSC how to exercise its enforcement discretion).

### Does a DOJ policy of not searching employee emails without consent qualify as an “arbitrary or capricious withholding” under FOIA for § 1216 purposes?
Question:
Section 1216(a)(3) covers “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” Is a systemic search-limitation policy (not searching certain repositories absent employee consent) properly characterized as a FOIA “withholding,” or does that term refer only to refusing to disclose located records based on exemptions? How, if at all, have courts or OSC treated search-adequacy issues in the context of § 552(a)(4)(F) referrals or § 1216 investigations?
Answer:
Short answer
The D.C. Circuit in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer did not resolve whether a DOJ policy of refusing to search employee e‑mail without consent qualifies as an “arbitrary or capricious withholding” under FOIA for purposes of 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). AFL alleged that such a search‑limitation is a FOIA “withholding” and asked the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate; OSC refused unless the three statutory triggers in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were met; the district court disagreed with OSC on that legal point but declined to compel relief; the D.C. Circuit avoided the merits and dismissed for lack of Article III standing. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Jamieson Greer, No. 24‑5168 (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025).

Why (and what the opinion actually says)
- Statutory framework the opinion summarizes:
  - §1216(a)(3) directs OSC to “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” (Op. I.A.)
  - FOIA independently provides that if a court (1) orders production, (2) awards attorneys’ fees, and (3) “specifically questions whether the agency ‘acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,’” then OSC “shall promptly initiate” a proceeding. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) (quoted in the opinion). (Op. I.A–B.)
- What happened here:
  - AFL alleged DOJ’s policy of not searching employee e‑mail without consent amounted to an arbitrary FOIA withholding and asked OSC to investigate under §1216(a)(3). (Op. I.B.)
  - OSC declined to investigate on the ground that the §552(a)(4)(F)(i) prerequisites were not met and that that provision governs OSC’s FOIA enforcement. AFL argued §1216 independently compels OSC to investigate. (Op. I.B.)
  - The district court thought §552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s §1216 authority but nonetheless remanded because §1216(c) leaves investigative choices to OSC’s discretion. (Op. I.B.)
  - The D.C. Circuit did not decide whether the search‑policy is itself a FOIA “withholding” or whether §1216 or §552(a)(4)(F)(i) controls OSC’s obligation; instead it dismissed AFL’s suit for lack of Article III standing. The court explained AFL’s asserted injuries were too attenuated and not likely redressable by a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate. (Op. II–III.)

Practical and doctrinal takeaways (from the opinion)
- The statutory text of §1216(a)(3) is broad, but America First did not interpret “withholding” to include—or exclude—search‑limitation policies; that merits question remains unresolved by the D.C. Circuit.
- The opinion makes clear two enforcement routes:
  1. The clear statutory trigger in FOIA (§552(a)(4)(F)(i)): a court order, fee award, and a judicial finding/question that the agency acted arbitrarily — that combination mandates OSC action (the statute says OSC “shall promptly initiate”).
  2. A §1216(a)(3) complaint to OSC: OSC treats investigation/discipline as an enforcement/prosecutorial exercise and may decline to investigate; courts are generally reluctant to compel such prosecutorial discretion (the opinion cites Heckler v. Chaney principles and notes Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns. on OSC’s procedural discretion). (Op. I.A, I.B, II.)
- Litigation strategy implication emphasized by the opinion: if your goal is to produce a binding referral to OSC, the safer route is to litigate the FOIA claim and seek the judicial findings and fee award that trigger §552(a)(4)(F)(i). Relying solely on a §1216 petition (asking OSC to investigate a systemic search policy) risks both OSC refusal as an exercise of discretion and the standing problems the D.C. Circuit identified (attenuated causation and lack of redressability). (Op. I.B, II.)

Bottom line
America First leaves the merits question open: a policy of declining to search employee e‑mail can be alleged as a FOIA “withholding,” but the D.C. Circuit did not rule that it is one. The opinion instead highlights (1) the separate, mandatory FOIA trigger for OSC in §552(a)(4)(F)(i), (2) OSC’s enforcement discretion under §1216, and (3) practical obstacles (including standing and non‑reviewability of prosecutorial decisions) to forcing OSC to investigate a policy absent the clear FOIA referral triggers. (See Am. First Legal Found. v. Jamieson Greer, No. 24‑5168 (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025).)
