Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Background and Procedural Posture

## AFL’s FOIA request to DOJ and DOJ’s asserted practice
The controversy began when America First Legal Foundation (AFL) submitted a Freedom of Information Act request to the Department of Justice’s Civil Division seeking emails related to judicial recusals. AFL alleged that the Civil Division refused to search employee email accounts for responsive records unless the individual employee consented to the search. Treating that practice as an institutional FOIA policy of withholding, AFL also filed a separate FOIA suit against DOJ to obtain the requested records. DOJ has produced records in that suit and continues to produce additional material; the district court and the D.C. Circuit treat the sufficiency of that production as not squarely before them in this collateral challenge.

## AFL’s complaint to the Office of Special Counsel under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)
Because OSC has statutory authority to investigate certain forms of employee misconduct and to seek corrective or disciplinary action before the Merit Systems Protection Board, AFL asked the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552” (the FOIA provision). OSC’s investigatory tools include pursuing corrective action under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 (with procedures that include notice to a complaining party) and disciplinary proceedings under § 1215; AFL sought an OSC inquiry as a route to remedy alleged FOIA misconduct by DOJ employees.

## OSC’s refusal to investigate and FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)
OSC declined AFL’s request for an investigation. OSC’s stated reason was that FOIA contains an explicit OSC-enforcement provision—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—that directs OSC to “promptly initiate a proceeding” only after three judicial predicates are satisfied: (1) a court orders production of improperly withheld records, (2) the court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) the court specifically questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.” Because those three prerequisites were not present, OSC disclaimed authority to pursue an OSC-initiated proceeding in response to AFL’s complaint. AFL maintained that § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” language imposed an independent obligation on OSC that did not depend on the FOIA provision’s three predicates.

## District court proceedings: finding legal error but remanding; final dismissal
AFL sued OSC in district court seeking to compel an investigation. The district court agreed with AFL on one legal point: it held that FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC’s investigative authority under § 1216, so OSC’s initial categorical refusal to investigate rested on a legal error. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Even so, the district court declined to order OSC to open an investigation. It reasoned that § 1216(c) leaves to OSC discretion whether to apply § 1214’s procedural protections when pursuing corrective action and thus committed the decision how to proceed to the agency; accordingly the court remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration rather than directing a particular investigatory outcome. After OSC again declined to investigate on remand, AFL sought entry of final judgment; the district court then dismissed the remaining claims, and AFL appealed.

## Appeal to the D.C. Circuit and the emergence of the standing question
On appeal to the D.C. Circuit, the court stated that it reviews the district court’s legal rulings de novo. Because neither party had originally briefed Article III standing—a jurisdictional prerequisite—the panel ordered supplemental briefing on whether AFL had standing to sue OSC. Standing, and whether AFL’s asserted injuries were sufficiently concrete, causally connected to OSC’s refusal, and likely redressed by a court order, thus became the dispositive issue on appeal. The D.C. Circuit ultimately concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing, vacated the district court’s judgment, and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# Statutory Framework: OSC, the CSRA, and FOIA’s Separate OSC Trigger

This chapter explains the statutory architecture the court relied on in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer and the basic ways the statutes interact. The Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) created the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) as an investigative and prosecutorial body that can use the remedial machinery of the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). Two CSRA provisions—5 U.S.C. §§ 1214–1216—define OSC’s enforcement tools and process; FOIA itself (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) contains a separate, narrower provision that independently can trigger OSC action after certain judicial findings. Understanding the text and the practical links among these provisions is necessary to follow the parties’ arguments and the court’s analysis in the opinion.

## OSC’s role under the Civil Service Reform Act

OSC is an independent investigative and prosecutorial agency created to protect the integrity of the federal civil service. As the court summarizes, OSC exists to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.2d 150, 162 (D.C. Cir. 1982). Under the CSRA, OSC investigates allegations of “prohibited personnel practices” and, when an investigation supports enforcement, may pursue corrective relief or disciplinary proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board. That structure places OSC at the front end of administrative enforcement for employee misconduct and personnel abuses: it investigates, decides whether an enforcement action is warranted, and, if so, invokes MSPB processes to obtain remedy or discipline.

## Section 1214 and § 1215: corrective and disciplinary remedies; § 1214’s notification procedures

The CSRA divides OSC’s remedial tools between corrective actions and disciplinary measures. Section 1214 provides the procedures for seeking corrective action for prohibited personnel practices; section 1215 authorizes OSC to seek disciplinary action against employees. Importantly for litigants, § 1214 does more than authorize corrective relief: it prescribes procedures governing how corrective investigations are handled, including statutory provisions that require OSC to give the complaining party certain notices and information about the progress of an investigation (see, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)). Those procedural provisions are the source of some claimants’ asserted informational interests—i.e., that they were entitled to notice or updates when OSC investigated their complaint.

## Section 1216(a)(3) and § 1216(c): OSC’s FOIA investigatory duty and the statutory bridge to §§ 1214–1215

Section 1216 brings FOIA-related allegations into OSC’s statutory ambit. Subsection 1216(a)(3) provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552,” thereby expressly authorizing OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings. Subsection 1216(c) then connects those FOIA investigations to the CSRA’s enforcement tools by providing that OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That language creates a statutory bridge: FOIA-withholding allegations can, in principle, be investigated by OSC and put through the same corrective or disciplinary channels that apply in ordinary prohibited-personnel-practice cases.

The bridge, however, raises interpretive and practical questions. The text of § 1216(a)(3) uses “shall,” which suggests a mandatory duty to investigate credible FOIA-withholding allegations, but § 1216(c) frames the available remedies as possibilities that are pursued “in the same way as if” a prohibited personnel practice were involved. Those cross-references mean that, at minimum, OSC has the power to deploy § 1214 and § 1215 remedies in FOIA cases; whether § 1216 itself imposes an absolute, judicially enforceable obligation to investigate every FOIA allegation or whether OSC retains meaningful enforcement discretion is a contested issue in practice and in the courts.

## FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i): FOIA’s separate, post‑judgment OSC trigger

FOIA contains a distinct enforcement provision that specifically involves OSC. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) states that when (1) a court orders production of records improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) the court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) the court “specifically question[s] whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” In short, FOIA creates a narrow, post‑judgment, mandatory OSC trigger tied to specific judicial findings. That provision is distinct from § 1216: it applies only after a court has resolved the FOIA litigation in a particular way, and it imposes an explicit duty on OSC to initiate a disciplinary proceeding in that post‑judgment context.

The text and narrow scope of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were central to the dispute in the America First case: OSC declined to investigate AFL’s complaint in part because the FOIA statutory prerequisites for the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger had not occurred, whereas AFL relied on § 1216’s investigatory language to press for OSC action without those FOIA-specific judicial findings.

## Whether §1214 procedures apply to §1216 investigations (and relevant case law)

A crucial doctrinal question is whether the procedural protections and notice obligations that § 1214 creates for complainants in ordinary prohibited-personnel-practice investigations automatically follow into § 1216 FOIA investigations. The statutory cross-reference in § 1216(c) makes § 1214 and § 1215 remedies available “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” but that phrasing has been read by some courts to preserve OSC’s discretion about whether to apply § 1214’s procedural apparatus in a § 1216 inquiry.

The opinion in America First cites this line of authority. It acknowledges that courts have treated OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation as discretionary (see Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)). That means a complainant who hopes to rely on § 1214’s notice or information rights must reckon with the possibility that OSC will investigate under § 1216 without invoking § 1214’s procedural protections. In the district-court proceedings in this case, the court concluded that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not limit OSC’s power under § 1216 and therefore treated OSC’s initial refusal to investigate as legally erroneous, but the same district court declined to order OSC to take enforcement steps because it viewed § 1216(c) as committing the exercise of enforcement authority to OSC’s discretion. The D.C. Circuit did not resolve that statutory conflict on the merits here because it disposed of the appeal on standing grounds, but the competing readings—§ 1216 as an independent investigatory duty versus FOIA’s narrow, post‑judgment OSC trigger and the agency’s residual enforcement discretion—frame the statutory debate underlying the parties’ claims.

Conclusion: In short, the CSRA gives OSC investigatory and prosecutorial authority and supplies the MSPB remedies described in §§ 1214–1215; § 1216 brings FOIA-withholding allegations within OSC’s investigatory orbit and ties those inquiries to §§ 1214–1215; and FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides a distinct, narrower, court‑triggered obligation to OSC after certain judicial findings. How those provisions interact—particularly whether § 1214’s procedural protections are automatically available in § 1216 investigations and whether § 1216 imposes a judicially enforceable duty to investigate apart from FOIA’s post‑judgment trigger—has produced differing judicial views and was a central point of dispute in the America First litigation.

# Standing Doctrines the Court Applies

## Article III case-or-controversy: injury in fact, causation, and redressability (Lujan)

Article III limits federal courts to actual “Cases” or “Controversies,” and that limitation is implemented through the familiar three-part standing test: a plaintiff must allege (1) an injury in fact that is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent; (2) causation—i.e., that the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct; and (3) redressability—i.e., that a favorable court decision will likely remedy the injury. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992), is the leading statement of that framework, and the court in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer applied it as the threshold jurisdictional question before reaching any merits.

Two points about posture are important here. First, standing is a subject‑matter‑jurisdiction question that courts must resolve even if the parties do not litigate it; the panel therefore ordered supplemental briefing and resolved standing before addressing the merits (Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83 (1998)). Second, at the pleading stage the plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each of Lujan’s elements—a nonconclusory factual showing, not merely labels (Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905 (D.C. Cir. 2015)).

Applied to AFL’s case, the court treated AFL’s asserted injuries under Lujan. AFL claimed two informational injuries: (a) DOJ’s alleged failure to produce FOIA records it requested, and (b) OSC’s failure to provide notices or other information that AFL expected would follow from an OSC investigation. The panel concluded that AFL failed Lujan on causation and redressability for the first theory and failed to show a concrete, statutory entitlement to information for the second—so neither theory satisfied Article III.

## Procedural injury framework: relaxed imminence and redressability only if tied to a threatened concrete interest (Center for Law & Education; Florida Audubon)

Courts recognize a special posture for alleged procedural injuries: when a plaintiff asserts that a statutory procedural right was violated and that procedure was designed to protect some concrete interest, courts may relax (but do not eliminate) the imminence and redressability requirements. See Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005); Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Crucially, however, the plaintiff must still allege an injury in fact and a causal link between the procedural violation and a concrete, particularized harm.

The panel recognized that procedural‑rights doctrine but noted that it provides only a limited relaxation. The court framed AFL’s claim as an asserted procedural right to an OSC investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Even applying the relaxed approach, the panel required AFL to show that the alleged denial of procedure resulted in a threatened concrete interest. AFL could not do so: its first alleged concrete interest (receiving DOJ’s FOIA records) was not plausibly caused by OSC’s investigatory decision, and its second alleged interest (receiving OSC investigative notices) was not an entitlement created by the statute. Because AFL could not satisfy the relaxed standards, the panel declined to resolve whether the asserted injuries were procedural or substantive.

## No standing for a procedural right in vacuo (Summers; Citizens for Constitutional Integrity)

The Supreme Court’s Summers v. Earth Island Institute, 555 U.S. 488 (2009), supplies the blunt statement of the problem: the deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone and without some concrete interest affected by that deprivation, is insufficient to confer Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit has applied that rule in similar contexts (see Citizens for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618 (D.C. Cir. 2024)), and the panel relied on that line of authority.

Grounding this principle in the facts, the court rejected AFL’s attempt to rest standing simply on a statutory right to have OSC investigate. AFL’s reliance on a “procedural right in vacuo” failed because AFL did not identify a concrete, particularized interest that was actually harmed by OSC’s decision not to investigate. Thus, even if § 1216 imposed a procedural obligation, the mere nonperformance of that obligation—without a demonstrable concrete harm traceable to OSC—was insufficient to establish Article III jurisdiction.

## Informational standing: plaintiff must be denied information a statute entitles them to receive (Akins; Public Citizen; ASPCA; Zivotofsky)

A special species of standing recognizes informational injuries: a plaintiff can have a concrete injury when a statute entitles the plaintiff to particular information and the defendant withholds it. See FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998); Public Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440 (1989); Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13 (D.C. Cir. 2011); see also Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614 (D.C. Cir. 2006).

The panel applied that rule to both of AFL’s informational theories and reached different conclusions as to why each failed. For AFL’s first theory—the FOIA records themselves—the court said any informational injury would be traceable to DOJ’s withholding, not OSC’s decision; the causal chain from an OSC investigation to DOJ’s production was too speculative. That chain required several independent, discretionary steps (OSC choosing to prosecute, the MSPB imposing sanctions, DOJ thereafter producing records), and standing cannot rest on such conjectural links or on speculative actions by independent actors (citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367 (2024), and Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), on agency enforcement discretion).

For AFL’s second theory—the denial of notices and investigative information from OSC—the court focused on entitlement. Informational standing requires that a statute actually entitle the plaintiff to the information. Section 1216(a)(3) may require OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but it does not themselves create a right in outside requesters to receive OSC’s investigative notices. AFL’s fallback—that section 1214’s notice provisions would attach automatically to a § 1216 investigation—was foreclosed by the parties’ own acknowledgement of controlling authority (Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011)) that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 matters is discretionary. Because AFL identified no statutory entitlement to the investigative information it seeks, it lacks informational standing on that theory as well.

## Pleading-stage burden: plausible factual allegations for each element (Food & Water Watch); appellate court’s obligation to ensure jurisdiction (Steel Co.)

Finally, the court’s analysis reflects the standards that govern standing at the pleading stage and the appellate duty to police jurisdiction. A plaintiff must plead factual matter sufficient to make each element of standing plausible, not merely assert legal conclusions (Food & Water Watch). Because neither of AFL’s theories survived that factual‑pleading screen—one faltering on traceability and redressability, the other on statutory entitlement—the panel held AFL had not carried its burden.

The court also exercised its responsibility to ensure subject‑matter jurisdiction even though the parties had not pressed the issue at first. Citing Steel Co., the panel required supplemental briefing and then resolved standing sua sponte on appeal. Having concluded Article III standing was lacking, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction, without reaching the merits of AFL’s statutory arguments.

Takeaway for students: when a plaintiff invokes a statutory procedural right (including to an investigation) or claims an informational injury, the courts will ask three things: (1) is there a concrete, particularized injury traceable to the defendant; (2) would a favorable decision likely redress that injury; and (3) if the injury is informational, does the statute actually entitle the plaintiff to the withheld information? Procedural‑rights doctrines relax some requirements only when the procedural right is designed to protect a concrete interest; they do not allow suit for a procedural right standing alone. In practice, that often means a plaintiff must plead a plausible causal chain linking the defendant’s conduct to a tangible, redressable harm—something AFL was unable to do in this case.

# Applying Standing to AFL’s FOIA-Records Theory

## AFL’s theory: OSC’s failure to investigate under § 1216 prevented DOJ from producing records requested under FOIA

AFL’s central standing argument was simple: because OSC refused to investigate DOJ under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, DOJ never produced the emails AFL had requested under FOIA, and that non‑production is the concrete informational injury that gives AFL Article III standing. The factual background matters here. AFL sought certain emails from DOJ’s Civil Division and sued DOJ under FOIA after DOJ declined to search employee emails without consent. Separately, AFL asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails. OSC declined to open a section 1216 inquiry on the ground that the FOIA enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), requires particular judicial findings before OSC “shall promptly initiate” disciplinary proceedings. AFL insists § 1216 independently requires OSC to investigate, and that OSC’s refusal deprived AFL of the requested information.

The court framed AFL’s argument as an informational‑injury theory: the deprivation of FOIA records allegedly flowed from OSC’s failure to investigate. To evaluate that theory the court applied the familiar Article III triplet—injury in fact, causation (traceability), and redressability—and focused on whether AFL had plausibly pleaded that its informational injury was caused by OSC and could be remedied by a favorable ruling against OSC.

## Traceability: any informational injury from non-disclosure is attributable to DOJ, not OSC

Traceability requires that the plaintiff’s injury be fairly traceable to the defendant’s challenged conduct rather than to the independent action of some third party. The opinion applied that principle directly: the harm AFL complains of is DOJ’s non‑disclosure of the requested records, and that non‑disclosure is “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.” In other words, DOJ’s decision about whether to produce records is the proximate source of the asserted informational injury; OSC’s refusal to investigate is, at best, one step removed.

The court stressed that a plaintiff cannot bootstrap standing by pointing to remote or attenuated links between the defendant’s conduct and the ultimate harm. To be traceable, the causal connection must not rest on conjecture about how third parties (here, OSC, MSPB, and DOJ) would act in response to one another. If the injury instead depends on a chain of speculative choices by independent actors, it fails the traceability requirement.

## Speculative causal chain: OSC might investigate; might pursue § 1214/§ 1215 action; MSPB might sanction; DOJ might then produce records—reliance on independent third‑party decisions (FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine)

The opinion set out the causal chain AFL needed to prove for traceability and showed why each link is too speculative. AFL’s chain runs: OSC would have investigated under § 1216; OSC might then have sought corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) might have imposed sanctions on the DOJ employees responsible for the withholding; and, in response to OSC or MSPB action (or the mere prospect of sanction), DOJ might have produced the requested emails. The court treated this sequence as an impermissibly attenuated theory of causation.

Two features make the chain speculative. First, it depends on the discretionary choices of multiple independent entities that are not parties to the litigation (OSC’s enforcement choices, the MSPB’s adjudication and sanctioning decisions, and DOJ’s production choices). As the court quoted from the Supreme Court, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” Relying on such an unpredictable sequence does not satisfy the plaintiff’s burden to plead a plausible causal link at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage. Second, even if OSC were ordered to investigate, what OSC would do thereafter—whether it would bring corrective or disciplinary proceedings—is itself the kind of enforcement decision courts typically cannot compel or predict reliably.

## Limits on compelling agency enforcement discretion (Heckler v. Chaney)

The court reinforced the speculative nature of AFL’s chain by invoking the familiar doctrine that agency enforcement decisions are presumptively committed to agency discretion. Heckler v. Chaney stands for the proposition that courts normally may not direct agencies to institute enforcement proceedings or dictate the manner of enforcement. That rule undercuts AFL’s premise that an OSC investigation would lead inexorably to corrective or disciplinary proceedings and, thence, to DOJ production. Because OSC’s choice to pursue § 1214 or § 1215 action is discretionary (and would itself be subject to choice and judgment), a plaintiff cannot rely on a court order against OSC as a reliable mechanism to compel downstream action by other independent actors.

## Redressability must turn on relief binding the parties, not the potential persuasive effect on nonparties (Haaland v. Brackeen); need for a ‘very likely’ link to spur next steps (Hecate Energy v. FERC)

Closely related to traceability is redressability: a favorable judicial decision must make it likely—not merely possible—that the plaintiff’s injury will be remedied. The court emphasized two principles. First, redressability generally depends on how a judgment would bind the defendant before the court, not on whether the judgment’s reasoning might persuade nonparties to act differently. Haaland v. Brackeen makes this point: a remedy that only might induce independent actors to change course is insufficient. Second, the court relied on the recent D.C. Circuit formulation requiring that relief would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury. An order compelling OSC to investigate would not itself bind DOJ or the MSPB, and AFL had not shown that such an order would very likely precipitate the chain of corrective steps it posited. Because the relief sought would not directly control the conduct of the primary wrongdoer (DOJ) and instead depended on speculative responses by other actors, redressability failed.

## Result: no standing under the first theory due to failures of causation and redressability

Taken together, the lack of a non‑speculative causal link and the weak prospects for redress doomed AFL’s FOIA‑records standing theory. The court held that any informational injury from DOJ’s refusal to disclose records was not fairly traceable to OSC’s refusal to investigate, and that even a favorable ruling against OSC would not be likely to remedy the alleged injury. Because AFL could not satisfy Article III’s causation and redressability requirements (even under the more relaxed approach sometimes applied to procedural‑rights claims), the court concluded AFL lacked standing on this theory and did not reach the merits.

For students reading the opinion, the takeaway is structural: when a plaintiff seeks to establish standing based on the supposed effects of one government actor’s inaction on another government actor’s behavior, closely map the causal chain the plaintiff must rely on. Courts will treat chains that depend on discretionary decisions by independent agencies as speculative for both traceability and redressability purposes, and they will ask whether a judicial order would bind the relevant decisionmakers or merely hope to persuade them.

# Applying Standing to AFL’s OSC-Information Theory; Disposition and Significance

## AFL’s claim to investigation-related information

AFL advanced a second, distinct standing theory based on an alleged informational injury: it said OSC’s refusal to conduct a §1216 investigation deprived AFL of the notices and updates that OSC would (or might) provide if it investigated FOIA withholdings in the same manner as it handles prohibited personnel practices under §1214. That framing treats the missing investigative communications themselves as the injury—an informational denial—and relies on the idea that courts relax some standing requirements when a statutory procedural right is withheld. Practically, AFL argued that even if OSC’s failure to investigate did not itself cause the DOJ’s withholding, the denial of the investigative process (and the attendant notices) is nonetheless a cognizable concrete interest that the court can vindicate.

## No statutory entitlement

The D.C. Circuit rejected this theory at the threshold. The court emphasized that an informational injury gives Article III standing only when the plaintiff is denied information to which a statute entitles him. The opinion follows the line that a mere procedural deprivation “in vacuo” is insufficient; a plaintiff must point to a statutory entitlement to receive the information. Applying that test here, the court observed that §1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings, but the statutory language does not itself create a right for complainants to receive the communications that §1214 furnishes when OSC handles a prohibited personnel practice. In short, §1216 may command investigation, but it does not—by text—entitle a private complainant to be notified of investigatory progress in the manner §1214 describes. Because AFL could not point to any statutory provision that affirmatively entitled it to the notices and updates it sought, its asserted informational injury failed the necessary “statute-entitled” prerequisite for informational standing.

## Discretionary application of §1214 procedures in §1216 investigations (Carson)

The court reinforced its statutory-text conclusion by pointing to precedent treating the application of §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations as discretionary. The Sixth Circuit’s decision in Carson (which AFL itself acknowledged) holds that OSC’s choice whether to follow §1214’s notice and other procedural requirements when it acts under §1216 is not mandatory. That discretion matters two ways: it undercuts AFL’s claim of entitlement (because §1214 notices are not automatically grafted onto §1216 investigations), and it undermines any claim that a judicial order to investigate would necessarily deliver the procedural communications AFL wants. Where the statute vests OSC with discretion to apply or not apply §1214 procedures, a plaintiff cannot ground standing on an asserted right to receive those procedures unless the statute says otherwise.

## Redressability

Even apart from entitlement and concrete injury, the court found redressability problems. Redressability requires that a favorable judicial decision make it likely—not merely possible—that the plaintiff’s injury will be remedied. The opinion explains that ordering OSC to investigate would not be “reasonably likely” to produce §1214-style notices because OSC can choose whether to apply §1214 procedures in a §1216 investigation. More broadly, where relief depends on a chain of discretionary actions by agencies and boards (OSC deciding to take corrective or disciplinary action, the MSPB imposing sanctions, DOJ then producing records), causation and redressability rest on speculation about independent actors’ choices. The court relied on the familiar principle that standing cannot be sustained by conjecture about how non‑parties will act, and it invoked Heckler v. Chaney to underline that courts generally cannot compel or second-guess enforcement discretion. Thus, even under the relaxed rules that sometimes attend procedural-right claims, AFL could not show that a judicial order directing an OSC investigation would likely produce the specific informational disclosures it sought.

## Disposition

Because AFL failed to show a concrete, traceable, and likely-to-be-redressed informational injury, the D.C. Circuit concluded AFL lacked Article III standing. Standing is jurisdictional, so the court vacated the district court’s merits decision and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. In other words, the appellate court did not reach or resolve whether §1216 in fact requires OSC to investigate or whether OSC legally erred; it disposed of the case on the jurisdictional threshold and instructed dismissal rather than affirming or reversing the district court’s merits rulings.

## Significance

This portion of the opinion illustrates several important points for students reading standing opinions. First, informational or procedural injuries require a statutory entitlement to the information or process claimed; without that entitlement a plaintiff’s grievance is a procedural right in vacuo and will not sustain Article III jurisdiction. Second, plaintiffs cannot establish standing by pointing to chains of discretionary, independent decisions that might—if everything went one way—produce the relief they want; courts require a non‑speculative causal link and a remedy that will bind the defendant in a way likely to redress the injury. Third, agency enforcement routes are a precarious vehicle for third‑party relief: asking a court to force an enforcement agency to investigate, in the hope that that investigation will trigger relief from other actors, risks dismissal for lack of standing. For litigants who want FOIA disclosures, the safer path is to press FOIA claims directly against the records-holding agency; using a separate enforcement mechanism to obtain investigatory updates or to indirectly compel disclosure faces substantial jurisdictional hurdles.
