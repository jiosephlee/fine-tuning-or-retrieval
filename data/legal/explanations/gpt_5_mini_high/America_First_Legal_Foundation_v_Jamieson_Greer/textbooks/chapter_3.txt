Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # Standing Doctrines the Court Applies

## Article III case-or-controversy: injury in fact, causation, and redressability (Lujan)

Article III limits federal courts to actual “Cases” or “Controversies,” and that limitation is implemented through the familiar three-part standing test: a plaintiff must allege (1) an injury in fact that is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent; (2) causation—i.e., that the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct; and (3) redressability—i.e., that a favorable court decision will likely remedy the injury. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992), is the leading statement of that framework, and the court in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer applied it as the threshold jurisdictional question before reaching any merits.

Two points about posture are important here. First, standing is a subject‑matter‑jurisdiction question that courts must resolve even if the parties do not litigate it; the panel therefore ordered supplemental briefing and resolved standing before addressing the merits (Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83 (1998)). Second, at the pleading stage the plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each of Lujan’s elements—a nonconclusory factual showing, not merely labels (Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905 (D.C. Cir. 2015)).

Applied to AFL’s case, the court treated AFL’s asserted injuries under Lujan. AFL claimed two informational injuries: (a) DOJ’s alleged failure to produce FOIA records it requested, and (b) OSC’s failure to provide notices or other information that AFL expected would follow from an OSC investigation. The panel concluded that AFL failed Lujan on causation and redressability for the first theory and failed to show a concrete, statutory entitlement to information for the second—so neither theory satisfied Article III.

## Procedural injury framework: relaxed imminence and redressability only if tied to a threatened concrete interest (Center for Law & Education; Florida Audubon)

Courts recognize a special posture for alleged procedural injuries: when a plaintiff asserts that a statutory procedural right was violated and that procedure was designed to protect some concrete interest, courts may relax (but do not eliminate) the imminence and redressability requirements. See Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005); Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen, 94 F.3d 658 (D.C. Cir. 1996) (en banc). Crucially, however, the plaintiff must still allege an injury in fact and a causal link between the procedural violation and a concrete, particularized harm.

The panel recognized that procedural‑rights doctrine but noted that it provides only a limited relaxation. The court framed AFL’s claim as an asserted procedural right to an OSC investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). Even applying the relaxed approach, the panel required AFL to show that the alleged denial of procedure resulted in a threatened concrete interest. AFL could not do so: its first alleged concrete interest (receiving DOJ’s FOIA records) was not plausibly caused by OSC’s investigatory decision, and its second alleged interest (receiving OSC investigative notices) was not an entitlement created by the statute. Because AFL could not satisfy the relaxed standards, the panel declined to resolve whether the asserted injuries were procedural or substantive.

## No standing for a procedural right in vacuo (Summers; Citizens for Constitutional Integrity)

The Supreme Court’s Summers v. Earth Island Institute, 555 U.S. 488 (2009), supplies the blunt statement of the problem: the deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone and without some concrete interest affected by that deprivation, is insufficient to confer Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit has applied that rule in similar contexts (see Citizens for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau, 115 F.4th 618 (D.C. Cir. 2024)), and the panel relied on that line of authority.

Grounding this principle in the facts, the court rejected AFL’s attempt to rest standing simply on a statutory right to have OSC investigate. AFL’s reliance on a “procedural right in vacuo” failed because AFL did not identify a concrete, particularized interest that was actually harmed by OSC’s decision not to investigate. Thus, even if § 1216 imposed a procedural obligation, the mere nonperformance of that obligation—without a demonstrable concrete harm traceable to OSC—was insufficient to establish Article III jurisdiction.

## Informational standing: plaintiff must be denied information a statute entitles them to receive (Akins; Public Citizen; ASPCA; Zivotofsky)

A special species of standing recognizes informational injuries: a plaintiff can have a concrete injury when a statute entitles the plaintiff to particular information and the defendant withholds it. See FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998); Public Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440 (1989); Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13 (D.C. Cir. 2011); see also Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Sec’y of State, 444 F.3d 614 (D.C. Cir. 2006).

The panel applied that rule to both of AFL’s informational theories and reached different conclusions as to why each failed. For AFL’s first theory—the FOIA records themselves—the court said any informational injury would be traceable to DOJ’s withholding, not OSC’s decision; the causal chain from an OSC investigation to DOJ’s production was too speculative. That chain required several independent, discretionary steps (OSC choosing to prosecute, the MSPB imposing sanctions, DOJ thereafter producing records), and standing cannot rest on such conjectural links or on speculative actions by independent actors (citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367 (2024), and Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), on agency enforcement discretion).

For AFL’s second theory—the denial of notices and investigative information from OSC—the court focused on entitlement. Informational standing requires that a statute actually entitle the plaintiff to the information. Section 1216(a)(3) may require OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but it does not themselves create a right in outside requesters to receive OSC’s investigative notices. AFL’s fallback—that section 1214’s notice provisions would attach automatically to a § 1216 investigation—was foreclosed by the parties’ own acknowledgement of controlling authority (Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011)) that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 matters is discretionary. Because AFL identified no statutory entitlement to the investigative information it seeks, it lacks informational standing on that theory as well.

## Pleading-stage burden: plausible factual allegations for each element (Food & Water Watch); appellate court’s obligation to ensure jurisdiction (Steel Co.)

Finally, the court’s analysis reflects the standards that govern standing at the pleading stage and the appellate duty to police jurisdiction. A plaintiff must plead factual matter sufficient to make each element of standing plausible, not merely assert legal conclusions (Food & Water Watch). Because neither of AFL’s theories survived that factual‑pleading screen—one faltering on traceability and redressability, the other on statutory entitlement—the panel held AFL had not carried its burden.

The court also exercised its responsibility to ensure subject‑matter jurisdiction even though the parties had not pressed the issue at first. Citing Steel Co., the panel required supplemental briefing and then resolved standing sua sponte on appeal. Having concluded Article III standing was lacking, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction, without reaching the merits of AFL’s statutory arguments.

Takeaway for students: when a plaintiff invokes a statutory procedural right (including to an investigation) or claims an informational injury, the courts will ask three things: (1) is there a concrete, particularized injury traceable to the defendant; (2) would a favorable decision likely redress that injury; and (3) if the injury is informational, does the statute actually entitle the plaintiff to the withheld information? Procedural‑rights doctrines relax some requirements only when the procedural right is designed to protect a concrete interest; they do not allow suit for a procedural right standing alone. In practice, that often means a plaintiff must plead a plausible causal chain linking the defendant’s conduct to a tangible, redressable harm—something AFL was unable to do in this case.
