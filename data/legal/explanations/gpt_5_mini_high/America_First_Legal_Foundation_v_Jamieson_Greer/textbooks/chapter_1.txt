Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 1: # Background and Procedural Posture

## AFL’s FOIA request to DOJ and DOJ’s asserted practice
The controversy began when America First Legal Foundation (AFL) submitted a Freedom of Information Act request to the Department of Justice’s Civil Division seeking emails related to judicial recusals. AFL alleged that the Civil Division refused to search employee email accounts for responsive records unless the individual employee consented to the search. Treating that practice as an institutional FOIA policy of withholding, AFL also filed a separate FOIA suit against DOJ to obtain the requested records. DOJ has produced records in that suit and continues to produce additional material; the district court and the D.C. Circuit treat the sufficiency of that production as not squarely before them in this collateral challenge.

## AFL’s complaint to the Office of Special Counsel under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)
Because OSC has statutory authority to investigate certain forms of employee misconduct and to seek corrective or disciplinary action before the Merit Systems Protection Board, AFL asked the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552” (the FOIA provision). OSC’s investigatory tools include pursuing corrective action under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 (with procedures that include notice to a complaining party) and disciplinary proceedings under § 1215; AFL sought an OSC inquiry as a route to remedy alleged FOIA misconduct by DOJ employees.

## OSC’s refusal to investigate and FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)
OSC declined AFL’s request for an investigation. OSC’s stated reason was that FOIA contains an explicit OSC-enforcement provision—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—that directs OSC to “promptly initiate a proceeding” only after three judicial predicates are satisfied: (1) a court orders production of improperly withheld records, (2) the court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) the court specifically questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.” Because those three prerequisites were not present, OSC disclaimed authority to pursue an OSC-initiated proceeding in response to AFL’s complaint. AFL maintained that § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” language imposed an independent obligation on OSC that did not depend on the FOIA provision’s three predicates.

## District court proceedings: finding legal error but remanding; final dismissal
AFL sued OSC in district court seeking to compel an investigation. The district court agreed with AFL on one legal point: it held that FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC’s investigative authority under § 1216, so OSC’s initial categorical refusal to investigate rested on a legal error. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023). Even so, the district court declined to order OSC to open an investigation. It reasoned that § 1216(c) leaves to OSC discretion whether to apply § 1214’s procedural protections when pursuing corrective action and thus committed the decision how to proceed to the agency; accordingly the court remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration rather than directing a particular investigatory outcome. After OSC again declined to investigate on remand, AFL sought entry of final judgment; the district court then dismissed the remaining claims, and AFL appealed.

## Appeal to the D.C. Circuit and the emergence of the standing question
On appeal to the D.C. Circuit, the court stated that it reviews the district court’s legal rulings de novo. Because neither party had originally briefed Article III standing—a jurisdictional prerequisite—the panel ordered supplemental briefing on whether AFL had standing to sue OSC. Standing, and whether AFL’s asserted injuries were sufficiently concrete, causally connected to OSC’s refusal, and likely redressed by a court order, thus became the dispositive issue on appeal. The D.C. Circuit ultimately concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing, vacated the district court’s judgment, and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
