Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Standing, Speculation, and FOIA: The D.C. Circuit Closes the Door on Suits to Force OSC Investigations

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is an important drill-down on the boundaries of Article III standing when plaintiffs try to use the courts to compel an executive-branch enforcement body to act. At stake is a recurring strategic question in FOIA and administrative-law litigation: can a requester sue a watchdog or prosecutor (here, the Office of Special Counsel) to force an investigation into an agency’s alleged withholding of records, or must a challenger proceed directly against the agency that withheld the records?

The panel’s answer is emphatic: not here. By treating the asserted injuries as either traceable to the withholding agency (DOJ) or as too attenuated to be redressed by a court order compelling OSC to investigate, the opinion tightens both causation and redressability requirements for procedural- and informational-injury claims. That puts a practical limit on efforts to use the courts to marshal third-party enforcement agencies into action.

## What the court held

America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought DOJ emails under FOIA and separately asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged practice of not searching employee email accounts without consent. OSC declined, on the view that a separate FOIA-triggered enforcement provision (§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) had not been satisfied. AFL sued; the district court found error but ultimately remanded. On appeal the D.C. Circuit did not reach the statutory question. Instead it ordered supplemental briefing on Article III standing and concluded that AFL lacked standing. The court vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

The court rejected both of AFL’s standing theories. First, any informational injury from DOJ’s withholding was traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain by which an OSC investigation might lead to MSPB sanctions and then to DOJ compliance was too speculative to support redressability. Second, AFL’s claimed informational interest in receiving notices or investigative fruits from OSC failed because § 1216, even if it requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, creates no entitlement to information—section 1214’s notice rules do not automatically attach in § 1216 investigations and, in any event, OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 matters is discretionary. Because AFL could not show a concrete injury likely to be redressed by the relief sought, it lacked Article III standing.

## How the court reasoned — tightening causation and redressability

The panel’s analysis rests on well-known Article III building blocks: injury-in-fact, causation (traceability), and redressability. But its application of those principles is notable for how insistently it applies recent Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit precedent to reject attenuated causation theories.

On AFL’s first theory—that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused AFL’s informational injury from DOJ’s continued nondisclosure—the court emphasized that the immediate source of the alleged harm was DOJ’s withholding decision. Any remedial sequence that would transform an OSC investigation into document production required several contingent events by independent actors (OSC’s decision to investigate aggressively, the MSPB’s choice and potential sanctions, DOJ’s reaction). Citing the Supreme Court’s recent boundary-setting on speculative causation and redressability, the court treated that multi-step chain as too conjectural to sustain standing. The point is doctrinally familiar: causation cannot rest on speculation about the “unfettered choices” of actors not before the court.

On the second theory—that AFL had a standalone informational right to notices or investigation outcomes from OSC—the panel leaned on the principle that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive information. It concluded § 1216, as read here, does not create such an entitlement to OSC-produced information. Nor does § 1216 automatically import § 1214’s notice regime; the latter’s application to § 1216 investigations is discretionary under controlling precedent. Without an identifiable statutory entitlement to the investigatory information, AFL’s injury was not the kind of concrete informational injury that carries Article III standing.

Throughout, the opinion anchors itself in Summers’ warning against “procedural rights in vacuo” and recent decisions underscoring that a procedural-statute claim must be tethered to a concrete, redressable interest. In short, relaxed standing for procedural claims is not a license to sue whenever a plaintiff can allege a statutory procedural entitlement in the abstract.

## Why this ruling matters doctrinally — and what it leaves open

Doctrinally, the decision reinforces two strands of modern standing law. First, it underscores that the relaxed or “procedural” standing line remains narrow: plaintiffs must show (not just allege) that the violation of a procedural right injured some concrete interest and that litigation will likely redress that injury. Second, it reiterates that redressability is measured against how a court’s order will bind the defendant, not against how persuasive a court’s reasoning might be to third parties.

Those emphases make sense in institutional terms. They keep courts from being turned into after-the-fact managers of executive enforcement choices and preserve the core of Heckler v. Chaney—that an agency’s prosecutorial discretion is usually not subject to judicial compulsion. Standing doctrine, in this view, functions as a guardrail against federal judicial micromanagement of executive priorities.

But important questions remain open. The D.C. Circuit expressly declined to reach the underlying statutory interpretation: whether § 1216 independently imposes a duty on OSC to investigate FOIA-withholding claims regardless of the narrow FOIA-trigger set out in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The district court had thought § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not limit § 1216, but because AFL lacked standing the panel did not and did not need to decide that statutory dispute. That leaves substantive statutory questions unresolved, and it preserves a path for future litigation if a plaintiff can establish the requisite standing facts.

The ruling also leaves unsettled whether a different kind of plaintiff—a person able to show a more direct injury traceable to OSC’s nonaction, or a plaintiff seeking different relief that would bind OSC more directly—could clear the Article III bar. The panel treated as decisive the speculative nature of the chain from OSC non-investigation to eventual production; a case presenting a tighter causal link (for example, where OSC’s investigatory powers produce statutorily required notices that themselves would compel agency action) could yield a different standing result.

## Practical effects and downstream implications

For FOIA practitioners and public-interest litigants, the immediate practical takeaway is straightforward: if your goal is disclosure, your most reliable route remains litigation directly against the withholding agency under FOIA rather than suing a separate enforcement body to force it into action. Victory in a FOIA suit that produces a court order and attorney’s fees can trigger OSC’s FOIA-specific enforcement obligation under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), but absent those judicial predicates plaintiffs cannot count on using OSC as a backdoor to compel production.

More broadly, the decision likely will be cited to resist suits that seek to compel investigative action by other oversight actors—inspectors general, independent counsel offices, or similar entities—where any remedy would depend on successive decisions by other agencies or political actors. The combination of standing scrutiny and Heckler’s protection of enforcement discretion will make such suits difficult unless the plaintiff can identify a direct, legally protected interest harmed by the overseer’s refusal to act.

That institutional insulation could, however, have democratic costs. OSC’s willingness to investigate can be an important check when FOIA litigation stalls or when systemic agency practices make it difficult for individual requesters to obtain relief. If litigants cannot hold a watchdog accountable in court for declining to investigate, enforcement will be left to internal executive incentives, political oversight, or Congress. That dynamic may encourage legislative responses: Congress could clarify OSC’s investigatory duties or create a clearer entitlement to particular investigatory information to stabilize the standing analysis. Absent such changes, critics will likely argue that the decision lets an important layer of accountability escape meaningful judicial scrutiny.

## Bottom line

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer tightens the Article III constraints on attempting to use the courts to force executive enforcement action. The D.C. Circuit’s refusal to rest standing on hypothetical chains of agency action reinforces recent Supreme Court emphases on non-speculative causation and meaningful redressability, and it preserves the executive’s discretion not to prosecute or investigate. The ruling narrows one avenue that FOIA challengers have used to seek accountability, leaving litigants to pursue more direct FOIA claims against withholding agencies or to press Congress for clearer enforcement tools. What it does not do—because it did not need to—is resolve the statutory question about the scope of OSC’s investigatory duty under § 1216, so that issue remains available for another day if a litigant can clear Article III’s gate.
