Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Chaney’s Long Shadow: Why Private Plaintiffs Can’t Conscript Executive Enforcement

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer (Oct. 3, 2025) is a short, crisp reminder that Article III standing and the nonreviewability of enforcement choices remain powerful limits on efforts to use the federal courts to police executive-branch priorities. At stake was a familiar if consequential tactic: a private requester who sued under FOIA tried to force the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) — a statutory watchdog with authority to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings — to open an investigation. The panel did not reach the novel statutory question whether OSC’s “shall … conduct an investigation” language is self-executing. Instead, relying on standing doctrine and the logic of Heckler v. Chaney, the court concluded AFL lacked Article III standing and sent the case back for dismissal. The practical lesson is straightforward: plaintiffs cannot generally conscript the Executive’s enforcement machinery unless they can show a concrete, redressable injury traceable to the defendant.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

Judge Katsas’s opinion begins with familiar standing basics: a plaintiff must show injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability. AFL framed its injury as procedural (OSC’s alleged statutory duty to investigate) but buttressed that claim with two putative informational injuries. First, AFL said OSC’s refusal to investigate had prevented it from obtaining the FOIA records it sought from DOJ. Second, AFL said it was deprived of the notices and information that OSC would provide in a §1214-style corrective investigation.

The court disposed of both theories. The claimed FOIA-disclosure injury was (the court held) traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain from an OSC investigation to DOJ’s production was too speculative to support standing. An OSC probe might lead to corrective or disciplinary action, which might lead the MSPB to sanction employees, which might lead DOJ to produce records — a chain the court characterized as dependent on independent actors and discretionary choices. That analysis leaned on Chaney’s core insight about prosecutorial discretion and recent standing decisions cautioning against speculation about the choices of third parties.

The second informational theory failed for a different reason. Informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive information; AFL could not point to any statutory right to the notices it sought. To the contrary, AFL conceded — and the court relied on precedent — that OSC’s decision whether to apply §1214’s procedural protections in a §1216 investigation is discretionary. Absent an entitlement to information, there was no concrete informational injury that a judicial order could redress.

Because AFL could not clear the relaxed standing hurdles that apply when a procedural right is asserted, the D.C. Circuit vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

## Chaney vs. Norton v. SUWA — the doctrinal fault line

This opinion is best read as the D.C. Circuit steering between two established doctrines. Norton v. SUWA created a narrow route for compelled action suits: where a statute imposes a discrete, nondiscretionary duty, courts may compel the agency to act. Heckler v. Chaney, by contrast, announced the baseline rule that where enforcement and prosecutorial choices are involved, courts generally should not substitute judicial judgment for executive prioritization.

Here the court did not directly settle whether §1216’s “shall … conduct an investigation” is in the SUWA category of judicially enforceable, nondiscretionary obligations. Instead it closed the case on standing grounds, applying Chaney’s policy logic in the redressability and causation analysis: the relief AFL sought (records from DOJ, or investigatory notices from OSC) would depend on a sequence of discretionary choices by agencies not all before the court. That made redressability remote even if a court ordered OSC to investigate. So while SUWA remains theoretically available when a statute imposes a clear, individualized duty, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion signals caution about stretching that doctrine to convert generalized oversight commands into private entitlements.

## What this means going forward

The decision has practical and doctrinal ripple effects:

First, FOIA requesters remain anchored to FOIA litigation for immediate relief. Using an independent oversight body to force disclosure looks like an uphill climb: a plaintiff must show the denial to the oversight body caused an injury and that a judicial order would directly produce the sought-after relief.

Second, the opinion discourages a popular litigation strategy of “conscript[ing]” executive watchdogs — OSC, Inspectors General, ethics offices, and similar bodies — as proxy enforcers simply because Congress gave them investigatory authority. Unless the statute creates a plaintiff-centered entitlement or the plaintiff can demonstrate a concrete, traceable injury, Article III will bar the suit.

Third, the ruling reinforces separation-of-powers concerns. Courts are wary of turning themselves into referees of executive enforcement priorities. That restraint preserves executive discretion over resource allocation and avoids micromanaging enforcement decisions, but it also narrows private avenues for accountability when internal enforcement offices decline to act.

Finally, Congress that cares about private enforcement or accountability has levers if it wants a different outcome: it can create clear, non-discretionary duties, provide an express private right of action, or structure statutory remedies so that a plaintiff can show a concrete, individualized entitlement to information or relief. Absent that, litigants seeking to compel investigatory action will face an uphill Article III barrier.

## Bottom line

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a tidy chapter in an ongoing story: procedural statutory language that looks commanding on paper does not automatically give private parties standing to drag enforcement agencies into court. The D.C. Circuit did not decide whether OSC’s statutory “shall” is substantive and judicially enforceable; it found that, on the facts here, AFL lacked a concrete, redressable injury, and that the relief it sought would depend on discretionary choices by independent actors. For plaintiffs hoping to make enforcement offices into litigation tools, Chaney’s long shadow remains a powerful deterrent.
