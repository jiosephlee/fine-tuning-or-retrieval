Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 5: # Factor One, Interdependence of Factors, and Disposition

## Interdependence: commercial impression under factor one is informed by marketplace strength/weakness developed under factor six

The court’s disposition emphasizes a basic, practical point about the DuPont inquiry: the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks “in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression”) cannot be evaluated in a vacuum. The Board’s assessment of the marks’ “commercial impression” is necessarily informed by its factual findings about the mark’s marketplace strength or weakness under the sixth DuPont factor (the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”). If a term is shown to be widely used in a particular field—through third‑party registrations and marketplace use—its commercial strength is reduced and the scope of protection narrows; that factual backdrop changes how the first-factor comparison will read to a typical consumer. Conversely, a finding that a mark is commercially strong widens the scope of protection and makes the first-factor comparison more likely to favor confusion. The Federal Circuit therefore treats the sixth-factor findings as context for the commercial‑impression analysis under factor one.

In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the Board found the services offered under the competing marks to be highly similar (the second DuPont factor), but then constrained its sixth-factor review to third‑party marks tied only to “credit card services.” The court held that narrowing the field for factor six while embracing a broad similarity of services for factor two was legally inconsistent: where services are found to overlap substantially, the relevant universe of third‑party marks for the sixth factor must reflect that overlap. Because the commercial‑impression inquiry under factor one depends on whether ASPIRE is a crowded (weak) or exclusive (strong) term in the relevant marketplace, any error in the sixth‑factor assessment undermines the Board’s factor‑one analysis.

## Vacatur of factor one: because re‑evaluating factor six may alter the perceived strength of ASPIRE, the Board’s factor‑one analysis must be reconsidered

The court vacated the Board’s sixth-factor finding because the Board improperly limited the universe of third‑party marks to credit‑card‑only uses even though it had earlier determined that banking and credit‑card services are highly similar (and partly legally identical). That legal error required remand on factor six so the Board could consider third‑party uses across the broader set of banking and financing services the parties identified and the Board itself found to overlap.

Because the Board’s first-factor analysis relied upon the prior (flawed) sixth‑factor conclusion about ASPIRE’s marketplace strength, the court necessarily vacated the Board’s factor‑one findings as well. The court explained that reconsideration of factor six may produce a different conclusion about ASPIRE’s commercial strength or weakness, and a revised strength finding could change the overall commercial impression of ASPIRE as compared to Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks. Where an underpinning factual finding that informs the marks’ commercial impression is subject to a legal error, the first-factor legal conclusion cannot stand and must be redone consistent with the corrected factual record.

## Scope of reconsideration: compare ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK (word and design) in their entireties—appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—taking into account any revised strength findings

On remand the Board must take two steps in sequence and keep the analytical scope consistent. First, it should reexamine the sixth DuPont factor using a universe of third‑party marks tied to services that are similar to the parties’ services as the Board itself defined them for factor two. That means considering third‑party uses of ASPIRE and ASPIRE‑formative marks across banking and financing services (not only credit‑card programs) to determine whether the field is “crowded” and whether ASPIRE is entitled to a narrow or normal scope of protection. The Board should make detailed factual findings about the number, nature, and relevance of those third‑party uses (including registrations and evidence of marketplace use), and how closely those third‑party services overlap the parties’ channels of trade and consumer classes.

Second, armed with any revised findings about ASPIRE’s commercial strength, the Board must redo the factor‑one comparison “in their entireties.” That comparison requires attention to appearance (including any design/stylization in Apex’s ASPIRE BANK design mark), sound (how the marks are spoken or abbreviated), connotation (what consumers interpret the marks to mean, including the effect of the word “BANK”), and overall commercial impression. The Board must weigh whether the addition of “BANK” and any design elements meaningfully distinguish Apex’s marks from CC Serve’s ASPIRE in the mind of the relevant consumer class, and it must do so against the backdrop of the record about how crowded the field is. The court’s instruction is procedural and factual rather than predictive: the Board must make fresh, supported findings on both factor six and—because factor one depends on it—factor one.

## Outcome: affirmed‑in‑part (factor two), vacated‑in‑part (factors six and one), remanded for further proceedings; costs to appellant

As to the overall disposition, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor: substantial evidence supported the Board’s conclusion that Apex’s banking-and-financing services and CC Serve’s credit‑card services are highly similar (indeed, partly legally identical in scope). But the court vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth factor because of the overly narrow universe of third‑party marks considered, and—given the interdependence between the sixth and first factors—vacated the Board’s first‑factor analysis as well. The case was remanded to the Board to reconsider the sixth and first DuPont factors in light of consistent scope and any new factual findings. The Federal Circuit awarded costs to appellant (Apex), reflecting the partial reversal.

On remand the Board must apply the correct scope for third‑party‑use evidence, make detailed factual findings under factor six, and then reassess the first‑factor comparison in light of those findings before reaching a final conclusion on likelihood of confusion.

## Key takeaways

This decision reinforces a few pragmatic lessons for practitioners and students learning to read Board opinions. First, DuPont factors are interrelated; factual findings under one factor—especially those that define the commercial backdrop for consumers—can and often should inform the analysis under other factors. A finding that goods or services are highly similar should ordinarily produce a consistently broad universe of comparable third‑party marks when the Board analyzes factor six.

Second, building a robust record on third‑party use matters. If a party seeks to show that a term like ASPIRE is weak, it should offer third‑party registrations and, importantly, evidence of third‑party marketplace use across all services that are meaningfully similar to the parties’ services (not only identical subclasses). Conversely, a registrant seeking to preserve strength should submit evidence of exclusive use, consumer recognition, advertising, or market share to rebut assertions of crowding.

Third, identification of services in the application and in Board proceedings drives the inquiry. The scope of the parties’ identifications determines (and should be consistently applied to) what counts as a “similar” mark and who the relevant consumers are. Parties should press the Board to adopt a coherent definition of the relevant public and goods/services at the earliest opportunity.

Finally, when comparing marks under factor one, the Board must compare the marks in their entireties—visual, aural, and conceptual—while taking any revised strength findings into account. Remands like this one are procedural: they require careful factfinding and explanations, not presumptions about whether the addition of a word like “BANK” or a design element will inevitably prevent confusion.
