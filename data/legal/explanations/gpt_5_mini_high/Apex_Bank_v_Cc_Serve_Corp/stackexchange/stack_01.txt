Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Did the Board have to use the same 'similarity of goods/services' scope for DuPont factor six as it used for factor two?
Question:
The Board found under factor two that Apex's 'banking and financing services' are highly similar to CC Serve's 'credit card services,' even 'legally identical, in part.' But for factor six, it limited the relevant public to 'consumers of credit card services' and treated Aspire uses for other banking/financing services as 'essentially irrelevant.' The Federal Circuit says that was legal error because factor six, like factor two, deals with 'similar' goods/services. Does 'similar' need to be defined consistently across the DuPont factors once the Board has made a finding under factor two, or can the Board legitimately narrow the scope for factor six? How should the Board decide the proper scope of 'similar' goods/services for the crowded-field analysis when it has already found a high degree of similarity under factor two?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Once the Board made a factual finding under DuPont factor two that the parties’ services were “highly similar” (even “legally identical, in part”), the Federal Circuit held the Board should have used that same scope when assessing DuPont factor six. In Apex Bank v. CC Serve the court reversed because the Board narrowed factor six to “credit card services” only and excluded other banking/finance uses even though it had already found those services highly similar under factor two. (Apex Bank v. CC Serve, No. 2023‑2143, slip op. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) (J.A. 27–28, 47).)

Why (doctrine and the court’s reasoning)
- DuPont factor two asks whether the goods/services are similar; the Board here found credit‑card services and “banking and financing services” to be highly similar and “legally identical, in part.” (J.A. 25–28, 33–35.)
- DuPont factor six asks about “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” The Federal Circuit reiterated that factor six does not require identical goods—only similar ones. (Op. at J.A. 46–47 (citing Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation).)
- Because factor six depends on what counts as “similar goods,” the court held that a Board finding under factor two fixes the relevant universe of “similar” goods for use in the other factors unless the Board gives a valid, evidence‑based reason to do otherwise. The court put it plainly: “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar . . . the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.” (J.A. 47–48.)

What the Board must do on remand (practical rule)
- Start from the Board’s factor‑two finding: identify precisely the services the Board found similar (here, the Board’s finding that “banking and financing services” overlap with “credit card services”). (J.A. 25–28, 33–35.)
- Use that same scope to collect and evaluate third‑party marks for factor six: include third‑party uses of ASPIRE (and Aspire‑formative marks) on services that fall within the factor‑two similarity finding (i.e., the broader banking/finance universe), not only those labeled “credit card services.” (The court vacated because the Board excluded banking/finance uses from factor six despite its factor‑two finding.) (J.A. 47–48.)
- Apply the crowding analysis to that fuller set of marks: count and assess the “number and nature” of similar marks and decide whether they show commercial weakness or a crowded field (the court contrasted the small set the Board considered with the “voluminous” examples in Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation). (J.A. 47 (discussing Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation).)
- Explain any departures. If the Board nonetheless wishes to narrow the universe for a particular factor, it must explain and support that departure with substantial evidence showing why the factor‑two finding should not control the scope for that factor (the opinion faulted the Board for imposing a more stringent standard without justification). (J.A. 47–48.)
- Reassess related factors (e.g., factor one). The court expressly vacated the Board’s factor‑one analysis because a broadened factor‑six finding could change the mark’s commercial strength and thus the overall commercial impression. (J.A. 48–49.)

Bottom line
Apex requires consistency: a Board finding that goods/services are highly similar under DuPont factor two should govern what counts as “similar” for factor six (and other factors that turn on similarity) unless the Board articulates and supports a reasoned, evidence‑based basis for a narrower universe. The Board’s narrowing to only credit‑card services was reversible error in this case; on remand it must consider third‑party uses across the full scope the Board already found similar and explain its analysis with substantial evidence. (See J.A. 27–28, 47–48.)
