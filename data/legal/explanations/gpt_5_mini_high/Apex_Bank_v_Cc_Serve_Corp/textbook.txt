Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Case Background and Procedural Path

## Parties and marks: CC Serve’s ASPIRE (Reg. No. 2126948; priority October 17, 1996) for credit card services; Apex Bank’s planned ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for banking and financing services
This dispute pits an earlier-registered mark, CC Serve’s ASPIRE, against Apex Bank’s proposed ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. CC Serve owns U.S. Registration No. 2,126,948 for the standard-character mark ASPIRE; the registration issued in 1998 and claims priority back to October 17, 1996. Apex Bank sought federal registration, on an intent-to-use basis, of ASPIRE BANK (both word and design forms) for “banking and financing services.” Because CC Serve’s registration pre-dates Apex’s applications, CC Serve is the senior registrant and the case turns on whether Apex’s proposed registrations would be likely to cause consumer confusion with that earlier ASPIRE registration.

## Nature of services: CC Serve partners with issuing banks to offer ASPIRE-branded credit cards and services; Apex is a Tennessee retail bank with no current credit card offerings, planning an internet bank under ASPIRE BANK
The parties operate in overlapping parts of the financial-services sector but with different business models. CC Serve provides credit-card-related services: it partners with banks, which issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards and accounts to consumers, and CC Serve (and its affiliates) services those accounts. Apex is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank with 18 branches that offers deposit accounts, mortgages and loans; at the time of the dispute Apex did not itself issue credit cards. Apex planned to create an internet bank using the ASPIRE BANK marks and applied to register those marks for “banking and financing services.” The factual overlap—credit-card services on one side and broader banking and financing services on the other—is the factual core for the Lanham Act Section 2(d) likelihood-of-confusion inquiry.

## USPTO prosecution: 2019 intent-to-use filings; CC Serve’s letter of protest; examining attorney approval; publication
Apex filed intent-to-use trademark applications for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks in August 2019. During prosecution, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest raising the asserted conflict with its ASPIRE registration, but the examining attorney nevertheless approved Apex’s applications for publication. The ASPIRE BANK marks published in the Official Gazette on December 17, 2019, which starts the period in which third parties may file an opposition. Following publication, CC Serve filed a formal opposition before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) in February 2020. It’s useful to note the procedural distinction: a letter of protest is an ex parte submission during examination that may be considered by the examiner, while an opposition is an inter partes TTAB proceeding after publication where a registrant can litigate issues such as priority and likelihood of confusion.

## TTAB proceeding: Section 2(d) opposition filed by CC Serve; Board sustains opposition for likelihood of confusion
CC Serve’s opposition invoked Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, which bars registration of a mark that is likely to cause confusion with a prior registrant’s mark. The TTAB applied the DuPont likelihood-of-confusion framework (a multi-factor test) and focused on those DuPont factors it deemed relevant and supported by the record. Two factual determinations the Board made are especially important to the path of the case: first, because the Trademark ID Manual no longer carried a discrete entry for “credit card services,” the Board defined that term broadly to include activities such as issuing credit cards to finance purchases, credit-card account management, processing of credit-card transactions and authorizations, credit-card monitoring and alerts, and counseling regarding credit-card debt. Second, the Board compared those services to Apex’s identified “banking and financing services,” and—relying on dictionary definitions and third-party registrations linking credit-card services with banking/finance—concluded that the parties’ services were highly similar and “legally identical, in part.” On that basis the Board found the second DuPont factor (similarity of the goods/services) weighed heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion.

On the sixth DuPont factor (the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods), the Board examined third‑party uses of “Aspire” marks but limited its inquiry to marks tied to credit-card services. It identified nine Aspire-formative third‑party marks in that category and concluded this level of third‑party use did not demonstrate the sort of widespread usage that would render CC Serve’s mark weak or entitled to a narrow scope of protection. The Board also found that the marks themselves (the first DuPont factor) favored CC Serve. In light of the overall DuPont analysis, the TTAB sustained CC Serve’s opposition and refused registration of Apex’s ASPIRE BANK applications.

## Appeal: Apex appeals to the Federal Circuit; jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B); issues raised on appeal (errors in DuPont factors 2, 6, and 1)
Apex appealed the TTAB decision to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (docket No. 2023-2143). The Federal Circuit has jurisdiction over TTAB appeals under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B). On appeal the court reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and tests factual findings for substantial evidence. Apex argued the Board erred in its likelihood-of-confusion analysis—challenging the Board’s treatment of the second DuPont factor (similarity of services), the sixth factor (third‑party use and strength), and the first factor (similarity of the marks and commercial impression).

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor, concluding that substantial evidence supported the Board’s determination that the parties’ services are highly similar (and partially legally identical). But the court found legal error in the Board’s treatment of the sixth factor: once the Board had found a high degree of similarity between banking and credit-card services, it was inconsistent to narrow the universe of third‑party Aspire uses to only those tied to “credit card services.” Because the sixth factor should consider similar (not identical) goods and services, the Board must reassess third‑party evidence using the same scope it used in its second-factor finding. Because the sixth-factor analysis can bear on the perceived commercial strength of the senior mark and thus affect the first-factor commercial-impression analysis, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s sixth- and first-factor findings and remanded for reconsideration consistent with the court’s opinion. The final disposition was therefore affirmed‑in‑part (DuPont factor 2), vacated‑in‑part (DuPont factors 6 and 1), and remanded; the panel awarded costs to Apex (the appellant).

# Governing Law and Standards

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: refusal of registration where concurrent use is likely to cause confusion
Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act bars registration when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” That statutory rule is the starting point for the TTAB’s opposition decision and for this appeal. In practice, the Board asks whether the applicant’s proposed mark, used on the services it identifies in its application, is likely to be confused with a prior user’s mark in light of the marketplace. In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., CC Serve relied on its earlier ASPIRE registration to oppose Apex’s ASPIRE BANK filings; the Board refused registration under Section 2(d) after finding a likelihood of confusion. The court’s review therefore centers on whether the Board applied the correct legal framework and whether its factual findings supporting that likelihood-of-confusion determination are supported by substantial evidence.

## DuPont framework: thirteen non‑exhaustive factors; Board need consider only those relevant and of record
The Board measures likelihood of confusion under the multi‑factor DuPont framework, which enumerates thirteen non‑exhaustive factors bearing on whether confusion is likely. Those factors include, among others, similarity of the marks, similarity or relatedness of the goods/services, the strength of the prior mark (including third‑party use), channels of trade, conditions of purchase, evidence of actual confusion, and the defendant’s intent. The DuPont factors are flexible: the Board need not address every factor, only those that are relevant and supported by the record. Importantly, likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion informed by findings under the DuPont factors; the factors supply the factual predicate for the legal determination. In Apex Bank, the parties focused on three DuPont factors that the Board had relied on—first (similarity of the marks), second (relatedness of services), and sixth (number and nature of similar marks)—so the Federal Circuit’s review concentrates on whether the Board correctly applied the DuPont framework to those factors.

## Standard of review: legal conclusions de novo; each DuPont factor is a factual finding reviewed for substantial evidence
Procedurally, the Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and its findings of fact for substantial evidence. That means the court will independently assess whether the Board applied the correct legal standards (for example, whether it used the right scope of goods or the right legal test for strength), but it will defer to the Board’s factual determinations so long as there is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Each DuPont factor typically presents a question of fact; those findings are tested on appeal under the substantial‑evidence standard. Because likelihood of confusion is a question of law grounded in those factual findings, an appellate court will affirm when the Board’s legal analysis is correct and its factual findings are supported by substantial evidence, and will vacate or remand when the Board’s legal framing or scope of inquiry was erroneous.

## Second DuPont factor (relatedness of goods/services): products/services need not be identical; relatedness and marketing circumstances sufficient to create source confusion
The second DuPont factor asks how similar or related the parties’ goods or services are. The governing principle is that the goods/services need not be identical; it is enough that they are related in a way that consumers could reasonably conclude they come from the same source or that the circumstances of marketing make confusion likely. Courts and the Board rely on a range of evidence to decide relatedness: the identifications in the application/registration, dictionary definitions, trade publications, third‑party registrations showing combined offerings, and evidence of how the parties actually interact in the marketplace (for example, partnerships or common channels of trade). In Apex Bank the Board examined the parties’ identifications, dictionary definitions of “banking” and “finance,” and third‑party registrations and concluded that credit‑card services and banking/financing services significantly overlap—even partly legally identical—because issuing or extending credit (including credit cards) falls within banking/finance. Because substantial evidence supported that factual finding, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar.

## Sixth DuPont factor (number and nature of similar marks): third‑party use and registrations on similar goods/services can show commercial weakness and a crowded field
The sixth DuPont factor evaluates the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” Evidence of third‑party registrations or marketplace use of similar marks is relevant to show whether the prior mark is relatively weak and therefore entitled to a narrower scope of protection. The policy behind the factor is that in a crowded field of similar marks, consumers become practiced at distinguishing marks and are less likely to be confused; voluminous third‑party use can therefore undercut a claim of likelihood of confusion. The inquiry, however, must be tied to the same conception of similarity used elsewhere in the DuPont analysis: the Board should consider third‑party uses for goods/services that are similar to those at issue, and similarity for this purpose does not require identity. In Apex Bank the Board limited its sixth‑factor inquiry to marks used only for credit‑card services, excluding “Aspire” uses in broader banking or financing services. The Federal Circuit found that restriction inconsistent with the Board’s own earlier finding that the parties’ services were highly similar across banking/finance, and therefore vacated the sixth‑factor finding and remanded. The lesson is that the Board must use a consistent scope of goods/services when deciding what third‑party uses are comparable.

## First DuPont factor (similarity of the marks): comparison in their entireties for appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression; commercial impression can be influenced by marketplace strength/weakness
The first DuPont factor requires comparison of the marks in their entirety with attention to appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. This is not a purely formal exercise: the commercial impression that consumers take away from a mark may be affected by the mark’s marketplace strength or weakness. A relatively weak or descriptive mark is entitled to narrower protection, and that context can alter how similar two marks must be before confusion is likely. In Apex Bank the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s first‑factor analysis for the simple reason that a corrected sixth‑factor assessment (about third‑party use and strength) could change the commercial impression of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark and therefore change the outcome of the first‑factor comparison. Put differently, because the first factor’s result depends in part on the mark’s strength as measured by the sixth factor, the court required the Board to reassess the first factor after it reconsiders the sixth.

Concluding point: these standards show how interconnected the DuPont factors are in practice and how appellate review operates. The Board must identify and apply the correct legal scope for the goods/services at issue, rely on adequate evidentiary support for its factual findings under each relevant DuPont factor, and weigh those factors coherently. On appeal the Federal Circuit will uphold factual findings that meet the substantial‑evidence standard but will vacate and remand when the Board's legal framing of the inquiry—such as an inconsistent or unduly narrow definition of the comparable goods/services or marks—undermines the reliability of its DuPont analysis.

# Factor Two: Similarity of Services and the Board’s Affirmed Determination

## Defining CC Serve’s identified services
The Board began its analysis by fixing what CC Serve’s registration actually covered. Because the Trademark ID Manual entry for the phrase “credit card services” had been deleted after CC Serve’s 1998 registration, the Board construed the term itself. It found that “credit card services” includes issuing credit cards to finance purchases, counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit-card authorization, credit-card monitoring and alerts, managing credit-card accounts, and providing access to credit scores (J.A. 25). That factual construction of the identified services is the baseline for comparing the parties’ services under DuPont factor two.

## Dictionary definitions: “bank,” “banking,” and “finance” include extending credit and issuing credit cards
To determine whether Apex’s identified services overlapped with CC Serve’s, the Board consulted dictionary definitions for “bank,” “banking,” and “finance.” The Board concluded those definitions encompass extending credit and providing funds by issuing credit instruments—so that “banking and financing services,” as identified in Apex’s intent-to-use applications, can include issuance of credit cards (J.A. 27–28). By using ordinary-language definitions the Board translated Apex’s broad identifications into the same functional activities listed under CC Serve’s identified services.

## Overlap finding: partial legal identity between the parties’ services
Putting the two constructions together, the Board concluded that Apex’s “banking and financing services” are, at least in part, legally identical to CC Serve’s “credit card services.” In trademark practice “legal identity” refers to identity as set out in the registration/application identifications: two services are legally identical if the identifications overlap or if one identification encompasses the other. The Board’s factual finding here was that some of Apex’s identified services (as defined by the dictionaries and the application language) cover the same activities listed under CC Serve’s credit-card-identification—so the services are “highly similar, in part legally identical” (J.A. 27–28).

## Third‑party registrations showing common offering under a single mark
The Board did not rely only on dictionary meanings. It also considered third‑party registrations listing both credit-card services and broader banking/finance services under the same marks. The Board found those registrations support the commonsense inference that entities frequently offer both types of services under a single mark and that consumers could reasonably view them as related or emanating from a single source (J.A. 33–34). That third‑party evidence bolstered the conclusion that the services are related in the marketplace even where the parties’ current business models differ.

## Board’s conclusion: factor two weighs heavily for CC Serve
Weighing its constructions and the third‑party registrations, the Board concluded there was a high degree of similarity between the parties’ services and that DuPont factor two therefore weighed heavily in favor of CC Serve (J.A. 35). The Board treated the partial legal identity and the demonstrated market overlap as strong evidence that the services are sufficiently related to support a likelihood‑of‑confusion finding.

## Apex’s counterarguments and the Board’s response
Apex urged that the services are not the same and are not directly competitive: CC Serve partners with banks to run card programs rather than offering banking services itself, and Apex did not (and did not plan to) issue credit cards. The Board, and the Federal Circuit on review, rejected those arguments because the dispute turns on the parties’ identifications and on how those identifications map to real‑world activities. The Board carefully evaluated the descriptions and supporting evidence (dictionaries and third‑party registrations) and concluded the identifications overlap. The court explained that this factual determination—whether the identified services are similar and partially legally identical—is reviewed for substantial evidence and was adequately supported here (J.A. 27–28; App. Br. rejection at J.A. 35).

## Federal Circuit holding: substantial‑evidence affirmation of factor two
On appeal the Federal Circuit applied the familiar standard: legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, while the Board’s factual findings are tested for substantial evidence. The court held that the Board committed no legal error in its approach to factor two and that substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the services are highly similar and partially legally identical. Accordingly, the court affirmed the Board’s conclusion that DuPont factor two weighs heavily in favor of CC Serve.

Concluding note for readers: when reading likelihood‑of‑confusion opinions, pay close attention to how the Board construes identifications (especially when ID Manual entries change), how ordinary‑language definitions and third‑party registrations are used to show marketplace relatedness, and that those factual constructions are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard.

# Factor Six: Third-Party Use, the Board’s Legal Error, and Remand Instructions

## Board’s approach: limiting the relevant public to credit card services
In its analysis of DuPont factor six, the Board confined its inquiry to third‑party marks that used “Aspire” in connection specifically with credit card services. The Board treated Aspire‑formative uses tied to other banking or financing services as “essentially irrelevant” because, in the Board’s view, the pertinent consumer class was purchasers of credit card services. That narrowing followed the Board’s earlier definitional work: with the Trademark ID Manual no longer listing “credit card services,” the Board defined that phrase broadly to include issuance, authorization, processing, account management, counseling, and related functions. Having defined “credit card services” in that way, the Board then limited factor six evidence to third‑party marks that were identified for that narrow category.

## Board’s evidentiary finding: nine Aspire‑formative marks and insufficiency
Applying the credit‑card‑only lens, the Board counted nine third‑party Aspire‑formative marks that it considered relevant to factor six. The Board concluded nine examples did not amount to a “considerable” or “ubiquitous” body of use that would demonstrate commercial weakness in CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark (and therefore justify narrowing its scope of protection). On that basis the Board declined to find the mark weak or entitled to reduced protection under factor six.

## Legal error identified: factor six does not require identical goods or services
The Federal Circuit reversed that legal posture. The court explained that factor six asks about “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods,” not identical goods, and it cited precedent holding that similarity, not identity, is the operative requirement. Because the Board had already found under DuPont factor two that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services are highly similar (partially legally identical), the Board’s decision to treat only credit‑card‑specific third‑party uses as relevant imposed a stricter standard for factor six than it had applied under factor two. That mismatch was a legal error: once the Board has found the parties’ services to be highly similar, it must consider third‑party marks used on services that are similar in scope to those services when weighing factor six.

## Consistency principle: carry the scope of similarity from factor two into factor six
The court’s ruling rests on a consistency principle that students should note: DuPont factors draw from the same underlying evidence about goods, services, channels of trade, and consumers. When the Board adopts a particular factual conclusion about similarity in analyzing factor two, it must retain that same factual scope when evaluating other DuPont factors that depend on similarity (including factor six). Applying different and more stringent similarity standards to different factors risks undercounting relevant third‑party uses and skewing the assessment of mark strength. The Federal Circuit therefore directed that the scope of “similar goods/services” used under factor two must govern which third‑party marks are eligible for consideration under factor six.

## Remand directive: what the Board must do on remand
On remand the Board must reopen its factor six inquiry in light of its factor two finding that the parties’ services are highly similar and partially legally identical. Concretely, that means the Board must consider third‑party Aspire‑formative marks used on banking and financing services in addition to those tied expressly to credit card services (Apex argued there were many additional Aspire uses—beyond the nine the Board counted). The Board should assess both the number and the nature (quality and actual market use) of those marks, not merely rely on a narrow registration category. In doing so the Board should:

- Treat the relevant zone of similarity consistently with its factor two findings, so third‑party marks across similar banking/finance services are included;
- Evaluate quantitative evidence (how many independent third‑party uses/registrations) and qualitative evidence (extent of marketplace use, consumer exposure, prominence), distinguishing registrations from demonstrated market use when appropriate;
- Explain whether the enlarged body of third‑party evidence shows that consumers are accustomed to multiple ASPIR E‑formative marks in the financial services field and therefore are likely able to distinguish among them—i.e., whether ASPIRE is commercially weak and thus entitled only to a narrower scope of protection.

Because the Board’s factor six reassessment can alter the commercial strength finding for ASPIRE, the court also instructed the Board to revisit factor one (the marks’ overall commercial impression) after completing factor six.

## Doctrinal significance: crowded‑field evidence across similar services can narrow protection
The Federal Circuit’s decision underscores a core doctrinal point about factor six: evidence that a term or formative is widely used across a relevant field reduces the senior mark’s commercial strength and narrows its protective scope. That mitigating effect does not require identical goods; it suffices that the third‑party uses appear on goods or services similar to those of the parties. Practically, when a trademark dispute involves related services (here, credit cards vs. broader banking/finance), a proper factor‑six inquiry must look beyond hyper‑technical category labels and ask whether third‑party uses in the same commercial field would educate consumers to distinguish similar marks. On remand the Board must apply that approach, explain its reasoning with reference to both the number and nature of third‑party uses it finds probative, and then reassess the first DuPont factor and the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion in light of any revised finding about ASPIRE’s commercial strength.

# Factor One, Interdependence of Factors, and Disposition

## Interdependence: commercial impression under factor one is informed by marketplace strength/weakness developed under factor six

The court’s disposition emphasizes a basic, practical point about the DuPont inquiry: the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks “in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression”) cannot be evaluated in a vacuum. The Board’s assessment of the marks’ “commercial impression” is necessarily informed by its factual findings about the mark’s marketplace strength or weakness under the sixth DuPont factor (the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”). If a term is shown to be widely used in a particular field—through third‑party registrations and marketplace use—its commercial strength is reduced and the scope of protection narrows; that factual backdrop changes how the first-factor comparison will read to a typical consumer. Conversely, a finding that a mark is commercially strong widens the scope of protection and makes the first-factor comparison more likely to favor confusion. The Federal Circuit therefore treats the sixth-factor findings as context for the commercial‑impression analysis under factor one.

In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the Board found the services offered under the competing marks to be highly similar (the second DuPont factor), but then constrained its sixth-factor review to third‑party marks tied only to “credit card services.” The court held that narrowing the field for factor six while embracing a broad similarity of services for factor two was legally inconsistent: where services are found to overlap substantially, the relevant universe of third‑party marks for the sixth factor must reflect that overlap. Because the commercial‑impression inquiry under factor one depends on whether ASPIRE is a crowded (weak) or exclusive (strong) term in the relevant marketplace, any error in the sixth‑factor assessment undermines the Board’s factor‑one analysis.

## Vacatur of factor one: because re‑evaluating factor six may alter the perceived strength of ASPIRE, the Board’s factor‑one analysis must be reconsidered

The court vacated the Board’s sixth-factor finding because the Board improperly limited the universe of third‑party marks to credit‑card‑only uses even though it had earlier determined that banking and credit‑card services are highly similar (and partly legally identical). That legal error required remand on factor six so the Board could consider third‑party uses across the broader set of banking and financing services the parties identified and the Board itself found to overlap.

Because the Board’s first-factor analysis relied upon the prior (flawed) sixth‑factor conclusion about ASPIRE’s marketplace strength, the court necessarily vacated the Board’s factor‑one findings as well. The court explained that reconsideration of factor six may produce a different conclusion about ASPIRE’s commercial strength or weakness, and a revised strength finding could change the overall commercial impression of ASPIRE as compared to Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks. Where an underpinning factual finding that informs the marks’ commercial impression is subject to a legal error, the first-factor legal conclusion cannot stand and must be redone consistent with the corrected factual record.

## Scope of reconsideration: compare ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK (word and design) in their entireties—appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—taking into account any revised strength findings

On remand the Board must take two steps in sequence and keep the analytical scope consistent. First, it should reexamine the sixth DuPont factor using a universe of third‑party marks tied to services that are similar to the parties’ services as the Board itself defined them for factor two. That means considering third‑party uses of ASPIRE and ASPIRE‑formative marks across banking and financing services (not only credit‑card programs) to determine whether the field is “crowded” and whether ASPIRE is entitled to a narrow or normal scope of protection. The Board should make detailed factual findings about the number, nature, and relevance of those third‑party uses (including registrations and evidence of marketplace use), and how closely those third‑party services overlap the parties’ channels of trade and consumer classes.

Second, armed with any revised findings about ASPIRE’s commercial strength, the Board must redo the factor‑one comparison “in their entireties.” That comparison requires attention to appearance (including any design/stylization in Apex’s ASPIRE BANK design mark), sound (how the marks are spoken or abbreviated), connotation (what consumers interpret the marks to mean, including the effect of the word “BANK”), and overall commercial impression. The Board must weigh whether the addition of “BANK” and any design elements meaningfully distinguish Apex’s marks from CC Serve’s ASPIRE in the mind of the relevant consumer class, and it must do so against the backdrop of the record about how crowded the field is. The court’s instruction is procedural and factual rather than predictive: the Board must make fresh, supported findings on both factor six and—because factor one depends on it—factor one.

## Outcome: affirmed‑in‑part (factor two), vacated‑in‑part (factors six and one), remanded for further proceedings; costs to appellant

As to the overall disposition, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor: substantial evidence supported the Board’s conclusion that Apex’s banking-and-financing services and CC Serve’s credit‑card services are highly similar (indeed, partly legally identical in scope). But the court vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth factor because of the overly narrow universe of third‑party marks considered, and—given the interdependence between the sixth and first factors—vacated the Board’s first‑factor analysis as well. The case was remanded to the Board to reconsider the sixth and first DuPont factors in light of consistent scope and any new factual findings. The Federal Circuit awarded costs to appellant (Apex), reflecting the partial reversal.

On remand the Board must apply the correct scope for third‑party‑use evidence, make detailed factual findings under factor six, and then reassess the first‑factor comparison in light of those findings before reaching a final conclusion on likelihood of confusion.

## Key takeaways

This decision reinforces a few pragmatic lessons for practitioners and students learning to read Board opinions. First, DuPont factors are interrelated; factual findings under one factor—especially those that define the commercial backdrop for consumers—can and often should inform the analysis under other factors. A finding that goods or services are highly similar should ordinarily produce a consistently broad universe of comparable third‑party marks when the Board analyzes factor six.

Second, building a robust record on third‑party use matters. If a party seeks to show that a term like ASPIRE is weak, it should offer third‑party registrations and, importantly, evidence of third‑party marketplace use across all services that are meaningfully similar to the parties’ services (not only identical subclasses). Conversely, a registrant seeking to preserve strength should submit evidence of exclusive use, consumer recognition, advertising, or market share to rebut assertions of crowding.

Third, identification of services in the application and in Board proceedings drives the inquiry. The scope of the parties’ identifications determines (and should be consistently applied to) what counts as a “similar” mark and who the relevant consumers are. Parties should press the Board to adopt a coherent definition of the relevant public and goods/services at the earliest opportunity.

Finally, when comparing marks under factor one, the Board must compare the marks in their entireties—visual, aural, and conceptual—while taking any revised strength findings into account. Remands like this one are procedural: they require careful factfinding and explanations, not presumptions about whether the addition of a word like “BANK” or a design element will inevitably prevent confusion.
