Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# The Same‑Scope Principle: Federal Circuit Faults TTAB for Narrowing DuPont Factor Six After Broad Factor Two

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a short but important decision about internal consistency in the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry. The Federal Circuit rejected a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) maneuver that is not uncommon in practice: after finding a broad overlap between parties’ services under DuPont factor two, the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it would consider under DuPont factor six. The court held that was legal error. The ruling reinforces a simple practical rule for TTAB practice and litigation strategy: once the Board defines the overlap between goods or services, it should carry that scope forward when evaluating other factors that depend on the same notion of “similarity,” or risk a remand.

## The court’s reasoning in the Apex/ASPIRE fight

The dispute was straightforward. CC Serve owns a 1996 registration for the word mark ASPIRE in connection with “credit card services.” Apex Bank seeks registration of ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” The TTAB concluded the services were highly similar—indeed “legally identical, in part”—and sustained CC Serve’s opposition under Section 2(d). But when the Board analyzed DuPont factor six (the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”), it limited its crowding inquiry to third‑party uses of ASPIRE on credit‑card services only, excluding third‑party Aspire marks used for other banking and financing services. The Board found only nine such credit card entries and concluded that showed no pervasive crowding; it therefore treated CC Serve’s mark as entitled to a normal scope of protection.

On appeal the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s conclusion on factor two—that Apex’s banking/financing services and CC Serve’s credit‑card services are highly similar—but it vacated the Board’s factor six decision. The court explained that factor six looks to similar, not identical, goods and therefore the Board’s narrowing was inconsistent with its own prior finding of broad similarity under factor two. “We find the Board’s definition of similarity to be too narrow,” the court said, citing Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation, and other precedent that asked only for similarity (not perfect identity) when counting third‑party marks. Because factor one (similarity of the marks) turns in part on the commercial strength of the prior mark—which in turn can be shown or rebutted by factor six evidence—the court also vacated the Board’s factor‑one analysis and remanded.

## Why the court’s approach matters (and its limits)

The Federal Circuit’s correction is doctrinally tidy and practically useful. The DuPont inquiry is a multi‑factor analysis, but it cannot tolerate ad hoc, inconsistent redefinitions of the relevant universe from factor to factor. If the Board concludes the services are broad enough to be “highly similar” or “legally identical in part,” it undermines the coherence of the overall analysis to permit a narrower definition when deciding whether the mark is weakened by a crowded field.

That said, the opinion is not a license to demand identical scope in every possible DuPont permutation. The court’s reasoning is fact‑driven: because the Board itself had already found high similarity under factor two, it was improper to selectively constrict factor six. In cases where the Board has not made such a finding, or where different factors legitimately ask different questions about channels, purchasers, or uses, a narrower focus may sometimes be appropriate. The key takeaway is consistency tied to the Board’s own factual findings, not a categorical rule that every factor must use the exact same labels.

## Practice takeaways for litigators and the TTAB

For applicants and registrants, Apex instructs two clear tactical moves. If you want to limit an opponent’s ability to introduce broad third‑party evidence, fight hard at the outset to prevent a finding of broad goods‑or‑services similarity under factor two. Conversely, if you want to show the prior mark is weak, build and push a comprehensive record of third‑party uses across the full universe that will be implicated by any factor‑two finding; make the Board adopt a clear definition of the relevant public and keep pushing for that same definition in later factors.

For the TTAB and examiners, the decision counsels more disciplined record statements: when the Board defines the scope of overlap it should explain whether and why that scope will (or will not) inform other DuPont factors. That explanation reduces the risk of remand and improves predictability.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Apex is a modest but important policing of internal consistency in the DuPont framework. Expect litigators to cite it when the Board appears to be “moving the goalposts” from factor to factor, and expect the TTAB to be more explicit in tying its factor‑two findings to later inquiries about crowding and commercial strength. That, in turn, will increase the tactical value of robust third‑party evidence and careful record building on channels of trade and the identity of the relevant consumer. Over time, Apex may make it harder for boards to treat word elements that appear across the financial services spectrum as insulated from each other simply by recasting the identified services in narrow terms at the factor‑six stage.

Bottom line: the Federal Circuit’s message is simple and sensible—define the overlap once, and carry that scope forward where the law calls for the same notion of “similarity.” Failing to do so invites correction on appeal.
