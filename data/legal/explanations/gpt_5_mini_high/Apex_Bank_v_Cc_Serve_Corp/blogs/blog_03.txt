Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Broad IDs, Broad Problems: Why “Banking and Financing Services” Sank Apex on DuPont Factor Two

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a straightforward—and sobering—reminder for trademark drafters: what you write in the identification of services can determine whether your mark ever clears opposition. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s finding under DuPont factor two that Apex’s broadly worded “banking and financing services” are highly similar (indeed “legally identical, in part”) to CC Serve’s registered “credit card services,” even though Apex does not currently issue credit cards. The decision shows how dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations can be used to construe broad identifications to sweep in overlapping services, and it highlights drafting pitfalls that routinely trip up intent‑to‑use applicants—especially in the financial‑services and fintech space.

## How the Board and court reached the conclusion

The facts are familiar. CC Serve owns a 1998 registration for the standard‑character mark ASPIRE for “credit card services.” Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications for ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services,” though Apex currently operates a retail bank with no credit card products and only plans an internet bank under the new brand. CC Serve opposed registration under Section 2(d).

In addressing DuPont factor two—the relatedness of the parties’ services—the Board reconstructed the meaning of “credit card services” (the Trademark ID Manual entry for that phrase had been deleted) using dictionary definitions and found the term to encompass a suite of credit‑card activities: issuing cards, processing payments and authorizations, account management, monitoring/alerts, counseling, and similar functions. The Board further relied on third‑party registrations that covered both credit‑card and banking/finance services; those registrations supported the inference that the two sets of services may emanate from a single source. On that record the Board concluded the services were highly similar and partly legally identical, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that conclusion as supported by substantial evidence.

The appellate court did not bless every part of the Board’s opinion. It vacated and remanded the Board’s analysis of DuPont factor six (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods) because the Board had limited its consideration to third‑party marks used only for credit‑card services, even though it had already concluded the services were highly similar. The court also vacated the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, and commercial impression) for reconsideration in light of any revised commercial‑strength analysis under factor six. But on the core point that a broadly worded “banking and financing” identification may encompass credit‑card activities, the Federal Circuit agreed with the Board.

## An analytical perspective

Two themes explain why the court’s outcome should be unsurprising to trademark practitioners, even if it’s unwelcome to applicants who believe their current business model is distinct from an incumbent’s.

First, TTAB and appellate practice long treat the identification in the application as the controlling baseline for likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis. The court’s affirmation reflects that canonical principle: the inquiry asks whether the applicant’s identified services, as written, overlap with the registrant’s goods or services. That inquiry does not turn on whether the applicant presently offers a particular function if the ID itself covers it. Apex’s lack of credit‑card products did not immunize its ASPIRE BANK ITU application because the ID swept broadly.

Second, the decision underscores the practical power of dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations. Where a TMID entry has been deleted or is otherwise unhelpful, the Board will reconstruct meanings and look to marketplace practice. Third‑party registrations that bridge the categories—e.g., covers for both “credit card services” and broader “banking and financing” services—are persuasive evidence that consumers could reasonably think the services come from the same source under one mark.

That said, practitioners should note the appellate court’s willingness to police internal inconsistency. Once the Board treated the services as highly similar, it could not then constrict the universe of third‑party marks when assessing the mark’s commercial strength. Consistency across DuPont factors matters; narrowing the scope for one factor after adopting a broad scope for another invites remand.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Apex cements a practical rule rather than a new doctrinal twist: broad, catch‑all identifications can and will be read expansively. For banks, fintechs, and program managers—entities that often operate through partnerships or white‑label arrangements—the decision is a caution: services provided as part of an ecosystem (card issuance, processing, account servicing) may be read as encompassed by broader banking or financing IDs even when the label “credit card services” is absent.

The remand on factor six is also important for litigators. If the Board finds a high degree of relatedness, parties should contest or develop the third‑party‑use record broadly across the entire ecosystem—not selectively. Parties trying to show weakness (or strength) of a mark must present third‑party evidence aligned with the scope the Board has adopted for the services, because commercial strength is a function of the relevant marketplace.

Finally, the case reminds us that TMID deletions are not a safe harbor. The absence of a TMID entry will not prevent the Board from construing an identification from first principles, using dictionaries, industry materials, and registrations.

## Practical drafting takeaways

Avoid laundry‑list identifications. “Banking and financing services” is a classic example of a catch‑all that invites overlap with existing registrations. If you mean to limit your claim, say so expressly in the ID: specify particular services (e.g., “retail deposit account services; consumer and business loans”) and exclude categories you do not intend to cover.

If you do not plan to issue or service credit cards, carve it out. An express exclusion—“not including credit card issuance, processing, account‑servicing or related credit‑card customer services”—can materially change the analysis because the Board evaluates the services as written.

Think about channels of trade and users when drafting. Limiting the identification to particular channels (e.g., “branch banking services” or “online retail banking services to individual consumers, not including business payment programs”) or to particular customer classes can narrow potential overlap.

Before filing an ITU, run clearances with an eye to how broadly your ID could be read and be prepared to narrow the ID during prosecution. If an opposition is filed, present evidence on how the services actually operate in the marketplace and on the absence of overlap—but do so framed against the identification as written.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank is a reminder that in trademark prosecutions, words matter. A seemingly generic umbrella like “banking and financing services” can reach into another party’s long‑standing credit‑card niche and doom registration, even where the applicant does not currently perform those functions. Careful, specific drafting—and consistent evidentiary development across DuPont factors—remains the best prophylactic against unpleasant surprises at the TTAB and on appeal.
