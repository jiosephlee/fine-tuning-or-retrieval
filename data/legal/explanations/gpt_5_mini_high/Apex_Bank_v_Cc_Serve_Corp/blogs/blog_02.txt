Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Counting Crowded Fields in Finance: From Nine “Aspire” Cards to Dozens of Financial “Aspire” Marks

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a reminder that DuPont’s sixth factor—the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods—turns as much on the scope of what the Board considers as on raw counting. For brand owners and challengers in financial services and fintech, the Federal Circuit’s decision makes clear that the Board cannot cabin its inquiry about third‑party marks to a narrower universe than the one it has already found relevant under DuPont factor two. That consistency requirement can materially change whether a plaintiff’s mark is treated as commercially strong (broad protection) or relatively weak (narrow protection), and thus whether consumer confusion is likely.

## What the Federal Circuit did

The underlying dispute is straightforward: CC Serve owns a long‑standing registration for ASPIRE in connection with credit‑card services; Apex Bank applied to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” The TTAB concluded the services were highly similar and sustained the opposition, but in assessing DuPont factor six it limited the relevant third‑party evidence to marks used on credit‑card services only. The Board found nine third‑party “Aspire” marks for credit cards and concluded that was not a “considerable” number suggesting weakness, treating roughly 42 other “Aspire” uses across the financial services spectrum as “essentially irrelevant.”

On appeal the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding under DuPont factor two (the services are highly similar, partly legally identical), but it vacated the Board’s DuPont factor six analysis—and, for linked reasons, its DuPont factor one analysis as to mark similarity—because the Board had improperly narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it considered. The court did not invent a numerical bright line: rather, it instructed the Board to reconsider the sixth factor using a scope of third‑party marks consistent with its prior finding that the parties’ services overlap. The first factor must be revisited on remand because the commercial strength of the ASPIRE mark (which DuPont six informs) affects the overall commercial impression comparison.

## The doctrinal core: why scope matters more than a magic number

DuPont factor six is about commercial weakness: if the field is crowded with similar marks used on similar goods or services, consumers are accustomed to distinguishing among similar marks and the putative senior mark is entitled to a narrower range of exclusive protection. The Federal Circuit’s opinion anchors on a simple doctrinal point: once the Board has defined the services as highly similar (and recognized partial legal identity), it cannot then evaluate third‑party use under a tighter definition of similarity. Third‑party marks need not be identical to be relevant; they need only be similar in kind. That principle tracks prior precedent the Board and courts have relied on—see Juice Generation (treating some 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number”) and Jack Wolfskin (treating a voluminous pattern of use as probative)—but the court here refused to convert those cases into a numeric test.

So the absence of a “magic number” is deliberate: the question is contextual. How many third‑party uses exist, and what is their nature—do they actually place the word or concept before the same class of consumers, through similar channels of trade and at similar prominence? Are those marks registered only or shown to be used meaningfully in commerce? Those qualitative features, not a single cut‑off, determine whether a mark’s commercial strength should be downgraded.

## How a broader “Aspire” universe could change the calculus on remand

If the Board, on remand, treats the 42 additional “Aspire” uses in banking/finance as relevant, the practical effect could be significant. A larger, cross‑service dataset could show that “Aspire” is widely used throughout the financial services field, which tends to support a finding of commercial weakness. That, in turn, would narrow the scope of protection accorded CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark and make it harder for CC Serve to prove that Apex’s ASPIRE BANK will cause confusion.

Two caveats, however. First, commercial weakness is never dispositive on its own; DuPont requires a multi‑factor balancing. The Federal Circuit already affirmed that the services are highly similar—a strong point for CC Serve. Second, the probative value of the additional marks will depend on their nature: are they national brands with consumer exposure, or minor local uses and registrations? Do they appear on the same consumer touchpoints (banking websites, card packaging, co‑branded card offerings)? The Board must evaluate the “number and nature” of those third‑party marks, not just their count. It is conceivable that even with dozens of “Aspire” uses, a Board could still conclude the senior mark retains sufficient commercial strength to warrant refusal; but it cannot dismiss the broader evidence out of hand.

## Practical implications for trademark practice in finance and fintech

This decision signals three concrete lessons for practitioners:

First, collect and present third‑party evidence across the whole field the Board has already found relevant. If your client litigates services that overlap with a broader class (e.g., banking, card issuance, payment processing), limit your opponent at your peril—courts will require consistency.

Second, quality beats a raw headcount. Registrations without evidence of marketplace use are weaker; screenshots, promotional materials, market‑share data, and evidence of consumer exposure will be far more persuasive than a list of filings. Demonstrate where the marks appear and to whom.

Third, plan for the interplay among DuPont factors. A showing of commercial weakness under factor six will meaningfully reshape factor one’s “commercial impression” analysis. Conversely, if an opposer cannot defend the distinctiveness or strength of a mark, it should shore up other DuPont levers—channels of trade and customer sophistication, for example—to preserve its case.

## Bottom line

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is not a decision that hands victory to applicants whenever a plaintiff’s mark appears in multiple registrations. It is, instead, a doctrinal correction: the Board must analyze third‑party use under DuPont six on at least the same footprint it uses under DuPont two. For financial services marks—where banks, processors, and card issuers increasingly blur the lines between products and channels—that consistency can be decisive. On remand the fate of ASPIRE will turn on whether the broader “Aspire” landscape in banking and finance shows a crowded field that meaningfully erodes the senior registrant’s commercial strength.
