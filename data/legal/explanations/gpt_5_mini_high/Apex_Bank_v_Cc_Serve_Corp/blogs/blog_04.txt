Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# What Does “BANK” Buy You? Reassessing the First DuPont Factor for ASPIRE BANK After Remand

## Why this Federal Circuit decision matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is an important reminder that DuPont factors cannot be parsed in isolation. By affirming the Board’s finding that Apex and CC Serve provide highly similar financial services but vacating the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor—and, because of that, its first-factor analysis—the court signaled that a corrected view of the marketplace can change how the marks themselves are judged. For litigants in crowded industries (finance being a prime example), the practical question is simple: does adding a descriptive or generic term like “BANK” to a common formative—here, ASPIRE—move the needle on consumer perception enough to overcome likely confusion?

The panel held that the Board erred by narrowing the universe of third‑party uses it considered under factor six to only those uses tied to “credit card services,” after the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (indeed, partially legally identical). Because mark strength (and the commercial impression that follows from it) depends on that larger competitive picture, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s first‑factor conclusion as well and remanded.

## What the court told the Board to fix

Two lessons come out of the opinion: first, the Board must be consistent in its definition of the relevant services across DuPont factors; second, when a field is crowded with similar marks across closely related services, third‑party uses beyond a single subcategory matter. The court relied on settled law that factor six is about the “number and nature” of similar marks in use on similar goods and services, and that similarity does not require identity. So the Board cannot accept a broad finding of service similarity for factor two and then, in factor six, constrict the pool of relevant third‑party marks to a narrower slice. Because the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis could materially affect the perceived commercial strength of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark, the first factor—which asks whether ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK are similar in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—must be revisited in light of a corrected sixth‑factor record.

## How “BANK” generally performs as a trademark element

In practical and doctrinal terms, “bank” is a generic or at least highly descriptive word for banking and financial services; courts and the Board routinely treat it as an informational term that adds little to the distinctiveness of the rest of a mark. That means ASPIRE will be the dominant element of both marks in ordinary consumer perception: consumers tend to focus on distinctive, memorable words rather than the generic service describer appended to them. Put differently, “bank” often doesn’t buy you much—not because it’s irrelevant, but because it usually contributes little source‑identifying power.

But the real world is messier. Whether “BANK” shifts the commercial impression depends on context. Stylization, logos, house‑mark families, the nature of use (an online, direct‑to‑consumer bank vs. a co‑branded card program), and the degree to which the formative “ASPIRE” has become associated in the marketplace with one source or many will shape how a typical consumer reads ASPIRE versus ASPIRE BANK.

## How the remand might play out

There are two plausible pathways on remand, and which one the Board reaches will hinge primarily on its corrected sixth‑factor assessment.

If the Board finds widespread third‑party use of ASPIRE(‑formative) marks across the broader banking and finance space—beyond the credit‑card niche—the likely conclusion is that CC Serve’s ASPIRE is conceptually or commercially weak and entitled only to a narrow scope of protection. That weakens the weight of similarity in the first DuPont factor. In that scenario, an Apex showing that “BANK” is generic and that Apex’s stylized presentation, channels of trade, or consumer targeting differ from CC Serve’s uses could tip the balance toward registration. In crowded fields, incremental differences (a distinctive logo, a particular stylization, or demographic targeting) can matter a lot when the core word is thin.

On the other hand, if the Board determines that ASPIRE enjoys commercial strength—because CC Serve’s prior use is long, prominent, and concentrated in a way that has built consumer association—the first factor may still favor CC Serve even after accounting for the “BANK” suffix. Given the Board’s original finding that the parties’ services are highly similar, a robust senior‑user showing of distinctiveness and marketplace recognition could sustain a refusal. Remember that mark weakness does not automatically resolve confusion in favor of the newcomer; it simply narrows the senior user’s zone of exclusive rights.

Two points of nuance are worth stressing. First, factor six is not a headcount exercise: the Board must assess the nature and quality of third‑party use—how extensive, how commercial, how national or regional, and how close the uses are to the parties’ services. Second, the commercial impression inquiry under factor one asks how consumers view the marks in the marketplace, not how counsel parses them in briefs. Thus “ASPIRE” as a stand‑alone brand for a credit‑card program might already carry banking connotations if consumers routinely see ASPIRE co‑branded with issuing banks. That context could diminish whatever gap “BANK” otherwise would create.

## What brand owners should do while we wait for remand

Both sides can and should sharpen the record now. The party defending the senior mark should assemble evidence of its mark’s commercial strength: long use, advertising expenditures, scope of distribution and co‑branding, actual instances of consumer recognition or confusion, and detailed evidence showing that consumers identify ASPIRE with CC Serve’s program rather than with a generic category. The applicant (Apex) should gather evidence aimed at showing that ASPIRE is diffuse in the market (screenshots and sales figures for third‑party uses across banking and finance), that “BANK” is generic or descriptive in context, and that Apex’s actual and intended channels (for an internet bank) and stylization produce a distinct commercial impression. High‑quality consumer survey evidence can be decisive in close cases—but only if the survey is properly designed to reflect the relevant marketplace and the Board’s legal standards.

Both sides should make the Board’s job easy by connecting the third‑party evidence to the services the court already found to be highly similar. The Federal Circuit’s remand is effectively an instruction to treat the marketplace holistically, not compartmentalize it by labels on registration certificates.

## Bottom line

The Federal Circuit’s remand doesn’t resolve whether ASPIRE BANK will register, but it clarifies the right analytic path. “Bank” is usually a cheap add‑on: it tells consumers what the service is, but it rarely provides distinctive source identification on its own. Where that additive element will matter is in a crowded field where the formative word is weak; in that setting, careful evidence about third‑party use and stylization can make the difference between registration and refusal. The upcoming remand will be a useful test of how the Board evaluates crowded‑field evidence when the services overlap closely, and it offers a textbook set of lessons for trademark practitioners about the critical interplay between mark strength (factor six) and commercial impression (factor one).
