Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Did the Board have to use the same 'similarity of goods/services' scope for DuPont factor six as it used for factor two?
Question:
The Board found under factor two that Apex's 'banking and financing services' are highly similar to CC Serve's 'credit card services,' even 'legally identical, in part.' But for factor six, it limited the relevant public to 'consumers of credit card services' and treated Aspire uses for other banking/financing services as 'essentially irrelevant.' The Federal Circuit says that was legal error because factor six, like factor two, deals with 'similar' goods/services. Does 'similar' need to be defined consistently across the DuPont factors once the Board has made a finding under factor two, or can the Board legitimately narrow the scope for factor six? How should the Board decide the proper scope of 'similar' goods/services for the crowded-field analysis when it has already found a high degree of similarity under factor two?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Once the Board made a factual finding under DuPont factor two that the parties’ services were “highly similar” (even “legally identical, in part”), the Federal Circuit held the Board should have used that same scope when assessing DuPont factor six. In Apex Bank v. CC Serve the court reversed because the Board narrowed factor six to “credit card services” only and excluded other banking/finance uses even though it had already found those services highly similar under factor two. (Apex Bank v. CC Serve, No. 2023‑2143, slip op. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) (J.A. 27–28, 47).)

Why (doctrine and the court’s reasoning)
- DuPont factor two asks whether the goods/services are similar; the Board here found credit‑card services and “banking and financing services” to be highly similar and “legally identical, in part.” (J.A. 25–28, 33–35.)
- DuPont factor six asks about “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” The Federal Circuit reiterated that factor six does not require identical goods—only similar ones. (Op. at J.A. 46–47 (citing Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation).)
- Because factor six depends on what counts as “similar goods,” the court held that a Board finding under factor two fixes the relevant universe of “similar” goods for use in the other factors unless the Board gives a valid, evidence‑based reason to do otherwise. The court put it plainly: “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar . . . the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.” (J.A. 47–48.)

What the Board must do on remand (practical rule)
- Start from the Board’s factor‑two finding: identify precisely the services the Board found similar (here, the Board’s finding that “banking and financing services” overlap with “credit card services”). (J.A. 25–28, 33–35.)
- Use that same scope to collect and evaluate third‑party marks for factor six: include third‑party uses of ASPIRE (and Aspire‑formative marks) on services that fall within the factor‑two similarity finding (i.e., the broader banking/finance universe), not only those labeled “credit card services.” (The court vacated because the Board excluded banking/finance uses from factor six despite its factor‑two finding.) (J.A. 47–48.)
- Apply the crowding analysis to that fuller set of marks: count and assess the “number and nature” of similar marks and decide whether they show commercial weakness or a crowded field (the court contrasted the small set the Board considered with the “voluminous” examples in Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation). (J.A. 47 (discussing Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation).)
- Explain any departures. If the Board nonetheless wishes to narrow the universe for a particular factor, it must explain and support that departure with substantial evidence showing why the factor‑two finding should not control the scope for that factor (the opinion faulted the Board for imposing a more stringent standard without justification). (J.A. 47–48.)
- Reassess related factors (e.g., factor one). The court expressly vacated the Board’s factor‑one analysis because a broadened factor‑six finding could change the mark’s commercial strength and thus the overall commercial impression. (J.A. 48–49.)

Bottom line
Apex requires consistency: a Board finding that goods/services are highly similar under DuPont factor two should govern what counts as “similar” for factor six (and other factors that turn on similarity) unless the Board articulates and supports a reasoned, evidence‑based basis for a narrower universe. The Board’s narrowing to only credit‑card services was reversible error in this case; on remand it must consider third‑party uses across the full scope the Board already found similar and explain its analysis with substantial evidence. (See J.A. 27–28, 47–48.)

### What legal effect does deletion of 'credit card services' from the USPTO ID Manual have on interpreting CC Serve’s 1998 identification?
Question:
The opinion notes the 'credit card services' entry was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration. The Board then used dictionaries to construe 'credit card services' broadly (issuing cards, processing transactions, account management, monitoring, etc.). Does removal of a term from the ID Manual have any retroactive effect on an existing registration’s scope? Is the ID Manual binding or merely persuasive when construing legacy identifications? Are there cases on how to interpret vague or outdated identifications when the Manual has changed, and what sources (dictionaries, industry usage, prosecution history) are appropriate?
Answer:
Short answer
No — deletion of an entry from the Trademark ID Manual after a registration does not automatically rewrite or narrow that earlier registration. Apex Bank v. CC Serve shows that the Board must (and here did) independently construe the legacy identification when the Manual no longer contains a standardized entry, using ordinary‑meaning tools and record evidence (dictionaries, third‑party registrations, evidence of how services are actually provided). The Manual’s absence pushed the Board to construe “credit card services”; the Board relied on dictionaries and third‑party registrations (J.A. 25; 27–28; 33–34), and the Federal Circuit affirmed that factual/legal analysis (as to the second DuPont factor) where supported by substantial evidence (J.A. 35).

What the opinion actually did and why it matters
- The Board treated the Manual deletion as a reason to determine the meaning of the registered identification, not as a ground to retroactively change the registration. The opinion explains: “Because the entry for ‘credit card services’ was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first determined the meaning of the identified services” (J.A. 25).  
- To fix that meaning the Board relied on dictionary definitions for terms like “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” and on third‑party registrations showing that the same provider commonly offers credit‑card and banking/finance services; based on that record the Board concluded the services were “legally identical, in part” (J.A. 25; 27–28; 33–34). The court affirmed that factual finding under the substantial‑evidence standard (J.A. 35).

Practical rule you can draw from the opinion
- The ID Manual is a reference tool, not an automatic retroactive limiter: if an entry disappears after a registration issued, the Board will construe the registration’s wording using ordinary‑meaning sources and record evidence rather than assuming the Manual’s later state controls. Apex Bank v. CC Serve demonstrates that approach. The opinion does not proclaim a categorical new rule about every Manual change, but it shows how the Board will proceed in practice and that the Federal Circuit will uphold the Board’s construction if supported by the record (J.A. 25; 27–28; 33–35).

Sources the opinion treats as appropriate for construing a legacy identification
- Dictionary definitions/ordinary meaning (the Board used them to define the services) (J.A. 25; 27–28).  
- Third‑party registrations and third‑party use evidence to show that different service labels are commonly offered by the same source or that customers encounter both services under one mark (J.A. 33–34; J.A. 40–46).  
- Evidence in the record about how the registrant actually offers the services (the opinion recites CC Serve’s business model — partnering with a bank to issue cards and servicing accounts — and the Board relied on the record in its analysis) (J.A. 6927–6930; see J.A. 25–28).

A caution the opinion highlights
- Be consistent across DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s analyses of factors six and one because the Board, after finding the services highly similar, then improperly limited the universe of third‑party marks it considered under factor six (and that inconsistency required remand) (J.A. 6–8; J.A. 47). In short, once the Board fixes the operative scope of the services for the second DuPont factor, it should retain that scope when evaluating the other factors.

Takeaway for practice and litigation
- Don’t assume a later ID Manual deletion automatically narrows or expands a prior registration. If the Manual no longer defines a term, expect the Board to construe the term using dictionaries, third‑party registrations/use evidence, and the record of how services are provided — and the Federal Circuit will uphold that construction if supported by substantial evidence (Apex Bank v. CC Serve, passim). If you want to resist a broad construction, put on record evidence showing a narrower commercial meaning or that third‑party evidence/dictionaries do not support the broader reading.

### In a §2(d) opposition, why did the TTAB compare the identifications rather than Apex’s actual services (it doesn’t issue credit cards)?
Question:
Apex says it does not offer credit cards; it runs retail banking and planned an online bank. The Board nevertheless treated Apex’s 'banking and financing services' as encompassing credit card issuance/credit extension, concluding the services are 'legally identical, in part.' I thought likelihood-of-confusion analysis considers real-world marketplace conditions. Why didn’t Apex’s actual non-offering of credit cards matter? Is there a rule that the TTAB compares services as identified in the application/registration regardless of actual use, and could Apex have narrowed its identification (e.g., with limitations) to avoid overlap? How do channel-of-trade presumptions play into this?
Answer:
Short answer
The Board compared the services as written in the registrations/applications because the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry turns on the legal scope of the parties’ identified goods/services. Here Apex had applied for “banking and financing services,” and the Board (relying on dictionary definitions, the Trademark ID Manual, and third‑party registrations) reasonably concluded those terms encompass credit‑card/credit‑extension services. Apex’s present business (it does not now issue credit cards) therefore did not defeat the Board’s finding that the identifications overlap; the Federal Circuit affirmed that conclusion for the second DuPont factor. (Apex Bank v. CC Serve, J.A. 25–28, 7029; opinion §III.A.)

Why the Board compared the identifications (not just what Apex actually does)
- The DuPont second factor asks whether the parties’ goods/services are related or marketed so that consumers might think they come from the same source. The Board assessed that question by construing the services as they are identified in the registration/application and by defining those terms using the Trademark ID Manual and dictionary definitions. The opinion describes that process: the Board “determined the meaning of the identified services,” defined “credit card services,” and then concluded that “banking” and “finance” encompass credit‑card issuance, making the services “legally identical, in part.” (J.A. 25, 27–28; citing Coach Servs., quoted at §III.A.)
- The opinion records that Apex does not currently offer credit cards (J.A. 7029) and that Apex made arguments based on the parties’ business arrangements, but the court nonetheless affirmed the Board’s legal/constructional analysis of the identifications: “The Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services, and substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. We affirm the Board’s finding as to the second DuPont factor.” (§III.A.)

Is there a “rule” that the TTAB compares identifications regardless of actual use?
- The decision illustrates the Board’s and the court’s practice: the inquiry focuses on the scope of the identified goods/services and whether those identifications are sufficiently related (or legally identical in part) to support a likelihood of confusion finding. The Board may rely on dictionary/ID Manual definitions and third‑party registrations to define that scope (J.A. 25–28, 33–34).
- Put simply: the wording of the identification controls the legal analysis unless the record narrows that wording. Apex’s choice of the broad phrase “banking and financing services” is what the Board analyzed and what the court reviewed.

Could Apex have avoided overlap by narrowing its identification?
- The opinion does not narrate any attempted amendment or limitation by Apex, but the practical implication in the opinion is clear: the Board’s analysis turned on the breadth of Apex’s claimed identification. Because the Board construed “banking and financing” to include credit‑card services, that construction produced the overlap. The takeaway from the opinion is that the breadth of an identification matters — a narrower, more specific identification that excluded credit‑card issuance would not have been subject to the same construction. (See J.A. 27–28.)

How channel‑of‑trade presumptions played into this decision
- The Board, after finding the services highly similar/partly legally identical, defined the relevant public narrowly as “consumers of ‘credit card services’” and limited its sixth DuPont (third‑party marks/marketplace crowding) analysis to marks used on credit‑card services. (J.A. 47.)
- The Federal Circuit held that was legally inconsistent: once the Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar (and partly identical) for the second DuPont factor, it should keep that same scope when considering similarity under other DuPont factors (including the sixth). The court vacated the sixth‑factor finding because the Board improperly narrowed the universe of third‑party marks to only credit‑card services when its earlier finding required a broader view. (J.A. 27–28, 33–34, 47; §III.B.) In short: when identifications are identical or very similar, the Board may presume overlapping channels/purchasers and must consider third‑party uses across the full scope established by its own similarity finding rather than artificially narrow the relevant trade for later factors.

Bottom line
The Board evaluated the legal scope of the parties’ identifications (not solely Apex’s current product mix). Because Apex claimed broad “banking and financing services” and the Board reasonably construed those terms to include credit‑card services, Apex’s lack of current credit‑card issuance did not prevent a finding of overlap. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s second‑factor ruling but remanded the first and sixth factors for reconsideration consistent with the court’s instruction to use the same scope across factors. The practical lesson (implicit in the opinion) is that the precise wording of your identification determines the scope of the conflict—draft identifications carefully and, if necessary, seek or preserve limiting language during prosecution.

### How many third-party 'Aspire' uses are needed to show weakness, and do registrations versus marketplace uses carry different weight?
Question:
The Board counted nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services and held that was not 'considerable' or 'ubiquitous' enough to show weakness (citing Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation). Apex pointed to 42 additional Aspire uses in broader financial services, which the Board disregarded but the Federal Circuit says should be reconsidered. Is there any benchmark for how much third-party evidence establishes a 'crowded field,' or is it entirely context-specific? Do third-party registrations have the same probative value as evidence of actual marketplace use when assessing conceptual and/or commercial weakness?
Answer:
Short answer
No bright‑line numeric benchmark exists — the inquiry is context‑specific. Apex confirms that a court/board must evaluate the “number and nature” of third‑party marks in the factual context of the case (DuPont factor six) and must use the same scope of goods/services it adopted under factor two. The Federal Circuit did not convert Jack Wolfskin’s 14 examples or Juice Generation’s 26 examples into a universal cutoff; those numbers are illustrative, not dispositive. And while both third‑party registrations and evidence of marketplace use are relevant, the opinion (consistent with Palm Bay and the DuPont framework) treats actual third‑party “use” as the probative species of evidence you need to show commercial weakness — registrations can help, but carry different (often lesser) persuasive weight absent evidence of use.

Why — doctrine and how Apex applies it
- DuPont factor six looks to “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” (DuPont, quoted in Apex at J.A. 6–7.) The purpose is to show whether the field is “crowded” and whether consumers have been educated to distinguish similar marks (see Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin as cited in Apex at J.A. 6–7).
- In Apex the Board counted only nine Aspire‑formative marks for credit‑card services and deemed that number insufficient to show weakness (the Board called the nine uses not “considerable” or “ubiquitous,” J.A. 47). Apex pointed to 42 additional Aspire uses across broader banking/finance services. The Board excluded those because it limited the relevant public to “credit card services.” The Federal Circuit held that was legal error: because the Board had already found the parties’ services “highly similar” (and partially legally identical) under DuPont factor two, it must retain that same scope when considering factor six and therefore should have considered the broader banking/finance Aspire uses (Apex at J.A. 27–28, 6–8). The court vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding and remanded for reconsideration.
- The opinion expressly cites Jack Wolfskin (which relied on “voluminous evidence” and highlighted 14 examples) and Juice Generation (which referred to 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number”) — but those citations are used as points of comparison, not as numerical rules. Apex’s remand shows the correct legal approach is a contextual assessment of both quantity and character of third‑party marks, measured against the relevant goods/services scope the Board has already found.

Registrations v. marketplace use — what matters
- Apex and the cases it cites treat “third‑party use” as the core probative concept for commercial weakness (see Palm Bay quoted in Apex at J.A. 6). The Board in Apex did use third‑party registrations to help show relatedness under factor two (J.A. 33–34), but the court rejected the Board’s narrowing of factor six evidence.
- Practically, then: registrations are relevant (and can be helpful to show that others consider certain words/designs adoptable across related services), but registrations that do not show consumer exposure or actual marketplace use are generally less persuasive on the question of commercial weakness than evidence that third parties actually use the mark in the marketplace. Apex underscores that the Board must weigh the “number and nature” of marks in use — not just counts of registrations — within the correct scope.

Takeaway for analysis or briefing
- Don’t expect a numeric safe harbor — cite Jack Wolfskin/Juice Generation as persuasive examples when your record shows many examples, but emphasize the quality of the evidence (are the marks actually used in the relevant market/scope the Board found under factor two?).  
- If the Board finds services highly similar under factor two, insist it use that same service scope when evaluating factor six (that is the precise error the Federal Circuit corrected in Apex).  
- Where possible, submit evidence of actual marketplace use (consumer‑facing use) for third‑party marks covering the full scope of services found similar — that is the most probative way to show conceptual/commercial weakness.

References from the opinion: DuPont factor six standard and Palm Bay/Jack Wolfskin/Juice Generation citations (Apex Bank v. CC Serve, Sept. 25, 2025, J.A. 6–8, 33–34, 40–47).

### How does factor six’s strength/weakness finding influence factor one’s 'commercial impression' analysis?
Question:
The court vacated factor one because a different determination on factor six (third-party use/strength) could change the marks’ 'overall commercial impression.' Do tribunals fold a mark’s conceptual/commercial strength into the factor-one similarity analysis (appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression), or is 'strength' supposed to be assessed under separate DuPont factors? Practically, if ASPIRE is found weak in financial services on remand, how would that affect whether adding 'BANK' to ASPIRE meaningfully changes the commercial impression and reduces likely confusion?
Answer:
Short answer
They are separate DuPont inquiries, but not siloed. Factor Six (third‑party use/registrations) is the canonical place to decide a mark’s relative strength; that strength (or weakness) is a material input to Factor One’s assessment of the marks’ “overall commercial impression.” The Federal Circuit did exactly that here—vacating the Board’s Factor Six finding and remanding Factor One because a different strength finding could change the commercial impression. (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., Sept. 25, 2025.)

Why — legal doctrine and what the opinion says
- DuPont factor structure: Factor One asks whether the marks are similar “in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” Factor Six asks about “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods,” i.e., third‑party evidence relevant to whether the mark is relatively weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection. (Apex Bank slip op. citing DuPont and Palm Bay.)
- The opinion ties the two together: the court explained that “commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor,” and therefore if a reconsideration of Factor Six yields a different finding about commercial strength or weakness, the Board’s Factor One analysis must be revisited. The court remanded both factors for that reason. (Apex Bank slip op.: “Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression. Therefore, we remand for reconsideration of the first factor as well.”)

Practical application to ASPIRE / ASPIRE BANK on remand
- If the Board (on remand) finds ASPIRE to be weak in financial services because there are many third‑party Aspire or Aspire‑formative marks in the relevant field, that finding does two things:
  1. It narrows ASPIRE’s scope of protection (Factor Six significance, per Palm Bay/ Juice Generation reasoning cited in the opinion).
  2. It alters the likely commercial impression (Factor One): consumers accustomed to many “Aspire” marks are more able to distinguish between variants, so the addition of a descriptive/narrowing term like “BANK” is more likely to be seen as a meaningful differentiator of source.
- But it is not automatic. The opinion makes clear the relationship is factual and contextual: a finding of weakness under Factor Six is an input to Factor One but does not itself decide likelihood of confusion. The Board must still weigh all pertinent DuPont factors (and the Federal Circuit already affirmed Factor Two here, which heavily favors confusion because the services are highly similar). So a weakness finding could materially reduce the weight of Factor One (and thus reduce overall likelihood of confusion), but it might not overcome other strong factors.

A few concrete procedural takeaways the opinion emphasizes
- Don’t narrow the universe of third‑party marks inconsistently: the court faulted the Board for limiting Factor Six to credit‑card marks even though it had found the parties’ services highly similar in its Factor Two analysis. The Board must use a consistent scope of “similar goods/services” when considering third‑party uses. (Apex Bank slip op.: “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar … the Board should retain the same scope …”)
- Factor Six is where strength is evaluated; Factor One is where you measure overall commercial impression — but the latter must be re‑examined in light of the former.

Bottom line
Strength belongs in Factor Six, but the strength determination is a relevant, and sometimes dispositive, input to Factor One’s commercial‑impression inquiry. In Apex, the Federal Circuit remanded precisely because a different Factor‑Six outcome could alter the Factor‑One result. If ASPIRE is found weak on remand, the Board may find that adding “BANK” meaningfully changes the commercial impression and therefore reduces the weight of Factor One — but whether that change defeats a likelihood of confusion is fact‑specific and must be balanced against the other DuPont factors (notably the strongly similar services here).

### Does CC Serve’s partnering with issuing banks affect whether it really offers 'credit card services' for §2(d) purposes?
Question:
CC Serve partners with a bank that issues ASPIRE-branded cards and accounts, while CC Serve services the accounts and manages the program. Apex argues CC Serve is 'not providing banking services' itself. For priority and similarity-of-services, does providing program-management/servicing under the mark qualify as 'credit card services' even if a partner bank is the legal issuer? Is this analyzed under the 'related company' doctrine (Lanham Act §1055) or quality/control principles, and does it affect the second DuPont factor analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. the Federal Circuit treats CC Serve’s role as a program manager/servicer as “credit card services” for §2(d) purposes. The court affirmed the Board’s conclusion that CC Serve’s activities (joining with a bank that issues ASPIRE cards and servicing the accounts) fall within the Board’s broad definition of “credit card services” and therefore are highly similar—indeed “legally identical, in part”—to Apex’s banking/finance services for DuPont factor two. See J.A. 6927–30, J.A. 25, J.A. 27–28.

Why (legal standard and the Court’s application)
- Governing test: DuPont factor two asks whether the parties’ goods/services are related or marketed such that consumers might believe they come from the same source; “the services need not be identical” — only relatedness or marketing circumstances that could cause confusion. (Court cites Coach Servs., and restates that principle. J.A. 24–25.)
- How the Board defined “credit card services”: the Board expressly included a range of functions under that identification—“issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25.
- Factual fit: CC Serve “joins with a bank, and the bank issues ASPIRE‑branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers,” and “the accounts are serviced by CC Serve and its affiliates.” The court rejected Apex’s contention that CC Serve’s partnership role meant it was “not providing banking services” and affirmed the Board’s finding that the services are highly similar (partially legally identical). J.A. 6930; J.A. 27–28; see also J.A. 35 (second DuPont factor weighed heavily for confusion).

What doctrines were applied (and what were not)
- The opinion resolves this question under the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion framework—specifically the second DuPont factor (similarity of services). It treats the practical functions performed under the ASPIRE mark (including program management and account servicing) as part of the “credit card services” category for that inquiry. J.A. 24–28; J.A. 35.
- The opinion does not invoke a separate “related company” doctrine or a distinct Lanham Act §1055/quality‑control analysis to resolve whether a servicer counts as the provider of the identified services. In short, the Court used the DuPont relatedness/marketing test rather than a standalone related‑company/quality‑control doctrine in this case.

Practical takeaway from the opinion
- On this record, branding and functional role matter more than the label “issuer” vs. “bank”: providing program management, servicing and other credit‑card functions under the ASPIRE mark was enough for the Board (and the Court) to treat CC Serve as offering “credit card services” for §2(d) analysis. J.A. 25, J.A. 6930.
- The court did, however, remand other DuPont factors (first and sixth) for further consideration in light of the Board’s finding of high similarity—so while the servicer role sufficed for factor two here, the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion outcome may turn on those other factors on remand. J.A. 47, J.A. 51–52; see also discussion vacating factors one and six.

Bottom line
If a party provides program management/servicing under a mark (even where a banking partner is the formal issuer), Apex v. CC Serve shows those activities can qualify as the identified “credit card services” for DuPont factor two. The Court treated that question as part of the services‑similarity inquiry rather than by applying a separate related‑company or quality‑control doctrine.

### Why could the Federal Circuit affirm factor two on 'substantial evidence' but vacate factor six as a 'legal error'?
Question:
The court reviews legal conclusions de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. It affirmed the Board’s factor-two finding (high similarity of services) as supported by substantial evidence, but vacated factor six because the Board applied an unduly narrow legal standard for 'similar' goods/services. How do we draw the line between a factual finding supported by substantial evidence and a legal error in applying the correct standard within the DuPont framework? Did the Federal Circuit reweigh factor-six evidence, or did it just correct the legal framework and remand for the Board to reweigh?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit affirmed DuPont factor two because that was a factual finding — the Board “carefully considered” the services and the court found “substantial evidence supports” the Board’s finding that the services were highly similar. It vacated factor six because the Board applied an incorrect legal standard — it narrowed the universe of third‑party marks to credit‑card services only despite its earlier factual finding of high similarity — and that legal error is reviewed and corrected by the court. The Federal Circuit did not reweigh the factor‑six evidence itself; it corrected the legal framework and remanded for the Board to reconsider factor six (and factor one) under the proper standard.

Why (doctrinally)
- Standard of review: the court states upfront that it reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence (citing M2 Software and Consol. Edison). So when the issue is whether the Board’s factfinding is supported by evidence, the appellate court defers if substantial evidence exists; when the issue is the legal standard or scope the Board applied, the court reviews that de novo.
- What happened here: the Board made a factual finding (in its factor‑two analysis) that Apex’s “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s “credit card services” are highly similar (even “legally identical, in part”), and the Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supported that factual conclusion and therefore affirmed factor two. By contrast, when the Board analyzed factor six it limited consideration to marks used for credit‑card services only and excluded Aspire‑marks used for other banking/finance services. The court concluded that limitation was a legal mistake because factor six asks about marks used on similar (not necessarily identical) goods/services — and the Board had already found the services to be highly similar. The opinion says plainly: “That was an error,” and “we see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.”
- Consequence: Because the error was one of legal scope/standard (not merely a dispute about whether the evidence supports a particular factual conclusion), the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding and remanded so the Board can apply the correct standard and reassess the third‑party mark evidence in light of its factor‑two finding.

Did the court reweigh factor‑six evidence?
No. The court did not substitute its own factfinding or reweigh the third‑party evidence. It identified a legal error in the Board’s approach and remanded for reconsideration: “We thus vacate the Board’s finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” The court also remanded factor one because the commercial impression could change after a correct factor‑six analysis. The appellate court left the fact‑finding and balancing to the Board on remand under the corrected legal framework.

Practical rule you can take away
If the Board’s conclusion is a supported finding of fact, the Federal Circuit will normally affirm it under substantial‑evidence review. But if the Board applies an incorrect legal standard or defines the comparison class inconsistently with its own factual findings (for example, requiring “identity” where the law requires only “similarity”), that is a legal error subject to vacatur and remand so the Board can reapply the proper legal standard and reweigh the evidence.

### On remand, can the TTAB consider more third-party 'Aspire' evidence, or is it limited to the existing record?
Question:
This was a direct appeal to the Federal Circuit on the existing TTAB record. The court vacated and remanded for reconsideration of factors six and one. Procedurally, can the TTAB reopen the record to allow additional third-party use/registration evidence beyond the nine credit-card uses the Board counted, or is the Board generally constrained to the current record and just required to reassess it under the correct legal standard? If reopening is possible, under what authority and subject to what limitations?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Federal Circuit’s remand requires the Board to reassess DuPont factors six and one using the correct scope: because the Board found the parties’ services “highly similar,” it must consider third‑party Aspire‑formative marks used for banking/financing services as well as credit‑card services (i.e., the broader set of marks the parties put before the Board). Apex’s argument that the Board should have considered the “42 other” Aspire marks was explicitly accepted as a legal point; the court vacated the sixth‑factor finding and remanded “for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration” in light of the Board’s finding that the services are highly similar (Opinion, J.A. 27–28, 47–48).

- The opinion does not direct the Board to admit new, post‑record evidence on remand. The remand contemplates reconsideration of the record under the proper legal standard; whether to reopen the evidentiary record is a procedural matter the Board must decide. Any additional factfinding the Board makes on remand will remain subject to the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence review (Opinion, Sec. II).

Why (legal doctrine from the opinion)
- DuPont factor six: third‑party use/registration evidence is probative of a mark’s commercial weakness and helps define the mark’s scope of protection. The Board may consider marks used on similar—not necessarily identical—goods or services. The Federal Circuit criticized the Board for treating only credit‑card uses as “relevant” after itself finding the parties’ services highly similar, and held that the Board’s narrow universe was legally erroneous (Opinion, Sec. III.B; citing Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation, Palm Bay, Jack Wolfskin, J.A. 40–51).

- DuPont factor one: the commercial impression inquiry can be affected by the mark’s perceived strength/weakness under factor six, so the Court vacated the Board’s first‑factor analysis as well and remanded for reassessment in light of whatever correct sixth‑factor conclusion the Board reaches (Opinion, Sec. III.C).

What that means on remand (practical application of the opinion)
1. If the additional third‑party “Aspire” evidence was already in the TTAB record (for example, the exhibits Apex submitted and that the Board previously labeled “essentially irrelevant”), the Board must now consider that evidence under the broader scope the court requires. The Court specifically agreed with Apex that the Board’s exclusion of non‑credit‑card Aspire uses was legally flawed and remanded for reconsideration (Opinion, J.A. 47–48).

2. If a party seeks to introduce entirely new third‑party evidence that was not part of the administrative record on appeal, the Federal Circuit’s opinion does not itself authorize or foreclose that step. The remand was for reconsideration; it did not instruct the Board to take new evidence. Reopening the record therefore is a procedural decision the Board must make (and explain) in the course of the remand. Any new findings based on newly admitted evidence will still be reviewed for substantial evidence on any future appeal (Opinion, Sec. II).

3. In short: the Board is required to broaden its consideration of third‑party Aspire marks that are properly part of the record; whether it may (or should) accept additional material beyond that record is a separate procedural question left to the Board to resolve and to justify on the remand record.

Bottom line
- The Federal Circuit gave the Board a substantive correction: don’t confine factor six to credit‑card‑only uses when you have found the services highly similar—consider the broader set of Aspire uses. The opinion compels reconsideration of the third‑party evidence already before the Board; it does not itself order the reception of new evidence, so any reopening of the record on remand is a procedural choice the Board must make and explain, with resulting findings subject to the court’s usual substantial‑evidence review.
