Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# The Same‑Scope Principle: Federal Circuit Faults TTAB for Narrowing DuPont Factor Six After Broad Factor Two

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a short but important decision about internal consistency in the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry. The Federal Circuit rejected a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) maneuver that is not uncommon in practice: after finding a broad overlap between parties’ services under DuPont factor two, the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it would consider under DuPont factor six. The court held that was legal error. The ruling reinforces a simple practical rule for TTAB practice and litigation strategy: once the Board defines the overlap between goods or services, it should carry that scope forward when evaluating other factors that depend on the same notion of “similarity,” or risk a remand.

## The court’s reasoning in the Apex/ASPIRE fight

The dispute was straightforward. CC Serve owns a 1996 registration for the word mark ASPIRE in connection with “credit card services.” Apex Bank seeks registration of ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” The TTAB concluded the services were highly similar—indeed “legally identical, in part”—and sustained CC Serve’s opposition under Section 2(d). But when the Board analyzed DuPont factor six (the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”), it limited its crowding inquiry to third‑party uses of ASPIRE on credit‑card services only, excluding third‑party Aspire marks used for other banking and financing services. The Board found only nine such credit card entries and concluded that showed no pervasive crowding; it therefore treated CC Serve’s mark as entitled to a normal scope of protection.

On appeal the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s conclusion on factor two—that Apex’s banking/financing services and CC Serve’s credit‑card services are highly similar—but it vacated the Board’s factor six decision. The court explained that factor six looks to similar, not identical, goods and therefore the Board’s narrowing was inconsistent with its own prior finding of broad similarity under factor two. “We find the Board’s definition of similarity to be too narrow,” the court said, citing Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation, and other precedent that asked only for similarity (not perfect identity) when counting third‑party marks. Because factor one (similarity of the marks) turns in part on the commercial strength of the prior mark—which in turn can be shown or rebutted by factor six evidence—the court also vacated the Board’s factor‑one analysis and remanded.

## Why the court’s approach matters (and its limits)

The Federal Circuit’s correction is doctrinally tidy and practically useful. The DuPont inquiry is a multi‑factor analysis, but it cannot tolerate ad hoc, inconsistent redefinitions of the relevant universe from factor to factor. If the Board concludes the services are broad enough to be “highly similar” or “legally identical in part,” it undermines the coherence of the overall analysis to permit a narrower definition when deciding whether the mark is weakened by a crowded field.

That said, the opinion is not a license to demand identical scope in every possible DuPont permutation. The court’s reasoning is fact‑driven: because the Board itself had already found high similarity under factor two, it was improper to selectively constrict factor six. In cases where the Board has not made such a finding, or where different factors legitimately ask different questions about channels, purchasers, or uses, a narrower focus may sometimes be appropriate. The key takeaway is consistency tied to the Board’s own factual findings, not a categorical rule that every factor must use the exact same labels.

## Practice takeaways for litigators and the TTAB

For applicants and registrants, Apex instructs two clear tactical moves. If you want to limit an opponent’s ability to introduce broad third‑party evidence, fight hard at the outset to prevent a finding of broad goods‑or‑services similarity under factor two. Conversely, if you want to show the prior mark is weak, build and push a comprehensive record of third‑party uses across the full universe that will be implicated by any factor‑two finding; make the Board adopt a clear definition of the relevant public and keep pushing for that same definition in later factors.

For the TTAB and examiners, the decision counsels more disciplined record statements: when the Board defines the scope of overlap it should explain whether and why that scope will (or will not) inform other DuPont factors. That explanation reduces the risk of remand and improves predictability.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Apex is a modest but important policing of internal consistency in the DuPont framework. Expect litigators to cite it when the Board appears to be “moving the goalposts” from factor to factor, and expect the TTAB to be more explicit in tying its factor‑two findings to later inquiries about crowding and commercial strength. That, in turn, will increase the tactical value of robust third‑party evidence and careful record building on channels of trade and the identity of the relevant consumer. Over time, Apex may make it harder for boards to treat word elements that appear across the financial services spectrum as insulated from each other simply by recasting the identified services in narrow terms at the factor‑six stage.

Bottom line: the Federal Circuit’s message is simple and sensible—define the overlap once, and carry that scope forward where the law calls for the same notion of “similarity.” Failing to do so invites correction on appeal.



# Counting Crowded Fields in Finance: From Nine “Aspire” Cards to Dozens of Financial “Aspire” Marks

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a reminder that DuPont’s sixth factor—the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods—turns as much on the scope of what the Board considers as on raw counting. For brand owners and challengers in financial services and fintech, the Federal Circuit’s decision makes clear that the Board cannot cabin its inquiry about third‑party marks to a narrower universe than the one it has already found relevant under DuPont factor two. That consistency requirement can materially change whether a plaintiff’s mark is treated as commercially strong (broad protection) or relatively weak (narrow protection), and thus whether consumer confusion is likely.

## What the Federal Circuit did

The underlying dispute is straightforward: CC Serve owns a long‑standing registration for ASPIRE in connection with credit‑card services; Apex Bank applied to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” The TTAB concluded the services were highly similar and sustained the opposition, but in assessing DuPont factor six it limited the relevant third‑party evidence to marks used on credit‑card services only. The Board found nine third‑party “Aspire” marks for credit cards and concluded that was not a “considerable” number suggesting weakness, treating roughly 42 other “Aspire” uses across the financial services spectrum as “essentially irrelevant.”

On appeal the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding under DuPont factor two (the services are highly similar, partly legally identical), but it vacated the Board’s DuPont factor six analysis—and, for linked reasons, its DuPont factor one analysis as to mark similarity—because the Board had improperly narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it considered. The court did not invent a numerical bright line: rather, it instructed the Board to reconsider the sixth factor using a scope of third‑party marks consistent with its prior finding that the parties’ services overlap. The first factor must be revisited on remand because the commercial strength of the ASPIRE mark (which DuPont six informs) affects the overall commercial impression comparison.

## The doctrinal core: why scope matters more than a magic number

DuPont factor six is about commercial weakness: if the field is crowded with similar marks used on similar goods or services, consumers are accustomed to distinguishing among similar marks and the putative senior mark is entitled to a narrower range of exclusive protection. The Federal Circuit’s opinion anchors on a simple doctrinal point: once the Board has defined the services as highly similar (and recognized partial legal identity), it cannot then evaluate third‑party use under a tighter definition of similarity. Third‑party marks need not be identical to be relevant; they need only be similar in kind. That principle tracks prior precedent the Board and courts have relied on—see Juice Generation (treating some 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number”) and Jack Wolfskin (treating a voluminous pattern of use as probative)—but the court here refused to convert those cases into a numeric test.

So the absence of a “magic number” is deliberate: the question is contextual. How many third‑party uses exist, and what is their nature—do they actually place the word or concept before the same class of consumers, through similar channels of trade and at similar prominence? Are those marks registered only or shown to be used meaningfully in commerce? Those qualitative features, not a single cut‑off, determine whether a mark’s commercial strength should be downgraded.

## How a broader “Aspire” universe could change the calculus on remand

If the Board, on remand, treats the 42 additional “Aspire” uses in banking/finance as relevant, the practical effect could be significant. A larger, cross‑service dataset could show that “Aspire” is widely used throughout the financial services field, which tends to support a finding of commercial weakness. That, in turn, would narrow the scope of protection accorded CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark and make it harder for CC Serve to prove that Apex’s ASPIRE BANK will cause confusion.

Two caveats, however. First, commercial weakness is never dispositive on its own; DuPont requires a multi‑factor balancing. The Federal Circuit already affirmed that the services are highly similar—a strong point for CC Serve. Second, the probative value of the additional marks will depend on their nature: are they national brands with consumer exposure, or minor local uses and registrations? Do they appear on the same consumer touchpoints (banking websites, card packaging, co‑branded card offerings)? The Board must evaluate the “number and nature” of those third‑party marks, not just their count. It is conceivable that even with dozens of “Aspire” uses, a Board could still conclude the senior mark retains sufficient commercial strength to warrant refusal; but it cannot dismiss the broader evidence out of hand.

## Practical implications for trademark practice in finance and fintech

This decision signals three concrete lessons for practitioners:

First, collect and present third‑party evidence across the whole field the Board has already found relevant. If your client litigates services that overlap with a broader class (e.g., banking, card issuance, payment processing), limit your opponent at your peril—courts will require consistency.

Second, quality beats a raw headcount. Registrations without evidence of marketplace use are weaker; screenshots, promotional materials, market‑share data, and evidence of consumer exposure will be far more persuasive than a list of filings. Demonstrate where the marks appear and to whom.

Third, plan for the interplay among DuPont factors. A showing of commercial weakness under factor six will meaningfully reshape factor one’s “commercial impression” analysis. Conversely, if an opposer cannot defend the distinctiveness or strength of a mark, it should shore up other DuPont levers—channels of trade and customer sophistication, for example—to preserve its case.

## Bottom line

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is not a decision that hands victory to applicants whenever a plaintiff’s mark appears in multiple registrations. It is, instead, a doctrinal correction: the Board must analyze third‑party use under DuPont six on at least the same footprint it uses under DuPont two. For financial services marks—where banks, processors, and card issuers increasingly blur the lines between products and channels—that consistency can be decisive. On remand the fate of ASPIRE will turn on whether the broader “Aspire” landscape in banking and finance shows a crowded field that meaningfully erodes the senior registrant’s commercial strength.



# Broad IDs, Broad Problems: Why “Banking and Financing Services” Sank Apex on DuPont Factor Two

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a straightforward—and sobering—reminder for trademark drafters: what you write in the identification of services can determine whether your mark ever clears opposition. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s finding under DuPont factor two that Apex’s broadly worded “banking and financing services” are highly similar (indeed “legally identical, in part”) to CC Serve’s registered “credit card services,” even though Apex does not currently issue credit cards. The decision shows how dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations can be used to construe broad identifications to sweep in overlapping services, and it highlights drafting pitfalls that routinely trip up intent‑to‑use applicants—especially in the financial‑services and fintech space.

## How the Board and court reached the conclusion

The facts are familiar. CC Serve owns a 1998 registration for the standard‑character mark ASPIRE for “credit card services.” Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications for ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services,” though Apex currently operates a retail bank with no credit card products and only plans an internet bank under the new brand. CC Serve opposed registration under Section 2(d).

In addressing DuPont factor two—the relatedness of the parties’ services—the Board reconstructed the meaning of “credit card services” (the Trademark ID Manual entry for that phrase had been deleted) using dictionary definitions and found the term to encompass a suite of credit‑card activities: issuing cards, processing payments and authorizations, account management, monitoring/alerts, counseling, and similar functions. The Board further relied on third‑party registrations that covered both credit‑card and banking/finance services; those registrations supported the inference that the two sets of services may emanate from a single source. On that record the Board concluded the services were highly similar and partly legally identical, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that conclusion as supported by substantial evidence.

The appellate court did not bless every part of the Board’s opinion. It vacated and remanded the Board’s analysis of DuPont factor six (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods) because the Board had limited its consideration to third‑party marks used only for credit‑card services, even though it had already concluded the services were highly similar. The court also vacated the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, and commercial impression) for reconsideration in light of any revised commercial‑strength analysis under factor six. But on the core point that a broadly worded “banking and financing” identification may encompass credit‑card activities, the Federal Circuit agreed with the Board.

## An analytical perspective

Two themes explain why the court’s outcome should be unsurprising to trademark practitioners, even if it’s unwelcome to applicants who believe their current business model is distinct from an incumbent’s.

First, TTAB and appellate practice long treat the identification in the application as the controlling baseline for likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis. The court’s affirmation reflects that canonical principle: the inquiry asks whether the applicant’s identified services, as written, overlap with the registrant’s goods or services. That inquiry does not turn on whether the applicant presently offers a particular function if the ID itself covers it. Apex’s lack of credit‑card products did not immunize its ASPIRE BANK ITU application because the ID swept broadly.

Second, the decision underscores the practical power of dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations. Where a TMID entry has been deleted or is otherwise unhelpful, the Board will reconstruct meanings and look to marketplace practice. Third‑party registrations that bridge the categories—e.g., covers for both “credit card services” and broader “banking and financing” services—are persuasive evidence that consumers could reasonably think the services come from the same source under one mark.

That said, practitioners should note the appellate court’s willingness to police internal inconsistency. Once the Board treated the services as highly similar, it could not then constrict the universe of third‑party marks when assessing the mark’s commercial strength. Consistency across DuPont factors matters; narrowing the scope for one factor after adopting a broad scope for another invites remand.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Apex cements a practical rule rather than a new doctrinal twist: broad, catch‑all identifications can and will be read expansively. For banks, fintechs, and program managers—entities that often operate through partnerships or white‑label arrangements—the decision is a caution: services provided as part of an ecosystem (card issuance, processing, account servicing) may be read as encompassed by broader banking or financing IDs even when the label “credit card services” is absent.

The remand on factor six is also important for litigators. If the Board finds a high degree of relatedness, parties should contest or develop the third‑party‑use record broadly across the entire ecosystem—not selectively. Parties trying to show weakness (or strength) of a mark must present third‑party evidence aligned with the scope the Board has adopted for the services, because commercial strength is a function of the relevant marketplace.

Finally, the case reminds us that TMID deletions are not a safe harbor. The absence of a TMID entry will not prevent the Board from construing an identification from first principles, using dictionaries, industry materials, and registrations.

## Practical drafting takeaways

Avoid laundry‑list identifications. “Banking and financing services” is a classic example of a catch‑all that invites overlap with existing registrations. If you mean to limit your claim, say so expressly in the ID: specify particular services (e.g., “retail deposit account services; consumer and business loans”) and exclude categories you do not intend to cover.

If you do not plan to issue or service credit cards, carve it out. An express exclusion—“not including credit card issuance, processing, account‑servicing or related credit‑card customer services”—can materially change the analysis because the Board evaluates the services as written.

Think about channels of trade and users when drafting. Limiting the identification to particular channels (e.g., “branch banking services” or “online retail banking services to individual consumers, not including business payment programs”) or to particular customer classes can narrow potential overlap.

Before filing an ITU, run clearances with an eye to how broadly your ID could be read and be prepared to narrow the ID during prosecution. If an opposition is filed, present evidence on how the services actually operate in the marketplace and on the absence of overlap—but do so framed against the identification as written.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank is a reminder that in trademark prosecutions, words matter. A seemingly generic umbrella like “banking and financing services” can reach into another party’s long‑standing credit‑card niche and doom registration, even where the applicant does not currently perform those functions. Careful, specific drafting—and consistent evidentiary development across DuPont factors—remains the best prophylactic against unpleasant surprises at the TTAB and on appeal.



# What Does “BANK” Buy You? Reassessing the First DuPont Factor for ASPIRE BANK After Remand

## Why this Federal Circuit decision matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is an important reminder that DuPont factors cannot be parsed in isolation. By affirming the Board’s finding that Apex and CC Serve provide highly similar financial services but vacating the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor—and, because of that, its first-factor analysis—the court signaled that a corrected view of the marketplace can change how the marks themselves are judged. For litigants in crowded industries (finance being a prime example), the practical question is simple: does adding a descriptive or generic term like “BANK” to a common formative—here, ASPIRE—move the needle on consumer perception enough to overcome likely confusion?

The panel held that the Board erred by narrowing the universe of third‑party uses it considered under factor six to only those uses tied to “credit card services,” after the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (indeed, partially legally identical). Because mark strength (and the commercial impression that follows from it) depends on that larger competitive picture, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s first‑factor conclusion as well and remanded.

## What the court told the Board to fix

Two lessons come out of the opinion: first, the Board must be consistent in its definition of the relevant services across DuPont factors; second, when a field is crowded with similar marks across closely related services, third‑party uses beyond a single subcategory matter. The court relied on settled law that factor six is about the “number and nature” of similar marks in use on similar goods and services, and that similarity does not require identity. So the Board cannot accept a broad finding of service similarity for factor two and then, in factor six, constrict the pool of relevant third‑party marks to a narrower slice. Because the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis could materially affect the perceived commercial strength of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark, the first factor—which asks whether ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK are similar in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—must be revisited in light of a corrected sixth‑factor record.

## How “BANK” generally performs as a trademark element

In practical and doctrinal terms, “bank” is a generic or at least highly descriptive word for banking and financial services; courts and the Board routinely treat it as an informational term that adds little to the distinctiveness of the rest of a mark. That means ASPIRE will be the dominant element of both marks in ordinary consumer perception: consumers tend to focus on distinctive, memorable words rather than the generic service describer appended to them. Put differently, “bank” often doesn’t buy you much—not because it’s irrelevant, but because it usually contributes little source‑identifying power.

But the real world is messier. Whether “BANK” shifts the commercial impression depends on context. Stylization, logos, house‑mark families, the nature of use (an online, direct‑to‑consumer bank vs. a co‑branded card program), and the degree to which the formative “ASPIRE” has become associated in the marketplace with one source or many will shape how a typical consumer reads ASPIRE versus ASPIRE BANK.

## How the remand might play out

There are two plausible pathways on remand, and which one the Board reaches will hinge primarily on its corrected sixth‑factor assessment.

If the Board finds widespread third‑party use of ASPIRE(‑formative) marks across the broader banking and finance space—beyond the credit‑card niche—the likely conclusion is that CC Serve’s ASPIRE is conceptually or commercially weak and entitled only to a narrow scope of protection. That weakens the weight of similarity in the first DuPont factor. In that scenario, an Apex showing that “BANK” is generic and that Apex’s stylized presentation, channels of trade, or consumer targeting differ from CC Serve’s uses could tip the balance toward registration. In crowded fields, incremental differences (a distinctive logo, a particular stylization, or demographic targeting) can matter a lot when the core word is thin.

On the other hand, if the Board determines that ASPIRE enjoys commercial strength—because CC Serve’s prior use is long, prominent, and concentrated in a way that has built consumer association—the first factor may still favor CC Serve even after accounting for the “BANK” suffix. Given the Board’s original finding that the parties’ services are highly similar, a robust senior‑user showing of distinctiveness and marketplace recognition could sustain a refusal. Remember that mark weakness does not automatically resolve confusion in favor of the newcomer; it simply narrows the senior user’s zone of exclusive rights.

Two points of nuance are worth stressing. First, factor six is not a headcount exercise: the Board must assess the nature and quality of third‑party use—how extensive, how commercial, how national or regional, and how close the uses are to the parties’ services. Second, the commercial impression inquiry under factor one asks how consumers view the marks in the marketplace, not how counsel parses them in briefs. Thus “ASPIRE” as a stand‑alone brand for a credit‑card program might already carry banking connotations if consumers routinely see ASPIRE co‑branded with issuing banks. That context could diminish whatever gap “BANK” otherwise would create.

## What brand owners should do while we wait for remand

Both sides can and should sharpen the record now. The party defending the senior mark should assemble evidence of its mark’s commercial strength: long use, advertising expenditures, scope of distribution and co‑branding, actual instances of consumer recognition or confusion, and detailed evidence showing that consumers identify ASPIRE with CC Serve’s program rather than with a generic category. The applicant (Apex) should gather evidence aimed at showing that ASPIRE is diffuse in the market (screenshots and sales figures for third‑party uses across banking and finance), that “BANK” is generic or descriptive in context, and that Apex’s actual and intended channels (for an internet bank) and stylization produce a distinct commercial impression. High‑quality consumer survey evidence can be decisive in close cases—but only if the survey is properly designed to reflect the relevant marketplace and the Board’s legal standards.

Both sides should make the Board’s job easy by connecting the third‑party evidence to the services the court already found to be highly similar. The Federal Circuit’s remand is effectively an instruction to treat the marketplace holistically, not compartmentalize it by labels on registration certificates.

## Bottom line

The Federal Circuit’s remand doesn’t resolve whether ASPIRE BANK will register, but it clarifies the right analytic path. “Bank” is usually a cheap add‑on: it tells consumers what the service is, but it rarely provides distinctive source identification on its own. Where that additive element will matter is in a crowded field where the formative word is weak; in that setting, careful evidence about third‑party use and stylization can make the difference between registration and refusal. The upcoming remand will be a useful test of how the Board evaluates crowded‑field evidence when the services overlap closely, and it offers a textbook set of lessons for trademark practitioners about the critical interplay between mark strength (factor six) and commercial impression (factor one).
