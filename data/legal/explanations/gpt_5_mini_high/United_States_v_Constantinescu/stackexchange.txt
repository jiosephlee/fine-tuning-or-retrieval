Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What can a court consider on a Rule 12 motion to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit says it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo and must take the allegations as true, citing Rafoi and Fontenot. The district court dismissed for not alleging deprivation of a traditional property interest. I'm confused about the scope of what the district court could look at. On a Rule 12(b)(3)(B)(v) motion, can the court consider evidence that investors received market-value securities or that the posts were opinions/puffery, or is it strictly confined to the four corners of the indictment? When (if ever) can a court resolve factual disputes pretrial in this posture?
Answer:
Short answer: On a Rule 12(b)(3)(B)(v) motion to dismiss for failure to state an offense the court asks whether the indictment’s allegations, taken as true, state each element of the charged crime. It should not weigh extrinsic evidence or try to prove (or disprove) contested facts at that stage. Only where the charging papers themselves, when accepted as true, fail as a matter of law may the court dispose of the case on the pleadings.

Why (and where the Constantinescu opinion says so)
- The Fifth Circuit in United States v. Constantinescu reiterates the governing standard: “We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo” and “[t]he court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.” (quoting Rafoi and Fontenot). An indictment must allege “each material element of the offense.” (Gaytan; Guzman‑Ocampo quoted in Constantinescu).
- The panel applied those rules to the securities‑fraud counts: the question is whether the indictment, as pleaded, alleges a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” (and thus a fraudulent‑inducement to obtain money or property). The court reversed dismissal because the indictment alleged that defendants induced followers to buy securities by false or misleading posts — i.e., alleged fraudulent inducement to obtain money/property (and Constantinescu relied on the Supreme Court’s Kousisis and Ciminelli framework to explain why that theory is legally sufficient).

Applying that to your specific examples
- Market‑value securities. The Fifth Circuit expressly cautioned that “the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm” and that “receipt of market‑value securities does not obviate the fraud charges” (relying on Kousisis). More importantly for Rule 12, the district court should not dismiss by accepting extrinsic proof that investors actually got fair value. The proper inquiry at the 12(b) stage is whether the indictment alleges fraudulent inducement to obtain money/property; if it does (as the indictment did here), that is sufficient.
- Opinion/puffery. If the indictment itself, on its face, pleads only nonactionable puffery or merely opinion (i.e., it fails to allege a material false representation), then the indictment might fail to state an offense and dismissal could be appropriate as a legal matter. But the court cannot, at the Rule 12 stage, take extrinsic evidence to recharacterize allegations that the indictment itself asserts were false and misleading. In short: the court decides whether the pleaded facts (accepted as true) amount to a legally cognizable misrepresentation; it does not resolve credibility or factual disputes about what statements really meant or what evidence would show.

When (if ever) a court may resolve factual disputes pretrial in this posture
- Constantinescu makes clear that factual disputes that require weighing evidence are for later proceedings, not a Rule 12 dismissal. A court may resolve a pure legal question that can be decided on the face of the indictment (for example, whether the pleaded theory is foreclosed as a matter of law under Ciminelli), but it may not grant dismissal by relying on contested extrinsic facts that contradict the indictment’s allegations.

Bottom line
- On a Rule 12(b)(3)(B)(v) motion a court is largely confined to the four corners of the indictment and must accept its allegations as true. Evidence that investors received market‑value securities or that the posts were actually opinion only defeats the indictment if the indictment itself, as pleaded, fails to allege the necessary elements; factual disputes about those points belong to trial (or a later motion) rather than to a motion to dismiss. Constantinescu reversed the district court for failing to follow that framework.

### How exactly does the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory differ from the rejected 'right-to-control' theory, and why does it fit a pump-and-dump?
Question:
The panel says Ciminelli bars convictions based solely on depriving victims of 'valuable economic information,' but Kousisis approves the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory where a lie tricks the victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property. In this pump-and-dump, the defendants allegedly misrepresented positions/price targets, followers bought, and defendants sold. Is the object here truly to 'obtain the victim's money' even though the victims got stock in return? Does it matter if the defendants didn't sell directly to their followers but into the market generally? How does that distinction play into the fraudulent-inducement framing?
Answer:
Short answer
- The right-to-control theory (Ciminelli) fails because it treats a victim’s loss as the deprivation of “valuable economic information,” which the Supreme Court held is not a protected, traditional property interest. United States v. Ciminelli, 598 U.S. 306 (2023).
- The fraudulent‑inducement theory (Kousisis) is different: it targets a lie that causes a victim to enter a transaction that requires handing over money or property. Kousisis makes clear this is not a repackaged right‑to‑control claim and that protecting money/property is what the fraud statutes are about. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025).
- The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu applied Kousisis: a pump‑and‑dump fits fraudulent inducement because the indictment alleges the defendants’ misrepresentations induced followers to buy (i.e., hand over money) and the defendants then sold at the inflated price to obtain proceeds.

Why the theories differ (doctrinally)
- Ciminelli: federal fraud statutes protect “traditional property interests,” not a bare “right to valuable economic information.” So a prosecution cannot rest solely on alleging that a defendant deprived a victim of information necessary to make economic decisions. (Constantinescu, discussing Ciminelli.)
- Kousisis: fraudulent inducement is a theory under which the defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” It therefore targets the transfer of money/property, not the mere withholding of information, and “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory.” (Constantinescu quoting Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1398.)

How that fits a pump‑and‑dump (applying Constantinescu)
- What matters is that the indictment alleges deception that causes followers to purchase securities: “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” (Indictment ¶ 13, quoted in Constantinescu.)
- Those purchases are transfers of money/property by the victims. Under Kousisis, a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced such a deprivation “under materially false pretenses,” even if the victim received stock in return or is not left economically worse off. (Constantinescu citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1394 n.5, 1391.)
- The panel therefore treats the pump stage as the fraudulent inducement (inducing victims to hand over money to buy) and the dump stage as the mechanism by which the fraudster obtains money (selling at the inflated price). The indictment’s allegation that defendants “dump” by secretly selling for profit (and alleged $114 million in proceeds) shows the object was to obtain money. (Constantinescu, §§ I & III.A–B.)

Does it matter that victims got stock in return?
- No — not for the fraudulent‑inducement theory as stated in Kousisis and applied in Constantinescu. The Supreme Court and the Fifth Circuit emphasize that liability does not depend on leaving the victim economically worse off; the protected interest is the money/property that the victim was tricked into parting with, even if something of value was provided in return. (See Kousisis language quoted in Constantinescu.)

Does it matter that the defendants didn’t sell directly to their followers?
- No. The key question is whether the misrepresentation induced the followers to purchase (i.e., hand over money or property). Criminal liability under fraudulent inducement does not require the same dollar to pass directly from victim to fraudster or a “mirror image” of loss=gain. The court relied on precedents saying the statutes do not impose a mirror‑image requirement. What the indictment alleges is the followers’ purchases inflated the market price and that the defendants then sold into that market to realize proceeds — a classic pump‑and‑dump chain that satisfies fraudulent inducement. (Constantinescu § III.B; citing Baker and Kousisis language.)

Bottom line
- Right‑to‑control = impermissible theory in Ciminelli because it criminalizes withholding information (no traditional property interest). Fraudulent inducement = permissible in Kousisis and, on these allegations, in Constantinescu, because the defendants are alleged to have lied to cause followers to hand over money (buy stock), and the defendants then obtained money by selling at the inflated price. The indirectness of the defendants’ sales (selling into the market rather than directly to followers) does not defeat the fraudulent‑inducement framing so long as the indictment plausibly alleges the deceit induced the transfers that enabled the defendants to obtain money.

### Does § 1348(1) require that obtaining 'money or property' be an object of the scheme, or is that only in § 1348(2)?
Question:
Section 1348 has two clauses: (1) schemes to defraud in connection with a security, and (2) schemes to obtain money or property by false pretenses in connection with a security. The opinion repeatedly frames the elements as deception plus 'obtaining the victim’s money or property' being an object, relying on Kousisis. Is the court effectively importing a money-or-property objective into § 1348(1) as well? Is there any authority or circuit split on whether subsection (1) stands on its own without an 'obtain money or property' requirement?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — in Constantinescu the Fifth Circuit applies the Supreme Court’s recent guidance (Ciminelli and Kousisis) and treats the § 1348 “scheme to defraud” prong as requiring that the defendant’s deception be directed at obtaining money or property (or otherwise depriving the victim of a traditional property interest). Put another way, the court will not sustain a securities‑fraud charge under § 1348(1) based on a scheme that merely deprives victims of economic information; the indictment must allege a fraudulent‑inducement that aims to get the victim’s money or property.

Why the court says so (doctrine and textual reasoning)
- The opinion starts from the statute’s two clauses but then analyzes § 1348 under the familiar mail/wire‑fraud framework: securities fraud requires a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 (5th Cir. 2023), quoted in the opinion.  
- The court says the “scheme to defraud” element requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money.” It then summarizes the governing formulation from the Supreme Court: a defendant commits securities fraud when he “engaged in deception” and “[o]btaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of the fraud (quoting Kousisis, which itself relies on Ciminelli). The opinion (relying on Ciminelli) expressly rejects a right‑to‑control theory that would treat loss of economic information alone as a protected property interest. Quoted language from the opinion:
  - § 1348 covers both “schemes to ‘defraud any person…’” and schemes “to obtain … any money or property…,” but the court applies mail/wire fraud concepts and says the “scheme to defraud” “requires a material misrepresentation ‘intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money….’”  
  - “In sum, a defendant commits securities fraud when he ‘engaged in deception’ and ‘[o]btaining the victim’s money or property’ was ‘an object’ of his fraud.” (quoting Kousisis and Ciminelli).  
  - The court distinguishes Ciminelli’s rejection of the right‑to‑control theory from Kousisis’s approval of the fraudulent‑inducement theory and holds the indictment pleads a fraudulent inducement (followers were induced to buy securities, i.e., part with money).

Practical consequence and on the question of a circuit split
- Constantinescu effectively requires, for § 1348(1) prosecutions built on a fraudulent‑inducement theory, an allegation that the defendant sought to obtain the victim’s money or property; mere deprivation of valuable economic information is insufficient. The court applies the same money/property protection principle to the § 1348(1) “scheme to defraud” analysis rather than treating § 1348(1) as permitting convictions for informational deprivation alone.  
- The opinion notes “scant caselaw” in this circuit and relies on Supreme Court precedent (Ciminelli and Kousisis). It cites one Seventh Circuit decision applying Kousisis but does not identify any circuit split. Constantinescu therefore follows the controlling Supreme Court framework and does not announce a contrary circuit view.

Bottom line
If you’re charged (or drafting an indictment) under § 1348(1), Constantinescu signals that the government must plead (and a court will look for) that the deceptive scheme was aimed at obtaining money or property (or otherwise infringing a traditional property interest). Mere removal of economic information from a victim’s control—standing alone—won’t do after Ciminelli and as applied here.

### After Kousisis, does 'intent to defraud' require an intent to cause loss, or is intent to obtain money enough?
Question:
The defendants argued they only intended to profit, not harm anyone, but the panel says that's a distinction without a difference under Kousisis and cites that actual loss isn't required. Under Fifth Circuit law (Evans) and Kousisis, what exactly satisfies the 'intent to defraud' element? If a defendant believes the counterparty will receive equivalent value (e.g., stock at then-market price) or might even profit later, can 'intent to defraud' still be shown solely by intent to deceive and to obtain money via that deceit?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Under Fifth Circuit law as applied in Constantinescu (following Evans and the Supreme Court in Kousisis), “intent to defraud” is satisfied by an intent to deceive combined with an intent to obtain the victim’s money or property by that deceit. The government does not have to prove the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off or to cause a net pecuniary loss.

Why — the controlling test (Evans + Kousisis, as applied in Constantinescu)
- Evans (Fifth Circuit) frames the two relevant pieces: (1) a “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value,” and (2) “intent to defraud” requires an intent (1) to deceive and (2) to cause some harm to result from the deceit. Constantinescu, quoting Evans, repeats both prongs.  
- Kousisis (Supreme Court) then clarified the scope of the “harm” requirement under the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a fraudster violates the statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The Court explained that fraudulent inducement protects money/property and that a fraud is complete when a defendant induces a deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses even if the victim receives something of equal value or does not suffer a net loss. Constantinescu applies that holding to a pump‑and‑dump indictment.

How that applies to your hypotheticals
- If the proof shows the defendant knowingly lied (intent to deceive) and intended that those lies would induce the counterparty to hand over money or property (intent to obtain money/property as an object of the scheme), that satisfies intent to defraud after Kousisis — even if the counterparty actually received market‑price stock or later profited. Constantinescu explicitly rejects the defendants’ “profit, not harm” argument as “a distinction without a difference.”  
- What remains essential is the deception plus the objective to obtain money/property. If there is no intent to deceive (e.g., the speaker honestly believed the statements), then the intent‑to‑defraud element is not met.

Bottom line
Under Evans as shaped by Kousisis and applied in Constantinescu, intent to obtain money/property by deception is enough to satisfy “intent to defraud”; an intent to cause a victim’s net pecuniary loss is not required.

### Are social-media statements about one's trading positions and 'price targets' material misrepresentations under § 1348, or are they puffery/opinion?
Question:
The indictment alleges the defendants misrepresented their positions and potential prices to induce purchases. Are statements like 'I'm buying' or 'this could go to $10' considered actionable misrepresentations of fact for criminal securities fraud, or could they be non-actionable puffery/opinion? Does the Omnicare framework for opinion statements in securities cases apply in criminal § 1348 prosecutions, and what would make a 'price target' or 'I'm buying' statement materially false or misleading in this context?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — social‑media statements like “I’m buying” or “this could go to $10” can be charged as material misrepresentations under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, but only when the indictment (and ultimately the proof) alleges they were false or misleading, material, and made with intent to deceive to induce purchases. United States v. Constantinescu makes clear that allegations that influencers posted false or misleading information about their positions and price prospects to induce followers to buy securities fit the fraudulent‑inducement theory and survive a pleading‑stage challenge. But Constantinescu does not decide the fine line between non‑actionable puffery/opinion and an actionable false statement (and it does not adopt or discuss an Omnicare test for opinion statements).

Why Constantinescu matters (the applicable standard)
- The Fifth Circuit framed § 1348 liability around the familiar mail/wire‑fraud concepts: the government must allege (1) a scheme to defraud and (2) an intent to defraud. A “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money” and the indictment here pleaded that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” United States v. Constantinescu, slip op. § III (quoting Greenlaw and the indictment).  
- The court rejected the district court’s effort to characterize the conduct as a mere deprivation of “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli) and instead treated the allegations as a fraudulent‑inducement theory — i.e., the defendants used material misstatements to trick victims into handing over money or property by buying the securities. Constantinescu relies on the Supreme Court’s Kousisis formulation that fraudulent inducement “protects money and property” and that liability does not require proof the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. Id. § III.A–B.

How that applies to “I’m buying”
- A present‑tense statement about one’s current trading position or immediate intent (e.g., “I’m buying”) is the sort of factual representation the indictment in Constantinescu alleged. The opinion specifically describes the alleged scheme as posting false statements “misrepresenting their trading positions” to induce purchases. Id. § I.  
- Practically: at the pleading/trial stage, such a statement will be actionable if the government alleges/proves it was false or misleading (for example, the speaker was in fact selling, had already sold, or never intended to buy), was material to investors’ purchase decisions, and was made with the intent to deceive and obtain money/property.

How that applies to a price target or prediction (“this could go to $10”)
- Price targets and forward‑looking predictions are closer to opinion/puffery and therefore more context‑sensitive. Constantinescu does not set out a special test for opinion statements; it simply requires that the indictment allege false or misleading statements used to induce purchases. Id. § III.  
- A prediction can be actionable if it is pleaded as part of a scheme of deception — e.g., if it was presented as a fact, was objectively false in context, or was used as part of a deliberate campaign to induce purchases while the speaker secretly manipulated or sold the stock. Conversely, a vague optimistic projection or ordinary market puffery is less likely to be a material misrepresentation unless the indictment alleges facts showing it was used deceptively to obtain investors’ money.

Does the Omnicare opinion‑test govern criminal § 1348 prosecutions?
- Constantinescu does not address or adopt Omnicare (the opinion‑statement framework developed in civil securities cases) and therefore does not answer whether that specific framework applies in criminal prosecutions. The Fifth Circuit here relied on mail/wire‑fraud concepts and the Supreme Court’s Kousisis/Ciminelli guidance about fraudulent‑inducement vs. right‑to‑control. Id. § II–III.  
- Bottom line: Omnicare’s applicability is an open question under Constantinescu — defendants will press puffery/opinion defenses; prosecutors will rely on the indictment’s factual allegations that the statements were false/misleading and made to induce purchases.

Materiality and intent — the key elements
- Material misrepresentation: the court treated material misrepresentations as the heart of the “scheme to defraud” allegation; an allegation that false posts induced purchases is a textbook materiality allegation at the pleading stage. Id. § III.  
- Intent to deceive/obtain money: Constantinescu emphasizes fraudulent inducement — the fraud is aimed at obtaining money or property through misstatements, and Kousisis confirms liability does not require proof of net loss to the victim. Id. § III.A–B.

Practical takeaway for pleadings and motions
- At the indictment stage, Constantinescu says the government survives dismissal where it pleads that influencers posted false or misleading statements about their positions/price prospects to induce followers to buy and then sold for a profit. Id. § I, III.  
- Whether a particular social‑media phrase is legally actionable will be largely fact‑dependent: was it a factual statement or a nonactionable opinion/puff; was it false or knowingly misleading; and did the speaker intend to induce purchasers and obtain money? The Fifth Circuit’s opinion tells you that properly pleaded allegations of false position/price statements used to induce purchases are sufficient on their face — the puffery question is not resolved by this opinion and will be litigated on the facts.

If you’re drafting or attacking an indictment, focus on whether the charging document pleads (with concrete factual allegations) falsity, materiality, and deceptive intent for the specific statements at issue — that is exactly what Constantinescu found adequate at the pleading stage.

### What must the government show about 'in connection with' the purchase or sale—do the deceived buyers have to be the defendants’ counterparties?
Question:
The court treats the followers as victims who were induced to purchase, and the defendants as having obtained money from the scheme. In a dispersed market, however, induced buyers might purchase from other sellers, while defendants might dump to different buyers. For § 1348 liability, must the government prove the defendants obtained money specifically from the deceived purchasers, or is it enough that the scheme involved lies that induced market transactions in the relevant securities, during which the defendants monetized their positions?
Answer:
Short answer: No. Under Constantinescu the government need not allege (at the pleading stage) that each deceived purchaser was the literal counterparty who handed cash directly to the defendant. It is enough to allege a fraudulent‑inducement scheme that (1) used material misrepresentations to induce purchases or sales of the relevant securities and (2) had obtaining money or property as an object—so that the defendants were able to monetize their positions by selling into the inflated market.

Why (doctrinally and as the opinion explains)
- The court frames § 1348 claims using the familiar mail/wire‑fraud concepts: the elements are a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud,” and the “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” United States v. Constantinescu, No. 24‑20143, slip op. §III (5th Cir. Oct. 2, 2025) (quoting Greenlaw/Evans).  
- Constantinescu distinguishes the rejected “right‑to‑control” theory (Ciminelli) from the fraudulent‑inducement theory the government pleaded here. The fraudulent‑inducement theory protects money/property because it alleges that the defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Id. §III.A (quoting Kousisis).  
- Importantly, the court relied on the proposition that the fraud statutes do not demand a victim suffer a net pecuniary loss or require a “mirror‑image” transfer where the victim’s loss is exactly the defendant’s gain. See id. §III.B (citing Kousisis and Baker) — so the government does not have to show the deceived investor’s cash literally flowed into the defendant’s pocket.

How that applies to a dispersed market
- The opinion treats the indictment’s allegation (that defendants purchased, “pumped” via false posts to induce others to buy, and then “dumped” by secretly selling for profit) as sufficient to allege the necessary nexus “in connection with” purchases and sales. Id. §I & §III.  
- Put plainly: the critical showing is that the misrepresentations were intended to and did induce market transactions (victims parted with money/property), and that obtaining money was an object of the scheme so that defendants monetized their positions. The deceived buyers need not be shown to have transacted directly with the defendants for § 1348 liability to be properly alleged.

A caveat on posture
- Constantinescu resolved an indictment‑sufficiency challenge. The court held the indictment adequately pleaded the fraudulent‑inducement theory and the requisite intent. It did not settle what specific proof the government must produce at trial to meet its burden of proof; it simply holds that alleging inducement of purchases and that obtaining money was an object of the fraud satisfies § 1348 pleading requirements. See id. §§II–IV.

Bottom line: For pleading § 1348 securities fraud under Constantinescu, the government must allege a causal link between the defendants’ misrepresentations and purchases/sales (a fraudulent‑inducement scheme) and show that obtaining money/property was an object of that scheme. It does not have to allege that deceived buyers were the literal counterparties who directly paid the defendants.

### Do reliance, price impact, or loss causation play any role in a criminal § 1348 case compared to Rule 10b-5?
Question:
In civil 10b-5, plaintiffs must prove reliance, loss causation, and economic loss. This opinion suggests actual loss isn't required for § 1348 and focuses on scheme and intent to defraud. In a criminal § 1348 prosecution like this one, do the government and jury need to find investor reliance or price impact? If not, what evidentiary showings typically substitute for those civil elements to establish materiality and causation in the criminal context?
Answer:
Short answer
No — the government in an 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (criminal securities fraud) case does not have to prove the civil Rule 10b‑5 elements of reliance, market/price‑impact, or loss causation as separate, stand‑alone elements. Instead, as the Fifth Circuit explained in United States v. Constantinescu, the government must prove a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud — i.e., a material misrepresentation used to obtain money or property and an intent to deceive and obtain that value. Allegations (and proof) that the defendants “fraudulently induce[d]” investors to buy and that the defendants obtained money or profits from those transactions satisfy the criminal elements even if victims suffer no net pecuniary loss. (Constantinescu, Oct. 2, 2025, discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis.)

Why — the doctrinal frame from Constantinescu
- Elements the court insisted on: securities fraud under § 1348 requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” The court described the scheme element as requiring a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money” and the intent element as requiring “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” (Constantinescu § III (quoting Greenlaw/Evans).)
- Ciminelli vs. fraudulent‑inducement: Constantinescu reiterates that Ciminelli forbids prosecutions based solely on deprivation of valuable economic information (the “right‑to‑control” theory), but it distinguishes that from the fraudulent‑inducement theory — which is precisely what the indictment here alleged: defendants used material falsehoods to induce purchases (i.e., to make followers part with money/property). The court held that fraudulent inducement “protects money and property” and is a lawful basis for a § 1348 prosecution. (Constantinescu § III.A.)
- No requirement of net economic loss: consistent with Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit made clear that the government need not show that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss; a fraud is complete when the defendant induces the victim to give up money or property under materially false pretenses, even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return. (Constantinescu § III.B; quoting Kousisis.)

What replaces civil “reliance / price impact / loss causation” at trial
Think of the criminal proof as focused on (A) materiality and (B) the causal link between the misrepresentation and the defendant’s obtaining money/property (fraudulent inducement), proven beyond a reasonable doubt. Typical showings the government will (and in Constantinescu alleged it did) use to prove those points are:

- Allegations/evidence that the defendant made material false or misleading statements (content of social‑media posts, misstatements of holdings/intentions). (See indictment language quoted in Constantinescu ¶¶13–14.)
- Evidence that those statements were intended to and did induce others to buy (direct inducement): follower testimony, direct messages/advice, temporal correlation between posts and purchases, or other circumstantial proof that followers acted because of the posts. The court treated the indictment’s allegation that followers were “induced to purchase” as sufficient at the pleading stage.
- Evidence that the defendants obtained money or property as an object/result of the scheme: trading records showing defendants’ sales, proceeds/profits (the opinion notes the indictment alleges $114 million in profits), and timing of sales relative to the alleged pumping.
- Circumstantial market evidence (volume spikes, price movements after posts) can be used to show inducement or materiality, but Constantinescu makes clear it is not required as a separate element the way price‑impact can be in some civil doctrines. In short, market effects are evidence, not a necessary legal ingredient.

Bottom line (as the court put it)
Constantinescu reversed the dismissal because the indictment alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme — false postings that “induced” purchases and allowed the defendants to “dump” into inflated prices and realize profits. That theory satisfies § 1348 even though it does not rest on a right‑to‑control theory or require proof of net economic loss. So prosecutors and juries need proof that the defendant used material falsehoods to obtain money/property (i.e., induced transfers and obtained proceeds), not the civil checklist of reliance, price‑impact, and loss‑causation.

### How far can courts 'borrow' mail/wire fraud concepts to interpret § 1348, and are there limits to that approach?
Question:
The panel notes 'scant caselaw' on § 1348 and says courts borrow key concepts from mail and wire fraud. Is there Supreme Court or Fifth Circuit guidance on the propriety and limits of this borrowing? Could relying too heavily on mail/wire fraud doctrines (like Kousisis or Evans) risk importing elements or defenses that don't fit the text or structure of § 1348, which also sits alongside Rule 10b-5? Where have courts drawn boundaries on this analogical approach?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — courts routinely borrow mail/wire-fraud concepts to interpret 18 U.S.C. § 1348, but Constantinescu (5th Cir.) shows that borrowing is not limitless. The Fifth Circuit treats § 1348 as importing the mail/wire framework (scheme + intent) but insists the borrowed doctrines be applied in light of the statute’s text and the Supreme Court’s limits: you cannot base a criminal fraud conviction on mere deprivation of valuable economic information (the “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in Ciminelli), but a fraudulent‑inducement theory that alleges obtaining money or property (e.g., inducing purchases) is permissible (as explained in Kousisis). Constantinescu applies those boundaries to reverse dismissal of a pump‑and‑dump indictment.

What the opinion establishes (doctrine and limits)
- Borrowing is routine but tethered to § 1348’s text. The court begins by noting that “§ 1348 ‘borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,’” and so “courts have given the terms similar treatment” (quoting Greenlaw). Constantinescu therefore analyzes § 1348 under the familiar mail/wire fraud elements: a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” (citing Evans and Greenlaw).

- What those elements mean in § 1348 (as the opinion uses the mail/wire framework):
  - Scheme to defraud: requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived” (quoting Evans as adopted in the opinion).
  - Intent to defraud: requires an intent both “to deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit” (again quoting Evans).

- Supreme Court limits that cabin analogical importation:
  - Ciminelli: the Court held that the federal fraud statutes protect “traditional property interests,” and that the “right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” so a conviction cannot rest solely on a “right‑to‑control” (information‑deprivation) theory. Constantinescu cites Ciminelli to reject that route when it is the only theory alleged.
  - Kousisis: by contrast, the Court confirmed that the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory — where a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property — is not just a repackaged right‑to‑control theory and does protect money/property. Kousisis also clarified that proof of a defendant’s intent to inflict net pecuniary loss on the victim is unnecessary; it suffices that the object of the fraud was to obtain the victim’s money or property.

How Constantinescu applies those limits
- The indictment did not rest on a right‑to‑control theory; it alleged that defendants used false social‑media posts to induce followers to buy securities (i.e., to part with money/property). The Fifth Circuit therefore found the indictment adequate under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, consistent with Kousisis and Ciminelli’s limitation on right‑to‑control claims.
- The court also relied on Kousisis and Baker to reject a defense argument that liability requires the victim to have suffered a net pecuniary loss or that a “mirror‑image” loss requirement exists.

Practical takeaways (what litigants should do and expect)
- Prosecution: when borrowing mail/wire concepts for § 1348, plead facts that show deception plus an objective to obtain money or property (fraudulent inducement)—don’t rely on allegations that only the defendant deprived victims of information or control over discretionary decisions.
- Defense: press whether the indictment actually alleges that victims were induced to part with money/property (and not merely deprived of information); that is the principal line where Ciminelli will defeat an overbroad mail/wire analogical theory.
- Don’t assume doctrinal parity with civil securities doctrines (e.g., Rule 10b‑5): Constantinescu analyzes § 1348 under criminal‑fraud principles and does not purport to resolve every overlap with the civil regime; the opinion is focused on the statutory text and Supreme Court limits discussed above.

Bottom line
Constantinescu endorses borrowing the mail/wire‑fraud framework for § 1348 but enforces the Supreme Court’s limits: mail/wire doctrines are usable only so long as they are applied in a way that remains faithful to § 1348’s text — in particular, the protection of money/property — and do not resurrect the right‑to‑control/information‑deprivation theory that Ciminelli rejected.
