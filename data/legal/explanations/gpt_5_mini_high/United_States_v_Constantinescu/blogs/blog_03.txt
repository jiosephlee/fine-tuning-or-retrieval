Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Finfluencers, Beware: Constantinescu Signals Criminal Exposure for Social‑Media Touting

## Why this matters
The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Constantinescu (Oct. 2, 2025) is a clear signal that traditional pump‑and‑dump facts transposed to social media can sustain criminal securities fraud charges under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The court reversed a district‑court dismissal of an indictment that accuses prominent “finfluencers” of buying thinly traded securities, broadcasting false or misleading posts to induce followers to buy, and then “dumping” their positions for large profits. The decision matters because it explains how modern social‑media hype fits squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement theory of securities fraud — not the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli — and because it confirms that ordinary disclaimers such as “not financial advice” are no safe harbor when deceptive conduct is pleaded.

## The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning in brief
The court framed the governing law against the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis. Ciminelli said the government may not convict under a right‑to‑control theory that bases fraud solely on depriving someone of valuable economic information. The Fifth Circuit accepted that limit but explained that this indictment does not rest on the right‑to‑control theory. Instead, it alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants posted material misrepresentations to trick followers into buying securities, thereby causing followers to part with money or property and permitting the defendants to reap profits (the indictment alleges $114 million in proceeds).

The court relied on Kousisis for two crucial points. First, Kousisis treats the fraudulent‑inducement theory as protective of “money and property” — i.e., it is a proper basis for fraud charges where the defendant induces a victim to transfer money or property through deception. Second, Kousisis rejects the premise that a fraud conviction requires proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off; it is enough that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property by materially false pretenses. Applying those principles, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment adequately alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud: the alleged pattern of buying stock, broadcasting false or misleading statements to induce purchases, and secretly selling for profit plausibly shows the requisite intent and object of obtaining money or property.

Two procedural points are worth underscoring: the court reviewed the sufficiency of the indictment de novo and took the indictment’s factual allegations as true, and its ruling is a reversal of a dismissal at the pleading stage, not an adjudication on the merits.

## Analytical perspective: what the opinion does and does not do
Constantinescu carves a predictable but important path for prosecutions that mirror the old microcap pump‑and‑dump playbook in the age of influencers. It does not resurrect the discredited right‑to‑control theory. Instead, it demonstrates prosecutors can — and will — plead fraud as fraudulent inducement when they allege that posts caused followers to part with money or property. That formulation is a straightforward application of established doctrine, now sharpened by Kousisis.

The opinion also underscores prosecutorial leverage at the indictment stage: patterns of timing, coordination, secret selling, and large profits are classic circumstantial evidence of scienter. That said, the ruling is necessarily limited: an indictment survives pleading‑stage scrutiny; the government still must prove beyond a reasonable doubt at trial that particular statements were materially false, that defendants acted with scienter, and that followers relied in some fashion on the misrepresentations. Defendants retain viable defenses — arguing statements were puffery or opinion, contesting materiality, challenging causation or reliance, and presenting alternative benign explanations for timing and trading — but Constantinescu makes clear these are factual disputes for later resolution.

The decision also reduces a potentially useful defense: the argument that a follower received “something of value” (marketable securities or trading tips) so no fraud occurred. Kousisis, as applied in Constantinescu, says that furnishing something of value does not preclude criminal liability if the government has alleged that the defendant’s object was to obtain the victim’s money or property by deception.

## Doctrinal and enforcement implications
Constantinescu will likely accelerate two complementary trends. First, it will embolden DOJ to bring § 1348 charges in social‑media touting contexts where the facts show inducement of purchases. Second, it will consolidate a doctrinal split between theories that courts will accept (fraudulent inducement) and those they will not (pure right‑to‑control).

This criminal pathway sits alongside existing civil enforcement tools. The SEC long enforces anti‑fraud principles through Rule 10b‑5 and pursues undisclosed paid promotions and misrepresentations in endorsements under other Securities Act and Exchange Act authorities. Constantinescu signals that conduct now attracting civil penalties can also attract criminal culpability if the government can allege (and later prove) the required intent and a scheme to obtain money or property.

The decision also sharpens defense strategy and prosecutor drafting. Prosecutors will frame counts to show victims parted with money or property as a direct result of the misstatements (the fraudulent‑inducement frame), while defense counsel will continue to press materiality, opinion/puffery, lack of scienter, and causation at later stages.

There are ancillary policy considerations, too. The ruling raises concerns about chilling lawful market commentary, but the court’s reasoning stays within the established fraud exception: knowingly false statements intended to induce purchases remain unprotected. Courts will need to continue distinguishing hard facts and falsehoods from permissible opinion and market color.

## Practical compliance takeaways
Creators, funds, and platforms should treat Constantinescu as a wake‑up call, not a surprise.

For creators and influencers: be rigorous about disclosures. Clearly and conspicuously reveal any compensation, free stock, or other conflict of interest before making promotional statements. Do not claim to own, have bought, or have taken positions in a security if you have not; do not falsely assert inside knowledge or guaranteed price moves; avoid representing that you personally profited from specific trades when you did not. “Not financial advice” boilerplate is not a shield when the totality of the conduct and statements shows an intent to deceive. Keep contemporaneous records of trades and sponsorship arrangements and consult securities counsel before engaging in coordinated promotions.

For funds, sponsors, and firms that engage influencers: conduct careful due diligence on partners’ histories and content. Contractual safeguards should require accurate disclosures, pre‑approval rights for promotional materials, representations and warranties about compliance, and indemnities for false or misleading promotions. Treat influencer campaigns as regulated marketing programs — maintain audit trails, and ensure compliance teams review messages that could be treated as inducements to trade.

For platforms: review policies on paid promotions and coordinated market manipulation. Platforms should consider requiring disclosure tags for paid finance content, strengthening notice‑and‑takedown procedures for coordinated pump‑and‑dump activity, and maintaining retention policies that enable preservation of posts and metadata in the face of enforcement requests. Cooperation with regulators and law enforcement when credible manipulation is alleged will be important.

## Bottom line
Constantinescu confirms a basic point: the law that once policed pump‑and‑dump schemes in chat rooms and message boards applies in the Instagram, TikTok, and Twitter era. The Fifth Circuit did not announce a new theory of fraud; it applied the fraudulent‑inducement theory, as clarified by Kousisis, to social‑media touting. Influencers who knowingly mislead followers to induce purchases, fail to disclose material conflicts, or coordinate hype designed to generate personal gains now face a realistic prospect of criminal prosecution under § 1348 in addition to civil enforcement. For anyone operating at the intersection of social media and markets, the safest course is transparency, documentation, and legal compliance — and not relying on a cursorily worded disclaimer to avoid hard questions about intent and truthfulness.
