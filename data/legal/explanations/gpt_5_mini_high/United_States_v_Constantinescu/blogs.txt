Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# After Ciminelli, Fraudulent-Inducement Finds Its Footing in §1348

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is an important post‑Ciminelli calibration of securities‑fraud law for the social‑media age. The Fifth Circuit reversed the dismissal of a major pump‑and‑dump indictment — alleging that influencers used false and misleading posts to induce followers to buy securities and then sold into the artificially inflated market for roughly $114 million in gains — by treating the object of the scheme as money or property, not merely “valuable economic information.” In doing so the panel places §1348 prosecution squarely on the fraudulent‑inducement track the Supreme Court validated in Kousisis, while distinguishing the right‑to‑control line the Court rejected in Ciminelli. For prosecutors and defense counsel alike, Constantinescu signals how courts will sort post‑Ciminelli challenges at the pleading stage: allegations that misrepresentations induced purchases will survive.

## The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning, in brief

The panel started where any indictment sufficiency review must: take the indictment’s allegations as true and test whether they allege the statute’s elements. Under §1348, the government must plead a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud; the Fifth Circuit relied on its own precedents and on the Supreme Court’s recent duo of decisions to frame those elements.

Constantinescu distinguished two theories the Court has discussed. Ciminelli forbade the right‑to‑control theory — the idea that the “property” at stake is a victim’s right to obtain economic information needed to make discretionary decisions — because that theory seeks to protect something the Court views as non‑traditional property. But Kousisis, the Court made clear, left intact the fraudulent‑inducement theory, under which the fraudster “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” Kousisis emphasized that fraudulent inducement “is not a repackaging” of the right‑to‑control theory and that it “protects money and property.”

Applying that framework, the Fifth Circuit held the indictment alleged far more than an informational deprivation. It alleged defendants purchased securities, broadcast false and misleading claims to their followers to induce those followers to buy, and then sold their own positions — classic pump‑and‑dump mechanics. Those allegations, the court said, fit the fraudulent‑inducement model: the followers were induced to part with money (or otherwise transfer property) to acquire securities, and obtaining money was the defendants’ object. The panel also relied on Kousisis to reject the claim that the government must show the defendant sought to leave victims “economically worse off.” Under Kousisis, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to “obtain” the victim’s money or property, even if the fraudster furnishes something of equal nominal value or the victim is not left with a net loss.

Because the indictment specifically alleged deception that induced purchases, and that the defendants profited from the scheme, the Fifth Circuit concluded the pleading satisfied §1348 and reversed the district court’s dismissal.

## What the opinion does — and does not — do

Constantinescu does three things usefully and narrowly. First, it underscores the doctrinal boundary drawn by the Supreme Court: Ciminelli eliminated the right‑to‑control theory but left intact fraudulent inducement; allegations that a defendant caused victims to hand over money or property by misrepresentation remain prosecutable. Second, the decision operationalizes Kousisis in the securities context: in a pump‑and‑dump case the relevant “property” can be the purchase price (or other money/property acquired by the fraudster) rather than abstract informational entitlements. Third, the panel confirms that at the pleading stage courts will accept allegations that misstatements induced purchases as sufficient to allege a scheme to obtain money or property.

What Constantinescu does not do is decide issues that will matter at later stages. It does not resolve whether particular social‑media posts are actionable false statements of fact as opposed to protected opinion or puffery, nor does it make any evidentiary findings about causation, reliance, or scienter beyond accepting the indictment’s allegations for present purposes. Nor does it expand §1348 beyond Kousisis’s terms; the panel explicitly rejects the right‑to‑control theory and rests on the fraudulent‑inducement theory the Supreme Court endorsed.

## Downstream effects and practical takeaways

Practically, Constantinescu should make it harder for defendants in social‑media‑driven securities cases to win early dismissals by recasting alleged misconduct as mere informational withholding. If the government can plausibly plead that social‑media posts were used to induce followers to buy — that is, caused them to part with money or other property — courts following Constantinescu will likely find the indictment adequate under §1348. The Fifth Circuit’s approach tracks the Seventh Circuit in Smith, which applied Kousisis in a similar context, suggesting an emerging consensus among circuits that fraudulent‑inducement theory is the correct vehicle for these prosecutions.

That said, defenses remain. The more fact‑intensive disputes — whether particular statements were materially false, whether followers actually relied on the statements in making purchases, and whether the defendant acted with the requisite intent — will be litigated at summary judgment or trial, not at the indictment stage so long as the complaint pleads the core elements. Defense teams will also press First Amendment and opinion/puffery arguments where appropriate; Constantinescu does not foreclose those arguments, it only makes clear they are not a guaranteed route to dismiss a §1348 indictment that alleges inducement to pay.

Another consequence is prosecutorial strategy: to survive pretrial challenges post‑Ciminelli, prosecutors should allege a clear sequence linking defendant statements, follower purchases, and defendant sales — i.e., the classic pump‑and‑dump timeline. Documenting contemporaneous posts, timestamps, trading records, and follower trading activity will be central to turning an informational narrative into a fraudulent‑inducement pleading.

Finally, the decision underscores a policy tension Kousisis already highlighted: federal fraud statutes punish schemes to obtain money or property, not only schemes that cause victims net pecuniary loss. That is a statutory choice the Supreme Court has accepted; Constantinescu applies it to the securities‑fraud context and to influencer‑driven market manipulation in particular.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu is a clear and predictable application of Kousisis after Ciminelli. It draws the doctrinal line the Supreme Court invited: prosecutors cannot rely on a right‑to‑control/informational‑deprivation theory, but they can proceed under a fraudulent‑inducement theory when the complaint plausibly alleges that defendants used misrepresentations to induce investors to hand over money or property. For enforcement of social‑media pump‑and‑dump schemes, that distinction is decisive — and for defense counsel, it means that the most fruitful early challenges will not be to the existence of a property interest generally, but to the sufficiency and character of the alleged misrepresentations, the defendant’s scienter, and the causal link between posts and purchases.



# Profit Motive as Fraud Intent: Constantinescu’s Mens Rea Lesson for Pump‑and‑Dumps

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is a quick, clarifying stop on the road the Supreme Court began with Ciminelli and Kousisis. At stake is how criminal fraud law treats the modern “finfluencer” who uses social media to tout a thinly‑traded stock, drives buying interest, and sells into the rise. The Fifth Circuit reversed a dismissal of a high‑profile pump‑and‑dump indictment (the government alleges defendants reaped about $114 million), holding that an intent to profit by inducing followers to purchase securities is a classic fraud mens rea under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. The decision puts prosecutors and defense counsel on clearer footing about what must be pleaded and what remains for trial.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The indictment alleged a familiar pump‑and‑dump formula: defendants bought a security, posted false and misleading statements to inflate demand, induced followers to buy, and then secretly sold their positions for profit. The district court had dismissed, treating the case as an improper attempt to criminalize the deprivation of “valuable economic information” rather than a scheme to obtain money or property. The Fifth Circuit reversed.

Two Supreme Court decisions framed the analysis. Ciminelli rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory—criminal liability cannot rest solely on depriving someone of the right to make informed economic decisions because such information is not a traditional property interest. But Kousisis clarified the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant violates the fraud statutes when she “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether she seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The Fifth Circuit saw Constantinescu as a garden‑variety fraudulent inducement allegation, not a Ciminelli right‑to‑control claim. Because the indictment alleged that defendants used false statements to get followers to hand over money (by buying shares), it adequately pleaded both a scheme and the requisite intent to defraud.

## Mens rea: “money in mind” is enough

The important doctrinal point is simple but consequential: wanting to profit is compatible with wanting to defraud. Kousisis’s oft‑quoted line—that a defendant can have “money in mind”—means that prosecutors need not show the fraudster intended to leave the victim worse off in net terms. Fraud is completed when a defendant induces a victim to part with money or property under materially false pretenses. Constantinescu applies that rule in the social‑media pump‑and‑dump context and rejects a bright‑line defense that victims’ receipt of market‑value shares defeats criminal liability.

That does not trivialize mens rea. The government still must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knowingly made material misrepresentations (or omissions), intended to deceive, and sought to obtain money or property by that deceit. What Constantinescu does is prevent defendants from turning the “they got something in return” fact into a categorical escape hatch.

## What will matter at trial

The indictment survived, but proof is a different question. At trial, the government will want to show concrete indicia of a fraudulent inducement and of scienter. Timing will be central: trades showing defendants bought before the promotional rush and sold during or after it; time‑stamped social‑media posts that tout positions or prospects shortly before volume spikes; account records showing realized gains contemporaneous with the “pump.” Specificity of statements will matter too—assertions that they held large positions, that they had “insider” knowledge, or that a stock will “explode” are stronger evidence of actionable misrepresentation if presented as fact rather than opinion. Communications among promoters, deletion of posts after sales, coordination with third‑party accounts, and records demonstrating concealment of true positions will all help a jury infer intent to deceive and to obtain money.

Equally important will be evidence linking follower conduct to the posts: spikes in buy orders/settings, follower messages, or testimony from investors who relied on the posts. While the criminal law does not demand a showing of net loss, it does demand that the deceit induced the deprivation (the handing over of money or property).

## Limits on disclaimers and “market risk” defenses

Constantinescu also signals limits to common defensive arguments. Boilerplate disclaimers—“not investment advice,” “do your own research,” or disclosures that markets are risky—will not immunize a promoter who makes concrete, false factual claims (for example, falsely claiming to own a large stake, to have secured a deal, or to have bought at a particular price). Generalized warnings about volatility do not erase an affirmative misrepresentation that induces purchases. That said, if a statement is genuinely non‑actionable opinion, puffery, or a prediction, a defense based on non‑materiality or lack of false statement remains viable. The line between protected opinion and actionable false factual claim will be a live and fact‑intensive question at trial.

## Doctrinal implications and what to watch next

Constantinescu does not rewrite fraud law, but it narrows the leverage of a defense that sought to turn Ciminelli into a broad safe harbor for inaccurate or deceptive online market talk. For prosecutors, the message is clear: plead a fraudulent‑inducement theory and allege that victims were induced to part with money or property. For defendants, the case underscores the importance of developing factual defenses—demonstrating truth, lack of causation, absence of specific misstatements, or lack of deceptive intent.

Practically, expect renewed criminal and civil scrutiny of social‑media market promotions. The SEC and DOJ can now point to Fifth Circuit law when bringing actions against finfluencers. But prosecutors still face the usual hurdles at trial—proof of scienter, materiality, and causal inducement—so indictments survive, but convictions are not automatic.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu reaffirms a commonsense principle: wanting to profit by tricking others into handing over money squares with the fraud statutes’ mens rea. In the age of transactional, platform‑driven market chatter, that principle gives prosecutors a workable pathway to pursue pump‑and‑dump schemes, while leaving intact ordinary defenses that no material false statement was made or that alleged conduct was non‑deceptive commentary.



# Finfluencers, Beware: Constantinescu Signals Criminal Exposure for Social‑Media Touting

## Why this matters
The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Constantinescu (Oct. 2, 2025) is a clear signal that traditional pump‑and‑dump facts transposed to social media can sustain criminal securities fraud charges under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The court reversed a district‑court dismissal of an indictment that accuses prominent “finfluencers” of buying thinly traded securities, broadcasting false or misleading posts to induce followers to buy, and then “dumping” their positions for large profits. The decision matters because it explains how modern social‑media hype fits squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement theory of securities fraud — not the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli — and because it confirms that ordinary disclaimers such as “not financial advice” are no safe harbor when deceptive conduct is pleaded.

## The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning in brief
The court framed the governing law against the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis. Ciminelli said the government may not convict under a right‑to‑control theory that bases fraud solely on depriving someone of valuable economic information. The Fifth Circuit accepted that limit but explained that this indictment does not rest on the right‑to‑control theory. Instead, it alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants posted material misrepresentations to trick followers into buying securities, thereby causing followers to part with money or property and permitting the defendants to reap profits (the indictment alleges $114 million in proceeds).

The court relied on Kousisis for two crucial points. First, Kousisis treats the fraudulent‑inducement theory as protective of “money and property” — i.e., it is a proper basis for fraud charges where the defendant induces a victim to transfer money or property through deception. Second, Kousisis rejects the premise that a fraud conviction requires proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off; it is enough that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property by materially false pretenses. Applying those principles, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment adequately alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud: the alleged pattern of buying stock, broadcasting false or misleading statements to induce purchases, and secretly selling for profit plausibly shows the requisite intent and object of obtaining money or property.

Two procedural points are worth underscoring: the court reviewed the sufficiency of the indictment de novo and took the indictment’s factual allegations as true, and its ruling is a reversal of a dismissal at the pleading stage, not an adjudication on the merits.

## Analytical perspective: what the opinion does and does not do
Constantinescu carves a predictable but important path for prosecutions that mirror the old microcap pump‑and‑dump playbook in the age of influencers. It does not resurrect the discredited right‑to‑control theory. Instead, it demonstrates prosecutors can — and will — plead fraud as fraudulent inducement when they allege that posts caused followers to part with money or property. That formulation is a straightforward application of established doctrine, now sharpened by Kousisis.

The opinion also underscores prosecutorial leverage at the indictment stage: patterns of timing, coordination, secret selling, and large profits are classic circumstantial evidence of scienter. That said, the ruling is necessarily limited: an indictment survives pleading‑stage scrutiny; the government still must prove beyond a reasonable doubt at trial that particular statements were materially false, that defendants acted with scienter, and that followers relied in some fashion on the misrepresentations. Defendants retain viable defenses — arguing statements were puffery or opinion, contesting materiality, challenging causation or reliance, and presenting alternative benign explanations for timing and trading — but Constantinescu makes clear these are factual disputes for later resolution.

The decision also reduces a potentially useful defense: the argument that a follower received “something of value” (marketable securities or trading tips) so no fraud occurred. Kousisis, as applied in Constantinescu, says that furnishing something of value does not preclude criminal liability if the government has alleged that the defendant’s object was to obtain the victim’s money or property by deception.

## Doctrinal and enforcement implications
Constantinescu will likely accelerate two complementary trends. First, it will embolden DOJ to bring § 1348 charges in social‑media touting contexts where the facts show inducement of purchases. Second, it will consolidate a doctrinal split between theories that courts will accept (fraudulent inducement) and those they will not (pure right‑to‑control).

This criminal pathway sits alongside existing civil enforcement tools. The SEC long enforces anti‑fraud principles through Rule 10b‑5 and pursues undisclosed paid promotions and misrepresentations in endorsements under other Securities Act and Exchange Act authorities. Constantinescu signals that conduct now attracting civil penalties can also attract criminal culpability if the government can allege (and later prove) the required intent and a scheme to obtain money or property.

The decision also sharpens defense strategy and prosecutor drafting. Prosecutors will frame counts to show victims parted with money or property as a direct result of the misstatements (the fraudulent‑inducement frame), while defense counsel will continue to press materiality, opinion/puffery, lack of scienter, and causation at later stages.

There are ancillary policy considerations, too. The ruling raises concerns about chilling lawful market commentary, but the court’s reasoning stays within the established fraud exception: knowingly false statements intended to induce purchases remain unprotected. Courts will need to continue distinguishing hard facts and falsehoods from permissible opinion and market color.

## Practical compliance takeaways
Creators, funds, and platforms should treat Constantinescu as a wake‑up call, not a surprise.

For creators and influencers: be rigorous about disclosures. Clearly and conspicuously reveal any compensation, free stock, or other conflict of interest before making promotional statements. Do not claim to own, have bought, or have taken positions in a security if you have not; do not falsely assert inside knowledge or guaranteed price moves; avoid representing that you personally profited from specific trades when you did not. “Not financial advice” boilerplate is not a shield when the totality of the conduct and statements shows an intent to deceive. Keep contemporaneous records of trades and sponsorship arrangements and consult securities counsel before engaging in coordinated promotions.

For funds, sponsors, and firms that engage influencers: conduct careful due diligence on partners’ histories and content. Contractual safeguards should require accurate disclosures, pre‑approval rights for promotional materials, representations and warranties about compliance, and indemnities for false or misleading promotions. Treat influencer campaigns as regulated marketing programs — maintain audit trails, and ensure compliance teams review messages that could be treated as inducements to trade.

For platforms: review policies on paid promotions and coordinated market manipulation. Platforms should consider requiring disclosure tags for paid finance content, strengthening notice‑and‑takedown procedures for coordinated pump‑and‑dump activity, and maintaining retention policies that enable preservation of posts and metadata in the face of enforcement requests. Cooperation with regulators and law enforcement when credible manipulation is alleged will be important.

## Bottom line
Constantinescu confirms a basic point: the law that once policed pump‑and‑dump schemes in chat rooms and message boards applies in the Instagram, TikTok, and Twitter era. The Fifth Circuit did not announce a new theory of fraud; it applied the fraudulent‑inducement theory, as clarified by Kousisis, to social‑media touting. Influencers who knowingly mislead followers to induce purchases, fail to disclose material conflicts, or coordinate hype designed to generate personal gains now face a realistic prospect of criminal prosecution under § 1348 in addition to civil enforcement. For anyone operating at the intersection of social media and markets, the safest course is transparency, documentation, and legal compliance — and not relying on a cursorily worded disclaimer to avoid hard questions about intent and truthfulness.



# Charging Choices and Pleading Standards: Why §1348 Indictments Will Survive Motions to Dismiss

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu (5th Cir. Oct. 2, 2025) is a short but important roadmap for prosecutors and defense lawyers wrestling with pump‑and‑dump cases tied to social media. The Fifth Circuit reversed a district‑court dismissal of a §1348 indictment, holding that an indictment that (1) alleges a deceptive social‑media campaign and (2) alleges that the deception was used to induce followers to buy securities (thereby allowing the defendants to sell at a profit) pleads both a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite intent. In practical terms, Constantinescu signals that properly pleaded §1348 counts alleging fraudulent inducement to purchase securities are unlikely to be dismissed at the indictment stage simply because the defendants contend the real “take” was information rather than a traditional property interest.

That matters because prosecutors are increasingly using §1348 to target influencer‑driven market manipulation. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion explains in concise doctrinal terms how Ciminelli’s limit on the so‑called “right‑to‑control” theory of fraud does not foreclose fraud prosecutions that instead allege fraudulent inducement to part with money or property.

## The court’s reasoning in a nutshell

The government’s superseding indictment alleged that the defendants—social‑media personalities with large followings—bought securities, posted false or misleading claims to induce followers to purchase, and then sold into the resulting price rise, netting roughly $114 million. The district court dismissed, reasoning that the indictment only alleged a scheme to deprive followers of “valuable economic information” (a Ciminelli problem) rather than a traditional property interest.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It walked through §1348 by importing familiar mail/wire‑fraud concepts (as Greenlaw instructs), reminding that a securities‑fraud charge requires a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” The court distinguished two theories that frequently arise in fraud prosecutions: the Supreme Court’s rejection in Ciminelli of the right‑to‑control theory (which treats the victim’s “right to valuable economic information” as the relevant property) and the fraudulent‑inducement theory (which treats the property at stake as money or securities that the victim is tricked into handing over). Relying on Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment here alleged the latter: the defendants used material misstatements to induce purchases, and obtaining the victims’ money or property was an object of the fraud. Because an indictment need only allege the elements and plead facts plausibly showing the offense, the court held dismissal was improper.

Two points the court emphasized are worth flagging: (1) Ciminelli bars right‑to‑control prosecutions, but it does not undermine prosecutions premised on fraudulent inducement to buy or sell securities; and (2) Kousisis clarifies that fraudulent inducement does not require the victim to be made economically worse off—the government need only allege that the fraudster sought to obtain money or property by deception.

## Key doctrinal takeaways

Constantinescu is a primer on indictment sufficiency under §1348. First, at the pleading stage courts will treat allegations of deceptive conduct tied to induced purchases as enough to state a fraud charge. That follows from the basic indictment standard: take the allegations as true and ask whether the statutory elements are alleged. Second, the decision cements the distinction—drawn from Ciminelli and Kousisis—between (i) efforts to control another’s information environment (the right‑to‑control theory) and (ii) affirmative inducement to part with money or property (fraudulent inducement). The latter remains a viable theory for §1348 prosecutions. Third, the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on mail/wire fraud jurisprudence (via Greenlaw) confirms that courts will continue to look to that body of law for guidance in construing §1348.

One doctrinal ripple to watch: because Kousisis permits liability even when the fraudster provides something of value in return, prosecutors need not plead that victims suffered net pecuniary loss. That lowers one potential barrier to charging schemes in which victims end up holding securities that may, at face value, have value, but were purchased because of deceptive conduct.

## Prosecutorial strategy going forward

For prosecutors, Constantinescu is effectively an instruction manual for surviving pretrial dismissal motions in influencer‑driven pump‑and‑dump cases. The safest charging path is to allege the elements clearly and tie deceptive statements or omissions to concrete transactions: specify the false representations, identify the platforms and timing, allege that followers bought securities in reliance (or were induced to do so), and allege the defendant’s secret sales and profits. Framing the case as fraudulent inducement—alleging that the object of the fraud was to obtain money or securities—keeps the indictment on the right side of Ciminelli.

Because §1348 is phrased in terms borrowed from mail/wire fraud, prosecutors can and will continue to rely on that body of law for materiality, intent, and causation questions. But Constantinescu also shows that prosecutors should be prepared to resist defense attempts to recast the indictment as a right‑to‑control theory at the dismissal stage.

## What defense counsel can (and should) press before trial

Constantinescu does not leave defense counsel without tools. The decision addresses only indictment sufficiency, not the merits; many important disputes remain ripe for pretrial and trial‑time litigation.

First, particularity and notice: defendants should press Rule 7(c) and seek bills of particulars where indictments rely on voluminous social‑media activity. An indictment that alleges “false and misleading posts” without identifying who said what, when, and how followers responded invites a motion for particulars to identify the core allegations the government intends to prove.

Second, materiality and falsity: while these are often jury questions, a defendant can move to dismiss or obtain early rulings where the alleged statements are pure opinion, puffery, or immaterial predictions. Courts remain attentive to categorical defenses that certain kinds of speech—non‑factual opinion—are not actionable as fraud.

Third, scienter: an indictment must allege sufficient facts to support an inference of intent to deceive. Bare recitations that defendants “knew” or “intended” may survive an indictment motion, but defense counsel can push discovery and seek early resolution where the government’s theory depends on conduct inconsistent with the alleged fraudulent intent.

Fourth, causation and reliance: where the government alleges followers bought securities, defense counsel should press the government to show that the purchases were induced by the challenged posts and not by other market information. That fact‑intensive inquiry is normally for the jury, but it can be narrowed pretrial through motions and targeted discovery.

Finally, constitutional and statutory defenses—First Amendment protection for certain speech, selective or vindictive prosecution claims, and challenges under Title 15 if concurrent civil enforcement is implicated—remain available and can be litigated at the appropriate time.

## Broader implications and conclusion

Constantinescu will encourage prosecutors to press §1348 charges in social‑media pump‑and‑dump cases and will make early dismissals based on a Ciminelli right‑to‑control theory harder to obtain when the indictment alleges a classic fraudulent inducement to buy securities. At the same time, the decision leaves intact meaningful litigation terrain for defense counsel—particularity, falsity/materiality, scienter, causation, and constitutional defenses remain for pretrial and trial adjudication.

The practical lesson is straightforward: charge clearly, and defend precisely. For prosecutors, spell out the misrepresentations and the money‑object of the scheme. For defenders, force the government to do the factual heavy lifting before trial and remind courts that many of the most consequential questions—was the statement false and material, did the defendant act with scienter, and were purchases actually induced—are fact questions for the jury. Constantinescu tells us where the pleading battle will be won or lost; the evidentiary and constitutional fights that follow will decide the rest.
