Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Do reliance, price impact, or loss causation play any role in a criminal § 1348 case compared to Rule 10b-5?
Question:
In civil 10b-5, plaintiffs must prove reliance, loss causation, and economic loss. This opinion suggests actual loss isn't required for § 1348 and focuses on scheme and intent to defraud. In a criminal § 1348 prosecution like this one, do the government and jury need to find investor reliance or price impact? If not, what evidentiary showings typically substitute for those civil elements to establish materiality and causation in the criminal context?
Answer:
Short answer
No — the government in an 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (criminal securities fraud) case does not have to prove the civil Rule 10b‑5 elements of reliance, market/price‑impact, or loss causation as separate, stand‑alone elements. Instead, as the Fifth Circuit explained in United States v. Constantinescu, the government must prove a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud — i.e., a material misrepresentation used to obtain money or property and an intent to deceive and obtain that value. Allegations (and proof) that the defendants “fraudulently induce[d]” investors to buy and that the defendants obtained money or profits from those transactions satisfy the criminal elements even if victims suffer no net pecuniary loss. (Constantinescu, Oct. 2, 2025, discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis.)

Why — the doctrinal frame from Constantinescu
- Elements the court insisted on: securities fraud under § 1348 requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” The court described the scheme element as requiring a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money” and the intent element as requiring “an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.” (Constantinescu § III (quoting Greenlaw/Evans).)
- Ciminelli vs. fraudulent‑inducement: Constantinescu reiterates that Ciminelli forbids prosecutions based solely on deprivation of valuable economic information (the “right‑to‑control” theory), but it distinguishes that from the fraudulent‑inducement theory — which is precisely what the indictment here alleged: defendants used material falsehoods to induce purchases (i.e., to make followers part with money/property). The court held that fraudulent inducement “protects money and property” and is a lawful basis for a § 1348 prosecution. (Constantinescu § III.A.)
- No requirement of net economic loss: consistent with Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit made clear that the government need not show that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss; a fraud is complete when the defendant induces the victim to give up money or property under materially false pretenses, even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return. (Constantinescu § III.B; quoting Kousisis.)

What replaces civil “reliance / price impact / loss causation” at trial
Think of the criminal proof as focused on (A) materiality and (B) the causal link between the misrepresentation and the defendant’s obtaining money/property (fraudulent inducement), proven beyond a reasonable doubt. Typical showings the government will (and in Constantinescu alleged it did) use to prove those points are:

- Allegations/evidence that the defendant made material false or misleading statements (content of social‑media posts, misstatements of holdings/intentions). (See indictment language quoted in Constantinescu ¶¶13–14.)
- Evidence that those statements were intended to and did induce others to buy (direct inducement): follower testimony, direct messages/advice, temporal correlation between posts and purchases, or other circumstantial proof that followers acted because of the posts. The court treated the indictment’s allegation that followers were “induced to purchase” as sufficient at the pleading stage.
- Evidence that the defendants obtained money or property as an object/result of the scheme: trading records showing defendants’ sales, proceeds/profits (the opinion notes the indictment alleges $114 million in profits), and timing of sales relative to the alleged pumping.
- Circumstantial market evidence (volume spikes, price movements after posts) can be used to show inducement or materiality, but Constantinescu makes clear it is not required as a separate element the way price‑impact can be in some civil doctrines. In short, market effects are evidence, not a necessary legal ingredient.

Bottom line (as the court put it)
Constantinescu reversed the dismissal because the indictment alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme — false postings that “induced” purchases and allowed the defendants to “dump” into inflated prices and realize profits. That theory satisfies § 1348 even though it does not rest on a right‑to‑control theory or require proof of net economic loss. So prosecutors and juries need proof that the defendant used material falsehoods to obtain money/property (i.e., induced transfers and obtained proceeds), not the civil checklist of reliance, price‑impact, and loss‑causation.
