Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### After Kousisis, does 'intent to defraud' require an intent to cause loss, or is intent to obtain money enough?
Question:
The defendants argued they only intended to profit, not harm anyone, but the panel says that's a distinction without a difference under Kousisis and cites that actual loss isn't required. Under Fifth Circuit law (Evans) and Kousisis, what exactly satisfies the 'intent to defraud' element? If a defendant believes the counterparty will receive equivalent value (e.g., stock at then-market price) or might even profit later, can 'intent to defraud' still be shown solely by intent to deceive and to obtain money via that deceit?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Under Fifth Circuit law as applied in Constantinescu (following Evans and the Supreme Court in Kousisis), “intent to defraud” is satisfied by an intent to deceive combined with an intent to obtain the victim’s money or property by that deceit. The government does not have to prove the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off or to cause a net pecuniary loss.

Why — the controlling test (Evans + Kousisis, as applied in Constantinescu)
- Evans (Fifth Circuit) frames the two relevant pieces: (1) a “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value,” and (2) “intent to defraud” requires an intent (1) to deceive and (2) to cause some harm to result from the deceit. Constantinescu, quoting Evans, repeats both prongs.  
- Kousisis (Supreme Court) then clarified the scope of the “harm” requirement under the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a fraudster violates the statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The Court explained that fraudulent inducement protects money/property and that a fraud is complete when a defendant induces a deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses even if the victim receives something of equal value or does not suffer a net loss. Constantinescu applies that holding to a pump‑and‑dump indictment.

How that applies to your hypotheticals
- If the proof shows the defendant knowingly lied (intent to deceive) and intended that those lies would induce the counterparty to hand over money or property (intent to obtain money/property as an object of the scheme), that satisfies intent to defraud after Kousisis — even if the counterparty actually received market‑price stock or later profited. Constantinescu explicitly rejects the defendants’ “profit, not harm” argument as “a distinction without a difference.”  
- What remains essential is the deception plus the objective to obtain money/property. If there is no intent to deceive (e.g., the speaker honestly believed the statements), then the intent‑to‑defraud element is not met.

Bottom line
Under Evans as shaped by Kousisis and applied in Constantinescu, intent to obtain money/property by deception is enough to satisfy “intent to defraud”; an intent to cause a victim’s net pecuniary loss is not required.
