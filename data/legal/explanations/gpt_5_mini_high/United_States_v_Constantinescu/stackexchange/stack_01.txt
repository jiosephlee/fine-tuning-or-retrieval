Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What can a court consider on a Rule 12 motion to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit says it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo and must take the allegations as true, citing Rafoi and Fontenot. The district court dismissed for not alleging deprivation of a traditional property interest. I'm confused about the scope of what the district court could look at. On a Rule 12(b)(3)(B)(v) motion, can the court consider evidence that investors received market-value securities or that the posts were opinions/puffery, or is it strictly confined to the four corners of the indictment? When (if ever) can a court resolve factual disputes pretrial in this posture?
Answer:
Short answer: On a Rule 12(b)(3)(B)(v) motion to dismiss for failure to state an offense the court asks whether the indictment’s allegations, taken as true, state each element of the charged crime. It should not weigh extrinsic evidence or try to prove (or disprove) contested facts at that stage. Only where the charging papers themselves, when accepted as true, fail as a matter of law may the court dispose of the case on the pleadings.

Why (and where the Constantinescu opinion says so)
- The Fifth Circuit in United States v. Constantinescu reiterates the governing standard: “We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo” and “[t]he court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.” (quoting Rafoi and Fontenot). An indictment must allege “each material element of the offense.” (Gaytan; Guzman‑Ocampo quoted in Constantinescu).
- The panel applied those rules to the securities‑fraud counts: the question is whether the indictment, as pleaded, alleges a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” (and thus a fraudulent‑inducement to obtain money or property). The court reversed dismissal because the indictment alleged that defendants induced followers to buy securities by false or misleading posts — i.e., alleged fraudulent inducement to obtain money/property (and Constantinescu relied on the Supreme Court’s Kousisis and Ciminelli framework to explain why that theory is legally sufficient).

Applying that to your specific examples
- Market‑value securities. The Fifth Circuit expressly cautioned that “the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm” and that “receipt of market‑value securities does not obviate the fraud charges” (relying on Kousisis). More importantly for Rule 12, the district court should not dismiss by accepting extrinsic proof that investors actually got fair value. The proper inquiry at the 12(b) stage is whether the indictment alleges fraudulent inducement to obtain money/property; if it does (as the indictment did here), that is sufficient.
- Opinion/puffery. If the indictment itself, on its face, pleads only nonactionable puffery or merely opinion (i.e., it fails to allege a material false representation), then the indictment might fail to state an offense and dismissal could be appropriate as a legal matter. But the court cannot, at the Rule 12 stage, take extrinsic evidence to recharacterize allegations that the indictment itself asserts were false and misleading. In short: the court decides whether the pleaded facts (accepted as true) amount to a legally cognizable misrepresentation; it does not resolve credibility or factual disputes about what statements really meant or what evidence would show.

When (if ever) a court may resolve factual disputes pretrial in this posture
- Constantinescu makes clear that factual disputes that require weighing evidence are for later proceedings, not a Rule 12 dismissal. A court may resolve a pure legal question that can be decided on the face of the indictment (for example, whether the pleaded theory is foreclosed as a matter of law under Ciminelli), but it may not grant dismissal by relying on contested extrinsic facts that contradict the indictment’s allegations.

Bottom line
- On a Rule 12(b)(3)(B)(v) motion a court is largely confined to the four corners of the indictment and must accept its allegations as true. Evidence that investors received market‑value securities or that the posts were actually opinion only defeats the indictment if the indictment itself, as pleaded, fails to allege the necessary elements; factual disputes about those points belong to trial (or a later motion) rather than to a motion to dismiss. Constantinescu reversed the district court for failing to follow that framework.
