Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How exactly does the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory differ from the rejected 'right-to-control' theory, and why does it fit a pump-and-dump?
Question:
The panel says Ciminelli bars convictions based solely on depriving victims of 'valuable economic information,' but Kousisis approves the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory where a lie tricks the victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property. In this pump-and-dump, the defendants allegedly misrepresented positions/price targets, followers bought, and defendants sold. Is the object here truly to 'obtain the victim's money' even though the victims got stock in return? Does it matter if the defendants didn't sell directly to their followers but into the market generally? How does that distinction play into the fraudulent-inducement framing?
Answer:
Short answer
- The right-to-control theory (Ciminelli) fails because it treats a victim’s loss as the deprivation of “valuable economic information,” which the Supreme Court held is not a protected, traditional property interest. United States v. Ciminelli, 598 U.S. 306 (2023).
- The fraudulent‑inducement theory (Kousisis) is different: it targets a lie that causes a victim to enter a transaction that requires handing over money or property. Kousisis makes clear this is not a repackaged right‑to‑control claim and that protecting money/property is what the fraud statutes are about. Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025).
- The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu applied Kousisis: a pump‑and‑dump fits fraudulent inducement because the indictment alleges the defendants’ misrepresentations induced followers to buy (i.e., hand over money) and the defendants then sold at the inflated price to obtain proceeds.

Why the theories differ (doctrinally)
- Ciminelli: federal fraud statutes protect “traditional property interests,” not a bare “right to valuable economic information.” So a prosecution cannot rest solely on alleging that a defendant deprived a victim of information necessary to make economic decisions. (Constantinescu, discussing Ciminelli.)
- Kousisis: fraudulent inducement is a theory under which the defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” It therefore targets the transfer of money/property, not the mere withholding of information, and “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory.” (Constantinescu quoting Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1398.)

How that fits a pump‑and‑dump (applying Constantinescu)
- What matters is that the indictment alleges deception that causes followers to purchase securities: “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” (Indictment ¶ 13, quoted in Constantinescu.)
- Those purchases are transfers of money/property by the victims. Under Kousisis, a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced such a deprivation “under materially false pretenses,” even if the victim received stock in return or is not left economically worse off. (Constantinescu citing Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1394 n.5, 1391.)
- The panel therefore treats the pump stage as the fraudulent inducement (inducing victims to hand over money to buy) and the dump stage as the mechanism by which the fraudster obtains money (selling at the inflated price). The indictment’s allegation that defendants “dump” by secretly selling for profit (and alleged $114 million in proceeds) shows the object was to obtain money. (Constantinescu, §§ I & III.A–B.)

Does it matter that victims got stock in return?
- No — not for the fraudulent‑inducement theory as stated in Kousisis and applied in Constantinescu. The Supreme Court and the Fifth Circuit emphasize that liability does not depend on leaving the victim economically worse off; the protected interest is the money/property that the victim was tricked into parting with, even if something of value was provided in return. (See Kousisis language quoted in Constantinescu.)

Does it matter that the defendants didn’t sell directly to their followers?
- No. The key question is whether the misrepresentation induced the followers to purchase (i.e., hand over money or property). Criminal liability under fraudulent inducement does not require the same dollar to pass directly from victim to fraudster or a “mirror image” of loss=gain. The court relied on precedents saying the statutes do not impose a mirror‑image requirement. What the indictment alleges is the followers’ purchases inflated the market price and that the defendants then sold into that market to realize proceeds — a classic pump‑and‑dump chain that satisfies fraudulent inducement. (Constantinescu § III.B; citing Baker and Kousisis language.)

Bottom line
- Right‑to‑control = impermissible theory in Ciminelli because it criminalizes withholding information (no traditional property interest). Fraudulent inducement = permissible in Kousisis and, on these allegations, in Constantinescu, because the defendants are alleged to have lied to cause followers to hand over money (buy stock), and the defendants then obtained money by selling at the inflated price. The indirectness of the defendants’ sales (selling into the market rather than directly to followers) does not defeat the fraudulent‑inducement framing so long as the indictment plausibly alleges the deceit induced the transfers that enabled the defendants to obtain money.
