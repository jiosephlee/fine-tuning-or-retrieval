Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 3: # Scheme to Defraud: Property-Based Fraud vs. the Right-to-Control Theory

## Ciminelli’s holding: the right-to-control theory and its limits

Ciminelli is the starting point for any modern debate about what the federal fraud statutes protect. The Supreme Court there rejected the so‑called right‑to‑control theory, under which the government sought to treat a victim’s “right to valuable economic information” about how to manage assets as a protected property interest. The Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes reach only “traditional property interests,” and that a right to economic information is not one of them; therefore a prosecution that rests solely on depriving someone of that information cannot stand. Read in practice, Ciminelli means that an indictment that alleges only that a defendant withheld or distorted information so that a victim made a different discretionary decision (but did not part with money or property as a result) will not state a federal fraud offense.

## The government’s theory in Constantinescu: fraudulent‑inducement

The government did not pursue a right‑to‑control theory in Constantinescu; instead it framed the alleged conduct as a fraudulent‑inducement scheme. Fraudulent inducement, as the Supreme Court described in Kousisis and as the Fifth Circuit adopts here, is the classic fraud scenario: a defendant uses material misstatements to trick a victim into entering a contract or transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. That formulation focuses on the transfer of money or property as the object of the scheme, not on an informational injury. The distinction matters because fraudulent inducement seeks to protect money and property (a traditional category), whereas the right‑to‑control theory sought to protect a non‑proprietary informational interest.

## Why the indictment targets money or property

Reading the indictment as the court must (all allegations taken as true), the charged scheme is built around induced purchases. The indictment alleges that defendants bought a security, posted false and misleading statements about it on social media to “pump” its price, and thereby induced their followers to purchase the security—i.e., to part with cash (or otherwise transfer property) to acquire the shares—so that defendants could then “dump” their positions for profit. Those allegations describe precisely the transfer‑of‑property core of a fraudulent‑inducement offense: the posts were designed to cause followers to hand over money to buy securities. The indictment’s allegation that defendants realized roughly $114 million in profit reinforces that obtaining money was the object of the scheme.

## Kousisis: fraudulent‑inducement is distinct from right‑to‑control and need not produce a net loss

Kousisis clarifies two doctrinal points that the Fifth Circuit relied on. First, the Court held that the fraudulent‑inducement theory “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory.” That is, an allegation that a defendant procured a transaction by lying—thereby causing the victim to hand over money or property—does not run afoul of Ciminelli simply because the lie also affected the victim’s ability to make an informed decision. Second, Kousisis rejected the proposition that a fraud prosecution requires proof the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. The statute criminalizes schemes to “obtain” money or property; therefore a scheme is actionable when the defendant seeks to obtain the victim’s money or property through deception, even if the defendant also provides something of apparent value or the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. Applied to Constantinescu, Kousisis means that the defendants’ alleged aim to profit (and their alleged success in doing so) suffices to show the requisite object and intent of a fraud, regardless of whether followers ended up with securities or some offsetting value.

## Applying the law to the pleaded facts: misrepresentations, secret dumping, and materiality

To test whether the indictment pleads a property‑based scheme, map the statutory elements to the pleading. Section 1348 requires a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud,” committed “in connection with” a security or the purchase or sale thereof. The indictment alleges material misrepresentations—public posts misrepresenting defendants’ trading positions and promising or predicting price movements—that were intended to deceive followers. Those misrepresentations are alleged to have been deployed specifically to induce purchases of particular securities; that causal link is central. The indictment further alleges concealment of defendants’ sales (the secret “dump”), conduct that corroborates intent to mislead and to obtain the proceeds of the scheme. Materiality is implicated because the alleged statements concerned facts any follower would view as important to a buy decision (who is selling or buying, what price movement is expected). Taken together, the pleadings allege a classic fraudulent‑inducement: deception used to cause victims to transfer money or property in reliance.

## The “in connection with” requirement under § 1348

Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to defraud “in connection with” any security and schemes to obtain money or property “in connection with the purchase or sale of” a security. That language demands a transaction‑related nexus between the deception and securities trading. The indictment satisfies that nexus: the misrepresentations were statements about particular securities and were made for the purpose of inducing purchases of those securities by followers. In short, the alleged fraud targeted securities transactions themselves, not merely some abstract informational advantage, and therefore falls squarely within § 1348’s scope.

## Conclusion: why this is a property‑based fraud, not a right‑to‑control case

The Fifth Circuit reversed because the indictment alleges a fraudulent‑inducement—defendants used material misstatements to cause followers to hand over money to buy securities, and then sold for profit—not merely a deprivation of economic information. Ciminelli bars only prosecutions that rest solely on the right‑to‑control theory; it does not bar property‑based fraud charges like the one pleaded here. Kousisis confirms that a fraud is complete when a defendant induces the transfer of money or property under false pretenses, even if the victim does not sustain a net pecuniary loss. For the student reading the opinion, the key takeaways are to (1) identify which theory the government advances, (2) map the indictment’s factual allegations to the statutory elements (scheme, intent, and the securities nexus), and (3) check whether the charged theory seeks to protect traditional property (as here) or only an informational interest (as in Ciminelli).
