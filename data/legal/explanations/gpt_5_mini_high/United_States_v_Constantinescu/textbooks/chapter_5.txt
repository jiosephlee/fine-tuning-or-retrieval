Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 5: # Holding, Disposition, and Case Significance

## Holding: the indictment adequately alleges both a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud
The Fifth Circuit held that the superseding indictment in United States v. Constantinescu sufficiently pleaded the two central elements of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348: (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” The court framed the scheme element in the familiar terms borrowed from mail-and-wire fraud jurisprudence: the government must allege material misrepresentations made to deceive others in order to obtain something of value (money or property). Here, the indictment did more than assert misleading communications; it alleged a classic fraudulent‑inducement pump‑and‑dump: defendants bought securities, then made false and misleading social‑media posts that induced followers to purchase those securities, and then secretly sold for profit. Those allegations, taken as true at the pleading stage, describe misrepresentations that sought to procure money or property from the followers and thus satisfy the “scheme to defraud” element.

On intent, the Fifth Circuit relied on the Supreme Court’s recent clarification in Kousisis that the federal fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave a victim economically worse off. Rather, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain a victim’s money or property by means of materially false pretenses—so an object to obtain money or property is sufficient for intent even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. Applying that principle, the court found the indictment’s allegations that defendants “pumped” securities through false posts so that followers would buy them—combined with allegations that defendants sold into that demand and realized $114 million—adequately alleged that the defendants intended to obtain money or property by deception.

The court distinguished the indictment from the theory rejected in Ciminelli. Ciminelli disallowed a “right‑to‑control” theory that treated a victim’s right to valuable economic information (i.e., control over discretionary decisions) as a cognizable property interest. The Fifth Circuit explained that Constantinescu does not rest on depriving followers of information alone; it alleges fraudulent inducement that caused followers to part with money. Because the indictment charged obtaining money or property (not merely withholding information), it survived dismissal under the controlling Supreme Court precedent.

## Disposition: reversal of the district court’s dismissal and remand for further proceedings
The court reversed the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and remanded the case for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. Practically, that means the indictment will stand and the prosecution may proceed; evidentiary development and fact‑intensive disputes belong at trial (or in later pretrial litigation), not at the pleading stage. The Fifth Circuit’s ruling vacated the dismissal premised on a deficient characterization of the alleged wrong (as information‑deprivation only) and sent the case back for the district court to consider the case on the merits going forward.

## What survives a motion to dismiss: sufficiency of pleaded elements; evidentiary proof reserved for trial
Constantinescu is a reminder of the limited role a court plays on a motion to dismiss an indictment. The Fifth Circuit performed de novo review, accepted the indictment’s factual allegations as true, and asked only whether those allegations, if true, state each material element of the charged offense. An indictment must plead the elements, give fair notice of the charge, and protect against double jeopardy; it need not prove the government’s case. Thus, detailed factual allegations that tie misrepresentations to induced purchases and to an object of obtaining money or property will generally survive a sufficiency challenge. Conversely, allegations that only assert deprivation of economic information without alleging induced transfers of money or property are vulnerable after Ciminelli. Where the indictment contains particularized facts—dates, platforms, specific misstatements, timing of trades, and alleged profits—the court will typically leave credibility, causation, materiality, and intent to be resolved on the evidentiary record, not at the pleading stage.

## Key takeaway: fraudulent‑inducement is a valid theory under § 1348; Ciminelli bars right‑to‑control but not property‑focused inducement schemes
The doctrinal lesson of Constantinescu is straightforward: Ciminelli’s limitation on treating the right to economic information as a protected property interest remains binding, but it does not foreclose a fraudulent‑inducement prosecution that alleges the defendant used material falsehoods to cause victims to hand over money or property in securities transactions. Kousisis confirms that a fraudulent‑inducement theory targets money or property and can support conviction even when the fraudster provided some return value or the victim suffers no net loss. Put another way, prosecutors must avoid the “right‑to‑control” framing after Ciminelli and instead frame securities fraud cases around allegations that deceit induced transfers of money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of securities.

## Practical implications for social‑media promoters and traders
For influencers, promoters, and traders who use social media, Constantinescu demonstrates that criminal liability under § 1348 can attach to coordinated pump‑and‑dump schemes alleged through social posts. False or materially misleading representations about one’s holdings, trades, intentions, or the prospects of a company—if alleged to be designed to induce followers to buy so that the speaker can sell at an inflated price—fit squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement theory accepted by the Fifth Circuit. The decision therefore signals that social‑media‑driven trading schemes are proper subjects of securities‑fraud indictments when the government can allege that communications were false, material, and aimed at procuring transfers of money or property in securities transactions. Defenses that depend on factual disputes about materiality, intent, or causal effect remain available, but they are matters for trial, not for dismissal where the indictment contains sufficient factual detail.

## Guidance for drafting and challenging indictments post‑Ciminelli and Kousisis
For prosecutors: draft indictments to emphasize the property‑oriented nature of the alleged scheme. Include specific factual detail tying statements to economic transactions: identify the false and misleading statements (with dates and platforms), allege that followers purchased securities in reliance on those statements (or that the statements induced market demand), show the defendant’s trading activity timed to exploit that demand (buying before promotion, selling after), and quantify the defendant’s gains where possible. Explicitly plead that the object of the scheme was to obtain money or property in connection with securities purchases—avoid any framing that relies on a deprivation‑of‑information theory.

For defense counsel: challenge indictments that rely on amorphous assertions about “influence” or that fail to plead any transfer of money or property induced by the alleged misrepresentations. After Constantinescu, the most promising pretrial attacks will be those that expose gaps in the government’s alleged causal chain (no pleaded link between posts and purchases), lack of particularity about the alleged misstatements, or allegations that the communications were non‑actionable puffery or opinion rather than material misstatements of fact. If pleading is thin, seek a bill of particulars to force the government to specify the misstatements, the victims, and the transactions it alleges were induced. Remember, however, that well‑pleaded factual allegations—especially allegations of coordinated buys, timed promotions, secret sales, and quantifiable profits—will usually survive a motion to dismiss and push the dispute into discovery and trial.

Concluding note: Constantinescu aligns § 1348 pleading law with the Supreme Court’s recent guidance—protecting money and property in securities transactions while rejecting theories that attempt to criminalize mere control over information. For practitioners on both sides, the decision highlights the importance of precise factual pleading about who said what, who bought or sold when, and whether the object of the deception was to obtain money or property.
