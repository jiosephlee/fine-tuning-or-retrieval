Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Standards and Substantive Law Governing the Indictment

## Indictment sufficiency on a motion to dismiss: allegations taken as true; must allege each material element; fair notice and double jeopardy functions

When a district court tests an indictment on a motion to dismiss, the inquiry is legal and the standard of review on appeal is de novo. As the Fifth Circuit explained in United States v. Constantinescu, an appellate court must accept the allegations of the indictment as true and determine whether those allegations, if assumed true, state an offense. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023); United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011). That means the court does not resolve evidentiary disputes or weigh credibility at the dismissal stage; it asks whether the pleading, read as a whole, asserts the essential elements of the charged crime.

An indictment serves three core functions: (1) it must enumerate each prima facie element of the offense; (2) it must give fair notice to the defendant of the charges so the defendant can prepare a defense; and (3) it must supply the factual baseline necessary to protect the defendant against future prosecutions for the same offense (the double jeopardy function). United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Put simply, to survive a motion to dismiss the indictment must allege each material element of the crime charged. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000). In securities-fraud prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, that means the indictment must allege the elements the statute requires (discussed below) with enough specificity to inform the defendant of the charged conduct.

## Text and scope of 18 U.S.C. § 1348: schemes to defraud and schemes to obtain money or property in connection with securities transactions

Section 1348 criminalizes fraudulent schemes tied to securities. It reaches two related varieties of misconduct: schemes “to defraud any person in connection with … any security,” and schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of … any security.” The statute therefore captures both fraudulent deception directed at persons dealing in securities and schemes that seek to obtain money or property by fraud in the context of buying or selling securities.

This dual phrasing places emphasis on both the deceptive conduct (a “scheme to defraud”) and the victim’s property interest (the obtaining of money or property). Interpreting § 1348 therefore requires attention to whether the government’s theory rests on deception that was intended to obtain money or property connected to securities transactions — not on intangible harms that the statute does not protect.

## Borrowing from mail and wire fraud law to construe § 1348 (Greenlaw)

Because the Fifth Circuit has relatively few precedents construing § 1348, courts commonly borrow concepts from mail and wire fraud jurisprudence when applying the securities-fraud statute. As the court put it in Constantinescu, § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and courts “have given the terms similar treatment.” United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). That borrowing supplies definitions and analytical tools for the principal elements—most importantly what constitutes a “scheme to defraud” and what is required to show “intent to defraud.” Because Supreme Court and circuit decisions interpreting mail and wire fraud address those questions, they frequently guide § 1348 analysis.

Be attentive when reading opinions in this area: the court will often cite mail/wire fraud cases (e.g., Ciminelli, Kousisis, Evans) to define the operative concepts in a securities-fraud indictment and to resolve which theories of fraud the statute will tolerate.

## Core elements: scheme to defraud and intent to defraud (Evans/Greenlaw framework)

Under the mail/wire-fraud framework that the Fifth Circuit applies to § 1348, the government must allege two core elements: (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th at 339. The “scheme to defraud” element requires a material misrepresentation or omission intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value (for example, money) from the target of the deception. See United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018) (quoted with approval in Constantinescu). In other words, the scheme must involve deceptive conduct that is material — capable of influencing a target’s decisions — and that is calculated to secure some valuable thing.

The “intent to defraud” element is twofold: the defendant must have intended (1) to deceive, and (2) that some harm should result from the deceit. Greenlaw and Evans articulate this as intent to deceive plus intent to cause the harmful consequences that flow from the deception. The harmful consequence that the statute protects against is typically the unauthorized appropriation or obtaining of money or property; therefore, the indictment must allege that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the defendant’s deception. As the Supreme Court recently emphasized, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “engaged in deception” and “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of his fraud. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoted in Constantinescu).

In practice, when reviewing an indictment the court asks whether the allegations, taken as true, show deceptive, material misstatements (or omissions) and that the defendant acted with the requisite intent to deceive and to obtain money or property as part of the scheme.

## Material misrepresentation and the requirement that obtaining money or property be an object of the scheme

The interaction between material misrepresentation and the “object” requirement is central to modern fraud doctrine. Two recent Supreme Court decisions that the Fifth Circuit relied on in Constantinescu — Ciminelli and Kousisis — draw an important distinction that controls whether a particular theory of fraud will survive a pleading challenge.

In Ciminelli the Court rejected the so-called “right-to-control” theory of fraud: it held that depriving a person of the right to make discretionary economic decisions — or of valuable economic information necessary to make those decisions — is not a “traditional property interest” that the federal fraud statutes protect, and therefore a fraud prosecution cannot rest solely on that deprivation. Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 316 (2023). That decision means an indictment that alleges only that a defendant hid or misrepresented information so that another lost the ability to control assets or to receive valuable information will fail if no protected money-or-property object is alleged.

By contrast, the “fraudulent-inducement” theory — relied upon by the government in Constantinescu — alleges that the defendant used a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that required handing over money or property. As the Supreme Court explained in Kousisis, the fraudulent-inducement theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory” and instead “protects money and property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1398. Kousisis further clarified that the fraud statutes do not require that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off; it is sufficient that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property by deceit, even if the victim received something of value in return. Id. at 1391–92, 1394 n.5.

Applied to the Constantinescu indictment, these distinctions mattered. The district court had treated the allegations as if they only alleged deprivation of economic information (a right-to-control theory), which Ciminelli forecloses. The Fifth Circuit disagreed: the indictment here alleged that defendants posted false and misleading statements to induce their followers to purchase securities—i.e., to part with money or property—so that the defendants could sell at an inflated price. Those allegations invoke the fraudulent-inducement theory and therefore properly allege a scheme whose object was to obtain money or property in connection with securities transactions.

## Conspiracy under § 1349 rises or falls with the sufficiency of the underlying § 1348 fraud theory

Conspiracy to commit securities fraud is charged under 18 U.S.C. § 1349. The crime of conspiracy is parasitic on the substantive offense: to charge a conspiracy to commit § 1348 fraud, the indictment must adequately allege the elements of the underlying § 1348 offense that the conspirators agreed to commit. If the substantive fraud theory is deficient (for example, if it rests only on a right-to-control theory that does not allege obtaining money or property), then the conspiracy count cannot stand. Conversely, if the indictment properly pleads a fraudulent-inducement or other valid § 1348 theory that alleges deception plus an object of obtaining money or property, the conspiracy allegation — provided it pleads an agreement and furthering act(s) in the usual fashion — will survive the motion to dismiss. In Constantinescu, the court’s reversal of the district court’s dismissal restored both the substantive § 1348 counts and the § 1349 conspiracy charge because the indictment properly alleged a scheme and intent to defraud by inducing purchases of securities.

Conclusion

Read together, these standards mean that when you evaluate a securities-fraud indictment under § 1348 you should (1) treat the indictment’s factual assertions as true for the sufficiency analysis, (2) confirm that the indictment pleads the mail/wire-fraud–derived elements of a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud,” and (3) ensure the government has alleged that obtaining money or property was an object of the deception rather than merely alleging loss of informational control. Constantinescu applies these principles to hold that allegations of a “pump-and-dump” scheme—false social-media statements that induce followers to buy securities and thereby part with money—adequately plead a § 1348 fraud and support a § 1349 conspiracy charge.
