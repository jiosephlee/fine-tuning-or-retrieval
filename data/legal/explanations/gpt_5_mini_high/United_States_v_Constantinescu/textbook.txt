Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case arises from a federal indictment accusing a group of social-media influencers of running a classic “pump and dump” securities-fraud scheme. The indictment describes a coordinated course of conduct in which individuals with large online followings purported to trade stocks, posted misleading statements to stimulate their audiences to buy particular securities, and then secretly sold those securities at the inflated prices. The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it did not allege deprivation of a traditional property interest but instead alleged only the withholding of valuable economic information; the government appealed, and the Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded.

## Who the defendants are and their social-media based trading personas

The indictment names eight individuals: Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight. According to the charging document, each defendant maintained a substantial social-media following across various platforms and publicly presented himself as a skilled stock trader. The indictment alleges that they frequently posted about their trading activities and used those platforms to influence the investment decisions of their followers.

## Alleged scheme mechanics: buy, misrepresent to followers to induce buying, artificially inflate price, sell secretly for profit

The government’s theory, as pleaded in the superseding indictment, is a textbook pump-and-dump. The indictment alleges that defendants first purchased target securities. They then “pumped” the market for those securities by posting false or misleading statements on social media designed to induce their followers and other retail investors to buy. That increased demand, according to the indictment, artificially inflated the securities’ prices. With the price elevated, the defendants purportedly “dumped” their holdings—selling secretly to realize profits before the price fell back—leaving many followers holding shares purchased at inflated prices.

## Key allegations of deception: false statements about trading positions and price potential

Central to the indictment are allegations that defendants posted misrepresentations about their own trading positions and about a security’s price potential. The charging document alleges these public statements were material misrepresentations—designed to deceive followers about the true value or prospects of the securities and thereby to induce purchases the followers otherwise would not have made.

## Alleged proceeds of the scheme: approximately $114 million

The indictment quantifies the alleged financial gain: it charges that the defendants profited approximately $114 million from the scheme. That figure appears in the indictment as the aggregate proceeds the government attributes to the charged conduct.

## Charges: conspiracy to commit securities fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1349) and securities fraud (18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2); Knight’s guilty plea; others move to dismiss

Based on those allegations, the government charged the defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and with multiple substantive counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and § 2 (the latter addressing aiding and abetting). Daniel Knight pleaded guilty. The remaining defendants moved to dismiss the superseding indictment before trial.

## District court ruling: dismissal for failure to allege deprivation of a traditional property interest (viewing the theory as about economic information)

The district court granted the motion to dismiss. Its central conclusion was that the indictment failed to state an offense because, in the court’s view, it alleged only that the defendants sought to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information—not that they deprived victims of a traditional property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. In short, the court treated the theory as a “right-to-control” or information-deprivation theory and found that such a theory, standing alone, did not satisfy the property-focused requirements of federal fraud law.

## Government’s timely appeal to the Fifth Circuit

The government appealed the dismissal to the Fifth Circuit. On appeal the court undertook de novo review of the sufficiency of the indictment (the standard applicable to dismissals for failure to state an offense). The Fifth Circuit ultimately concluded the indictment properly alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory—that defendants used material misrepresentations to induce followers to part with money or property by buying securities—and reversed the district court’s dismissal, remanding the case for further proceedings.

# Standards and Substantive Law Governing the Indictment

## Indictment sufficiency on a motion to dismiss: allegations taken as true; must allege each material element; fair notice and double jeopardy functions

When a district court tests an indictment on a motion to dismiss, the inquiry is legal and the standard of review on appeal is de novo. As the Fifth Circuit explained in United States v. Constantinescu, an appellate court must accept the allegations of the indictment as true and determine whether those allegations, if assumed true, state an offense. United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023); United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011). That means the court does not resolve evidentiary disputes or weigh credibility at the dismissal stage; it asks whether the pleading, read as a whole, asserts the essential elements of the charged crime.

An indictment serves three core functions: (1) it must enumerate each prima facie element of the offense; (2) it must give fair notice to the defendant of the charges so the defendant can prepare a defense; and (3) it must supply the factual baseline necessary to protect the defendant against future prosecutions for the same offense (the double jeopardy function). United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996). Put simply, to survive a motion to dismiss the indictment must allege each material element of the crime charged. United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000). In securities-fraud prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, that means the indictment must allege the elements the statute requires (discussed below) with enough specificity to inform the defendant of the charged conduct.

## Text and scope of 18 U.S.C. § 1348: schemes to defraud and schemes to obtain money or property in connection with securities transactions

Section 1348 criminalizes fraudulent schemes tied to securities. It reaches two related varieties of misconduct: schemes “to defraud any person in connection with … any security,” and schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of … any security.” The statute therefore captures both fraudulent deception directed at persons dealing in securities and schemes that seek to obtain money or property by fraud in the context of buying or selling securities.

This dual phrasing places emphasis on both the deceptive conduct (a “scheme to defraud”) and the victim’s property interest (the obtaining of money or property). Interpreting § 1348 therefore requires attention to whether the government’s theory rests on deception that was intended to obtain money or property connected to securities transactions — not on intangible harms that the statute does not protect.

## Borrowing from mail and wire fraud law to construe § 1348 (Greenlaw)

Because the Fifth Circuit has relatively few precedents construing § 1348, courts commonly borrow concepts from mail and wire fraud jurisprudence when applying the securities-fraud statute. As the court put it in Constantinescu, § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and courts “have given the terms similar treatment.” United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023). That borrowing supplies definitions and analytical tools for the principal elements—most importantly what constitutes a “scheme to defraud” and what is required to show “intent to defraud.” Because Supreme Court and circuit decisions interpreting mail and wire fraud address those questions, they frequently guide § 1348 analysis.

Be attentive when reading opinions in this area: the court will often cite mail/wire fraud cases (e.g., Ciminelli, Kousisis, Evans) to define the operative concepts in a securities-fraud indictment and to resolve which theories of fraud the statute will tolerate.

## Core elements: scheme to defraud and intent to defraud (Evans/Greenlaw framework)

Under the mail/wire-fraud framework that the Fifth Circuit applies to § 1348, the government must allege two core elements: (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th at 339. The “scheme to defraud” element requires a material misrepresentation or omission intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value (for example, money) from the target of the deception. See United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018) (quoted with approval in Constantinescu). In other words, the scheme must involve deceptive conduct that is material — capable of influencing a target’s decisions — and that is calculated to secure some valuable thing.

The “intent to defraud” element is twofold: the defendant must have intended (1) to deceive, and (2) that some harm should result from the deceit. Greenlaw and Evans articulate this as intent to deceive plus intent to cause the harmful consequences that flow from the deception. The harmful consequence that the statute protects against is typically the unauthorized appropriation or obtaining of money or property; therefore, the indictment must allege that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the defendant’s deception. As the Supreme Court recently emphasized, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “engaged in deception” and “obtaining the victim’s money or property” was “an object” of his fraud. Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoted in Constantinescu).

In practice, when reviewing an indictment the court asks whether the allegations, taken as true, show deceptive, material misstatements (or omissions) and that the defendant acted with the requisite intent to deceive and to obtain money or property as part of the scheme.

## Material misrepresentation and the requirement that obtaining money or property be an object of the scheme

The interaction between material misrepresentation and the “object” requirement is central to modern fraud doctrine. Two recent Supreme Court decisions that the Fifth Circuit relied on in Constantinescu — Ciminelli and Kousisis — draw an important distinction that controls whether a particular theory of fraud will survive a pleading challenge.

In Ciminelli the Court rejected the so-called “right-to-control” theory of fraud: it held that depriving a person of the right to make discretionary economic decisions — or of valuable economic information necessary to make those decisions — is not a “traditional property interest” that the federal fraud statutes protect, and therefore a fraud prosecution cannot rest solely on that deprivation. Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 316 (2023). That decision means an indictment that alleges only that a defendant hid or misrepresented information so that another lost the ability to control assets or to receive valuable information will fail if no protected money-or-property object is alleged.

By contrast, the “fraudulent-inducement” theory — relied upon by the government in Constantinescu — alleges that the defendant used a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that required handing over money or property. As the Supreme Court explained in Kousisis, the fraudulent-inducement theory “is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory” and instead “protects money and property.” Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388, 1398. Kousisis further clarified that the fraud statutes do not require that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off; it is sufficient that the defendant schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property by deceit, even if the victim received something of value in return. Id. at 1391–92, 1394 n.5.

Applied to the Constantinescu indictment, these distinctions mattered. The district court had treated the allegations as if they only alleged deprivation of economic information (a right-to-control theory), which Ciminelli forecloses. The Fifth Circuit disagreed: the indictment here alleged that defendants posted false and misleading statements to induce their followers to purchase securities—i.e., to part with money or property—so that the defendants could sell at an inflated price. Those allegations invoke the fraudulent-inducement theory and therefore properly allege a scheme whose object was to obtain money or property in connection with securities transactions.

## Conspiracy under § 1349 rises or falls with the sufficiency of the underlying § 1348 fraud theory

Conspiracy to commit securities fraud is charged under 18 U.S.C. § 1349. The crime of conspiracy is parasitic on the substantive offense: to charge a conspiracy to commit § 1348 fraud, the indictment must adequately allege the elements of the underlying § 1348 offense that the conspirators agreed to commit. If the substantive fraud theory is deficient (for example, if it rests only on a right-to-control theory that does not allege obtaining money or property), then the conspiracy count cannot stand. Conversely, if the indictment properly pleads a fraudulent-inducement or other valid § 1348 theory that alleges deception plus an object of obtaining money or property, the conspiracy allegation — provided it pleads an agreement and furthering act(s) in the usual fashion — will survive the motion to dismiss. In Constantinescu, the court’s reversal of the district court’s dismissal restored both the substantive § 1348 counts and the § 1349 conspiracy charge because the indictment properly alleged a scheme and intent to defraud by inducing purchases of securities.

Conclusion

Read together, these standards mean that when you evaluate a securities-fraud indictment under § 1348 you should (1) treat the indictment’s factual assertions as true for the sufficiency analysis, (2) confirm that the indictment pleads the mail/wire-fraud–derived elements of a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud,” and (3) ensure the government has alleged that obtaining money or property was an object of the deception rather than merely alleging loss of informational control. Constantinescu applies these principles to hold that allegations of a “pump-and-dump” scheme—false social-media statements that induce followers to buy securities and thereby part with money—adequately plead a § 1348 fraud and support a § 1349 conspiracy charge.

# Scheme to Defraud: Property-Based Fraud vs. the Right-to-Control Theory

## Ciminelli’s holding: the right-to-control theory and its limits

Ciminelli is the starting point for any modern debate about what the federal fraud statutes protect. The Supreme Court there rejected the so‑called right‑to‑control theory, under which the government sought to treat a victim’s “right to valuable economic information” about how to manage assets as a protected property interest. The Court concluded that the federal fraud statutes reach only “traditional property interests,” and that a right to economic information is not one of them; therefore a prosecution that rests solely on depriving someone of that information cannot stand. Read in practice, Ciminelli means that an indictment that alleges only that a defendant withheld or distorted information so that a victim made a different discretionary decision (but did not part with money or property as a result) will not state a federal fraud offense.

## The government’s theory in Constantinescu: fraudulent‑inducement

The government did not pursue a right‑to‑control theory in Constantinescu; instead it framed the alleged conduct as a fraudulent‑inducement scheme. Fraudulent inducement, as the Supreme Court described in Kousisis and as the Fifth Circuit adopts here, is the classic fraud scenario: a defendant uses material misstatements to trick a victim into entering a contract or transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. That formulation focuses on the transfer of money or property as the object of the scheme, not on an informational injury. The distinction matters because fraudulent inducement seeks to protect money and property (a traditional category), whereas the right‑to‑control theory sought to protect a non‑proprietary informational interest.

## Why the indictment targets money or property

Reading the indictment as the court must (all allegations taken as true), the charged scheme is built around induced purchases. The indictment alleges that defendants bought a security, posted false and misleading statements about it on social media to “pump” its price, and thereby induced their followers to purchase the security—i.e., to part with cash (or otherwise transfer property) to acquire the shares—so that defendants could then “dump” their positions for profit. Those allegations describe precisely the transfer‑of‑property core of a fraudulent‑inducement offense: the posts were designed to cause followers to hand over money to buy securities. The indictment’s allegation that defendants realized roughly $114 million in profit reinforces that obtaining money was the object of the scheme.

## Kousisis: fraudulent‑inducement is distinct from right‑to‑control and need not produce a net loss

Kousisis clarifies two doctrinal points that the Fifth Circuit relied on. First, the Court held that the fraudulent‑inducement theory “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory.” That is, an allegation that a defendant procured a transaction by lying—thereby causing the victim to hand over money or property—does not run afoul of Ciminelli simply because the lie also affected the victim’s ability to make an informed decision. Second, Kousisis rejected the proposition that a fraud prosecution requires proof the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. The statute criminalizes schemes to “obtain” money or property; therefore a scheme is actionable when the defendant seeks to obtain the victim’s money or property through deception, even if the defendant also provides something of apparent value or the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. Applied to Constantinescu, Kousisis means that the defendants’ alleged aim to profit (and their alleged success in doing so) suffices to show the requisite object and intent of a fraud, regardless of whether followers ended up with securities or some offsetting value.

## Applying the law to the pleaded facts: misrepresentations, secret dumping, and materiality

To test whether the indictment pleads a property‑based scheme, map the statutory elements to the pleading. Section 1348 requires a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud,” committed “in connection with” a security or the purchase or sale thereof. The indictment alleges material misrepresentations—public posts misrepresenting defendants’ trading positions and promising or predicting price movements—that were intended to deceive followers. Those misrepresentations are alleged to have been deployed specifically to induce purchases of particular securities; that causal link is central. The indictment further alleges concealment of defendants’ sales (the secret “dump”), conduct that corroborates intent to mislead and to obtain the proceeds of the scheme. Materiality is implicated because the alleged statements concerned facts any follower would view as important to a buy decision (who is selling or buying, what price movement is expected). Taken together, the pleadings allege a classic fraudulent‑inducement: deception used to cause victims to transfer money or property in reliance.

## The “in connection with” requirement under § 1348

Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to defraud “in connection with” any security and schemes to obtain money or property “in connection with the purchase or sale of” a security. That language demands a transaction‑related nexus between the deception and securities trading. The indictment satisfies that nexus: the misrepresentations were statements about particular securities and were made for the purpose of inducing purchases of those securities by followers. In short, the alleged fraud targeted securities transactions themselves, not merely some abstract informational advantage, and therefore falls squarely within § 1348’s scope.

## Conclusion: why this is a property‑based fraud, not a right‑to‑control case

The Fifth Circuit reversed because the indictment alleges a fraudulent‑inducement—defendants used material misstatements to cause followers to hand over money to buy securities, and then sold for profit—not merely a deprivation of economic information. Ciminelli bars only prosecutions that rest solely on the right‑to‑control theory; it does not bar property‑based fraud charges like the one pleaded here. Kousisis confirms that a fraud is complete when a defendant induces the transfer of money or property under false pretenses, even if the victim does not sustain a net pecuniary loss. For the student reading the opinion, the key takeaways are to (1) identify which theory the government advances, (2) map the indictment’s factual allegations to the statutory elements (scheme, intent, and the securities nexus), and (3) check whether the charged theory seeks to protect traditional property (as here) or only an informational interest (as in Ciminelli).

# Intent to Defraud: Profit Motive, Harm, and the Impact of Kousisis

This chapter explains how the Fifth Circuit treated the “intent to defraud” element in United States v. Constantinescu, focusing on why a defendant’s profit-seeking intent—together with allegations that he induced transactions by deception—satisfies the mens rea even where the indictment does not allege that victims suffered a net pecuniary loss. The court’s analysis rests on the circuit’s ordinary formulation of intent to defraud, the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications in Kousisis (and its distinction from Ciminelli), and how those rules map onto the indictment’s allegations of a classic “pump-and-dump” scheme.

## Intent to defraud defined: intent to deceive and intent to cause harm from the deceit (Evans/Greenlaw)

Under § 1348 (and by analogy to mail and wire fraud jurisprudence), the element “intent to defraud” is twofold: the defendant must intend (1) to deceive and (2) to cause some harm to result from that deceit. The Fifth Circuit follows this formulation (drawing on Evans and Greenlaw) when assessing securities-fraud charges: a “scheme to defraud” requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value, and the mens rea element requires both the deception and the defendant’s purpose that the deceit bring about a harmful result. When reviewing an indictment, the court takes the indictment’s factual allegations as true and asks whether those allegations, if proved, would allege each material element of the offense.

Keep in mind the operative point for this case: the second prong—an intent to cause harm by the deceit—has not traditionally been interpreted to require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off in net terms. What matters is that the defendant intended that the deceit produce a deprivation of money or property (a legally cognizable harm), not that the victim’s ledger show an ultimate loss.

## Kousisis’s rule: fraud is complete when the defendant schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property through material deception; no need to seek net pecuniary loss

Kousisis clarified the scope of the fraudulent-inducement theory. The Supreme Court held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The Court distinguished this fraudulent-inducement theory from the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli: the latter treated the victim’s “right to valuable economic information” as the property interest, which Ciminelli found not to be a traditional property interest. By contrast, the fraudulent-inducement theory targets transfers of money or property themselves—the paradigmatic kinds of interests the federal fraud statutes protect. Importantly, Kousisis made clear that a fraud can be complete at the point the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return and even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss.

Two consequences follow for intent analysis. First, “intent to cause harm” is satisfied when the defendant intends the deceit to bring about a transfer or deprivation of money or property; it is not defeated by the defendant’s belief that the victim will receive some countervailing economic benefit. Second, a defendant’s objective to obtain money or property is itself probative—and often dispositive—of the mens rea.

## Application: the indictment alleges defendants posted false information to induce purchases—showing they had money in mind and aimed to obtain victims’ funds

The indictment in Constantinescu alleges a conventional fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants bought securities, promoted them on social media by posting false and misleading information, induced followers to purchase and thereby inflate the price, and then “dumped” by secretly selling for a profit. The indictment plainly alleges deception (the posts were false and misleading) and an object to obtain money or property (the secret sale for profit and an alleged $114 million in proceeds). For example, Indictment ¶ 13 describes defendants who “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Those concrete allegations map directly onto the two prongs of intent to defraud: the misrepresentations supply the intent to deceive; the inducement to purchase (and the alleged profits) supply the intent that the deceit bring about a deprivation of money or property.

Framed by Kousisis, the indictment does not need to allege that the followers ultimately suffered a net economic loss. What matters is that the defendants intended to and did induce transactions by deception, thereby obtaining the victims’ money or property as the object of the scheme. The court therefore treated the defendants’ profit motive and the alleged mechanics of the pump‑and‑dump as sufficient to plead the requisite intent.

## Why receipt of market-value securities by followers does not defeat intent or injury under fraudulent-inducement (Kousisis)

A recurring defense in fraud cases is to point out that a purported victim received something—e.g., a security—so there was no “loss” in the sense of giving up value. Kousisis forecloses this as a dispositive answer: receiving an item in exchange does not eliminate a fraud charge where the recipient was induced to transfer money or property by materially false pretenses. The statutory concern is the defendant’s obtaining of money or property through deception, not a strict accounting of whether the recipient’s overall economic position worsened.

Applied here, even if followers ended up with securities that had market value at the moment of purchase, the indictment alleges they were induced to make those purchases by false statements. That inducement—i.e., causing victims to part with money under materially false pretenses—is the injury the fraud statutes protect against. The fact that the victim later might hold market‑value assets or that the fraudster also furnished some return does not defeat the inference that the defendant intended to and did obtain money by deception.

## Fifth Circuit support that loss need not mirror defendant’s gain (Baker) and alignment with other circuits post-Kousisis (Seventh Circuit Smith)

The Fifth Circuit has previously rejected a “mirror‑image” rule requiring the victim’s loss to equal the defendant’s gain. In United States v. Baker the court explained that the fraud statutes do not impose such a matching requirement; a defendant may be liable where his gain supplied the means of the victim’s loss without exact equivalence. Constantinescu follows that logic and applies Kousisis to say that the absence of an alleged net pecuniary loss to followers does not defeat an intent‑to‑defraud theory when the indictment alleges fraudulent inducement to part with money or property.

Other circuits have reached similar results post‑Kousisis. The Seventh Circuit, for example, applied Kousisis in a comparable context and upheld indictments that rested on fraudulent inducement even where the victims received something in return. These decisions show a consensus that fraudulent‑inducement charges survive the argument that no net economic harm was intended or proved.

## Use of the indictment’s language to illustrate specific allegations of deceitful inducement to purchase

When you read the Constantinescu opinion, focus on how the court maps specific indictment language to the statutory elements. Key phrases to notice include the allegation that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (Indictment ¶ 13), the description of the scheme to “pump” and “dump” (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14), and the specific allegation that defendants “dumped” by “secretly selling the security for a profit” and thereby netted roughly $114 million. Each of these allegations draws a straight line to the elements recognized in Evans, Greenlaw, and Kousisis: a material misrepresentation (deception) used to induce a transaction (the transfer of money/property), with the defendant’s objective to obtain money (the mens rea). Under de novo review the court was obliged to accept those allegations as true and determine whether they, if proved, state an offense. The Fifth Circuit concluded they do.

Conclusion: For students, the lesson is practical and narrow. Under Constantinescu, read the indictment for allegations that the defendant both intended to deceive and intended that the deception produce a transfer of money or property. If the indictment alleges deceptive inducement to buy or exchange—paired with an allegation that the defendant stood to obtain money or property—then Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit treat those allegations as sufficient to plead intent to defraud, even without an allegation that the victims suffered a net pecuniary loss.

# Holding, Disposition, and Case Significance

## Holding: the indictment adequately alleges both a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud
The Fifth Circuit held that the superseding indictment in United States v. Constantinescu sufficiently pleaded the two central elements of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348: (1) a “scheme to defraud,” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” The court framed the scheme element in the familiar terms borrowed from mail-and-wire fraud jurisprudence: the government must allege material misrepresentations made to deceive others in order to obtain something of value (money or property). Here, the indictment did more than assert misleading communications; it alleged a classic fraudulent‑inducement pump‑and‑dump: defendants bought securities, then made false and misleading social‑media posts that induced followers to purchase those securities, and then secretly sold for profit. Those allegations, taken as true at the pleading stage, describe misrepresentations that sought to procure money or property from the followers and thus satisfy the “scheme to defraud” element.

On intent, the Fifth Circuit relied on the Supreme Court’s recent clarification in Kousisis that the federal fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave a victim economically worse off. Rather, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain a victim’s money or property by means of materially false pretenses—so an object to obtain money or property is sufficient for intent even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. Applying that principle, the court found the indictment’s allegations that defendants “pumped” securities through false posts so that followers would buy them—combined with allegations that defendants sold into that demand and realized $114 million—adequately alleged that the defendants intended to obtain money or property by deception.

The court distinguished the indictment from the theory rejected in Ciminelli. Ciminelli disallowed a “right‑to‑control” theory that treated a victim’s right to valuable economic information (i.e., control over discretionary decisions) as a cognizable property interest. The Fifth Circuit explained that Constantinescu does not rest on depriving followers of information alone; it alleges fraudulent inducement that caused followers to part with money. Because the indictment charged obtaining money or property (not merely withholding information), it survived dismissal under the controlling Supreme Court precedent.

## Disposition: reversal of the district court’s dismissal and remand for further proceedings
The court reversed the district court’s order dismissing the indictment and remanded the case for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. Practically, that means the indictment will stand and the prosecution may proceed; evidentiary development and fact‑intensive disputes belong at trial (or in later pretrial litigation), not at the pleading stage. The Fifth Circuit’s ruling vacated the dismissal premised on a deficient characterization of the alleged wrong (as information‑deprivation only) and sent the case back for the district court to consider the case on the merits going forward.

## What survives a motion to dismiss: sufficiency of pleaded elements; evidentiary proof reserved for trial
Constantinescu is a reminder of the limited role a court plays on a motion to dismiss an indictment. The Fifth Circuit performed de novo review, accepted the indictment’s factual allegations as true, and asked only whether those allegations, if true, state each material element of the charged offense. An indictment must plead the elements, give fair notice of the charge, and protect against double jeopardy; it need not prove the government’s case. Thus, detailed factual allegations that tie misrepresentations to induced purchases and to an object of obtaining money or property will generally survive a sufficiency challenge. Conversely, allegations that only assert deprivation of economic information without alleging induced transfers of money or property are vulnerable after Ciminelli. Where the indictment contains particularized facts—dates, platforms, specific misstatements, timing of trades, and alleged profits—the court will typically leave credibility, causation, materiality, and intent to be resolved on the evidentiary record, not at the pleading stage.

## Key takeaway: fraudulent‑inducement is a valid theory under § 1348; Ciminelli bars right‑to‑control but not property‑focused inducement schemes
The doctrinal lesson of Constantinescu is straightforward: Ciminelli’s limitation on treating the right to economic information as a protected property interest remains binding, but it does not foreclose a fraudulent‑inducement prosecution that alleges the defendant used material falsehoods to cause victims to hand over money or property in securities transactions. Kousisis confirms that a fraudulent‑inducement theory targets money or property and can support conviction even when the fraudster provided some return value or the victim suffers no net loss. Put another way, prosecutors must avoid the “right‑to‑control” framing after Ciminelli and instead frame securities fraud cases around allegations that deceit induced transfers of money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of securities.

## Practical implications for social‑media promoters and traders
For influencers, promoters, and traders who use social media, Constantinescu demonstrates that criminal liability under § 1348 can attach to coordinated pump‑and‑dump schemes alleged through social posts. False or materially misleading representations about one’s holdings, trades, intentions, or the prospects of a company—if alleged to be designed to induce followers to buy so that the speaker can sell at an inflated price—fit squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement theory accepted by the Fifth Circuit. The decision therefore signals that social‑media‑driven trading schemes are proper subjects of securities‑fraud indictments when the government can allege that communications were false, material, and aimed at procuring transfers of money or property in securities transactions. Defenses that depend on factual disputes about materiality, intent, or causal effect remain available, but they are matters for trial, not for dismissal where the indictment contains sufficient factual detail.

## Guidance for drafting and challenging indictments post‑Ciminelli and Kousisis
For prosecutors: draft indictments to emphasize the property‑oriented nature of the alleged scheme. Include specific factual detail tying statements to economic transactions: identify the false and misleading statements (with dates and platforms), allege that followers purchased securities in reliance on those statements (or that the statements induced market demand), show the defendant’s trading activity timed to exploit that demand (buying before promotion, selling after), and quantify the defendant’s gains where possible. Explicitly plead that the object of the scheme was to obtain money or property in connection with securities purchases—avoid any framing that relies on a deprivation‑of‑information theory.

For defense counsel: challenge indictments that rely on amorphous assertions about “influence” or that fail to plead any transfer of money or property induced by the alleged misrepresentations. After Constantinescu, the most promising pretrial attacks will be those that expose gaps in the government’s alleged causal chain (no pleaded link between posts and purchases), lack of particularity about the alleged misstatements, or allegations that the communications were non‑actionable puffery or opinion rather than material misstatements of fact. If pleading is thin, seek a bill of particulars to force the government to specify the misstatements, the victims, and the transactions it alleges were induced. Remember, however, that well‑pleaded factual allegations—especially allegations of coordinated buys, timed promotions, secret sales, and quantifiable profits—will usually survive a motion to dismiss and push the dispute into discovery and trial.

Concluding note: Constantinescu aligns § 1348 pleading law with the Supreme Court’s recent guidance—protecting money and property in securities transactions while rejecting theories that attempt to criminalize mere control over information. For practitioners on both sides, the decision highlights the importance of precise factual pleading about who said what, who bought or sold when, and whether the object of the deception was to obtain money or property.
