Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Under Minn. Stat. § 181.101, can a private employee ever recover “average daily wage” penalties, or are they exclusive to the Commissioner despite § 181.171’s “other appropriate relief” language?
Question:
The court holds that only the Commissioner may “charge and collect” penalties under § 181.101(a), even though § 181.171, subd. 1 authorizes private suits for violations of § 181.101 and allows “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief,” plus “the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” How do these provisions fit together? If § 181.101 contains an average-daily-wage penalty but assigns enforcement to the Commissioner, does § 181.171 categorically bar private plaintiffs from obtaining that penalty? What relief (beyond unpaid wages) remains available to an employee suing directly under § 181.101 via § 181.171?
Answer:
Short answer
No. As the Eighth Circuit held in Cohen v. Consilio, the “average daily wage” penalty in Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) is enforceable only by the Commissioner; § 181.171’s private‑suit language does not convert that particular penalty into a remedy an employee may obtain in a direct suit.

Explanation (how the statutes fit together)
- Section 181.171 authorizes private civil actions for violations of certain MPWA provisions and says an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated” and for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Cohen, slip op. § II.A (quoting Minn. Stat. § 181.171, subd. 1).
- But § 181.101(a) ties the average‑daily‑wage penalty to the Commissioner’s enforcement process: the Commissioner must first serve a demand for payment and then, if payment is not made within ten days, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Cohen, slip op. § II.A (quoting Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a)).
- The court applied the plain‑language rule and concluded those two provisions must be read together: because § 181.101 assigns the power to “charge and collect” that penalty to the Commissioner, that penalty is not a remedy a private plaintiff may obtain in a direct suit under § 181.171. The opinion puts it succinctly: “Because we conclude that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court did not err.” Cohen, slip op. § II.A.

Why that reading is justified
- The opinion notes that the Legislature knows how to empower an employee to “charge and collect a penalty” when it intends to do so (citing examples in other MPWA provisions, e.g., §§ 181.13 and 181.14). The absence of similar language in § 181.101 supports the conclusion that the penalty there is intended to be enforced by the Commissioner, not by private plaintiffs. Cohen, slip op. § II.A.

What relief a private plaintiff can get under § 181.171 when suing on § 181.101
- An employee may prosecute a claim for unpaid wages (the statute explicitly preserves the employee’s ability to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages”). Cohen, slip op. § II.A.
- More generally, § 181.171 allows “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” and authorizes recovery of civil penalties only if the specific MPWA section at issue provides for a privately recoverable penalty. Cohen makes clear that the § 181.101 average‑daily‑wage penalty is not such a privately recoverable penalty because enforcement is committed to the Commissioner.
- A practical corollary: if the Commissioner enforces § 181.101 and collects a penalty, “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned,” so an employee can still benefit from a penalty recovered through the Commissioner’s enforcement—but not by obtaining that penalty directly in a private suit. Cohen, slip op. § II.A.

Bottom line
Section 181.171 authorizes private suits for MPWA violations and preserves whatever penalties a particular MPWA provision itself makes available to private litigants. Under Cohen, the average‑daily‑wage penalty in § 181.101 is not one of those privately available remedies because § 181.101 assigns its enforcement to the Commissioner. Private plaintiffs suing under § 181.101 can pursue unpaid wages (and whatever compensatory or other relief the statute plainly authorizes), but not the § 181.101 average‑daily‑wage penalty in a direct action.

### What is the statutory basis and Article III redressability for a private plaintiff to seek MFLSA civil penalties that are payable only to the Commissioner?
Question:
The panel notes both sides agree MFLSA statutory penalties are paid to the Commissioner, not to Cohen, yet suggests penalties might still afford redress under Laidlaw if the unlawful policy continues. Which MFLSA provision actually authorizes a private plaintiff to seek imposition of those state-payable penalties? If the money goes to the state, how is redressability satisfied for the private plaintiff’s injury? How do differences between Laidlaw’s federal citizen-suit structure and Minnesota’s MFLSA enforcement scheme affect the standing analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
- The panel did not point to any MFLSA provision in the opinion that gives a private plaintiff the right to recover penalties payable to the Commissioner. Cohen sued under Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8, but the court noted that “the parties agree that any statutory penalties imposed…are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen,” and therefore expressed “a serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue” and remanded. (Opinion, Part II.B.)

Statutory basis (what the opinion actually says)
- Cohen’s MFLSA claims were pleaded under Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8 (the opinion records those citations). The panel did not hold that those provisions authorize a private plaintiff to obtain Commissioner‑payable penalties. Instead, the court treated the parties’ concession (that penalties, unlike wages and liquidated damages, are payable to the Commissioner) as a trigger for an Article III jurisdictional inquiry and remanded for the district court to resolve it. (Opinion, Part II.B.)

Article III redressability (how the panel framed it)
- The panel invoked the Supreme Court’s Laidlaw principle: “Civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may ‘afford redress to citizen plaintiffs…because such penalties may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.”’” (Opinion, quoting Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000)). But the panel also reiterated the black‑letter standing/mootness rules: a plaintiff must establish standing for each claim and form of relief and that standing continue throughout the litigation. (Opinion, citing Town of Chester and Sisney.)
- Applied here, the panel’s rule of decision was: penalties payable to the state could in principle redress a private plaintiff’s injury (via deterrence) under Laidlaw, but only if the plaintiff can show an ongoing or reasonably likely recurrence of the unlawful conduct such that imposition of penalties would likely deter that conduct. The court therefore remanded so the district court can decide whether Consilio’s allegedly violative overtime policy remains (if it remains, the penalties claim “may still be viable”; if the policy has been discontinued and recurrence is not reasonably expected, the claim may be moot). (Opinion, Part II.B.)

How Laidlaw’s citizen‑suit posture differs from the MFLSA scheme — and why it matters
- The opinion’s concurrence flagged this exact point: the panel did not decide whether Laidlaw’s logic transfers cleanly because “there are differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA,” and “neither party briefed the issue.” (Kobes, J., concurring specially.)
- In short (the way the panel framed it): Laidlaw involved (and the Court relied on) a statutory enforcement framework in which citizen plaintiffs brought suit under a federal statute and the deterrent effect of penalties was integral to the citizen‑enforcement mechanism. By contrast, the MFLSA penalties here are acknowledged to be payable to the Commissioner, not the private plaintiff. That structural difference can weaken the causal link required by Article III between the requested relief (state‑payable penalties) and the private plaintiff’s concrete redress, so the court left open whether Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale suffices under Minnesota’s enforcement scheme. (Opinion, Part II.B; concurring op.)

What the district court should decide on remand (as framed by the opinion)
- Whether the MFLSA provisions Cohen invoked actually permit a private plaintiff to obtain the penalties he seeks (the panel left the statutory/enforcement question unresolved).
- Whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy still exists or is likely to recur; if it remains, penalties might deter future violations and thus could redress Cohen under Laidlaw; if not, the penalties claim may be moot. (Opinion, Part II.B.)

Bottom line
- The opinion does not identify an MFLSA provision that authorizes a private plaintiff to recover Commissioner‑payable penalties; it records the parties’ concession that penalties go to the Commissioner and remands because Article III redressability is in doubt. Under Laidlaw a government‑paid penalty can sometimes redress a private plaintiff by deterring ongoing or future harms, but the Eighth Circuit left open whether that deterrence link — and therefore standing — exists under Minnesota’s MFLSA enforcement scheme and asked the district court to decide. (Opinion, Part II.B; concurring op.)

### On remand, who bears the burden to prove mootness via voluntary cessation, and what evidence would make it “absolutely clear” Consilio’s overtime policy won’t recur?
Question:
The court vacates the MFLSA ruling and remands to determine whether Cohen’s penalties claim is moot if Consilio ended its policy and it is “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur. My understanding is that the defendant bears a heavy burden under the voluntary cessation doctrine. Is that the standard the district court should apply here? What kinds of proof (formal policy rescission, sworn declarations, duration of compliance, operational changes, audits, etc.) typically satisfy or fail the “absolutely clear” requirement in wage-and-hour disputes?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. The Eighth Circuit remanded expressly to apply the Supreme Court’s “voluntary cessation” / Friends of the Earth test — that it must be “absolutely clear” the wrongful behavior cannot reasonably be expected to recur. That is the standard the district court should use, and the party asserting mootness (here, Consilio) must make the showing. The panel’s opinion makes clear that payment to Cohen alone did not resolve the jurisdictional question because the existence of the policy remains in dispute.

Legal rule (from the opinion)
- The court quoted Friends of the Earth: a claim “might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC, No. 24‑2079, slip op. at II.B (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (quoting Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 189).  
- The opinion also repeated the settled corollary: “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice.” Id. (quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).  
- Because those are the controlling principles, the district court must decide whether Consilio has shown (1) the policy has in fact ended and (2) it is “absolutely clear” the wrongful conduct will not recur.

Who bears the burden
- The party asserting mootness must carry the burden of persuasion. The opinion’s remand frames the question against Consilio’s prior payment to Cohen and the unresolved factual dispute about whether the violative policy remains in effect; that context makes clear Consilio will have to prove mootness on remand.

What kinds of proof will (and will not) satisfy “absolutely clear” in this wage‑and‑hour context
The opinion gives no checklist, but its reasoning (and the cited Supreme Court authorities) makes clear the standard is demanding: objective, verifiable, and durable changes are required — not merely ephemeral promises or payments. Examples of evidence that would be persuasive versus evidence that will likely fail:

Evidence that is likely persuasive (singly or best, in combination)
- A formal, company‑wide rescission of the 2019 policy (clear written notice signed/issued by an appropriate corporate officer) and an updated employee handbook distributed and acknowledged by employees.  
- Documentary payroll proof (payroll registers, timekeeping records, pay stubs) showing consistent payment of overtime premiums to the class of workers at issue for an extended period after rescission.  
- Detailed, sworn declarations from HR/payroll/compliance officers describing how the rescission was implemented, with documentary attachments (emails to managers, training logs, revised SOPs). Declarations should be corroborated by documents, not conclusory.  
- Structural or operational changes that eliminate the cause of the violation (e.g., changed compensation schemes, new approval/monitoring processes for overtime, personnel changes removing the decision‑makers who adopted the policy).  
- Independent verification: internal or third‑party audits showing compliance, or a government agency finding no continuing violation.  
- A binding, enforceable commitment (consent decree, stipulated injunction, or other court‑enforceable remedy with monitoring/reporting) that would make recurrence less likely and gives the court a mechanism to police compliance.

Evidence that is unlikely to suffice
- Payment to the plaintiff alone (as here): the panel remanded despite payments because the operative policy’s status remained disputed. See slip op. II.B.  
- Bare, uncorroborated statements or conclusory affidavits that “we won’t do it again” without documentary proof of changed practice.  
- Short periods of compliance without structural changes or monitoring (temporary fixes are weak).  
- Ambiguous rescission language or policies that permit the same conduct in practice (exceptions, carve‑outs, or undisclosed local practices).

Practical points for the district court on remand
- Require admissible, objective proof — documents and corroborated testimony — not just litigant assertions. The Eighth Circuit’s remand was specifically to determine whether the policy remains and, if not, whether recurrence is “absolutely clear.” Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. at II.B.  
- If facts remain in dispute about the policy’s existence or scope, limited discovery targeted to payroll records, policy documents, communications about the policy, and audits is appropriate. The court may decide mootness as a jurisdictional threshold once the record addressing recurrence is developed. (The opinion vacated and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction.)  
- Remember that statutory penalties here go to the Commissioner, which is why the Court flagged standing/mootness concerns; proving the policy is gone and will not recur is essential to show a lack of a continuing interest.

Bottom line
Apply the Friends of the Earth/voluntary‑cessation standard the Eighth Circuit described: Consilio must prove with objective, verifiable, and durable evidence that the policy has ended and that recurrence is not reasonably to be expected. Mere payment to Cohen did not carry that day in the panel’s view; on remand the district court should demand documentary proof, corroborated sworn statements, structural reforms or independent verification to find the claim moot.

### Does the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (Minn. Stat. § 181.032) provide a private right to civil penalties for failure to give written notice of pay changes?
Question:
The panel affirms dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA claim for lack of statutory authority to recover civil penalties. Section 181.032(d)(4) requires written notice of pay changes, and I thought enforcement could run through MFLSA § 177.27 (at least for the Commissioner). Is there any private remedy (damages, statutory penalties, or injunctive relief) for a § 181.032 notice violation, or is enforcement exclusively via the Commissioner? If a private remedy exists, what is it and where is it found?
Answer:
Short answer
No — at least on the facts of Cohen v. Consilio the Eighth Circuit did not recognize a private right to collect civil penalties under the MWTA notice provision (Minn. Stat. § 181.032(d)(4)). The court affirmed dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA claim because Cohen “points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” Cohen v. Consilio, No. 24‑2079, slip op. at II.C (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025).

Why (brief explanation of the legal reasoning)
- The court’s disposition turned on statutory text and the plaintiff’s burden to identify a statutory remedy. The opinion says Cohen “stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him, and he points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” Because Cohen offered no statutory basis for a private penalty claim, the court declined to disturb the district court’s dismissal. Id.  
- The court applied the familiar principle that statutory penalties are creatures of statute: when the legislature wants to authorize private recovery it does so expressly (the opinion makes this point in its MPWA discussion, noting other Minnesota wage statutes explicitly authorize private penalties or designate the Commissioner as the recipient/enforcer). See id. Part II.A.  
- The panel also relied on waiver/non‑argument doctrine: “With no meaningful argument in support of reversal, we will not disturb the district court’s order dismissing this claim.” Id. (citing Milligan v. City of Red Oak).

What this means for remedies and enforcement
- Under Cohen, you cannot rely on § 181.032(d)(4) alone to obtain a private civil penalty unless you can point to statutory language that (1) creates the penalty and (2) permits private enforcement. The Eighth Circuit found Cohen failed to do that.  
- The opinion does not undertake a broader statutory‑scheme ruling that the Commissioner is the exclusive enforcer of MWTA penalties; it simply affirms dismissal because no private penalty provision was identified. The court did, however, make clear in its MPWA discussion that some Minnesota wage statutes expressly entrust penalty enforcement to the Commissioner (and that where the legislature intends private penalties it knows how to say so). See id. Part II.A.  
- The court also did not decide injunctive relief under § 181.032 because Cohen’s injunctive request was not properly pleaded or argued; that issue was not resolved on the merits. See id. Part II.D.

Bottom line for litigation
If you want to pursue a private statutory penalty for a § 181.032 notice violation you need to point to explicit statutory language authorizing such a private remedy. Absent that (and absent a properly pleaded claim for equitable relief), Cohen shows the Eighth Circuit will not create a private civil‑penalty remedy from the MWTA notice provision.

### Did Cohen need to amend his complaint to seek rescission of the 2019 policy, or could the court have granted that different injunction at summary judgment under Rule 54(c)?
Question:
The opinion says Cohen first requested rescission of the 2019 overtime policy in summary judgment briefing and that the district court properly declined to consider it, citing the principle that new claims must be raised via amendment. But Rule 54(c) allows courts to grant the relief a party is entitled to even if not demanded in the pleadings. In the Eighth Circuit, when is a change in requested injunctive relief treated as a new claim requiring amendment versus a permissible reconfiguration of remedies? Was Cohen’s rescission request a new claim or merely a different form of relief for the same violation?
Answer:
Short answer: Under this opinion Cohen needed to amend. The Eighth Circuit treated his request to have Consilio “rescind” the 2019 policy as a new form of relief that was first raised in summary‑judgment briefing and therefore was not properly before the district court. The court relied on the principle that new claims (and new claims for relief) must be pleaded or added by amendment, not sprung in opposition briefs. See Cohen v. Consilio, Dkt. No. 24‑2079, sec. II.D.

Why the Court reached that result (doctrine and application)
- The controlling principle the court cited is that “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs,” and that a party who wishes to add a claim or materially different relief must seek leave to amend under Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a). Cohen, slip op. § II.D (quoting InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6).
- The court’s factual application is straightforward: Cohen’s complaint sought “certain specific forms of injunctive relief,” but he “changed course and request[ed] different relief—withdrawing the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing.” The district court therefore did not consider the rescission request, and the appellate court held the issue was not properly before it. Id.
- In short, the Eighth Circuit treated rescission as a new claim for purposes of pleading practice because the relief was not pled and was first asserted in opposing papers rather than by amendment.

Where Rule 54(c) fits in (what the opinion shows)
- The opinion does not discuss Rule 54(c). It instead rests on InfoDeli and Rule 15(a): a party cannot reliably present what the court characterized as a “new” claim for the first time in summary‑judgment briefing and expect the court to resolve it on the merits.
- That means that, as a practical matter in the Eighth Circuit (at least under the approach in this opinion), a plaintiff who wants a materially different injunction than the complaint requests should add that request by amended complaint (or otherwise give the defendant clear notice and an opportunity to address the new relief) rather than springing it in opposition papers.

Bottom line / practical takeaway
- Cohen’s rescission request was treated as a new claim/remedy and therefore required amendment; the district court and this Court properly declined to consider it when it first appeared in summary‑judgment briefing. If you want an injunction different in kind from the relief pled, move to amend the complaint (or explicitly seek leave to assert that relief well before summary judgment) so the defendant has notice and the court can consider the claim properly.

### What is the effect of paying the named plaintiff in full on putative class claims that were never certified in this Minnesota wage case?
Question:
Cohen filed on behalf of a putative class, but before certification Consilio paid him all claimed overtime and MFLSA liquidated damages, and the parties stipulated he’d been paid in full (leaving only penalties and injunctive relief at issue). The opinion doesn’t address the putative class. Under Genesis Healthcare and Campbell-Ewald, does full payment to an individual plaintiff in a pre-certification Minnesota wage case moot the putative class claims? Does it matter that this was an actual payment accepted via stipulation rather than an unaccepted Rule 68 offer?
Answer:
Short answer
The Eighth Circuit opinion does not decide whether the defendant’s payment to Cohen mooted any putative class claims. The court accepted the parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid “for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” (and liquidated damages under the MFLSA), treated those individual monetary claims as satisfied, and narrowed the dispute to penalties and injunctive relief — but it expressly declined to resolve class-mootness and remanded the MFLSA penalties issue for the district court to determine whether a live controversy remains. In other words: Cohen’s individual money claims were treated as resolved by the accepted payment/stipulation, but the opinion says nothing definitive about the effect of that payment on un‑certified putative class claims.

Why (what the opinion actually says)
- The panel records the parties’ stipulation that Cohen “had been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” The court therefore focused its analysis on whether any live controversy remained as to statutory penalties and injunctive relief.  
- The court framed the problem in ordinary standing/mootness terms: “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought,” and the “personal interest … must continue throughout [the] existence” of the litigation. It noted that a claim “might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur,” but also reiterated that voluntary cessation does not automatically moot a case. Because it was unclear whether Consilio’s policy remained in effect, the court vacated the MFLSA summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction/mootness. (Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. Oct. 6, 2025.)

How Genesis HealthCare and Campbell‑Ewald fit (practical application)
- Campbell‑Ewald v. Gomez holds (in the Rule 23/class context) that an unaccepted Rule 68 offer of judgment does not moot a putative class claim. The key teaching for your fact pattern is that an unaccepted offer is legally different from actual satisfaction.  
- Genesis HealthCare (Symczyk) is often read to say that when a defendant truly satisfies the named plaintiff’s individual claim, the plaintiff may lose the personal stake necessary to press the litigation (so the suit can become moot) — although the precise boundaries of that rule are fact‑specific and have been the subject of debate.  
- Applying those principles to Cohen: the payment here was actual and accepted (by stipulation), not an unaccepted Rule 68 offer. That factual difference matters under the Campbell‑Ewald line: an unaccepted offer would not have the same mooting effect, but an actual, accepted payment is—as the Eighth Circuit treated it—satisfaction of Cohen’s individual monetary claims and thus removes his personal stake in those monetary claims. The Eighth Circuit, however, stopped short of ruling whether that satisfaction moots any putative class claims; it remanded the statutory‑penalty question because penalties and injunctive relief can preserve a live controversy even when individual money damages have been paid.

Bottom line for your question
- The Cohen opinion itself: does not resolve whether payment to the named plaintiff moots un‑certified putative class claims. It shows the court will treat an accepted payment/stipulation as satisfaction of the named plaintiff’s individual monetary claims and will then ask whether any other live interest (statutory penalties payable to the state, injunctive relief, a continuing policy) keeps the controversy alive.  
- Under Supreme Court precedent: an unaccepted Rule 68 offer generally will not moot a putative class claim (Campbell‑Ewald); an actual payment that the plaintiff accepts is materially different and is much more likely to eliminate the named plaintiff’s individual stake (and therefore to risk mooting the action if nothing else remains). Cohen demonstrates exactly that distinction in practice, but it leaves the class‑mootness question open for the district court to address on remand.

### Can the court affirm merits rulings on MPWA/MWTA while remanding MFLSA for a jurisdictional determination, or must all merits await a global Article III analysis?
Question:
The panel affirms on MPWA and MWTA but vacates and remands MFLSA to determine mootness. If Article III jurisdiction is uncertain post-payment, does Steel Co. require resolving jurisdiction before any merits ruling? Is it proper to decide the merits of some claims while remanding another for a jurisdictional inquiry because standing/mootness is assessed claim-by-claim, or does the employer’s payments potentially undermine jurisdiction across all claims?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The panel did exactly that — it affirmed the MPWA and MWTA rulings and vacated/remanded the MFLSA ruling for a jurisdictional (mootness) inquiry. The opinion treats standing/mootness as a claim-by-claim requirement and resolves claims on the merits where Article III concerns are not present or are resolved by statutory interpretation; it remands only the MFLSA claim because the facts leave jurisdiction in doubt.

Why that is lawful and how the opinion applies the doctrine
- Claim-by-claim Article III review. The court explicitly cites the settled rule that “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought,” and that the “requisite personal interest . . . must continue throughout its existence (standing) [and] (mootness).” (Opinion II.B, citing Town of Chester and Sisney.) That is the doctrinal hook that allows different treatment of separate claims in the same suit.

- How the panel used that rule here. The panel determined the MPWA claim could be decided on the merits because the statute’s text limits enforcement of the particular “average daily wage” penalty to the Commissioner — an issue of statutory interpretation independent of whether the employer later paid the employee. The court concluded “only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101,” and therefore “the district court did not err” in dismissing that claim (Opinion II.A). Similarly, the MWTA challenge failed for lack of a developed argument and was waived (Opinion II.C). Because those legal defects/waivers foreclosed relief, the court could affirm them without first resolving an across-the-board Article III question.

- Why MFLSA was treated differently. By contrast, Cohen stipulated he had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA, and the availability of statutory penalties (which are payable to the Commissioner) combined with uncertainty about whether the employer’s policy remains in place created a genuine mootness question. The panel said it “ha[d] a serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue” and therefore “vacate[d] the district court’s grant of summary judgment as to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand[ed] for the district court to determine its jurisdiction” (Opinion II.B). The opinion also notes Friends of the Earth’s teaching that government-directed penalties can sometimes still afford plaintiff redress, which is why the MFLSA penalty claim could not be summarily resolved on the record as to mootness (Opinion II.B).

Practical takeaway
- The employer’s post-suit payments do not automatically destroy jurisdiction for every claim in a multi-claim suit; courts assess standing/mootness claim-by-claim and may affirm on merits where jurisdictional problems do not exist (or where the merits resolution is controlled by statutory text or waiver), while remanding for a jurisdictional inquiry on a different claim that raises live Article III doubts. That is exactly what the Eighth Circuit did here. The concurrence also confirms the district court on remand “may decide the mootness question first.”

### What is the legal effect of the concurrence’s statement about vacating attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest when the majority is silent?
Question:
Judge Kobes states he “also vacate[s] the district court’s ruling as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest,” but the majority opinion does not mention those items. Does a separate concurrence alter the judgment or mandate in any way, or are fees/costs/interest unaffected unless addressed by the majority disposition? If the MFLSA penalties claim is found moot on remand, how should the district court handle requests for attorney’s fees under Minnesota wage statutes or other fee-shifting provisions?
Answer:
Short answer: No — a single judge’s concurrence does not change the panel’s judgment or the mandate. Only the majority’s disposition controls what the district court must do on remand. Here the court’s majority expressly “vacate[d] the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand[ed] for further proceedings” and “otherwise affirm[ed].” Because the majority did not vacate attorney’s‑fee, costs, or prejudgment‑interest rulings, Judge Kobes’s separate concurrence stating he “also vacate[s] the district court’s ruling as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest” is persuasive at most but does not alter the mandate.

Why that matters (grounded in the opinion)
- The opinion’s controlling instruction is the majority disposition: “We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. We otherwise affirm.” That is the panel’s judgment and what the mandate will implement.
- The concurrence is a separate view by one judge. It can suggest a course of action for the district court, but it does not change what the panel ordered.

Practical effect on remand
1. Follow the majority’s remand: the district court must determine whether it has jurisdiction over the MFLSA penalties claim. The panel framed that exact issue using the Article III/mootness standards (Friends of the Earth/Laidlaw): if it is “absolutely clear” the wrongful conduct will not recur, the claim may be moot; if not, the claim may proceed. The majority’s instruction was to remand for that jurisdictional inquiry.

2. If the district court concludes the MFLSA penalties claim survives, it can adjudicate penalties and any fee requests that are properly authorized by statute or other law.

3. If the district court concludes the MFLSA penalties claim is moot, it should not award attorney’s fees, costs, or prejudgment interest on the basis of MFLSA statutory penalties (the panel emphasized that the statutory penalties at issue are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen). The panel already held (as to the MPWA) that “only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101,” and it noted that statutory penalties under the MFLSA and MPWA are payable to the Commissioner. Those statutory conclusions constrain the district court’s ability to award private statutory penalties/fees under those wage statutes.

4. More generally, the district court should analyze any fee request against the applicable statutory or contractual fee‑shifting authority. If a fee claim is premised solely on a statutory penalty that the panel has said belongs to the Commissioner (or on a claim that the panel affirmed was dismissed), the district court lacks a statutory basis to award fees to Cohen. If there is an independent fee‑shifting provision (not adjudicated by the panel) or a basis to award taxable costs, the court should apply the usual governing law to those discrete claims.

Takeaway for the parties/district judge
- The concurrence does not change the mandate; the district court must obey the majority’s remand and affirmance.
- On remand the court should (a) decide the Article III/mootness question the majority sent back, and (b) resolve fee/cost/prejudgment‑interest requests only insofar as they rest on surviving claims or valid fee‑shifting authority — keeping in mind the panel’s statutory rulings that MPWA/MFLSA penalties (as presented) are for the Commissioner and that the MWTA offered no statutory civil‑penalty theory the panel would revive.
