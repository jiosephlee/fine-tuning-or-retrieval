Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case arises from an employment dispute in which Bruce Cohen, an hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer, sued his employer Consilio, LLC for allegedly failing to pay overtime. The litigation began after Consilio changed its overtime-pay practice in 2019. The district court resolved the dispute on cross-motions for summary judgment in favor of Consilio, and Cohen appealed. The posture of the case on appeal is shaped by a sequence of facts and stipulations: a company-wide payroll policy change announced by email, partial payments by the employer, narrow litigation focused on statutory penalties and injunctive relief, and the parties’ agreement to have the court decide a dispositive legal motion before further discovery. The Eighth Circuit reviewed the grant of summary judgment de novo and ultimately affirmed in part and vacated and remanded in part.

## Parties and roles

Consilio is described in the opinion as a nationwide legal document-review company with offices in Minnesota. It contracts or employs attorneys to perform document-review work for litigation and other legal matters. Bruce Cohen was one of those attorneys: he began working for Consilio in 2018 as an hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer based in Minnesota. The fact that Cohen was an hourly employee and a licensed attorney is important: the complaint centers on whether Consilio’s pay practices for hourly attorney-reviewers complied with Minnesota wage laws when employees worked overtime.

## 2019 policy change

In July 2019 Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney reviewers announcing a change to its overtime-pay practice effective in August 2019. The new policy eliminated overtime premium pay and stated that affected reviewers would be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked. That emailed policy change is the operative event giving rise to Cohen’s claims: he alleges the elimination of overtime premium pay left him and similarly situated employees underpaid for overtime hours.

## Claims and statutes invoked (remedies sought)

Cohen filed suit in August 2020 on behalf of himself and a putative class, alleging that Consilio’s change violated several Minnesota statutes. The complaint pleaded (1) the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), citing Minn. Stat. §§ 181.101 and 181.171; (2) the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), citing Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8; and (3) the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), citing Minn. Stat. §§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4), together with related statutory provisions. Cohen sought traditional wage remedies (unpaid overtime), MFLSA liquidated damages, statutory penalties under the various statutes, and injunctive relief directed at Consilio’s overtime policy. The complaint therefore combined remedial requests that are compensatory (overtime pay, liquidated damages) with requests for statutory penalties and prospective relief (injunction).

## Consilio’s payments and stipulations

Before the district court decided dispositive motions, Consilio paid a lump sum of $256,010.01 to Cohen and other Minnesota licensed-attorney reviewers to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages Cohen demanded under his MFLSA claim. Cohen’s individual share of those MFLSA payments was $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties stipulated expressly that “Cohen has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he did not dispute having been paid any liquidated damages to which he was entitled under the MFLSA. They did not, however, resolve the question of statutory penalties: Cohen sought $172,080 in “average daily wage” penalties under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA; Consilio argued that the statutes did not authorize those penalties.

Those payments and stipulations narrowed the live controversies in the case. With the compensatory MFLSA remedies conceded as paid, the remaining disputes were legal — whether the statutes permit the kinds of penalties Cohen sought and whether prospective relief (an injunction rescinding or altering Consilio’s policy) was appropriate. The parties also agreed that the penalties, if awarded, would be payable to the state Commissioner (not to Cohen), a point that later bears on standing and mootness questions.

## Procedural agreement and district-court disposition

Given the narrowed dispute, the parties jointly proposed that the district court resolve Consilio’s summary-judgment motion on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before further discovery. They represented to the court that doing so was “the most expedient and efficient path forward” and acknowledged that if the court granted the defendant’s motion in full, “no claims would remain and the case would be fully resolved.” The district court accepted the case posture and granted summary judgment to Consilio on all three statutory claims. Cohen appealed that judgment to the Eighth Circuit.

## Appellate standard of review and summary-judgment standard

On appeal the Eighth Circuit framed its review under the familiar summary-judgment standards. The panel explained that it reviews a district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo, viewing the evidence and reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. Summary judgment is appropriate only if there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Those standards matter here because the parties’ stipulations produced a record in which the core disputes are primarily legal questions of statutory interpretation (e.g., who may recover certain statutory penalties and under what circumstances), which appellate courts review anew rather than for clear error.

# MPWA Penalties: Statutory Structure Limits Penalties to the Commissioner

## Private right under § 181.171
Section 181.171 is the statutory hook that allows private parties to sue under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). It says in plain terms that “a person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of listed MPWA provisions and that an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” when a violation is found. But the statute also contains a limiting phrase: civil penalties or damages are recoverable by a private plaintiff only if those penalties are “provided for in the section violated.” The Eighth Circuit begins with that structure because Cohen relied on § 181.171 to pursue an “average daily wage” penalty he said was available under § 181.101. The threshold question therefore is statutory: does § 181.101 itself authorize a private plaintiff to recover the penalty Cohen seeks, or does the MPWA reserve enforcement of that penalty to the Commissioner?

## Text of § 181.101(a): penalties tied to the Commissioner
The court’s analysis turns on the language of § 181.101(a). That subsection describes a Commissioner-driven enforcement mechanism: the Commissioner may “serve a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee,” and if wages are not paid within ten days of that demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” The statute further instructs that any money collected by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned.” Critically for the Eighth Circuit, those penalty provisions are framed exclusively in terms of the Commissioner’s authority to act. The court applied Minnesota’s ordinary‑meaning rule of construction (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.) and concluded that the text is plain and unambiguous: the penalty in § 181.101 is part of the Commissioner’s demand‑and‑collection scheme, not a remedy that § 181.171 converts into a privately enforceable penalty.

## The employee’s preserved right under § 181.101(a)
Although § 181.101(a) ties penalty enforcement to the Commissioner, the statute expressly preserves an employee’s ability to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” The Eighth Circuit emphasized this carve‑out: employees retain a private right to bring wage claims, and § 181.171 supplies that private cause of action for remedies that the specific MPWA subsection makes available to private litigants. But because § 181.101 allocates the power to assess and collect its penalty to the Commissioner, an employee’s separate prosecutory right is limited to securing wages (and related substantive rights) — it does not convert § 181.101’s Commissioner‑administered penalty into a private cause of action for penalties.

## Textual canons and statutory comparators
The court reinforced its plain‑text reading by pointing to nearby statutes where the Legislature explicitly empowers employees to collect penalties. For example, §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14(2) provide that a discharged or quitting employee “may charge and collect a penalty.” The existence of unambiguous, employee‑directed penalty provisions elsewhere in the MPWA supports a negative inference here: the Legislature “knows how” to give employees a statutory right to penalties, and when it intended that outcome it said so. The Eighth Circuit relied on this comparator reasoning (citing Lagasse and Hubbard) to confirm that the absence of parallel language in § 181.101 is dispositive under ordinary canons of statutory interpretation.

## Supportive precedent and how it fits
The court’s interpretation is consonant with prior Minnesota authority and other courts’ readings. Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch. is cited for the point that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee — a feature that underscores the Commissioner’s role as the collector, not the private litigant’s. The district court decision in Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc. reached the same practical conclusion: § 181.101 limits enforcement of the penalty to the Commissioner. The Eighth Circuit adopted those readings as supportive authority for its plain‑text and structural analysis.

## Holding and disposition
Applying these textual and structural lessons, the court held that only the Commissioner may seek the average daily wage penalty created by § 181.101. Because Cohen’s claim sought that penalty through a private suit under § 181.171, and § 181.101 does not provide a private penalty remedy, the Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment against Cohen on his MPWA penalty claim. The practical upshot for litigants is straightforward: a private plaintiff may use § 181.171 to recover compensatory wages and any remedies a particular MPWA subsection explicitly makes available to private parties, but where a subsection confines penalty enforcement to the Commissioner, a private suit cannot independently recover that penalty.

# MFLSA Penalties: Standing, Mootness, and Voluntary Cessation Drive a Remand

## What remains of the MFLSA claim
By the time this case reached the Eighth Circuit, the factual and remedial landscape of Cohen’s MFLSA claim had materially changed. Consilio had paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed-attorney reviewers sums covering overtime wages and liquidated damages; the parties stipulated Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and does not dispute he has been paid any liquidated damages to which he is entitled under the MFLSA. What was left at issue on appeal was only statutory penalties — and both parties agreed those penalties, if imposed, would be payable to the state Commissioner rather than to Cohen personally. That narrow residual claim is the cause of the jurisdictional problem the court identifies: after the direct monetary injury (unpaid wages) has been removed, can Cohen still satisfy Article III’s requirements to pursue statutory penalties that do not flow to him?

## Article III requirements: standing for each claim and continuing interest
The court frames the problem with two bedrock Article III principles. First, a plaintiff must demonstrate standing “for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought” (Town of Chester). That means it is not enough that Cohen once had a personal economic injury from unpaid overtime; he must show an injury-in-fact that is traceable to the employer and redressable by the particular relief he now seeks (here, statutory penalties paid to the Commissioner). Second, standing must persist for the life of the litigation: the “requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)” (Sisney). Those two rules force the court to ask whether, with wages and liquidated damages conceded paid, Cohen still has a live, redressable stake in pursuing MFLSA penalties.

## Civil penalties as redress: Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale
The panel recognized a key precedent that complicates the usual “paid in full” mootness calculus. In Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, the Supreme Court explained that civil penalties — even when paid to the government — can nonetheless “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” because penalties can induce a defendant to stop unlawful conduct and deter future violations. Applied here, that doctrine means Cohen might still have standing even though he cannot recover the penalty payments personally: the prospect of statutory penalties could force Consilio to change (or refrain from reinstating) an unlawful overtime policy, thereby reducing the risk of future injury to Cohen and similarly situated employees. The Eighth Circuit acknowledged that possibility; it did not decide whether Laidlaw’s rationale definitively supplies Article III redress for MFLSA penalties, but it recognized Laidlaw creates a plausible route to standing.

## Voluntary cessation and mootness: when a live controversy ends
Voluntary cessation doctrine runs against a plaintiff when a defendant permanently abandons the challenged practice, but it does not automatically do so. The court reiterated the well-known standard: a case becomes moot by voluntary cessation only if it is “absolutely clear” that the allegedly wrongful behavior cannot reasonably be expected to recur (quoting Friends of the Earth and City of Mesquite). In this case the dispositive factual question is whether Consilio’s allegedly violative overtime policy still exists or has been rescinded. If the policy remains in effect (or if it might reasonably be expected to recur), civil penalties could still provide the deterrent redress Laidlaw contemplates and Cohen’s claim would not be moot. If, by contrast, Consilio has permanently and irrevocably ended the policy and can show recurrence is not reasonably possible, the MFLSA penalties claim may be moot because there would be no ongoing or threatened injury for penalties to remedy.

## Disposition on the MFLSA claim and what the remand requires
Because the record left “a serious concern” about jurisdiction — specifically, whether there was a live controversy for penalties after wages and liquidated damages were paid and whether Consilio’s policy remains — the Eighth Circuit did not reach the merits of Cohen’s entitlement to penalties. Instead it vacated the district court’s grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine whether it has jurisdiction (standing and/or mootness). Practically, that means the district court should develop the factual record about the current status of the overtime policy and apply the Article III standards the panel recited: determine whether Cohen still faces a threatened injury that penalties would likely redress (taking Laidlaw into account), or whether voluntary cessation has rendered the controversy moot (i.e., whether it is “absolutely clear” the offending policy will not recur). The appellate decision leaves open both possible outcomes; it sends the threshold jurisdictional question back to the court best placed to resolve disputed facts and make findings.

## Special concurrence (Judge Kobes)
Judge Kobes concurred but wrote separately to register two points useful for readers. He expressed doubt — not a decision — about whether Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale fits squarely with MFLSA statutory penalties; neither party fully briefed that question. He therefore agreed with vacatur and remand, but suggested the district court may properly consider mootness first on remand. He also indicated he would vacate the district court’s rulings as to ancillary awards (attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest) given the unresolved jurisdictional issue. His concurrence highlights that appellate courts sometimes leave open threshold doctrinal questions for the lower court to decide in the first instance when the parties have not fully litigated them on the record.

Takeaway for practitioners and students: when a defendant pays the primary monetary relief a plaintiff seeks, litigants and courts must not assume that Article III jurisdiction automatically survives. Where only penalties or other relief payable to a government official remain, the court must analyze whether those forms of relief can still redress a live injury (Laidlaw) and whether any defendant cessation is truly permanent (the “absolutely clear” voluntary cessation standard). The Eighth Circuit’s remand requires the district court to resolve those jurisdictional facts and apply the standing/mootness rules before proceeding to the merits.

# MWTA Notice Claim: No Identified Penalty Authority and Appellate Waiver

## MWTA theory: alleged failure to provide statutorily required written notice of change to overtime policy (§ 181.032(d)(4), (f))

Cohen’s MWTA claim rested on a simple factual-legal theory: Consilio changed its overtime-pay practice and, according to Cohen, failed to provide the written notice the Minnesota Wage Theft Act requires when an employer alters pay practices. The portions of the MWTA cited in the petition—§ 181.032(d)(4) and (f)—were invoked as the source of the notice obligation, and Cohen sought penalties tied to that alleged statutory violation. Read in the posture of the appeal, this is a notice-based statutory claim seeking statutory penalties as the principal remedy.

## Remedial gap: Cohen stipulated he received all pay owed; he identified no statutory authority for private civil penalties under the MWTA provisions invoked

Two facts narrowed the dispute on appeal. First, Cohen stipulated that he had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages that Consilio owed him; the monetary shortfall that might otherwise ground compensatory relief no longer existed. Second, and crucial to the court’s analysis, Cohen did not identify any statutory language in the MWTA provisions he relied on that authorizes a private plaintiff to recover civil penalties for a failure-to-notify violation. The panel treated that as a fatal gap: when a plaintiff seeks civil penalties, the entitlement to those penalties must trace to statutory authority. Because Cohen neither showed that the MWTA provisions he invoked confer a private right to recover penalties nor identified another statutory basis for such penalties, the court had nothing to remediate by reversal beyond the stipulation that he already had been paid.

## Appellate briefing deficiency: lack of meaningful argument or legal authority constitutes waiver (Milligan)

The court disposed of the claim largely on appellate procedure: an issue that is not supported with meaningful argument or legal citation is waived. The panel invoked Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000), to emphasize the point that appellate courts do not consider undeveloped arguments. Cohen complained that the district court “included little to no discussion” of the MWTA claim, but the appellate record showed he had offered no statutory interpretation, no authority, and no focused argument on appeal to show that a private penalty remedy existed under the MWTA provisions in question. Under Eighth Circuit practice, that failure to develop the argument in briefing means the issue is forfeited on appeal; the court will not craft or search out legal theories for a party who does not present them.

## Holding and disposition: affirm dismissal of the MWTA notice-based claim seeking penalties

Applying those points, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA notice claim. The court explained that Cohen had been paid the wages he sought and had not pointed to statutory authority allowing a private civil-penalty recovery under the MWTA subsections he invoked. Because Cohen presented no meaningful appellate argument or legal support for reversal, the panel declined to disturb the district court’s ruling and treated the issue as waived. For students reading this portion of the opinion, the core lessons are (1) identify the precise statutory source of any penalty remedy you seek and be prepared to show its applicability, and (2) develop that argument clearly on appeal—failure to do so will typically result in waiver.

# Injunctive Relief: Preserving Theories and the Limits of Summary-Judgment Briefing

## Shift in requested relief
In Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, the record shows a clear procedural shift. Cohen’s complaint originally sought particular, specified forms of injunctive relief; it did not, however, ask the district court to order the affirmative remedy he later requested at the summary‑judgment stage—namely, an order directing Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. Instead of filing an amended complaint to add or transform his equitable remedy, Cohen raised the rescission request for the first time in his summary‑judgment briefing. The panel described that move succinctly: “In his complaint, Cohen sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief, only to later change course and request different relief—withdrawing the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing.” Because the requested relief in the briefing was different in kind from the relief pleaded, the court treated it as a new theory not squarely before the district court.

## Procedural rule: amendment, notice, and Rule 15(a)
The governing procedural principle is straightforward: new claims or materially different forms of relief should be pleaded through an amended complaint, not sprung on the court or opposing party in opposition papers or a summary‑judgment brief. The Eighth Circuit relied on its recent precedent in InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6, which states that “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.” Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) supplies the mechanism: a party may amend its pleading with the opposing party’s written consent or the court’s leave, and the court should freely give leave when justice so requires. The rule, however, does not license sidestepping the amendment process. Recasting the remedy in a summary‑judgment brief denies the opponent notice and an opportunity to respond, may change the scope of necessary discovery and factual development, and deprives the district court of the orderly complaint→answer→motion framework that frames litigation and decisionmaking.

## Appellate posture: preservation and review
Because Cohen introduced the rescission request at summary judgment rather than by amendment, the district court “understandably” declined to consider it, and the Eighth Circuit held the issue was not properly before them on appeal. That conclusion follows settled preservation principles: appellate review ordinarily is limited to claims and issues that were presented to and decided (or at least ruled upon) by the district court. A party cannot preserve a new substantive theory or materially different remedy merely by arguing it in opposition papers; if the district court never entertained or ruled on that theory, there is nothing for an appellate court to review. The court therefore refused to address Cohen’s belated request for an affirmative order rescinding the policy.

## Overall disposition and takeaways
The court’s handling of the injunctive issue fits the broader disposition in the opinion: the Eighth Circuit vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalty ruling for the district court to determine jurisdictional questions, but otherwise affirmed the district court’s summary judgment (including the MPWA and MWTA rulings) and declined to disturb the district court’s treatment of the belated injunctive request. Practically, the opinion sends two clear messages to litigants and practitioners:

- If you seek forward‑looking relief (equitable remedies such as injunctions or rescission), plead it clearly in the complaint or move to amend promptly under Rule 15(a). Waiting to announce a new remedy at summary judgment risks forfeiture and deprives the court of the proper record to decide an equitable claim.
- Major changes to the nature of relief sought are not mere semantics; they can alter the scope of discovery, the legal standards to be applied, and the interests of non‑parties or public agencies. Raise them early so the defendant has notice and the district court can evaluate jurisdictional, factual, and equitable considerations.

Practice pointer: when a plaintiff seeks prospective relief, include detailed allegations about the continuing policy, the ongoing injury, and the specific form of relief in the original complaint; if circumstances change, move to amend promptly and explain why amendment is timely and would not prejudice the defendant. That preserves the issue for the district court and, if necessary, for meaningful appellate review.
