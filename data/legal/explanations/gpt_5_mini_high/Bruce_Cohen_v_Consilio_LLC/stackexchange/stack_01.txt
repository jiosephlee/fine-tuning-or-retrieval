Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Under Minn. Stat. § 181.101, can a private employee ever recover “average daily wage” penalties, or are they exclusive to the Commissioner despite § 181.171’s “other appropriate relief” language?
Question:
The court holds that only the Commissioner may “charge and collect” penalties under § 181.101(a), even though § 181.171, subd. 1 authorizes private suits for violations of § 181.101 and allows “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief,” plus “the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” How do these provisions fit together? If § 181.101 contains an average-daily-wage penalty but assigns enforcement to the Commissioner, does § 181.171 categorically bar private plaintiffs from obtaining that penalty? What relief (beyond unpaid wages) remains available to an employee suing directly under § 181.101 via § 181.171?
Answer:
Short answer
No. As the Eighth Circuit held in Cohen v. Consilio, the “average daily wage” penalty in Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) is enforceable only by the Commissioner; § 181.171’s private‑suit language does not convert that particular penalty into a remedy an employee may obtain in a direct suit.

Explanation (how the statutes fit together)
- Section 181.171 authorizes private civil actions for violations of certain MPWA provisions and says an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated” and for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” Cohen, slip op. § II.A (quoting Minn. Stat. § 181.171, subd. 1).
- But § 181.101(a) ties the average‑daily‑wage penalty to the Commissioner’s enforcement process: the Commissioner must first serve a demand for payment and then, if payment is not made within ten days, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” Cohen, slip op. § II.A (quoting Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a)).
- The court applied the plain‑language rule and concluded those two provisions must be read together: because § 181.101 assigns the power to “charge and collect” that penalty to the Commissioner, that penalty is not a remedy a private plaintiff may obtain in a direct suit under § 181.171. The opinion puts it succinctly: “Because we conclude that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court did not err.” Cohen, slip op. § II.A.

Why that reading is justified
- The opinion notes that the Legislature knows how to empower an employee to “charge and collect a penalty” when it intends to do so (citing examples in other MPWA provisions, e.g., §§ 181.13 and 181.14). The absence of similar language in § 181.101 supports the conclusion that the penalty there is intended to be enforced by the Commissioner, not by private plaintiffs. Cohen, slip op. § II.A.

What relief a private plaintiff can get under § 181.171 when suing on § 181.101
- An employee may prosecute a claim for unpaid wages (the statute explicitly preserves the employee’s ability to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages”). Cohen, slip op. § II.A.
- More generally, § 181.171 allows “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” and authorizes recovery of civil penalties only if the specific MPWA section at issue provides for a privately recoverable penalty. Cohen makes clear that the § 181.101 average‑daily‑wage penalty is not such a privately recoverable penalty because enforcement is committed to the Commissioner.
- A practical corollary: if the Commissioner enforces § 181.101 and collects a penalty, “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned,” so an employee can still benefit from a penalty recovered through the Commissioner’s enforcement—but not by obtaining that penalty directly in a private suit. Cohen, slip op. § II.A.

Bottom line
Section 181.171 authorizes private suits for MPWA violations and preserves whatever penalties a particular MPWA provision itself makes available to private litigants. Under Cohen, the average‑daily‑wage penalty in § 181.101 is not one of those privately available remedies because § 181.101 assigns its enforcement to the Commissioner. Private plaintiffs suing under § 181.101 can pursue unpaid wages (and whatever compensatory or other relief the statute plainly authorizes), but not the § 181.101 average‑daily‑wage penalty in a direct action.
