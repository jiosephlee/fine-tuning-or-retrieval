Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Only the Commissioner Can Seek MPWA “Average Daily Wage” Penalties

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (Oct. 6, 2025) draws a sharp line around who may recover the MPWA’s so-called “average daily wage” penalties. That may sound like a narrow statutory parsing, but it has practical bite: private plaintiffs pursuing wage claims in Minnesota cannot, at least under § 181.101, obtain those penalties directly in court. Instead, the statute gives the Commissioner of Labor and Industry the exclusive mechanism for demanding payment and collecting penalties. For plaintiff lawyers, employers, and courts, the ruling channels a class of remedies into the administrative arena and reshapes litigation and settlement incentives in wage cases.

## The case and the holding

The facts are straightforward. Consilio changed its overtime-pay practice, Cohen sued on behalf of himself and putative class members under several Minnesota statutes, and Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages that Cohen alleged he was owed. The parties, however, could not agree on statutory penalties. The district court granted summary judgment for Consilio on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims; Cohen appealed.

On the MPWA claim (Minn. Stat. § 181.101), the Eighth Circuit affirmed. The panel concluded that § 181.101—by its text and structure—empowers only the Commissioner to serve a demand for wages and, if unpaid within ten days, to “charge and collect” wages and a penalty. Because § 181.171(1) allows private suits for redress but limits a private plaintiff’s recovery of civil penalties to those “provided for in the section violated,” and because § 181.101 itself provides the penalty mechanism only to the Commissioner, the court held employees may not pursue the MPWA’s average daily wage penalties in private lawsuits.

## How the court reasoned

The opinion is doctrinally conventional and resolutely textual. The panel began with the statute’s plain wording: § 181.101(a) sets out a Commissioner-driven enforcement process—serve a demand, allow ten days for payment, then the Commissioner may collect wages and a penalty—and requires that “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Reading that framework as an exclusive enforcement scheme, the court concluded the Legislature intended the Commissioner, not private litigants, to be the actor who seeks the statute’s average daily wage penalty.

Two complementary interpretive moves buttressed the conclusion. First, the court relied on Minnesota precedent emphasizing that plain and unambiguous statutory language controls (citing Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co. and Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch.). Second, it applied a variant of the expressio unius canon: the Legislature knows how to give private plaintiffs a penalty remedy when it wants to (see Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13 and 181.14, which expressly permit discharged or resigned employees to “charge and collect a penalty”). That contrast—penalty authority expressly granted elsewhere in the wage statutes but not in § 181.101—supported the court’s conclusion that Congress did not intend a private right to those average daily wage penalties.

The panel also cited and followed a prior district-court decision, Hull v. ConvergeOne, which reached the same reading of § 181.101. In short, the Eighth Circuit declined to craft a private penalty remedy from a statutory enforcement process that squarely vests penalty collection power in an administrative officer.

## Where this reasoning fits in the broader interpretive landscape

The decision illustrates how a court committed to textualist tools treats statutes that combine private causes of action with administrative enforcement options. Section 181.171 gives private plaintiffs a right to bring “civil action[s] seeking redress” and subjects employers to liability for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” But the Eighth Circuit read that general grant in light of § 181.171’s limiting clause—that an employer is liable for “civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” That textual hook made the court’s job easy: if § 181.101 provides penalties only via the Commissioner, then private plaintiffs can’t recover them.

That approach is conservative in two senses. It resists reading broad remedial power into general language where a statute’s specific provisions set out a different enforcement pathway, and it respects the legislature’s drafting choices—if the Legislature wanted private suits to carry these particular penalties, it knew how to say so.

## Practical consequences for wage litigation in Minnesota

The decision has immediate, practical consequences:

- Private MPWA suits can still recover unpaid wages and related damages where the statute and its remedial provisions permit, but plaintiffs cannot recover the MPWA’s average daily wage penalties under § 181.101 themselves. Those penalties are reserved for the Commissioner’s enforcement process.

- Plaintiffs’ counsel who previously sought to extract statutory daily-wage penalties in private MPWA litigation will need to recalibrate. If alleged penalties are the principal leverage in settlement bargaining, defendants may be able to blunt that leverage by paying the underlying wages to the employee while signaling there will be no administrative action by the Commissioner.

- Because the penalty remedy remains available only to the Commissioner, aggrieved employees who want that form of relief will have to enlist the Department of Labor and Industry (or hope the Commissioner brings an action). That means the Commissioner’s enforcement priorities and resource constraints will play a bigger role in which wage disputes produce statutory penalties.

- Strategies will shift toward either (a) pursuing other statutory claims that permit private penalties or liquidated damages (for example, state or federal fair-labor statutes where private relief may be broader), or (b) filing parallel administrative complaints to prompt Commissioner action. But the Eighth Circuit’s opinion underscores that the availability of alternative state statutory penalties can be uncertain and, as the Cohen opinion shows, was not persuasive in the MWTA context when the plaintiff made no meaningful statutory argument.

- For class and collective litigation, the consequence is stark: private class members cannot obtain MPWA average daily wage penalties through a Rule 23 class action unless the Commissioner intervenes or brings a separate action. Plaintiffs’ counsel may need to consider aggregate federal FLSA claims or pushing the state administrative route.

It is worth noting that the Eighth Circuit left intact the private recovery of unpaid wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA—the procedural posture here, however, made the MFLSA penalties question complicated by standing and mootness concerns, and the court vacated and remanded that part of the district court’s judgment. The point for practitioners is that the remedy map in a multi-statute wage suit can be fragmented: some relief flows to the employee, some to the state, and the mix affects litigation posture and settlement bargaining.

## Policy considerations and the path forward

The decision raises a familiar policy concern: channeling penalties exclusively to an administrative agency can create enforcement gaps if the agency lacks resources or a political will to pursue every meritorious case. Courts, though, are limited in remedying such problems when the statutory text plainly allocates enforcement responsibility to an administrative officer. The more direct fix lies with the Legislature, which can amend § 181.101 or related provisions to authorize private recovery of average daily wage penalties, or expand the private remedy language in § 181.171.

From the agency side, the Commissioner’s office may respond to Cohen by articulating enforcement priorities, making it clearer when and how it will pursue § 181.101 penalties, and possibly increasing outreach to employees and counsel so that aggrieved workers know the administrative path for seeking such sanctions. Plaintiffs’ counsel, in turn, may increase the use of formal complaints to the Commissioner as a complementary—rather than alternative—enforcement strategy to private litigation.

## Bottom line

Cohen v. Consilio is a tidy example of statutory interpretation producing concrete procedural consequences: under § 181.101, Minnesota’s “average daily wage” penalty is the Commissioner’s tool, not the private plaintiff’s. For wage litigators in Minnesota, the decision narrows one path to statutory penalties and channels enforcement toward the state administrative apparatus. Practitioners should recalibrate complaint drafting, settlement strategy, and administrative filings accordingly—and legislators who care about private enforcement as a deterrent have a clear, textual fix if they choose to provide one.



# Laidlaw, Voluntary Cessation, and MFLSA Civil Penalties: A Standing and Mootness Rubicon

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (No. 24‑2079, Oct. 6, 2025) turns on an increasingly common fact pattern in wage litigation: an employer pays back wages (and sometimes liquidated damages) after a suit is filed, and the plaintiff presses on seeking statutory penalties that—critically—are payable to a government agency rather than to the employee. That posture forces courts to grapple with two doctrinal lines that do not always sit comfortably together: (1) Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw’s (528 U.S. 167 (2000)) recognition that civil penalties payable to the government can nonetheless “afford redress” to private plaintiffs through deterrence, and (2) the Article III requirement that a plaintiff demonstrate standing for each claim and each form of relief and that that standing persist through the litigation (Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433 (2017)). The Eighth Circuit took a cautious route: it affirmed dismissal of the state MPWA penalty claim (because the statute reserves the penalty mechanism to the Commissioner), left intact other rulings, but vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalty ruling for a threshold jurisdictional determination. The opinion flags important questions about when deterrence-based redress suffices under Article III and what showing will succeed or fail on remand.

## The short roadmap of the opinion

The facts are simple and familiar. Consilio changed its overtime practice and told Minnesota-based, hourly licensed-attorney reviewers that they would be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked. Cohen sued, alleging state and state‑analogous wage-law violations (MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA). Consilio then paid Cohen and others roughly $256,000 to cover overtime and liquidated damages under the MFLSA; Cohen personally received modest sums. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed, but they could not agree on statutory penalties.

The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all claims. The Eighth Circuit affirmed dismissal of Cohen’s MPWA claim: under the text of Minn. Stat. § 181.101, the Commissioner of Labor has the mechanism to pursue “average daily wage” penalties (the statute contemplates the Commissioner serving a demand and collecting penalties), and the court would not read the statute to allow private employees to invoke that penalty remedy. The court also affirmed dismissal of the MWTA claim for failure to make a developed argument.

But the MFLSA penalties posed a harder problem. The parties agreed that any MFLSA statutory penalties would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. That raised the specter of mootness and standing: Cohen’s compensatory injury (unpaid wages and liquidated damages) has been remedied, so can he still litigate penalties that would accrue to the state? The panel—invoking Laidlaw’s recognition that civil penalties can redress private plaintiffs by deterring ongoing or future violations—declined to resolve the issue itself and vacated the MFLSA ruling so the district court can decide jurisdiction (including whether Consilio’s overtime policy remains in place). Judge Kobes concurred in the remand but expressed skepticism whether Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale necessarily maps onto MFLSA penalties.

## Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale: a pathway to Article III redress, but not an easy one

Laidlaw is the plaintiff’s hook: the Supreme Court held that civil penalties can “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct” because penalties can “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.” 528 U.S. at 186. Laidlaw therefore teaches that a penalty payable to government is not per se incapable of redressing a private litigant’s Article III injury.

But Laidlaw does not license an automatic claim. The decision was grounded in the fact‑based showing that the defendant’s ongoing discharge of pollutants posed a continuing or threatened harm; the civil penalty was plausibly able to alleviate that future harm by changing the defendant’s incentives. Applied to wage litigation, that rationale can sometimes work: if a defendant’s policy or practice is ongoing (or readily repeatable), a statutory penalty might deter recurrence and so vindicate the plaintiff’s interest in avoiding future underpayments.

The Eighth Circuit sensibly declined to decide the contest here on a blank record. Whether Laidlaw controls for MFLSA penalties turns on whether the alleged violation is “ongoing or threatened,” whether penalties would meaningfully deter the employer, and whether that deterrence would redress the plaintiff’s interest. Those are fact‑intensive questions best resolved after district‑court factfinding.

## Town of Chester and the separate standing inquiry for each form of relief

Town of Chester emphasizes that a litigant must show standing for each claim and for each form of relief sought. 581 U.S. at 439. The stipulation here—Cohen had been fully paid his overtime and liquidated damages—eliminates the classic monetary injury for the MFLSA wage claim. That is why the penalty claim must live or die on a different basis: a credible showing of continuing or future injury that penalties would prevent. The Eighth Circuit’s remand keeps that procedural gatekeeping intact.

## Voluntary cessation: the “absolutely clear” burden and what it requires on remand

Once an employer claims it has fixed the problem, the voluntary cessation doctrine gives the defendant a heavy burden: a court may not find a case moot unless it is “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 189 (quoting United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp. Ass’n). City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, 455 U.S. 283 (1982), supplies the familiar rule that unilateral cessation ordinarily does not moot a case. The Eighth Circuit’s remand thus signals that Consilio must do more than deposit a check; to strip the court of jurisdiction it must make a persuasive, verifiable showing that the overtime practice has been abandoned in a way that makes recurrence implausible.

## What each side should build on remand

Plaintiff’s playbook: Cohen needs to make a concrete, documentary showing that the overtime policy either still exists in practice or that the defendant’s conduct creates a realistic risk of recurrence. Discoverable evidence will matter: contemporaneous internal emails and policy documents, payroll data showing ongoing pay‑at‑base‑rate for overtime hours, declarations from other reviewers who continue to be paid under the challenged regime, deposition testimony from HR or corporate witnesses that the policy is still in force (or was only temporarily set aside), and proof that the company lacks binding institutional changes (no contractual amendments, no court-ordered restraints, no independent audits). Evidence that the company’s financial incentives to revert to lower overtime payments remain intact—particularly if changes were informal or reversible—will bolster a Laidlaw‑type deterrence theory.

Defendant’s playbook: Consilio must translate voluntary corrective action into something courts will treat as durable. That means more than a payment and a garden‑variety affidavit. Defendants should supply clear, formal policy rescissions or revisions, company‑wide written notices showing rescission, amendments to employment agreements, substantive compliance programs (training, audits, independent monitoring), and — ideally — a binding concession enforceable by the court (consent decree or stipulated injunction). Evidence that the employer has no intention and no economic incentive to revert to the challenged practice, backed by structural safeguards, will be the surest path to satisfying the “absolutely clear” standard.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

If the district court (and ultimately the Eighth Circuit) accepts Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale for MFLSA penalties on an adequate factual record, plaintiffs will retain a key tool to protect against strategic payoffs designed to moot litigation: they can litigate penalties to vindicate deterrence even after being made whole monetarily. That would limit the effectiveness of “pay‑to‑moot” tactics by employers. Conversely, if courts narrow Laidlaw’s reach in the wage context—as Judge Kobes suggests may be sensible—employees risk losing the ability to pursue public‑facing penalties once their private losses are compensated, shifting enforcement responsibility back toward public actors (the Commissioner) and making private suits less potent as a deterrent.

A more pragmatic byproduct: litigants will fight aggressively over the remand record. Plaintiffs will push for expedited discovery targeted at corporate policies and payroll practices; defendants will move to present robust documentary proof of durable reform. The remand decision likely will be influential because it will define how easy it is for employers to moot penalty claims and how vigorously plaintiffs must litigate continuation or recurrence.

## Conclusion

Bruce Cohen v. Consilio is not a doctrinal revolution, but it is an important waypoint. The Eighth Circuit preserved Laidlaw’s opening while sensibly insisting on a concrete factual showing before blessing a deterrence‑based theory of Article III redress in the MFLSA context. Remand will test whether courts treat employer fixes as durable or as too easily reversible. Practitioners on both sides should prepare for a focused, evidence‑driven jurisdictional fight: plaintiffs must prove a credible risk of recurrence that penalties would deter; defendants must provide durable, verifiable changes that make recurrence implausible. The district court’s answer will tell us whether Laidlaw remains a reliable bridge for private wage plaintiffs seeking public penalties, or whether that bridge is narrower than many had assumed.



# Pleading Precision Matters: MWTA Penalties and Late-Shifted Injunctive Relief

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio is a compact lesson in the dangers of procedural and pleading sloppiness in wage-and-hour litigation. At stake were statutory penalties under Minnesota wage laws and a last-minute request for an affirmative injunction to rescind an overtime policy. What the panel did was unsurprising: treat statutory text seriously when deciding who may recover penalties, police waiver when a party fails to develop an argument, and insist that new remedies be pled rather than sprung at summary judgment. For litigators on both sides, the opinion highlights how settlement tactics, stipulations, and late-stage shifts in theory of relief can evaporate claims or leave jurisdictional questions unanswered.

## What the Eighth Circuit decided

Cohen, a Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer, sued his employer for unpaid overtime and sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief under Minnesota law. After Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages (and the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime and liquidated damages he alleged), the district court granted summary judgment to the employer on all claims. The Eighth Circuit affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded.

On the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) claim, the court affirmed. The panel read Minn. Stat. §181.101(a) to reserve the power to demand and collect penalties to the Commissioner of Labor; the employee’s statutory role, by contrast, is to prosecute claims for wages. The court relied on plain‑meaning analysis and the legislature’s demonstrated ability in nearby provisions (e.g., §§181.13, 181.14) to authorize employee recovery of penalties when it intends to do so. Because §181.101’s penalty mechanism runs through the Commissioner, Cohen could not himself recover the “average daily wage” penalty he sought under that provision.

The court vacated and remanded the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) ruling because of a live jurisdictional concern. Although Cohen had been paid his overtime and liquidated damages, the statutory penalties he sought are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. That raises standing and mootness concerns: a plaintiff must have a continuing personal interest for the court to adjudicate a claim or a form of relief, and a case may be mooted if the defendant’s wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The panel declined to resolve jurisdiction on appeal and sent the question back to the district court to determine whether the case remains justiciable (for example, whether Consilio’s overtime policy is still in effect). A concurring judge expressed separate doubts about whether Cohen even has standing to pursue MFLSA penalties under Friends of the Earth, but agreed remand was appropriate.

Finally, Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) notice-change claim fared poorly. He stipulated he had been paid all wages due, pointed to no statutory authority that allows him to recover civil penalties under the MWTA provision he invoked, and offered little in the way of appellate argument. The Eighth Circuit treated that failure as waiver and affirmed dismissal. The court likewise refused to entertain Cohen’s late request—first raised at summary judgment—for a prospective injunction to rescind the 2019 overtime policy because new claims belong in amended complaints, not opposition briefs or summary judgment papers (citing Rule 15(a) and the court’s InfoDeli precedent).

## Why the court’s approach is defensible — and what still nags

The panel’s statutory construction is textbook: if the statute makes the Commissioner the actor who may demand payment and impose penalties, courts should not rewrite the remedial scheme to give private plaintiffs a cause of action the legislature declined to provide. The court’s reliance on parallel provisions where the legislature did empower employees to recover penalties reflects standard canons of statutory interpretation (expressio unius). That approach promotes predictability and prevents courts from creating de facto private enforcement tools Congress or a state legislature did not authorize.

The court’s procedural policing is equally sensible. The waiver doctrine exists to prevent parties from sandbagging the court with undeveloped arguments, and the principle that new claims must be pleaded (not sprung at the eleventh hour) protects defendants from ambush and ensures the district court has a proper complaint and opportunity to develop the facts.

That said, the decision illustrates recurring friction points in wage litigation. Defendants confronting class or collective actions often pay off wages to individual plaintiffs in an attempt to moot suits; plaintiffs can counter by preserving injunctive claims or demonstrating a reasonable expectation of recurrence. The Eighth Circuit correctly recognized that these are fact-dependent questions about jurisdiction and voluntary cessation. But the panel’s remand also underscores how readily litigants can jeopardize their own leverage—stipulating to payment without preserving live claims (or failing to plead injunctive relief) can leave plaintiffs with nothing to litigate.

The concurring judge’s skepticism about standing under Friends of the Earth is important. Friends of the Earth permits citizen plaintiffs to seek civil penalties payable to the government when the relief will deter ongoing or future violations; but whether statutory penalties that flow to a state commissioner fit neatly in that doctrine is not a universal conclusion. That unresolved question may invite further litigation.

## Practical lessons for litigators

First, plead your affirmative relief early. If injunctive relief—rescission, cease-and-desist, declaratory relief—is important to the client’s leverage or to preserving jurisdiction, make it a core part of the complaint and refine it in amendments, not in summary-judgment briefing.

Second, anchor statutory penalty theories in the statute’s remedial language. If the statute channels penalties to an administrative actor, anticipate and address the consequence that private recovery may be unavailable. If you want private penalties, show the statute authorizes them; do not rely on sparsely argued analogies.

Third, think hard before stipulating away wages and liquidated damages. A stipulation that eliminates all traditional economic injury may eliminate Article III standing for certain forms of relief unless there is a separately pled and viable prospective or public‑interest remedy. If you accept payment, preserve in writing any claims (or requests for relief) you intend to pursue—e.g., injunctive relief, statutory penalties—so the record supports continued jurisdiction.

Fourth, preserve the record about whether the challenged policy remains in force. If the employer asserts it has discontinued the policy, obtain discovery and a clear factual record. Voluntary cessation can moot a claim, but courts will not lightly accept a defendant’s say-so if there is a plausible inference the policy might recur.

Finally, don’t shortchange appellate briefing. Courts will treat underdeveloped or citation-less arguments as waived.

## Broader doctrinal and strategic implications

Cohen v. Consilio is unlikely to reset the law, but it will alter day-to-day practice. Employers may be more tempted to purchase early peace by paying wages and liquidated damages, betting that plaintiffs who fail to preserve injunctions or statutory hooks will be left with mooted claims. Plaintiffs’ counsel must adapt by pleading and preserving non-monetary relief and by building a record about ongoing policies. The opinion also leaves open a potentially important question about when a private plaintiff may litigate penalties payable to a state official under Friends of the Earth frameworks—the concurring opinion signals that further clarification could come, perhaps on a different factual record.

## Conclusion

The Eighth Circuit’s decision is a reminder that litigation posture and pleading choices matter as much as statutory merits. For plaintiffs, the takeaway is clear: if you need a remedy beyond money (or hope to avoid mootness by a defendant’s pay-off), plead and preserve that remedy from day one. For defendants, the case affirms that buy‑offs can change the calculus—but only if the plaintiff has not kept jurisdictional hooks alive. Precision in complaint drafting, careful stipulation strategy, and an early focus on what relief is truly necessary will determine who keeps the keys to the courthouse door.



# Minnesota’s Wage Enforcement Architecture After Cohen: Private Suits, Public Penalties, and Litigation Strategy

## Why this matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is an important reminder that statutory design matters as much as the merits in wage-and-hour litigation. Cohen draws a clear map of Minnesota’s hybrid enforcement system: private plaintiffs can sue for unpaid wages and state-law liquidated damages, but certain statutory penalties are channeled through the state’s labor commissioner. That split limits private plaintiffs’ leverage, gives the agency a gatekeeper role, and makes litigation strategy — pleading, timing, interaction with the agency, and the handling of settlements — decisive. The court’s vacatur and remand on the MFLSA penalties question further leaves open a live doctrinal issue: can a private plaintiff obtain Article III standing to pursue penalties that are payable to the state on the theory that such penalties would deter future violations?

## The decision in a nutshell

The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s summary judgment dismissal of Cohen’s claim for average daily wage penalties under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. § 181.101, concluding that the statute vests the Commissioner — not private employees — with authority to demand and collect those penalties. The panel also affirmed dismissal of Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) claim as waived for lack of developed argument. But the court vacated and remanded the district court’s grant of summary judgment on Cohen’s Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties claim, because jurisdictional questions about standing and mootness remain: Cohen stipulated that he had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, but the parties dispute whether Consilio’s challenged overtime policy remains in force. That factual uncertainty means the MFLSA penalties claim might or might not be moot under the Supreme Court’s voluntary-cessation and standing precedents. A concurring judge expressed particular doubt about whether deterrence-based standing can sustain a private plaintiff’s request for penalties payable to the Commissioner, but joined the remand so the district court could address mootness.

## The court’s reasoning

On the MPWA issue, the court relied on the statute’s text: section 181.101 contemplates the Commissioner serving a demand on the employer and, if wages are unpaid after ten days, the Commissioner “may charge and collect” wages and a penalty — with any money collected paid to the employee. The panel read that scheme as placing the penalty enforcement mechanism squarely with the Commissioner, noting that the legislature elsewhere expressly authorizes private employees to “charge and collect a penalty” when it intends to do so. That textual comparison supported the court’s conclusion that private plaintiffs cannot invoke § 181.101 to recover the statute’s “average daily wage” penalty directly.

On the MFLSA claims, the panel confronted Article III standing and mootness problems created by Consilio’s payment of wages and liquidated damages. The court recognized that civil penalties payable to the government can, in some contexts, “afford redress” to private plaintiffs by inducing discontinuation of unlawful conduct and deterring future violations (the reasoning in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw). But whether that logic suffices when penalties go to the state is a close question here, particularly because the parties had not fleshed out the issue and because whether the challenged policy remains in force is disputed. Accordingly, the court vacated and remanded for the district court to determine whether a live controversy exists.

## Why the MPWA holding matters in practice

What may look like a narrow statutory parsing has outsized practical effects. By reading § 181.101 to reserve penalty enforcement to the Commissioner, the Eighth Circuit curtailed a familiar source of leverage for private plaintiffs in wage cases. The threat of statutory penalties often pressures employers to settle quickly and on favorable terms to plaintiffs; when those penalties are not privately recoverable, defendants can be less vulnerable to aggressive private litigation tactics. At the same time, the ruling underlines that Minnesota’s legislature intended a hybrid enforcement architecture — private recovery of wages and liquidated damages, agency enforcement of certain penalties — with the attendant policy trade-offs: centralized enforcement can promote uniformity and conserve resources, but it can also reduce private incentives to vindicate statutory norms.

## The open question: deterrence-based standing for penalties payable to the state

The Eighth Circuit left unresolved an important doctrinal knot. Friends of the Earth permits plaintiffs to sue for civil penalties that are payable to the government when those penalties redress the plaintiffs’ injuries by causing the defendant to stop unlawful conduct or deter future violations. But doubts remain about transposing that environmental-law reasoning to wage statutes where penalties are paid to the Commissioner. On remand, plaintiffs will argue that the prospect of statutory penalties reduces the risk of future unpaid overtime and therefore constitutes redress; defendants will counter that any deterrent effect is too speculative, and that payment to the state means plaintiffs obtain no concrete benefit. The district court’s factual findings about whether an unlawful policy still exists will be central. If the policy remains, deterrence-based standing has a stronger case; if Consilio truly rescinded the policy and made structural changes, a court may find it “absolutely clear” the conduct won’t recur and dismiss on mootness grounds.

## Litigation and settlement strategy going forward

Cohen is a practical how-to for litigants. Plaintiffs should avoid narrow stipulations that concede all monetary relief without preserving claims for penalties and injunctive relief; they should plead injunctive and declaratory relief affirmatively and seek early discovery about ongoing policies. Coordinating with the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry can be essential where statutes channel penalties through the Commissioner: agency engagement may produce a demand under § 181.101 or administrative action that supplements private litigation. For class and collective actions, plaintiffs should push for early certification or bring multiple class representatives so a single pay-off to the named plaintiff cannot easily moot the whole case.

Defendants, by contrast, may sensibly cure unlawful pay practices and document structural changes to support a voluntary-cessation defense, but the Eighth Circuit’s invocation of the “absolutely clear” standard from Friends of the Earth warns that mere recission-by-attorney-declaration may not be enough. Employers who pay only the named plaintiff but leave a policy in place risk protracted litigation over mootness and injunctive claims.

## Doctrinal ripple effects

Cohen leaves open circuit-wide questions about standing when plaintiffs seek penalties payable to the state — an issue ripe for doctrinal development. Lower courts will now wrestle with whether Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale is transferrable and, if so, how fact-intensive the inquiry should be in wage cases. The decision also underscores that textualist statutory analysis can substantially shape enforcement incentives; legislatures seeking robust private enforcement should be explicit if penalties are to be recoverable by employees.

## Bottom line

Cohen is a timely reminder that statutory structure — who gets the money and who has the authority to enforce — can determine litigation outcomes as decisively as substantive fault. For Minnesota wage litigators, the lesson is operational: plead injunctive relief early, keep the controversy alive with factual proof of ongoing policies, coordinate with the Commissioner when penalties are at issue, and think carefully before accepting stipulations that extinguish the nominal injury. For employers, cure-and-document is a reasonable defense, but it is not a guaranteed get-out-of-litigation-free card. The Eighth Circuit clarified one pathway (MPWA penalties are the Commissioner’s), left another open (MFLSA penalties and Article III standing), and ensured that litigators on both sides will be strategizing around agency involvement, timing, and the careful framing of relief.
