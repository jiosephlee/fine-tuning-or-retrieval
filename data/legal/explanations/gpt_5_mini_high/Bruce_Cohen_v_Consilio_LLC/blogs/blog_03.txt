Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Pleading Precision Matters: MWTA Penalties and Late-Shifted Injunctive Relief

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio is a compact lesson in the dangers of procedural and pleading sloppiness in wage-and-hour litigation. At stake were statutory penalties under Minnesota wage laws and a last-minute request for an affirmative injunction to rescind an overtime policy. What the panel did was unsurprising: treat statutory text seriously when deciding who may recover penalties, police waiver when a party fails to develop an argument, and insist that new remedies be pled rather than sprung at summary judgment. For litigators on both sides, the opinion highlights how settlement tactics, stipulations, and late-stage shifts in theory of relief can evaporate claims or leave jurisdictional questions unanswered.

## What the Eighth Circuit decided

Cohen, a Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer, sued his employer for unpaid overtime and sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief under Minnesota law. After Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages (and the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime and liquidated damages he alleged), the district court granted summary judgment to the employer on all claims. The Eighth Circuit affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded.

On the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) claim, the court affirmed. The panel read Minn. Stat. §181.101(a) to reserve the power to demand and collect penalties to the Commissioner of Labor; the employee’s statutory role, by contrast, is to prosecute claims for wages. The court relied on plain‑meaning analysis and the legislature’s demonstrated ability in nearby provisions (e.g., §§181.13, 181.14) to authorize employee recovery of penalties when it intends to do so. Because §181.101’s penalty mechanism runs through the Commissioner, Cohen could not himself recover the “average daily wage” penalty he sought under that provision.

The court vacated and remanded the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) ruling because of a live jurisdictional concern. Although Cohen had been paid his overtime and liquidated damages, the statutory penalties he sought are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. That raises standing and mootness concerns: a plaintiff must have a continuing personal interest for the court to adjudicate a claim or a form of relief, and a case may be mooted if the defendant’s wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The panel declined to resolve jurisdiction on appeal and sent the question back to the district court to determine whether the case remains justiciable (for example, whether Consilio’s overtime policy is still in effect). A concurring judge expressed separate doubts about whether Cohen even has standing to pursue MFLSA penalties under Friends of the Earth, but agreed remand was appropriate.

Finally, Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) notice-change claim fared poorly. He stipulated he had been paid all wages due, pointed to no statutory authority that allows him to recover civil penalties under the MWTA provision he invoked, and offered little in the way of appellate argument. The Eighth Circuit treated that failure as waiver and affirmed dismissal. The court likewise refused to entertain Cohen’s late request—first raised at summary judgment—for a prospective injunction to rescind the 2019 overtime policy because new claims belong in amended complaints, not opposition briefs or summary judgment papers (citing Rule 15(a) and the court’s InfoDeli precedent).

## Why the court’s approach is defensible — and what still nags

The panel’s statutory construction is textbook: if the statute makes the Commissioner the actor who may demand payment and impose penalties, courts should not rewrite the remedial scheme to give private plaintiffs a cause of action the legislature declined to provide. The court’s reliance on parallel provisions where the legislature did empower employees to recover penalties reflects standard canons of statutory interpretation (expressio unius). That approach promotes predictability and prevents courts from creating de facto private enforcement tools Congress or a state legislature did not authorize.

The court’s procedural policing is equally sensible. The waiver doctrine exists to prevent parties from sandbagging the court with undeveloped arguments, and the principle that new claims must be pleaded (not sprung at the eleventh hour) protects defendants from ambush and ensures the district court has a proper complaint and opportunity to develop the facts.

That said, the decision illustrates recurring friction points in wage litigation. Defendants confronting class or collective actions often pay off wages to individual plaintiffs in an attempt to moot suits; plaintiffs can counter by preserving injunctive claims or demonstrating a reasonable expectation of recurrence. The Eighth Circuit correctly recognized that these are fact-dependent questions about jurisdiction and voluntary cessation. But the panel’s remand also underscores how readily litigants can jeopardize their own leverage—stipulating to payment without preserving live claims (or failing to plead injunctive relief) can leave plaintiffs with nothing to litigate.

The concurring judge’s skepticism about standing under Friends of the Earth is important. Friends of the Earth permits citizen plaintiffs to seek civil penalties payable to the government when the relief will deter ongoing or future violations; but whether statutory penalties that flow to a state commissioner fit neatly in that doctrine is not a universal conclusion. That unresolved question may invite further litigation.

## Practical lessons for litigators

First, plead your affirmative relief early. If injunctive relief—rescission, cease-and-desist, declaratory relief—is important to the client’s leverage or to preserving jurisdiction, make it a core part of the complaint and refine it in amendments, not in summary-judgment briefing.

Second, anchor statutory penalty theories in the statute’s remedial language. If the statute channels penalties to an administrative actor, anticipate and address the consequence that private recovery may be unavailable. If you want private penalties, show the statute authorizes them; do not rely on sparsely argued analogies.

Third, think hard before stipulating away wages and liquidated damages. A stipulation that eliminates all traditional economic injury may eliminate Article III standing for certain forms of relief unless there is a separately pled and viable prospective or public‑interest remedy. If you accept payment, preserve in writing any claims (or requests for relief) you intend to pursue—e.g., injunctive relief, statutory penalties—so the record supports continued jurisdiction.

Fourth, preserve the record about whether the challenged policy remains in force. If the employer asserts it has discontinued the policy, obtain discovery and a clear factual record. Voluntary cessation can moot a claim, but courts will not lightly accept a defendant’s say-so if there is a plausible inference the policy might recur.

Finally, don’t shortchange appellate briefing. Courts will treat underdeveloped or citation-less arguments as waived.

## Broader doctrinal and strategic implications

Cohen v. Consilio is unlikely to reset the law, but it will alter day-to-day practice. Employers may be more tempted to purchase early peace by paying wages and liquidated damages, betting that plaintiffs who fail to preserve injunctions or statutory hooks will be left with mooted claims. Plaintiffs’ counsel must adapt by pleading and preserving non-monetary relief and by building a record about ongoing policies. The opinion also leaves open a potentially important question about when a private plaintiff may litigate penalties payable to a state official under Friends of the Earth frameworks—the concurring opinion signals that further clarification could come, perhaps on a different factual record.

## Conclusion

The Eighth Circuit’s decision is a reminder that litigation posture and pleading choices matter as much as statutory merits. For plaintiffs, the takeaway is clear: if you need a remedy beyond money (or hope to avoid mootness by a defendant’s pay-off), plead and preserve that remedy from day one. For defendants, the case affirms that buy‑offs can change the calculus—but only if the plaintiff has not kept jurisdictional hooks alive. Precision in complaint drafting, careful stipulation strategy, and an early focus on what relief is truly necessary will determine who keeps the keys to the courthouse door.
