Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### How did Coleman preserve appellate review of the suppression ruling after pleading guilty?
Question:
The defendant pled guilty but reserved the right to appeal the suppression denial. Am I right that this is a conditional plea under Fed. R. Crim. P. 11(a)(2)? What exactly must the parties and district court do to make that reservation effective (government consent, written specification of issues, etc.)? If he had entered an unconditional plea, would the suppression issue have been waived as a nonjurisdictional defect? Does the standard of review on appeal change in any way because of the conditional plea?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — what Coleman did is the sort of “conditional” guilty plea that Fed. R. Crim. P. 11(a)(2) permits: he pled guilty while expressly reserving the right to appeal the district court’s denial of his suppression motion. The Seventh Circuit accepted that reservation and reviewed the suppression ruling on the merits, applying the ordinary appellate standards (the court treated the voluntariness/scope findings as factual and reviewed them for clear error). See the opinion’s description of the plea and its statement of the standard of review (quoted below).

What Rule 11(a)(2) requires (practical checklist)
The Coleman opinion does not set out the plea colloquy or the written plea agreement, but the mechanics to make a reservation effective under Rule 11(a)(2) are well settled and are the steps you should check in the record:

- Make the reservation explicit and limited. The reservation must identify the specific pretrial ruling preserved (here, the denial of the motion to suppress). Rule 11(a)(2) contemplates a reservation “limited to a specified pretrial ruling.”  
- Put the reservation in the record (and typically in the written plea agreement). The Rule speaks of a conditional plea “reserving in writing the right” to appeal a specified pretrial ruling; at minimum the reservation should appear on the plea-agreement and/or in the plea colloquy so the appellate record is clear.  
- Have the district court accept the conditional plea. The Rule allows an appeal only if the court accepts the plea — if the court refuses to accept the plea it can reject the reservation.  
- Government “consent.” Rule 11(a)(2)’s text does not itself require that the government assent, but in practice plea agreements commonly state the government’s position and the government often signs off on the reservation. If the government opposes the reservation the court may still accept or reject the plea; whether the government’s consent is necessary in a particular circuit can be an issue of local practice or precedent. (Coleman’s opinion does not describe whether or how the government’s position was reflected in the plea paperwork; it simply reports that Coleman “ple[d] guilty while reserving his right on appeal.”)

Would an unconditional plea have waived the suppression issue?
The Coleman opinion does not analyze that hypothetical, but the practical point is why defendants use conditional pleas: an unconditional guilty plea ordinarily forecloses appellate review of antecedent, non‑jurisdictional rulings (including many Fourth Amendment suppression rulings) because the plea admits the facts and ends the controversy as to those pre‑trial errors. By reserving the suppression issue in a conditional plea the defendant preserves appellate review of that specific ruling — which is exactly what happened here.

Did the conditional plea change the standard of review?
No. The conditional plea simply preserved the issue for appeal; it did not change the usual standards the court applies. As this Court explained in Coleman, the Seventh Circuit reviewed the district court’s finding that Lisa’s consent was voluntary and the scope-of-consent question under its ordinary review regime: factual findings for clear error, legal questions de novo. The opinion puts it succinctly: “As a court of review, our role is more limited. We review a district court’s finding of voluntary consent and any challenge to its scope for clear error.” United States v. Coleman, No. 24‑3051, slip op. at § II(A) (7th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).

Takeaway for practice
If you want to preserve a suppression ruling after pleading guilty: (1) put an explicit, limited reservation in the plea agreement and/or on the record; (2) identify the exact ruling you’re reserving; (3) get the district court to accept the conditional plea; and (4) if possible, have the government’s position recorded in the agreement (or on the record) so there is no dispute on appeal that a conditional plea was intended. Coleman shows that when those steps are effectively done, the court will hear the preserved suppression issue and apply the ordinary appellate standards.
