Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Deference Reaffirmed: The D.C. Circuit’s Easy Path to Exemption 7(A) in National-Security Files

## Why this case matters
Foad Farahi v. FBI is the D.C. Circuit’s latest reminder that, in national‑security and counterterrorism FOIA disputes, courts remain inclined to give the FBI wide berth. The panel affirmed that an agency’s public attestation that an investigation is “ongoing,” supplemented by sealed, ex parte detail, can satisfy Exemption 7(A)’s twin requirements — that records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The opinion matters because it shows how the combination of agency status, precedent about investigatory secrecy, and acceptance of ex parte submissions together make it difficult for requesters to pry loose sensitive law‑enforcement files.

## The court’s reasoning in brief
The D.C. Circuit framed the analysis in familiar terms. Exemption 7(A) applies only to records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and only where disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its factual declarations are entitled to deference — but they still must establish a “rational nexus” between the records and law‑enforcement duties and show a connection between the subject and a possible security risk.

Farahi’s FOIA request produced more than 10,000 pages. The FBI first submitted a public declaration from David Hardy and a sealed, ex parte declaration with additional factual detail. After the district court expressed concern about staleness, the FBI supplemented with public and sealed declarations from Joseph Bender confirming that investigations remained ongoing. The district court — having already concluded the FBI “seemingly” met its burdens on the initial submissions — accepted the updates and found that the sealed material also adequately explained why redaction could not yield any “meaningful” releasable information. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, relying on CREW, Juarez, CNSS, Mead Data and related D.C. Circuit precedents: an agency can meet Exemption 7(A) by attesting to the existence of a continuing investigation; ex parte, sealed declarations may supply the necessary particularity; and where exempt and nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined, withholding in full can be justified.

On segregability the court reiterated Mead Data’s insight that FOIA is about releasing meaningful information, not isolated words or phrases, and accepted the district court’s conclusion — supported by the sealed submission — that the nonexempt snippets were not reasonably segregable.

## What to make of it
Two practical themes emerge. First, the opinion double‑downed on the D.C. Circuit’s long‑standing deference to agency declarations in national‑security contexts, while also hewing to CREW’s admonition that mere boilerplate may not suffice. In practice that means agencies still win so long as they (1) provide a public declaration establishing the basic law‑enforcement nexus and (2) back it up in camera or ex parte with the operational detail the public filing cannot safely disclose. The court explicitly treated the Bender submission as an update to, not a replacement for, the earlier Hardy declarations — a useful template for agencies facing the district court’s understandable concern about stale assertions.

Second, the panel declined to import any additional layer of “foreseeable harm” analysis from the FOIA Improvement Act into Exemption 7(A) showings. The court treated 7(A)’s statutory language — that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere” — as the operative interference standard, rather than requiring the agency to recast its case under the FOIAIA’s discretionary‑release foreseeability rubric. That reading keeps the pre‑existing Exemption 7(A) framework intact: FOIAIA’s foreseeability rules govern discretionary releases of nonexempt material, but they do not add a separate burden on an agency that invokes a specific statutory exemption whose text already requires a showing of interference.

Both features — acceptance of sealed ex parte detail and refusal to tack a separate FOIAIA foreseeability test onto 7(A) — make it harder for FOIA plaintiffs to win in counterterrorism investigations. Ex parte submissions are inherently asymmetric: the agency can provide the detail the court needs without disclosing it publicly, and the requester cannot meaningfully rebut those facts without discovery or other avenues the court may not allow.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Farahi reinforces several settled doctrinal lines in the D.C. Circuit. It reaffirms the “rational nexus” requirement for 7(A) but shows that the evidentiary bar to establishing that nexus remains modest so long as the agency supplies non‑boilerplate factual support somewhere on the record — even if some of that support is sealed. It reconfirms that the “pending or reasonably anticipated” formulation from CREW and Juarez can be met by agency attestations of continuing investigation, and that courts will accept updates rather than require a full re‑run of the initial evidentiary showing every time.

For FOIA practitioners, the decision has predictable practical consequences. Agencies will be encouraged to rely on an interplay of public and sealed declarations: provide enough in public to survive a judicial gloss (and to prevent a CREW‑style rejection for vagueness), and then fill in the sensitive operational particulars ex parte. Plaintiffs must press early — contest categorization and segregability in the district court, demand a sufficiently specific public declaration, and avoid forfeiture by preserving arguments rather than saving them for appeal. When ex parte submissions are unavoidable, litigants should press for as much in camera review as the court will permit, and for an explicit ruling on why particular items are nonsegregable.

From a transparency perspective, Farahi underscores the tension at FOIA’s core: courts will protect genuine investigative and national‑security interests, but in doing so they necessarily limit the public’s ability to test the government’s assertions. D.C. Circuit precedent continues to strike that balance in favor of operational secrecy where national security concerns are credibly shown.

## Conclusion
Farahi does not change the law, but it illustrates how existing precedent allows the FBI to meet Exemption 7(A) without exposing operational details to the public record. The D.C. Circuit required updated, non‑stale assertions — a modest protect against boilerplate — but otherwise leaned on deference, sealed substantiation, and the established interference standard in 7(A). For FOIA requesters seeking national‑security records, the lesson is practical: press for public particularity early, litigate segregability and category definitions in the district court, and be prepared that operational detail proving the agency’s case may remain invisible to both the public and the requester.



# When FOIA Records Grow Old: Managing Staleness Through Updated Declarations

## Why this case matters

Farahi v. FBI is a useful, practical decision about a recurring procedural problem in FOIA litigation: what happens when the agency’s showing that disclosure would “interfere with enforcement proceedings” becomes stale while a case inches through the courts. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion affirms two sensible propositions that practitioners should internalize. First, district courts may and should require agencies to refresh factual declarations when significant time has passed. Second, a concise status “update” can be adequate — but only when read in tandem with earlier, detailed submissions (including sealed ex parte material where necessary). For requesters and agencies alike, the case clarifies how to litigate and defend Exemption 7(A) claims over the long haul.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

Foad Farahi sought his FBI file; the FBI located roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs and withheld most of the material under Exemption 7(A), which covers law-enforcement records whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” After the government’s initial summary-judgment submission in 2019 (the Hardy declaration), the district court — mindful of the many intervening years — denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered an updated assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still “pending or reasonably anticipated.”

The FBI returned with two things: an ex parte, sealed elaboration and a public, concise update from a different RIDS official (the Bender declaration) confirming that the investigation “remain[ed] ongoing.” The district court credited that combination; the D.C. Circuit (Judge Katsas) affirmed. The panel emphasized three points that governed its conclusion: (1) Exemption 7(A) requires that proceedings be “pending or reasonably anticipated,” but courts may credit an attestation that an investigation exists and is ongoing; (2) the FBI (a law-enforcement agency) gets deference, especially in national-security contexts; and (3) where nonexempt material is scattered line-by-line through records, the agency can justify withholding on segregability grounds by showing the nonexempt content is inextricably intertwined with exempt material.

Importantly, the court acknowledged CREW’s caution that conclusory or “vague” assertions about ongoing investigations can fail. It held, however, that Bender’s terse confirmation was not considered in isolation; it functioned as a status update to the earlier, more detailed Hardy submissions and to the sealed ex parte material explaining the nature and scope of the investigation. That contextual reading rendered the update adequate.

## What makes an updated declaration adequate?

Farahi teaches a straightforward rule of thumb: an update need not be a full reprise of the original affidavit, but it must do one of two things. If it stands alone, it must provide the sort of particularized, nonconclusory information CREW requires (who, what, when, and why continued investigative activity makes release risky). If it is meant only to confirm that nothing material has changed since the earlier, detailed showing, then it must be clear on its face that it is an update and must be anchored to the earlier specifics (or to sealed ex parte elaboration that the court has seen).

Concretely, an adequate update will at minimum (a) identify the earlier submission(s) it is updating; (b) state with contemporaneous specificity that the investigation or anticipation of proceedings continues; and (c) either rearticulate — or incorporate by reference and supplement with sealed detail — the operational reasons why disclosure would interfere (e.g., tip-offs to subjects, compromise of investigative methods, jeopardizing evidence gathering). On segregability, an agency should explain why releasable material is intermingled in ways that make redaction impracticable or revealing. A bare, unexplained “it’s ongoing” will often be enough only if it is explicitly tied to an earlier affidavit that itself supplied the necessary particulars.

## Practical guidance for litigants

For FOIA requesters: do not treat an agency’s updated, bare-bones statement as the end of the story. If significant time has lapsed, press the district court for a fresh, particularized showing. Challenge conclusory updates under CREW and ask for in camera review of sealed material where possible. And preserve objections: Farahi’s challenge to the government’s categorical descriptions of records was deemed forfeited because he failed to meaningfully press those arguments below — a reminder to press every critical objection at the district-court stage.

For agencies: anticipate staleness. Draft initial declarations with enough operational specificity to support Exemption 7(A) well into the future, and preserve the investigative chain-of-command so an agent can confirm ongoing activity later. When the court orders a status refresh, provide a concise public update that identifies and ties back to the earlier, detailed affidavit and, if necessary, submit a sealed ex parte supplement explaining operational particulars and segregability. That combination — careful anchoring plus confidential elaboration — is exactly what the D.C. Circuit endorsed here.

Strategically, plaintiffs should think early about how to limit the use of ex parte filings: opposing them at the district court, seeking protective orders that permit counsel-only review, or requesting limited in camera inspection may reduce the asymmetry that sealed material creates.

## Doctrinal implications and likely downstream effects

Farahi does not rewrite Exemption 7(A). The decision reaffirms the settled standard — proceedings must be pending or reasonably anticipated — and reiterates the need for a “reasonably specific” government showing. Its doctrinal contribution is more procedural than substantive: it clarifies that courts should police staleness by ordering refreshers, and that an update can be modest if it is explicitly supplemental to a previously detailed showing or accompanied by sealed elaboration. That calibration preserves CREW’s protections against conclusory assertions while recognizing practical realities in national-security and counterterrorism contexts, where public disclosure of detail would itself cause harm.

We should expect several downstream effects. Agencies will be more likely to bolster initial declarations and to maintain seals with modular updates in mind. Requesters, for their part, will more frequently press for periodic certifications and contest the sufficiency of terse updates. And courts will continue to rely on ex parte and in camera procedures, balancing fairness against the core FOIA tension: the public’s right to know versus the government's obligation to protect ongoing investigations.

## Bottom line

Farahi is a reminder that FOIA battles are not won on paper once and for all; time is a litigant. District courts have tools to force agencies to refresh their factual showings when delay risks rendering those showings obsolete, and agencies can meet that obligation without repeating every prior detail if they clearly tie a short update to an earlier, particularized affidavit (and, when necessary, a sealed ex parte elaboration). For lawyers on both sides, the decision is a practical prompt: think now about how you will document, preserve, and, if required, refresh the factual predicate for Exemption 7(A) several years down the road.



# Segregability After Perioperative: Why 10,750 Pages and 80 CDs Yielded “No Meaningful Information”

## Why this case matters

Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation (D.C. Cir., Sept. 30, 2025) is a useful, and in some ways sobering, reminder of how the D.C. Circuit today thinks about segregability when national-security law‑enforcement records are at stake. At issue was not whether Exemption 7(A) could apply to a terrorism‑related investigation — the court gave the FBI deference on that point — but whether the agency nonetheless had to produce snippets of non‑exempt material from a massive collection: 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. The court affirmed that when non‑exempt words or phrases are scattered line‑by‑line through a document and meaningful information cannot be extracted without revealing the exempt context, an agency can withhold the lot. That holding tightens the practical bite of Mead’s “meaningful information” principle and ratifies Perioperative’s acceptance of sealed, ex parte submissions as a device for courts to evaluate segregability in sensitive cases.

For FOIA requesters, journalists, and immigration or criminal defendants whose cases implicate national‑security investigations, Farahi highlights both the limits of segregability as a transparency tool and the procedural levers plaintiffs must use early and deliberately if they hope to pry loose anything from voluminous agency files.

## What the court did (briefly)

Foad Farahi sought his FBI file; the FBI located over ten thousand pages and dozens of CDs and withheld most of it under Exemption 7(A). The district court — mindful of the passage of time — required the FBI to update stale declarations. The FBI responded with a mix of public declarations and sealed, ex parte declarations that described the ongoing terrorism investigation and explained why the withheld material was not reasonably segregable. The district court credited those explanations and denied relief. On appeal the D.C. Circuit affirmed.

Two features of that path matter for the segregability question. First, the court relied on the layered record of public and ex parte declarations: an initial detailed sealed declaration and later, terser public updates that confirmed nothing relevant had changed. Second, the court applied Mead’s line that “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down,” concluding that isolated words or phrases scattered throughout a document are not necessarily “reasonably segregable.”

## The court’s segregability analysis — Mead, Juarez, Perioperative

Farahi’s opinion strings together three familiar principles. Exemption 7(A) requires an agency to show documents were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are pending or reasonably anticipated. When withholding is asserted, FOIA nonetheless demands that agencies take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release non‑exempt information. But Mead long ago explained that FOIA’s focus is real information; if the only releasable material in a document would be isolated words or meaningless fragments, a court need not force a line‑by‑line redaction that would yield no useful information while risking exposure of context.

Juarez and related D.C. Circuit authority permit courts to rely on agency affidavits that explain, with reasonable specificity, why disclosure would interfere with proceedings and why non‑exempt material is not meaningfully separable. Perioperative, decided in 2023, endorsed the use of sealed, ex parte submissions in particularly sensitive FOIA cases where public disclosure of the government’s basis for withholding would itself reveal protected information. Farahi applies that framework: the FBI’s sealed and public declarations together explained the nature and scope of an ongoing terrorism investigation, and the court concluded that small non‑exempt fragments, scattered line‑by‑line across the records, would not amount to “meaningful information” and therefore were not “reasonably segregable.”

The opinion also underscores the district court’s power to require updated declarations when the agency’s original affidavits age — a useful check against stale ex parte claims — and it accepts the practical reality that, where an agency has provided a detailed sealed showing, a later, short public update can suffice as a status confirmation without repeating the sensitive substance.

## Why the decision matters in practice

Two practical realities flow from Farahi.

First, Mead + Perioperative gives the government a defensible way to protect large troves of material: if an agency can show (1) a law‑enforcement purpose and ongoing proceedings that would be jeopardized by disclosure and (2) that any non‑exempt material is so interspersed with exempt matter that only isolated words or phrases would remain, a court need not impose burdensome line‑by‑line redactions. In national‑security and counterterrorism contexts, where context often is the substance that makes a passage revealing, that rationale is especially persuasive.

Second, the opinion shows how a layered, sealed‑then‑public strategy can operate procedurally. The FBI provided a detailed sealed declaration and then a brief public confirmation that the investigation remained ongoing. The court treated the latter as an update to the earlier detailed sealed showing. Plaintiffs who consent to sealed, ex parte filings — as Farahi did with respect to at least some of the FBI’s submissions — lose a key procedural argument on appeal about reliance on sealed material.

## What FOIA litigants should do differently (and early)

Farahi suggests several practical moves for requesters who want to push back more effectively:

- Resist consents to ex parte or sealed submissions unless you have no alternative; if the court insists on sealed filings, ask for counsel‑only access under protective order or for in camera review by the judge and, where appropriate, for an independent amicus to receive sealed material so the government’s claims can be adversarially tested.
- Demand a detailed public Vaughn‑style index or as much public detail as possible up front; insist that the agency explain categorically and with specificity how documents were grouped and why each category is covered by the claimed exemption.
- Challenge categorical descriptions early and specifically. CREW and Gallant require categories to be “sufficiently distinct” to permit meaningful review; a bare, after‑the‑fact assertion that documents fall into broad buckets is an argument that courts will scrutinize if challengers preserve it.
- Seek sampling and in camera review of representative pages if the agency claims pervasive interspersion; a judge can review a sample to assess whether isolated disclosures would actually be meaningful.
- Narrow your request where practicable (by timeframe, custodian, file type) to reduce the opportunity for an agency to claim that responsive non‑exempt matter is hopelessly scattered.
- When litigation drags on, press for updated public declarations rather than accepting the convenience of a single sealed showing long in the past.

Those procedural choices shift the leverage back toward litigants: the more an agency is forced to justify its categories and to put a public record in play, the harder it will be for courts to rest an outcome purely on sealed assertions that the non‑exempt material is meaningless.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Farahi does not change the law, but it clarifies how the existing precedents operate in practice. By reiterating Mead’s “meaningful information” test and invoking Perioperative to bless ex parte showings in sensitive cases, the decision makes it more likely that courts will accept wholesale withholdings in circumstances where non‑exempt nuggets are genuinely embedded and contextual. That is a defensible result from a national‑security perspective, but it narrows FOIA’s remedial horizon for those seeking to understand their own files or to pry loose information about investigations.

At the same time, Farahi preserves some knobs that courts and plaintiffs can turn against overbroad secrecy: the district court’s insistence on updated declarations, the requirement that categories be sufficiently distinct, and the availability of in camera review and sampling are all tools for meaningful oversight. How aggressively courts use those tools will shape whether Farahi is read primarily as an agency win or as a manageable gloss on Mead and Perioperative.

## Conclusion

Farahi is a useful handbook example of how segregability doctrine operates at the intersection of Exemption 7(A), Mead’s “meaningful information” standard, and Perioperative’s acceptance of sealed submissions. For agencies, it confirms a defensible path to protect large investigative files when non‑exempt matter is scattered and context is the key. For requesters, it underscores the need to be procedurally aggressive: do not casually accede to sealed filings; demand public detail and precise category challenges; and ask courts for sampling or in camera review so that the adversary system can do its work even in national‑security cases. Without those moves, Farahi suggests, “no meaningful information” may too often be the end of the story.



# FOIA as Not-Discovery: The Farahi Case at the Intersection of Immigration and Counterterrorism

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is a useful reminder of two blunt truths at the intersection of FOIA, immigration adjudication, and national-security investigations: FOIA is not a discovery device for live law‑enforcement probes, and courts will give the FBI substantial deference when it says disclosure would jeopardize an ongoing counterterrorism investigation. That matters in concrete ways for noncitizens who confront allegations drawn from secret investigative files in removal proceedings — and who naturally try to pry those files loose through FOIA to vindicate their immigration or asylum claims.

Foad Farahi, an Iranian national facing removal, sought his FBI file after the Department of Homeland Security relied on FBI materials and agent testimony in his immigration case. The FBI located more than 10,000 pages of potentially responsive material but withheld most of it under FOIA exemptions. The D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI, holding that Exemption 7(A) — which covers records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” — shielded the records from disclosure. The opinion is compact, but it crystallizes how courts evaluate agency declarations, the role of sealed ex parte submissions, and the limits of FOIA when an investigation is live.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The court’s analysis tracked the familiar Exemption 7(A) framework. First, because the records were compiled by the FBI — a law‑enforcement agency — the court afforded the agency “a fair measure of deference” on whether the nexus to law‑enforcement existed. The public and sealed declarations from FBI officials (the Hardy and Bender statements) showed the files were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national‑security and terrorism‑related violations, satisfying the nexus requirement.

Second, the court concluded that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” because the government credibly represented that investigations were ongoing and that disclosure could tip off targets about the scope and methods of those investigations. The panel applied the settled D.C. Circuit lines in CREW and Juarez: ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings,” and so long as the investigation is collecting evidence for a possible future criminal case, premature public release can be blocked by Exemption 7(A). The district court had required the FBI to update its possibly stale declarations; the FBI did so by supplementing the public declaration and filing more detailed material ex parte and under seal. The court accepted the renewed showing, emphasizing that the terse public update should be read in the context of the earlier, more detailed submissions.

Finally, on segregability, the court accepted the FBI’s securely submitted explanation that non‑exempt material was inextricably interwoven with exempt content (i.e., interspersed line‑by‑line), such that meaningful disclosure was not “reasonably segregable.” The court therefore sustained withholding in bulk, consistent with Mead Data and later D.C. Circuit guidance.

## The analytical take: transparency, deference, and fairness

Farahi reaffirms some predictable doctrinal points: Exemption 7(A) is capacious in national‑security contexts; agency affidavits are entitled to deference; and courts will accept sealed, ex parte submissions where necessary to avoid public disclosure of information that the government says would sabotage an investigation. But a few tensions in the opinion are worth emphasizing.

First, the decision underscores that public airing of allegations in one forum (here, immigration court) does not automatically vitiate the government’s interest in keeping an investigative corpus secret. The opinion notes that many of the allegations against Farahi had been introduced in immigration proceedings, but that fact did not make the FBI’s entire investigative file safe to disclose. That outcome flows from the basic point that “allegations” or isolated pieces of evidence are not the same as the broader investigative context, sources, methods, and assessment processes that disclosure could reveal.

Second, and relatedly, the opinion highlights the practical limits of FOIA for people who need information about what the government has used against them. A FOIA request can be an important tool, but when an investigation is demonstrably ongoing — and the agency provides the kind of (sometimes sealed) factual proffer that courts accept — FOIA cannot function as discovery. That creates a real predicament for defense counsel in immigration and other civil enforcement contexts: the government may rely on information you cannot see, and FOIA will often not unblock it midstream.

Third, the court’s reliance on sealed, ex parte declarations — while doctrinally permissible and sometimes unavoidable — raises fairness concerns. The court noted that Farahi did not oppose the use of ex parte submissions, and the D.C. Circuit has repeatedly recognized that such filings are inherent where secrecy must be preserved. Still, ex parte submissions limit the challenger’s ability to test the government’s factual claims. Practitioners and courts should therefore press for procedural safeguards where possible: timely, reasonably detailed public declarations; in‑camera review by the judge; and, where feasible, a sufficiently informative Vaughn‑type index that allows meaningful judicial review without gratuitous disclosure.

## Downstream effects and practical advice

For noncitizens and their counsel, Farahi has several practical implications. Expect law‑enforcement agencies to resist FOIA searches for investigative files while probes — especially national‑security probes — remain active. If FOIA is your chosen route, press early and expect the government to file updated declarations; courts will ask for current information about whether an investigation remains “ongoing.” Consider paralleling FOIA litigation with efforts in the underlying proceeding to obtain the materials the government actually used (for example, seeking disclosure in immigration court, asking DHS to produce particular documents, or requesting in‑camera review). That is no silver bullet — immigration courts have different discovery rules and lack criminal‑court procedures like CIPA — but it is often a more promising way to get material that is crucial to the merits of an asylum or removal defense.

For transparency advocates, Farahi is a reminder that public oversight of counterterrorism investigations will often come too late for subjects of investigations. Where the government legitimately shows that investigations remain active, whistleblowing and post‑investigation disclosure become the principal routes to public accountability. For courts, the case underscores the continuing need to police agency declarations for specificity; judges should require greater particularity when the public record gives reason to doubt the continued need for secrecy.

## Takeaway

Farahi is a doctrinally orthodox decision that puts a fine point on an important practical rule: FOIA is not a midstream discovery tool for live law‑enforcement investigations, and the FBI can rely on Exemption 7(A) — supported by timely and sufficiently detailed declarations, including sealed ex parte submissions where justified — to shield investigative files. That gives agencies breathing room to pursue sensitive probes, but it also sharpens the dilemma faced by noncitizens and others who must litigate against evidence they cannot see. Counsel should plan for that reality: seek alternative disclosure routes in the underlying proceedings, press courts for meaningful (if sealed) judicial review of agency claims, and attack stale or conclusory declarations aggressively.
