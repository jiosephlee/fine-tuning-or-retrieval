Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What makes 7(A) withholding categories “sufficiently distinct,” and what did Farahi have to do to avoid forfeiting his challenge?
Question:
The panel notes that when an agency withholds by category, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to let the court assess the exemption’s application, citing Gallant and CREW. But it then says Farahi “forfeited” his overbreadth challenge by not explaining why the FBI’s categories were too broad. For preservation purposes:
- What level of specificity must a requester provide to challenge categories—do they need to identify particular categories and explain how they sweep in non-interfering records, or propose narrower alternatives?
- Does the challenge need to be raised in the district court with citations to the agency’s category definitions, or can it be developed on appeal?
- Any examples of category definitions that courts have found impermissibly broad under 7(A)?
Answer:
Short answer
- “Sufficiently distinct” means the agency’s category labels and definitions must be specific enough for a court to decide whether the claimed exemption fits each category. Farahi v. FBI quotes that standard directly: categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” Farahi, slip op. § IV.C (quoting CREW/Gallant).
- To avoid forfeiture you must develop a concrete overbreadth challenge below: identify the particular FBI category(ies) you’re attacking, point to how those category definitions (as the agency stated them in its declarations/filings) sweep in non‑interfering (nonexempt) material, and explain why the court therefore cannot apply Exemption 7(A) on a category‑wide basis. The D.C. Circuit rejected Farahi’s claim because he “provides no specific explanation” and therefore “forfeited his arguments on this score.” Farahi, § IV.C (citing Khine).
- The Farahi opinion itself does not give concrete examples of category definitions that courts have held impermissibly broad; it simply states the standard and cites CREW and Gallant. If you need examples, you must look to those precedents cited in Farahi.

Why the court treated Farahi’s point as forfeited
- The panel applied the familiar preservation/forfeiture rule: an appellate court will not consider a claim that was not developed with specificity below. Farahi failed to explain, with reference to the FBI’s own category definitions or declarations, why those categories were overbroad; the court therefore treated the contention as forfeited. See Farahi, § IV.C.

What a preserved, workable overbreadth challenge looks like (practical checklist grounded in Farahi)
- Point to the agency’s category definitions as they appear in the record (the agency’s declarations or other briefing). The Farahi court’s holding rests on the absence of any “specific explanation” tied to those definitions. Farahi, § IV.C.
- For each category you challenge: explain concretely how the category’s stated scope would include non‑exempt material (for example, show particular types/pages in the record that appear non‑interfering and nonetheless fall under the agency’s category label).
- Explain why those category boundaries prevent a court from assessing the exemption’s application (i.e., they are not “sufficiently distinct” under CREW/Gallant as quoted in Farahi).
- If available, offer narrower formulations or segregation/redaction proposals to show how the agency could define categories that would permit judicial review (Farahi does not require proposing alternatives, but a concrete alternative makes clear how the current category is overbroad and helps the court perform the required assessment).
- Make these arguments in the district court, with citation to the agency’s declarations and any sample pages or exhibits; do not reserve the argument for the first time on appeal (Farahi’s forfeiture holding makes this point).

On examples of impermissibly broad categories
- The Farahi opinion itself gives no specific “bad” category examples; it simply applies the CREW/Gallant standard and rejects Farahi’s underdeveloped attack. See Farahi, § IV.C. To find concrete illustrations, consult the precedents Farahi cites (CREW, Gallant, and cases cited for forfeiture like Khine) and the district‑court record in your own case.

Bottom line
- To preserve a categorical overbreadth challenge under Farahi you must (1) raise and develop it in the district court, (2) identify the exact agency categories you are attacking (with record citations), and (3) explain with specificity why those definitions sweep in non‑exempt material and prevent the court from applying Exemption 7(A) to the category. Failure to make that record‑grounded showing is what the D.C. Circuit called forfeiture in Farahi.
