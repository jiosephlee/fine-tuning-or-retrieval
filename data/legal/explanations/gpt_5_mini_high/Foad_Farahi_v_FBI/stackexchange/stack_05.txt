Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does Exemption 7(A)’s categorical approach square with FOIA’s “reasonably segregable” requirement?
Question:
The Supreme Court allows category-based withholdings under 7(A) when disclosure of each category would interfere with enforcement. At the same time, FOIA requires release of any “reasonably segregable” nonexempt portion. Here, the court accepted ex parte showings that nothing meaningful was segregable and emphasized that FOIA is about “information, not simply words,” so scattered non-sensitive terms need not be released. I’m trying to understand the boundaries:
- Must agencies still conduct a line-by-line review when they are withholding by category under 7(A), or can they conclude that entire documents (or CDs) are non-segregable if sensitive information is interspersed throughout?
- What counts as “meaningful information” for segregability—do headers, dates, form fields, or boilerplate qualify?
- Are courts satisfied by a categorical segregability declaration, or do they expect document-specific discussion or sample Vaughn indices even in 7(A) cases?
Answer:
Short answer
Farahi reaffirms the familiar (and fact‑specific) rules: an agency must take “reasonable steps” to segregate nonexempt material, but it does not have to perform a pointless line‑by‑line redaction when it can show that the remaining nonexempt bits are inextricably intertwined with exempt material or are not “meaningful information.” Courts will accept category‑based withholdings and even ex parte affidavits so long as the agency’s submissions provide the required “reasonable specificity” and the categories are “sufficiently distinct”; they do not automatically demand a full document‑by‑document Vaughn index in every 7(A) case. (Farahi v. FBI, No. 23‑5243, slip op. (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025).)

Explanation and doctrine (grounded in Farahi)
1) Line‑by‑line review vs. categorical withholding
- The statutory duty is to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt material. Farahi repeats that duty: agencies must take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” Slip op. § I–V (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)).  
- But the court also restates the settled exceptions: where nonexempt portions are “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions,” the government may withhold the record in its entirety. Slip op. § III (quoting Juarez and Mead). And where nonexempt material is “randomly interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document,” it is often not “reasonably segregable.” Slip op. § V (quoting Mead, 566 F.2d at 261).  
- Practical rule from Farahi: an agency need not do futile line‑by‑line redactions if it can show (by affidavit/declaration) that segregation would be impracticable or would disclose the very substance the exemption protects. The court accepted the FBI’s ex parte showing that the records were not reasonably segregable and affirmed. Slip op. §§ IV–V.

2) What counts as “meaningful information” for segregability?
- Farahi emphasizes Mead’s formulation that FOIA is about “information, not simply words which the Government has written down.” Slip op. § V (quoting Mead). The court thus treats “meaningful information” functionally: release is required only where the released material would convey substantive information, not merely isolated words or formatting.  
- That means whether a header, date, form field, boilerplate, or an isolated word is “meaningful” is context‑dependent. If such an item adds substantive factual or informational content (for example, revealing investigative targets, timelines, or other nontrivial facts), it may be “meaningful.” If it is purely perfunctory/boilerplate and conveys no substantive information, the court may accept that its disclosure is not required. Farahi reached the latter conclusion on the record before it: the district court — relying on the FBI’s detailed (partly ex parte) justification — found “no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.” Slip op. § V.

3) Are categorical segregability declarations enough, or do courts demand Vaughn/sample indices?
- Farahi says courts may rely on government affidavits that show “with reasonable specificity” why documents cannot be further segregated. Slip op. § III (citing Juarez). It also reiterates the rule that category‑by‑category withholding is permissible so long as the categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” Slip op. § IV.C (quoting CREW/Gallant).  
- The circuit accepted the FBI’s categorical approach here because the agency (through public and ex parte declarations) supplied the necessary detail and the plaintiff did not make the specific overbreadth arguments that would have undermined the categories (and in fact forfeited that challenge). Slip op. § IV.C. The court therefore did not require a public, document‑by‑document Vaughn index in this case.  
- Bottom line from Farahi: a categorical segregability declaration can be sufficient, but only if it provides the “reasonable specificity” courts require and the categories are sufficiently distinct. When an agency’s public showing is thin or the categories are overbroad, courts will expect a more granular showing (which may take the form of public detail, sample Vaughn entries, or — in national‑security/law‑enforcement matters — ex parte submissions).

Practical takeaways for litigants
- If you represent a requester, challenge category overbreadth with specific examples and ask the court for document‑specific justification or sample Vaughn entries if the agency’s public affidavit is conclusory. Farahi shows a failure to make such a specific argument can be fatal (forfeiture). Slip op. § IV.C.  
- If you represent an agency, provide an affidavit that explains with reasonable specificity why segregation would reveal the substance of the exempt material or why the nonexempt parts are merely isolated, non‑meaningful words — and make categories as concrete and distinct as possible; ex parte submissions are an available route in cases where public detail would itself reveal sensitive information. Slip op. §§ IV–V.

Conclusion
Farahi does not change the basic balancing: agencies must try to segregate, but they need not engage in pointless line‑by‑line redactions where the record‑level showing (public or ex parte) demonstrates that only non‑substantive words remain or that nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined. Whether a specific header, date, or boilerplate is “meaningful” is a contextual question courts will resolve based on the agency’s specificity and the distinctness of any categories.
