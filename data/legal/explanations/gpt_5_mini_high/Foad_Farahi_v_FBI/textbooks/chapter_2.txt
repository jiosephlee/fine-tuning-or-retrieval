Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Governing FOIA Framework and Standards of Review

## FOIA’s basic rule of disclosure and statutory exemptions
The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) creates a presumption of public access: an agency “shall make” its records available to any person who requests them, subject only to a set of statutory exemptions. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3) and (b). The operative question in Farahi v. FBI was therefore not whether an agency may keep records secret at all, but whether one of FOIA’s enumerated exemptions (here the law‑enforcement exemption) justified the FBI’s extensive withholdings.

In practice, FOIA litigation turns on whether the agency has met its burden under the statute: the government must show that the requested material falls within an exemption and that any non‑exempt material has been disclosed (or is not reasonably segregable). The Farahi opinion frames its analysis against that statutory structure—asking first whether the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and second whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”

## Exemption 7(A): law‑enforcement compilation and interference with proceedings
Exemption 7 applies to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and subsection 7(A) permits withholding when disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The D.C. Circuit has made clear that to trigger 7(A) there must be a “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding (Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, “CREW,” 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014), quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)). Thus Exemption 7(A) is a two‑part inquiry: (1) were the records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and (2) would release reasonably be expected to interfere with proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated?

What counts as “compiled for law enforcement purposes” is not satisfied by mere label‑calling; courts require a rational nexus between the files and the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and some showing that the records concern an individual or incident tied to a potential security risk or federal violation (Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998), citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)). In Farahi the FBI’s public declaration (and a more detailed sealed declaration) described investigations into national‑security and terrorism‑related activity connected to Farahi; the court concluded that those statements, taken with the broader factual context (including concessions made in related immigration proceedings), established the necessary nexus.

On the interference element, the D.C. Circuit has long recognized that ongoing criminal investigations are “proceedings” for 7(A) purposes and that the government may sometimes establish that an investigation is pending simply by attesting to its existence (CREW; Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 59–60 (D.C. Cir. 2008)). A common and accepted justification is that release could “tip off” targets about the scope of an investigation and thereby frustrate evidence gathering (see Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 929 (D.C. Cir. 2003)). In Farahi the FBI’s declarations asserted that disclosure would have that effect and confirmed that the investigation remained ongoing; the district court (and then the court of appeals) accepted that showing as satisfying 7(A)’s interference requirement.

## Standard of review and the government’s burden
When an agency’s withholding is resolved on summary judgment, appellate courts review the grant of summary judgment de novo (see, e.g., Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021)). FOIA places the burden on the government to prove that an exemption applies (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B)). That means courts independently evaluate whether the agency has carried its burden under the statutory text and governing precedents.

That said, courts give a “fair measure of deference” to the FBI when it invokes Exemption 7 because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency whose judgments about investigative needs are entitled to respect (Campbell; Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017)). The D.C. Circuit has also emphasized heightened deference where national‑security concerns are implicated (CNSS, 331 F.3d at 927–28). This deference affects how courts treat agency affidavits and factual characterizations, but it does not displace the court’s responsibility to ensure the agency’s showing contains sufficient detail to permit meaningful review.

## Proving Exemption 7(A) with agency declarations; use of ex parte submissions
Agencies typically rely on sworn declarations (or affidavits) to explain why material is exempt. The controlling principle is that a court can accept agency affidavits if they “show with reasonable specificity” why withheld material falls within an exemption (Juarez, 518 F.3d at 61). CREW warns, however, that conclusory or vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may not suffice; the affidavit must provide a factual basis for the asserted harm.

Farahi illustrates the common sequence: the FBI first filed public declarations (David Hardy’s) stating that records were law‑enforcement materials and that disclosure could interfere with investigations. Because the initial filings were several years old, the district court asked for updated statements to guard against “staleness.” The FBI then filed both public and sealed ex parte declarations (including a detailed ex parte Hardy declaration and later a Bender declaration) that supplied particulars about the investigation. A court may properly rely on sealed, ex parte declarations where disclosure of the details would defeat the very secrecy the agency seeks to protect; the D.C. Circuit has recognized this practice as sometimes necessary in FOIA suits (see Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. VA, 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C. Cir. 2023)). But ex parte submissions must still be sufficiently specific to permit judicial evaluation; if they are not, a court may require more.

In Farahi the court treated the public declarations, the sealed ex parte detail, and the updated confirmation from Bender together—crediting the initial detailed ex parte showing and treating Bender’s short public declaration as an update rather than a standalone justification.

## Ongoing criminal investigations as qualifying “proceedings”
A key practical point is that “proceedings” under Exemption 7(A) include investigations that are ongoing and aimed at developing evidence for potential future prosecution. Juarez articulated the functional test: if an investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible criminal case and premature release of evidence would jeopardize that case, 7(A) applies. CREW confirmed that a government attestation to the existence of an investigation can be enough when coupled with adequate supporting detail. In Farahi the FBI’s attestations (and sealed elaboration) that the investigation continued, together with the public factual background about terrorism‑related ties, furnished the court with a sufficient factual predicate to conclude a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” existed.

## Segregability and the “inextricably intertwined” doctrine
Even when an exemption applies, FOIA requires that agencies disclose any “reasonably segregable” non‑exempt portion of a record (5 U.S.C. § 552(b); see also § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) as cited in the opinion). The D.C. Circuit permits courts to accept agency affidavits explaining why no meaningful segregable material exists, but where nonexempt portions are merely “randomly interspersed line‑by‑line” with exempt material, segregation may be impracticable and the document may be withheld in full as “inextricably intertwined” (Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260–61 (D.C. Cir. 1977); Juarez).

The opinion stresses Mead’s teaching that FOIA’s focus is information, not isolated words, and that revealing stray non‑exempt words often reveals too much. In Farahi the FBI’s sealed submission explained why releasable material was scattered throughout the records and why disclosure of those fragments would effectively reveal exempt investigative information. The district court credited that explanation and found no meaningful segregable information; the D.C. Circuit affirmed that conclusion as a permissible application of the segregability standard.

## Category-by-category withholdings and forfeiture
When an agency withholds records on a category basis, the categories themselves must be sufficiently distinct to allow a court to evaluate whether the exemptions were properly applied (Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994); CREW). A FOIA plaintiff who challenges category‑based withholding must develop specific arguments showing why the categories are overbroad or why particular exemptions were misapplied. The Farahi court rejected the plaintiff’s challenge to the FBI’s categories because Farahi offered no concrete explanation for overbreadth and therefore forfeited the argument (Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019)).

## Clarifying note on a citation slip in the opinion
The opinion at one point cites 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B) when discussing the requirement that disclosure could be expected to “interfere with enforcement proceedings.” That is a citation error: the interference provision is Exemption 7(A) (not 7(B)). The court’s analysis throughout plainly concerns and applies Exemption 7(A); the slip is clerical and does not affect the substantive Exemption 7(A) analysis.

## How these standards controlled the court’s outcome in Farahi
Put together, these rules explain the decision path in Farahi. The court reviewed the grant of summary judgment de novo and required the government to update potentially stale affidavits. It gave the FBI a measure of deference because the agency is a specialized law‑enforcement body and the records implicated national security, but it insisted that the agency provide reasonably specific factual statements. The combination of public declarations, a detailed sealed declaration, and an unopposed ex parte update satisfied both elements of Exemption 7(A): the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The court also accepted the FBI’s segregability showing that non‑exempt snippets were inextricably intertwined with exempt material. Finally, the plaintiff’s category‑based objections were forfeited for lack of specific challenge. Those rulings, grounded in the statutory text and the precedents cited above, led the panel to affirm the district court’s judgment.
