Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Can a FOIA plaintiff meaningfully challenge ex parte declarations, and did Farahi waive that challenge by not opposing them?
Question:
The opinion says Farahi did not oppose the FBI’s motion to submit ex parte, sealed declarations, and later holds he “cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.” It also quotes Perioperative Services for the idea that ex parte review is “inherent” in some FOIA cases. I’m confused about the procedural and substantive limits here:
- What is the standard for when a FOIA court may rely on ex parte affidavits rather than a public Vaughn index or adversarial process?
- Is a failure to oppose the government’s motion to file ex parte a categorical waiver of any due process or adequacy challenge on appeal, or can a requester still argue that the court relied “too much” on them?
- Must the district court put as much as possible on the public record to satisfy its obligation to make segregability findings, or is a terse public order sufficient if the judge reviewed sealed material?
- On appeal, does the court of appeals review the sealed declarations de novo as part of the record, and is the requester entitled to any redacted versions?
Answer:
Short answer
- Courts may rely on sealed, ex parte government declarations in FOIA Exemption 7(A) cases so long as the government’s submissions (public + sealed) provide the court with the “reasonable specificity” needed to show the exemption applies and why non‑exempt material is not reasonably segregable. The Farahi court relied on Juarez/CREW-type standards and on Perioperative Servs.’ recognition that ex parte submissions are sometimes “the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.”
- In Farahi the appellant lost the right to press a challenge to the court’s reliance on the sealed declarations because he did not oppose the government’s motion to file them; the court plainly treated that failure as a procedural bar: “he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.”
- A district court need not put the sealed reasoning itself on the public docket so long as the sealed submissions supply the court with an adequate, sufficiently specific justification for its segregability and exemption rulings. The opinion expressly says “nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration.”
- On appeal the court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo and may (and here did) consider sealed ex parte declarations as part of the record; the Farahi opinion reviewed those sealed materials (but did not disclose their contents). The opinion does not announce any entitlement for the requester to obtain redacted copies of the sealed declarations.

Explanation and textual support from Farahi
1) When may a court rely on ex parte/ sealed declarations?
- The controlling idea in the opinion is that the court may rely on government affidavits that “show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” The opinion cites Juarez for that point and CREW for the rule that the government can show pending/anticipated proceedings by attesting to their “existence.” The opinion also emphasizes deference to the FBI in national‑security contexts (Campbell; CNSS).
- The court also invokes Perioperative Servs. to explain why ex parte review is sometimes necessary: when an ex parte submission is “the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret,” that problem is “inherent” in some FOIA cases. Farahi therefore treats sealed declarations as an accepted, sometimes indispensable tool when the sealed content is itself the thing that would be harmed by public disclosure.

2) Does failing to oppose the government’s motion to file ex parte declarations waive later challenges?
- In this case the answer is yes. The opinion says directly that because Farahi “did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.” That is the court’s dispositive procedural point: a failure to object in the district court deprived the appellant of the right to attack reliance on those particular sealed submissions on appeal.
- The opinion also shows other examples of forfeiture: Farahi’s insufficiently developed argument that the FBI’s document‑categories were overbroad was treated as forfeited because he “provide[d] no specific explanation” and thus “forfeited his arguments on this score.” The Farahi court relied on procedural preservation principles to deny appellate substantive review of certain points.

3) How much must the district court put on the public record about segregability?
- The statute requires agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt information, and the court must be satisfied that the agency met that burden. But Farahi makes clear that the district court need not publish its sealed analysis: the court found that the FBI had provided a “detailed justification” in Bender’s ex parte submission and concluded that this satisfied the agency’s burden, and the opinion states that “nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration.”
- Put differently: the public order must show (directly or indirectly via public and sealed submissions) that the court performed an adequate segregability inquiry, but the delicate factual detail justifying the segregability decision may remain in sealed filings when disclosure would itself defeat the exemption.

4) How does appellate review treat sealed declarations, and is the requester entitled to redacted versions?
- The panel reiterates that it reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo. That review includes consideration of the summary judgment record — which here included sealed ex parte declarations. The opinion shows the court read and relied on the sealed material (it even says “We cannot disclose the contents of Hardy’s ex parte declaration” while using it to explain why the FBI met its burden).
- The opinion does not create a rule that the requester is entitled to redacted versions of sealed declarations; in Farahi the sealed materials were considered by the court but were not disclosed to the public or to the requester. The decision therefore does not announce any entitlement to receive redacted copies on appeal.

Takeaway for a FOIA requester (practical, grounded in Farahi)
- If you want to preserve the right to contest the use or sufficiency of ex parte, sealed declarations, object in the district court when the government moves to file those declarations under seal/ex parte; Farahi shows that failing to oppose can foreclose a later attack on the court’s reliance.  
- Expect the court to require updated or supplemental (possibly sealed) declarations where initial submissions are stale; and expect the court to accept sealed detail as sufficient so long as the public and sealed record together supply the court with the reasonable specificity the law demands.

### What counts as “enforcement proceedings” under FOIA Exemption 7(A)—do civil removal proceedings or only criminal investigations qualify?
Question:
Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure could interfere with “enforcement proceedings,” which the D.C. Circuit has read to include “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings. The panel here says ongoing criminal investigations qualify and that the government can sometimes just attest to their existence. But the facts also mention that DHS used FBI evidence in Farahi’s immigration removal case. Do civil immigration removal proceedings themselves count as “enforcement proceedings” for 7(A) purposes, or is the focus here solely on possible criminal prosecution? More generally, does 7(A) cover civil enforcement proceedings (e.g., NLRB, SEC, immigration), and if so, what is the test for showing a nexus between the requested records and those civil proceedings?
Answer:
Short answer
Farahi reaffirms the D.C. Circuit’s familiar 7(A) framework but does not hold that civil immigration removal proceedings, by themselves, automatically qualify as “enforcement proceedings” under Exemption 7(A). In Farahi the court rested its withholding on ongoing (or reasonably anticipated) criminal investigations — and emphasized that the government may sometimes establish pendency simply by attesting to an investigation’s existence. The governing test the court applies (and that would govern any claimed civil or criminal “enforcement proceeding”) is: (1) the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; (2) a relevant enforcement proceeding is pending or reasonably anticipated; and (3) disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with that proceeding — shown with a sufficient factual nexus between the records and the agency’s law‑enforcement duties. (Farahi v. FBI, No. 23‑5243, slip op. (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025).)

Explanation and governing standards (all drawn from Farahi)
- What the court says about “proceedings”: Exemption 7(A) covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings,” and those proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Farahi quotes this rule and treats ongoing criminal investigations as a paradigmatic form of “proceeding.” (Slip op. §§ I–III; citing CREW/Mapother as the pending-or-reasonably-anticipated rule; slip op. III.B.)

- Criminal investigations in Farahi: The court explicitly states “Ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’ for these purposes,” and that the government may sometimes establish pendency “simply by attesting to [their] ‘existence.’” The court also relied on the Juarez formulation that “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.” (Slip op. III.B (quoting Juarez).)

- The “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” / nexus requirement: The opinion reiterates that an agency must show a rational “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” plus a connection between the individual/incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. In other words, the documents must be tied to an identifiable law‑enforcement mission or contemplated enforcement action — not asserted in a conclusory, untethered way. (Slip op. III.A (citing Campbell/Pratt).)

- What the court required to show interference: Farahi accepts the common 7(A) justifications (e.g., that disclosure would tip off targets about the scope of an investigation) and accepts public affidavits plus sealed/ex parte declarations when necessary to provide the detail the court needs without disclosing the very information the government seeks to protect. (Slip op. III.B, V.)

So do civil removal (immigration) proceedings count?
- Farahi’s facts: the opinion notes DHS used FBI materials in Farahi’s immigration removal proceedings (and that those proceedings provided context), but the court’s Exemption 7(A) decision rested on the FBI’s showing of an ongoing criminal investigation. The court did not hold that the civil removal proceedings themselves were the basis for exemption. (See slip op. II & III.B.)

- What that means doctrinally: Farahi does not answer the categorical question whether a civil enforcement proceeding (NLRB, SEC, immigration removal, etc.) alone qualifies as an “enforcement proceeding” for 7(A). The opinion makes clear, however, that whatever the type of enforcement action, the same core requirements apply: (i) the records must have been compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; (ii) the proceeding must be pending or reasonably anticipated; (iii) disclosure must be shown to be likely to interfere, and (iv) the agency must show a sufficient factual nexus tying the records to its law‑enforcement duties. (Slip op. III.A–B.)

Practical checklist (how an agency would need to proceed under Farahi)
1. Show the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes — i.e., a rational nexus to an agency enforcement duty and a connection to potential violations or security risks. (Slip op. III.A.)
2. Show the enforcement proceeding (civil or criminal) is pending or reasonably anticipated. CREW’s pending/anticipated rule governs; courts may accept a contemporaneous attestation of an ongoing investigation or proceeding, but “vague” assertions can fail. Farahi shows courts will accept attestation + sealed/ex parte elaboration when needed. (Slip op. III.B.)
3. Explain how disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere — e.g., by tipping off targets, jeopardizing evidence, chilling cooperation. Juarez’s formulation (investigation still gathering evidence for a possible case that would be jeopardized by premature release) is quoted approvingly. (Slip op. III.B (quoting Juarez).)
4. Tie specific categories of withheld records to the asserted proceeding — categories must be sufficiently distinct to permit judicial review. (Slip op. IV.C (discussing CREW/Gallant).)
5. Address segregability — show why nonexempt material is not reasonably separable; courts may rely on detailed agency affidavits (including ex parte submissions) to make that determination. (Slip op. V.)

Bottom line
- Farahi confirms that ongoing criminal investigations are “enforcement proceedings” under 7(A) and that attestation (backed by public and, if necessary, sealed/ex parte declarations) can satisfy the government’s burden. It does not, however, squarely hold that civil removal proceedings alone qualify. If an agency wants to withhold under 7(A) based on a civil enforcement matter, it must satisfy the same law‑enforcement‑purpose, pendency/anticipation, interference, nexus and segregability requirements the D.C. Circuit applies in Farahi.

### How did the FBI satisfy the “compiled for law enforcement purposes” requirement when the investigation appears national-security focused?
Question:
The court defers to the FBI as a law-enforcement agency but still requires a “rational nexus” to a law-enforcement duty and a connection to a possible security risk or violation of federal law (Pratt/Campbell). The public declaration here said the records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and the court supplements that with broader context from immigration proceedings about ties to designated terrorist groups and individuals. I’m struggling with the boundary between intelligence/national-security investigations and “law enforcement purposes”:
- Does “national security” by itself suffice, or must the agency tie the file to suspected violations of specific criminal statutes (e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 2339B)?
- How concrete must that showing be in the public record when much detail is in ex parte declarations?
- What distinguishes an inadequate “domestic intelligence” file (pretextual surveillance) from a valid law-enforcement file under Pratt?
- How does the deference owed to the FBI in national-security contexts interact with Pratt’s requirements—what can a requester point to in order to overcome that deference?
Answer:
Short answer
The court applied the familiar Pratt/Campbell test: the FBI’s withholding must show a rational “nexus” between the investigation and the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and a connection to a possible security risk or violation of federal law. “National security” language helps but does not automatically suffice on its own; it must be tied to that Pratt nexus (and to pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings). The Farahi court accepted the FBI’s showing because the public Hardy declaration expressly tied the file to “investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism … includ[ing] pending law enforcement proceedings,” and that public showing was reinforced by sealed ex parte detail plus Farahi’s own concessions in immigration filings.

What the opinion says about the legal standard
- Exemption 7(A) applies only to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and—even though the FBI gets “a fair measure of deference” because it is a law‑enforcement agency—its declarations must still “establish a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” and a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” (quoting Pratt/Campbell, as discussed in Farahi, § IV.A).
- The government must also show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (CREW/Juarez framework the opinion applies).

How the FBI satisfied the requirement in Farahi
- Public plus sealed evidence: the public Hardy declaration said the records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” The sealed (ex parte) Hardy declaration provided detail about the investigation, and a later update (Bender) confirmed the investigation remained ongoing. The court relied on that combination and on Farahi’s own concessions in parallel immigration proceedings tying him to designated groups and convicted terrorists, and concluded the Pratt nexus was established. (Farahi, § IV.A–B.)

Answers to your bullets, grounded in Farahi
1) Does “national security” by itself suffice?
- No, not automatically. Farahi reiterates Pratt: the label “national security” must be tied to the required law‑enforcement nexus and to a possible violation/security risk. In practice, an agency that merely invokes “national security” without showing a connection to enforcement duties or to prospective criminal or terrorism-related proceedings risks failure under Pratt (the opinion accepted the FBI here because the declaration tied national‑security investigations to pending/anticipated law‑enforcement proceedings and associations with persons involved in terrorism).

2) How concrete must the public showing be when details are ex parte?
- The public record must at least show the existence (and non‑staleness) of an ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceeding; CREW allows the government to show pendency by attestation to its “existence,” but CREW also warns that a merely “vague” assertion may not suffice. Farahi shows the court will accept ex parte declarations to provide the necessary specifics when disclosure would defeat the purpose of secrecy, but the public filings cannot be so conclusory or stale that the court must demand an update (the district court in Farahi required updated declarations because of lapse of time, and the later public + ex parte submissions cured the problem).

3) What separates an inadequate “domestic intelligence” (pretextual surveillance) file from a valid law‑enforcement file under Pratt?
- The decisive difference is factual nexus and purpose: a file compiled to support concrete law‑enforcement duties (investigations into possible violations, pending or reasonably anticipated prosecutions) meets Pratt; a file compiled principally for political monitoring, policy evaluation, or other domestic‑intelligence ends without a tie to enforcement duties does not. The Farahi Court looked to the content/context (allegations in immigration proceedings, ties to designated groups and convicted terrorists, and the FBI’s investigatory attestations) to conclude the file was law‑enforcement in purpose.

4) How does deference interact with Pratt, and what can a requester point to to overcome that deference?
- Deference: the FBI gets a “fair measure of deference,” especially in national‑security contexts (the opinion cites Campbell and CNSS). But that deference is not limitless—the agency still must carry the Pratt showing.
- What will overcome deference: show that the government’s attestations are conclusory, inconsistent, stale, or lack the Pratt nexus; point to public records or litigation filings that contradict the agency’s claimed law‑enforcement purpose; press the court to require updated or more specific declarations (the district court did this in Farahi when the initial submissions became dated). In short, attack vagueness, staleness, and absence of a connection to enforcement proceedings—the things the Farahi opinion treats as the real substance of the court’s review.

Bottom line
Farahi illustrates the working balance: (1) “national security” assertions are given weight, (2) but the agency must still show the Pratt nexus to law‑enforcement duties and a connection to a security risk or federal‑law violation, and (3) public attestations plus sealed ex parte detail can satisfy the court so long as the record is not merely vague or stale. A requester trying to overcome the FBI’s deference should focus on demonstrating lack of nexus, vagueness or staleness in the government’s public submissions, or contradictions in the public record.

### How specific must an agency be to prove “ongoing” proceedings under 7(A), and when do courts require updated declarations due to staleness?
Question:
The district court initially found the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden but required updated declarations because of the passage of time. On appeal, Farahi argued the Bender update was too conclusory (“remain ongoing”), citing CREW’s warning about vague assertions. The panel says context matters and that the government can sometimes just attest to the existence of ongoing proceedings. I’m unsure how to reconcile these points:
- What level of detail is required to demonstrate that enforcement proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated (e.g., describing targets, phases, timelines), without revealing sensitive details?
- What factors make a declaration “stale” such that a court should require an update—elapsed time, litigation delays, changes in the investigation?
- Is there any rule of thumb (years, milestones) for when a “remain ongoing” statement becomes insufficient without more particulars?
Answer:
Short answer
There is no bright‑line specificity or temporal rule. Exemption 7(A) can be satisfied by a relatively short attestation that a law‑enforcement investigation is ongoing — but only in context. A bare “remain ongoing” assertion will survive only when (1) it supplements an earlier, reasonably specific agency submission (public or sealed) that establishes the law‑enforcement nexus and explains how disclosure would interfere, or (2) the record otherwise makes ongoing activity plausible (and the court gives the agency deference, especially in national‑security cases). Courts will require updates when there has been “significant passage of time” or other changed circumstances that cast doubt on whether proceedings are still “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Farahi is the exemplar: the D.C. Circuit accepted Bender’s terse “remain ongoing” update only because it was an update to earlier detailed Hardy declarations (including an ex parte submission) and because the breadth of the underlying terrorism investigation made continued activity plausible. (Farahi v. FBI, slip op. Sept. 30, 2025.)

What the law requires (doctrine)
- Threshold: Exemption 7(A) covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir.) (quoted in Farahi).
- How to show pendency: The government may sometimes show the required “proceedings” simply by attesting to their “existence,” but CREW warned that a vague assertion of ongoing investigations “may not suffice” when considered in isolation. Farahi cites CREW and repeats that point: attestations to existence can be enough, but context matters. (Farahi, §§ III–IV.B.)
- Nexus and specificity: The agency must show a rational nexus between the records and the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and explain how disclosure would interfere (e.g., tipping off targets). Courts may rely on agency affidavits “that show with reasonable specificity” why records are exempt and not reasonably segregable. (Campbell/Pratt and Juarez authorities cited in Farahi.)
- Use of ex parte declarations: When details are sensitive, an ex parte sealed declaration can supply the specifics the court needs while protecting investigative material; courts may properly rely on such filings. (Farahi §§ IV–V; see Perioperative Servs. discussion.)

How to reconcile “attest to existence” vs. the CREW warning
- The two statements are not in tension once you read Farahi: an attest‑to‑existence can be sufficient, but only as part of the total record. If the record already contains a reasonably detailed explanation (even if that detailed material was submitted ex parte), a short update attesting continuity may suffice. Conversely, if the only agency submission is a conclusory, public “we remain ongoing,” a court may require more detail. Farahi: Bender’s short update was accepted because it supplemented earlier detailed Hardy declarations (public and ex parte) and because the district court had earlier concluded the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden. (Farahi § IV.B.)

When a declaration becomes “stale” (factors courts consider)
Farahi identifies the main trigger: the district court asked for an update because of the “significant passage of time.” Relevant, non‑exhaustive factors courts weigh include:
- Elapsed time since the last substantive declaration (Farahi required an update after the government’s initial declaration and the passage of multiple years);
- Litigation delay that might outlast the factual basis asserted in an earlier affidavit;
- Material changes in the status of the investigation or enforcement proceedings (e.g., arrests, indictments, closures) — anything that would undermine the prior factual basis for pendency;
- Thinness of the prior declaration (where prior statements were already conclusory, time makes them less persuasive);
- Nature and breadth of the investigation (national‑security/counterterrorism matters receive more deference and may make continuity more plausible). (See Farahi §§ II–IV.B.)

Practical rule of thumb (what Farahi suggests)
- No bright‑line temporal rule in Farahi or the cited authorities. The D.C. Circuit did not announce a fixed number of months or years.
- Illustrative datapoint from Farahi: the court ordered an update after an initial declaration in Oct. 2019 and further briefing that stretched into Nov. 2022 — the district court called this a “significant passage of time,” and the D.C. Cir. accepted a concise March 2023 confirmation only because it was an update to earlier detailed submissions. Use that timeline as an example that multi‑year gaps will often prompt an update demand, but context controls. (Farahi §§ II–IV.B.)
- Best practice (practical, not doctrinal): when supplying an initial affidavit, give reasonably specific (but non‑sensitive) information establishing the law‑enforcement nexus and explaining how disclosure would interfere; put operational specifics in a sealed ex parte submission if necessary. If more than a year or two elapses, or if any material events occur in the investigation or litigation, file an updated declaration. If the update merely confirms continuity and is supported by earlier detailed sworn statements, a concise attestation like “the investigation remains ongoing” is likely to be sufficient; if the earlier record was thin, the update should supply additional specifics (again, sealed if needed).

Bottom line
Farahi teaches a simple rule: context matters. “We may be able to accept a short attestation that an investigation ‘remains ongoing,’ but only when it is part of a record in which the agency has already provided the court with the substantive, specific basis for invoking Exemption 7(A)” (either publicly or ex parte). When there has been “significant passage of time” or other material developments, courts will expect the agency to refresh the factual showing. (Farahi §§ IV.B, V.)

### How does Exemption 7(A)’s categorical approach square with FOIA’s “reasonably segregable” requirement?
Question:
The Supreme Court allows category-based withholdings under 7(A) when disclosure of each category would interfere with enforcement. At the same time, FOIA requires release of any “reasonably segregable” nonexempt portion. Here, the court accepted ex parte showings that nothing meaningful was segregable and emphasized that FOIA is about “information, not simply words,” so scattered non-sensitive terms need not be released. I’m trying to understand the boundaries:
- Must agencies still conduct a line-by-line review when they are withholding by category under 7(A), or can they conclude that entire documents (or CDs) are non-segregable if sensitive information is interspersed throughout?
- What counts as “meaningful information” for segregability—do headers, dates, form fields, or boilerplate qualify?
- Are courts satisfied by a categorical segregability declaration, or do they expect document-specific discussion or sample Vaughn indices even in 7(A) cases?
Answer:
Short answer
Farahi reaffirms the familiar (and fact‑specific) rules: an agency must take “reasonable steps” to segregate nonexempt material, but it does not have to perform a pointless line‑by‑line redaction when it can show that the remaining nonexempt bits are inextricably intertwined with exempt material or are not “meaningful information.” Courts will accept category‑based withholdings and even ex parte affidavits so long as the agency’s submissions provide the required “reasonable specificity” and the categories are “sufficiently distinct”; they do not automatically demand a full document‑by‑document Vaughn index in every 7(A) case. (Farahi v. FBI, No. 23‑5243, slip op. (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025).)

Explanation and doctrine (grounded in Farahi)
1) Line‑by‑line review vs. categorical withholding
- The statutory duty is to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt material. Farahi repeats that duty: agencies must take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” Slip op. § I–V (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)).  
- But the court also restates the settled exceptions: where nonexempt portions are “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions,” the government may withhold the record in its entirety. Slip op. § III (quoting Juarez and Mead). And where nonexempt material is “randomly interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document,” it is often not “reasonably segregable.” Slip op. § V (quoting Mead, 566 F.2d at 261).  
- Practical rule from Farahi: an agency need not do futile line‑by‑line redactions if it can show (by affidavit/declaration) that segregation would be impracticable or would disclose the very substance the exemption protects. The court accepted the FBI’s ex parte showing that the records were not reasonably segregable and affirmed. Slip op. §§ IV–V.

2) What counts as “meaningful information” for segregability?
- Farahi emphasizes Mead’s formulation that FOIA is about “information, not simply words which the Government has written down.” Slip op. § V (quoting Mead). The court thus treats “meaningful information” functionally: release is required only where the released material would convey substantive information, not merely isolated words or formatting.  
- That means whether a header, date, form field, boilerplate, or an isolated word is “meaningful” is context‑dependent. If such an item adds substantive factual or informational content (for example, revealing investigative targets, timelines, or other nontrivial facts), it may be “meaningful.” If it is purely perfunctory/boilerplate and conveys no substantive information, the court may accept that its disclosure is not required. Farahi reached the latter conclusion on the record before it: the district court — relying on the FBI’s detailed (partly ex parte) justification — found “no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.” Slip op. § V.

3) Are categorical segregability declarations enough, or do courts demand Vaughn/sample indices?
- Farahi says courts may rely on government affidavits that show “with reasonable specificity” why documents cannot be further segregated. Slip op. § III (citing Juarez). It also reiterates the rule that category‑by‑category withholding is permissible so long as the categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” Slip op. § IV.C (quoting CREW/Gallant).  
- The circuit accepted the FBI’s categorical approach here because the agency (through public and ex parte declarations) supplied the necessary detail and the plaintiff did not make the specific overbreadth arguments that would have undermined the categories (and in fact forfeited that challenge). Slip op. § IV.C. The court therefore did not require a public, document‑by‑document Vaughn index in this case.  
- Bottom line from Farahi: a categorical segregability declaration can be sufficient, but only if it provides the “reasonable specificity” courts require and the categories are sufficiently distinct. When an agency’s public showing is thin or the categories are overbroad, courts will expect a more granular showing (which may take the form of public detail, sample Vaughn entries, or — in national‑security/law‑enforcement matters — ex parte submissions).

Practical takeaways for litigants
- If you represent a requester, challenge category overbreadth with specific examples and ask the court for document‑specific justification or sample Vaughn entries if the agency’s public affidavit is conclusory. Farahi shows a failure to make such a specific argument can be fatal (forfeiture). Slip op. § IV.C.  
- If you represent an agency, provide an affidavit that explains with reasonable specificity why segregation would reveal the substance of the exempt material or why the nonexempt parts are merely isolated, non‑meaningful words — and make categories as concrete and distinct as possible; ex parte submissions are an available route in cases where public detail would itself reveal sensitive information. Slip op. §§ IV–V.

Conclusion
Farahi does not change the basic balancing: agencies must try to segregate, but they need not engage in pointless line‑by‑line redactions where the record‑level showing (public or ex parte) demonstrates that only non‑substantive words remain or that nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined. Whether a specific header, date, or boilerplate is “meaningful” is a contextual question courts will resolve based on the agency’s specificity and the distinctness of any categories.

### What makes 7(A) withholding categories “sufficiently distinct,” and what did Farahi have to do to avoid forfeiting his challenge?
Question:
The panel notes that when an agency withholds by category, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to let the court assess the exemption’s application, citing Gallant and CREW. But it then says Farahi “forfeited” his overbreadth challenge by not explaining why the FBI’s categories were too broad. For preservation purposes:
- What level of specificity must a requester provide to challenge categories—do they need to identify particular categories and explain how they sweep in non-interfering records, or propose narrower alternatives?
- Does the challenge need to be raised in the district court with citations to the agency’s category definitions, or can it be developed on appeal?
- Any examples of category definitions that courts have found impermissibly broad under 7(A)?
Answer:
Short answer
- “Sufficiently distinct” means the agency’s category labels and definitions must be specific enough for a court to decide whether the claimed exemption fits each category. Farahi v. FBI quotes that standard directly: categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” Farahi, slip op. § IV.C (quoting CREW/Gallant).
- To avoid forfeiture you must develop a concrete overbreadth challenge below: identify the particular FBI category(ies) you’re attacking, point to how those category definitions (as the agency stated them in its declarations/filings) sweep in non‑interfering (nonexempt) material, and explain why the court therefore cannot apply Exemption 7(A) on a category‑wide basis. The D.C. Circuit rejected Farahi’s claim because he “provides no specific explanation” and therefore “forfeited his arguments on this score.” Farahi, § IV.C (citing Khine).
- The Farahi opinion itself does not give concrete examples of category definitions that courts have held impermissibly broad; it simply states the standard and cites CREW and Gallant. If you need examples, you must look to those precedents cited in Farahi.

Why the court treated Farahi’s point as forfeited
- The panel applied the familiar preservation/forfeiture rule: an appellate court will not consider a claim that was not developed with specificity below. Farahi failed to explain, with reference to the FBI’s own category definitions or declarations, why those categories were overbroad; the court therefore treated the contention as forfeited. See Farahi, § IV.C.

What a preserved, workable overbreadth challenge looks like (practical checklist grounded in Farahi)
- Point to the agency’s category definitions as they appear in the record (the agency’s declarations or other briefing). The Farahi court’s holding rests on the absence of any “specific explanation” tied to those definitions. Farahi, § IV.C.
- For each category you challenge: explain concretely how the category’s stated scope would include non‑exempt material (for example, show particular types/pages in the record that appear non‑interfering and nonetheless fall under the agency’s category label).
- Explain why those category boundaries prevent a court from assessing the exemption’s application (i.e., they are not “sufficiently distinct” under CREW/Gallant as quoted in Farahi).
- If available, offer narrower formulations or segregation/redaction proposals to show how the agency could define categories that would permit judicial review (Farahi does not require proposing alternatives, but a concrete alternative makes clear how the current category is overbroad and helps the court perform the required assessment).
- Make these arguments in the district court, with citation to the agency’s declarations and any sample pages or exhibits; do not reserve the argument for the first time on appeal (Farahi’s forfeiture holding makes this point).

On examples of impermissibly broad categories
- The Farahi opinion itself gives no specific “bad” category examples; it simply applies the CREW/Gallant standard and rejects Farahi’s underdeveloped attack. See Farahi, § IV.C. To find concrete illustrations, consult the precedents Farahi cites (CREW, Gallant, and cases cited for forfeiture like Khine) and the district‑court record in your own case.

Bottom line
- To preserve a categorical overbreadth challenge under Farahi you must (1) raise and develop it in the district court, (2) identify the exact agency categories you are attacking (with record citations), and (3) explain with specificity why those definitions sweep in non‑exempt material and prevent the court from applying Exemption 7(A) to the category. Failure to make that record‑grounded showing is what the D.C. Circuit called forfeiture in Farahi.

### Can an appellate court rely on allegations from other proceedings to uphold a FOIA exemption, or must it stick to the administrative/litigation record?
Question:
To support the “law enforcement purpose” and the plausibility that the investigation remains ongoing, the panel cites an Eleventh Circuit immigration decision summarizing allegations about Farahi’s ties to terrorist organizations and individuals. Is it proper in FOIA litigation for the court to rely on such external judicial findings or summaries to bolster the agency’s declarations? Does this fall under judicial notice, and are there limits given that FOIA review is typically based on the agency’s declarations and the district court record?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — an appellate court may consult public filings or findings from other proceedings as corroborative context in a FOIA case, but it may not use those outside allegations as a free-standing substitute for the agency’s required showing. In Farahi the D.C. Circuit relied principally on the FBI’s public and ex parte declarations (and Farahi’s own district‑court concessions) and only used the Eleventh Circuit immigration opinion as “broader context” to make the government’s showing plausible; the panel did not treat that outside opinion as the sole proof or as a judicial‑notice deus ex machina.

Why that is the rule (grounded in the Farahi opinion)
- FOIA’s Exemption 7(A) requires the government to show the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The opinion cites CREW and Juarez for these principles and reiterates that the government bears the burden and may show an investigation’s existence by attestation.  
- The opinion gives the FBI primacy: the court repeatedly rests its decision on the FBI’s public Hardy declaration, the ex parte Hardy submission, and Bender’s confirmatory declaration. For example the court explained that “[b]ased on the FBI’s public and sealed declarations, we conclude that the FBI has met its burden under Exemption 7(A) to show that a ‘concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding’ continues to exist.”  
- The Eleventh Circuit immigration opinion was invoked only as “broader context” (the panel says it “cannot disclose the contents of Hardy’s ex parte declaration, but we note what the FBI publicly alleged about Farahi in his immigration proceedings…”). That contextual material helped make the agency’s assertions plausible, but it was not treated as replacing the agency’s evidentiary showing. The court explicitly rejected treating Bender’s terse update “in isolation,” explaining that context (the earlier declarations and district court findings) made the update adequate.

Limits the opinion recognizes (and practical implications)
- External materials cannot rescue an inadequate agency record. The court faulted treating an update “in isolation” — it was only acceptable because it confirmed a prior, more detailed administrative/ex parte record and because the district court had already concluded the FBI “seemingly” met its burden earlier.  
- The outside material the court relied on was part of the public record (a published immigration decision) and was used to corroborate—not to furnish secret facts the FOIA plaintiff had no opportunity to contest. The court also noted that Farahi had conceded in the district court some of the underlying allegations, which reduced any unfairness from referencing the other proceeding.  
- Ex parte submissions are permissible in FOIA national-security/law‑enforcement cases when necessary, but they present fairness concerns; the opinion observes that Farahi did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot now complain about reliance on them.

Bottom line for FOIA practice
Courts may look to publicly available filings or decisions in other cases for corroboration and context, but the agency must still carry its burden on the FOIA record (public declarations and, where appropriate, sealed/ex parte submissions). The Farahi panel’s use of the Eleventh Circuit opinion was a permissible contextual corroboration, not an independent judicial‑notice holding or a substitute for the FBI’s own evidence.

### Why didn’t the FBI use a Glomar response here, and how do agencies choose between Glomar and Exemption 7(A) withholdings?
Question:
Instead of refusing to confirm or deny the existence of responsive records, the FBI acknowledged Farahi’s file and then withheld most records under 7(A). In cases where acknowledging the existence of records might itself tip off targets, agencies sometimes issue a Glomar response. What drives the choice between a Glomar response and acknowledging records but invoking 7(A)? Are there strategic or legal reasons (e.g., prior official acknowledgment, the requester’s own knowledge, parallel proceedings) that made Glomar unavailable or unhelpful here?
Answer:
Short answer
The court’s opinion shows why the FBI did not (and did not need to) use a Glomar “neither confirm nor deny” response: the agency could make the showing required by FOIA Exemption 7(A) while preserving secrecy through sealed (ex parte) declarations, and the public record (including prior disclosures and the plaintiff’s own concessions) already put the Bureau’s involvement into view. Because the FBI could credibly attest that enforcement proceedings were pending or reasonably anticipated and could explain why disclosure would “tip off” targets, the agency invoked 7(A) and the court accepted that route.

Why that choice makes sense (grounded in the opinion)
- Exemption 7(A) was available and provable. The opinion reiterates the statutory test—records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” may be withheld if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings,” and those proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court also noted that the government may establish pendency simply by attesting to the existence of an investigation. (See Part I and IV.B of the opinion (citing 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A) and CREW).)

- The FBI could make the required factual showing here. The agency submitted public declarations (Hardy) and an ex parte sealed declaration giving substantial detail about the investigation; the district court (and this Court) relied on those filings to find that disclosure would “tip off” targets and therefore could interfere with enforcement. The appellate opinion emphasizes that the agency’s public and sealed filings, together, satisfied the 7(A) showing. (See Parts II, IV.B.)

- The record already contained public acknowledgment of FBI involvement. The opinion records that DHS had used FBI documents and an FBI agent’s live testimony in Farahi’s immigration proceedings and that Farahi himself conceded FBI investigation of him. The FBI also located and disclosed some material to Farahi. Given those public facts, a blanket Glomar denial would have been inconsistent or pointless. (See Part II.)

- Ex parte submissions let the court inspect sensitive facts without public disclosure. The opinion explains that the FBI obtained leave to file sealed, ex parte declarations and that the district court relied on those sealed submissions (especially for segregability). The court explicitly recognized that ex parte filings, while disadvantaging plaintiffs, are sometimes the only way to resolve disputes about highly sensitive material without publicly disclosing it. That procedural path allowed the FBI to both preserve secrecy and withstand judicial review under 7(A). (See Parts II, V.)

Practical tradeoffs the opinion highlights
- Where an agency can substantiate (even if only to the court in camera) that an enforcement proceeding is pending or reasonably anticipated and that disclosure would interfere (e.g., by tipping off targets), Exemption 7(A) + ex parte support is a viable route. The opinion accepted exactly that combination here.
- By contrast (and as the CREW discussion the opinion cites warns), a bare or “vague” assertion that an investigation is ongoing may not carry the 7(A) burden; in cases where an agency cannot make a substantive showing and where acknowledging existence itself would irrevocably reveal sensitive operations, an agency might consider a Glomar response. The Farahi record, however, supports the opposite conclusion: the FBI could both acknowledge and justify withholding under 7(A).

Bottom line
The FBI took the route the record allowed and the court accepted: it acknowledged the file, submitted public and sealed declarations to prove the predicates for Exemption 7(A), and relied on ex parte review to preserve secrecy. The opinion shows that prior public disclosures and the agency’s ability to substantiate ongoing investigations made that choice legally defensible and strategically effective in this case.
