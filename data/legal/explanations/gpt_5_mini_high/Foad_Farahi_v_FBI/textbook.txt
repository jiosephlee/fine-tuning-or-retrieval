Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Foad Farahi’s immigration history and DHS removal proceedings; FBI agent’s testimony about alleged terrorist connections
Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. In 2002 the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings after concluding he had overstayed his visa. During those proceedings Farahi sought relief in the form of asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. To oppose relief, DHS introduced documentary evidence and presented live testimony from an FBI agent who — according to the record in the immigration litigation — “documented Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists.” The government publicly alleged in those proceedings that Farahi had ties to foreign terrorist organizations and to individuals later detained or convicted on terrorism-related charges; those allegations provide the law‑enforcement context that made Farahi’s FBI file especially sensitive and relevant.

## FOIA request to the FBI for Farahi’s file; scope of potentially responsive material (about 10,750 pages and 80 CDs)
Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act request to the FBI for his investigative file. The FBI’s search located a very large body of potentially responsive material — roughly 10,750 pages and 80 compact discs. That volume is important procedurally: it represents a substantial collection of investigative records, many of which the FBI later characterized as law‑enforcement records the disclosure of which could jeopardize ongoing or anticipated proceedings.

## Agency processing over several years; partial releases and extensive withholdings
After Farahi filed his FOIA request the agency took several years to process the materials. The FBI reviewed the located records, released some documents to Farahi, and withheld the bulk of the material under FOIA exemptions. Farahi sued after the FBI missed the statute’s deadline for responding to his request; the litigation thus began as a timing/default‑response complaint and evolved into a merits contest over whether the withheld records were exempt from disclosure. The long processing period and the large volume of records set up two recurring procedural themes in the case: (1) the adequacy and timeliness of the FBI’s sworn explanations for withholding, and (2) whether any nonexempt information could reasonably be segregated and released.

## FBI’s October 2019 summary‑judgment motion supported by a public declaration from David Hardy (RIDS Section Chief) and an ex parte, sealed supplemental declaration
In October 2019 the FBI moved for summary judgment, seeking a court determination that the records were exempt and need not be produced. The motion was supported by a public declaration from David Hardy, then the Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s public declaration asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that releasing them could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. The FBI also moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte so that the district court could see more sensitive factual detail without putting it on the public docket; Farahi did not oppose that motion, and the district court granted it in October 2020. These filings reflect a common FOIA practice: agencies use sworn declarations to carry their burden on exemption claims, and they sometimes provide additional classified or sensitive facts to the court in sealed, ex parte submissions.

## District court’s November 2022 order: finding the government had ‘seemingly’ met Exemption 7(A) but requiring updated proof due to staleness and more information on segregability; denial without prejudice
When the district court took up the FBI’s first summary‑judgment package in November 2022, it concluded that the FBI had established the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that the agency had “seemingly” met its burden to show disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. But because several years had elapsed since the Hardy declarations, the court worried the agency’s assertions might be stale: Exemption 7(A) protects records only where proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and courts expect up‑to‑date factual support for that condition. The court therefore ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated. The court also said it needed additional information on segregability — and, because that information was sensitive, it could not explain that need on the public record. For those reasons the court denied the FBI’s first summary‑judgment motion without prejudice, allowing the agency to submit refreshed, more detailed support.

## FBI’s renewed motion with public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender (then Acting RIDS Section Chief), confirming investigations remained ongoing and addressing segregability
In response the FBI renewed its motion, submitting both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender’s public declaration stated that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for supervising Farahi’s file and that the agent confirmed investigations connected to the file remained ongoing. Bender also provided a sealed, ex parte declaration that supplied additional factual detail about the investigations and explained why nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable from exempt content. The renewed package thus functioned as an update to the earlier Hardy submissions: the public declaration supplied a contemporaneous confirmation of ongoing investigative activity, and the ex parte declaration addressed the court’s segregability concerns with sensitive factual particularity that could not be disclosed on the public record.

## District court’s grant of summary judgment on the renewed motion; crediting the ongoing‑proceedings showing and the segregability explanation; Farahi’s appeal to the D.C. Circuit
After reviewing the renewed submissions, including the ex parte material in camera, the district court granted the FBI’s renewed motion for summary judgment. The court expressly credited the FBI’s confirmation “that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing,” and it accepted the sealed explanation for why potentially nonexempt information was not reasonably segregable from exempt material. The grant of summary judgment meant the district court found Exemption 7(A) applicable and that the FBI had satisfied its segregability obligation as to the challenged records. Farahi appealed that judgment to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, pressing, among other things, arguments about the sufficiency and timeliness of the FBI’s declarations, the use of ex parte submissions, and whether the agency had meaningfully segregated and released nonexempt information.

# Governing FOIA Framework and Standards of Review

## FOIA’s basic rule of disclosure and statutory exemptions
The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) creates a presumption of public access: an agency “shall make” its records available to any person who requests them, subject only to a set of statutory exemptions. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3) and (b). The operative question in Farahi v. FBI was therefore not whether an agency may keep records secret at all, but whether one of FOIA’s enumerated exemptions (here the law‑enforcement exemption) justified the FBI’s extensive withholdings.

In practice, FOIA litigation turns on whether the agency has met its burden under the statute: the government must show that the requested material falls within an exemption and that any non‑exempt material has been disclosed (or is not reasonably segregable). The Farahi opinion frames its analysis against that statutory structure—asking first whether the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and second whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”

## Exemption 7(A): law‑enforcement compilation and interference with proceedings
Exemption 7 applies to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and subsection 7(A) permits withholding when disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The D.C. Circuit has made clear that to trigger 7(A) there must be a “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding (Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ, “CREW,” 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014), quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)). Thus Exemption 7(A) is a two‑part inquiry: (1) were the records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, and (2) would release reasonably be expected to interfere with proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated?

What counts as “compiled for law enforcement purposes” is not satisfied by mere label‑calling; courts require a rational nexus between the files and the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and some showing that the records concern an individual or incident tied to a potential security risk or federal violation (Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998), citing Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)). In Farahi the FBI’s public declaration (and a more detailed sealed declaration) described investigations into national‑security and terrorism‑related activity connected to Farahi; the court concluded that those statements, taken with the broader factual context (including concessions made in related immigration proceedings), established the necessary nexus.

On the interference element, the D.C. Circuit has long recognized that ongoing criminal investigations are “proceedings” for 7(A) purposes and that the government may sometimes establish that an investigation is pending simply by attesting to its existence (CREW; Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 59–60 (D.C. Cir. 2008)). A common and accepted justification is that release could “tip off” targets about the scope of an investigation and thereby frustrate evidence gathering (see Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 929 (D.C. Cir. 2003)). In Farahi the FBI’s declarations asserted that disclosure would have that effect and confirmed that the investigation remained ongoing; the district court (and then the court of appeals) accepted that showing as satisfying 7(A)’s interference requirement.

## Standard of review and the government’s burden
When an agency’s withholding is resolved on summary judgment, appellate courts review the grant of summary judgment de novo (see, e.g., Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021)). FOIA places the burden on the government to prove that an exemption applies (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B)). That means courts independently evaluate whether the agency has carried its burden under the statutory text and governing precedents.

That said, courts give a “fair measure of deference” to the FBI when it invokes Exemption 7 because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency whose judgments about investigative needs are entitled to respect (Campbell; Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C. Cir. 2017)). The D.C. Circuit has also emphasized heightened deference where national‑security concerns are implicated (CNSS, 331 F.3d at 927–28). This deference affects how courts treat agency affidavits and factual characterizations, but it does not displace the court’s responsibility to ensure the agency’s showing contains sufficient detail to permit meaningful review.

## Proving Exemption 7(A) with agency declarations; use of ex parte submissions
Agencies typically rely on sworn declarations (or affidavits) to explain why material is exempt. The controlling principle is that a court can accept agency affidavits if they “show with reasonable specificity” why withheld material falls within an exemption (Juarez, 518 F.3d at 61). CREW warns, however, that conclusory or vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may not suffice; the affidavit must provide a factual basis for the asserted harm.

Farahi illustrates the common sequence: the FBI first filed public declarations (David Hardy’s) stating that records were law‑enforcement materials and that disclosure could interfere with investigations. Because the initial filings were several years old, the district court asked for updated statements to guard against “staleness.” The FBI then filed both public and sealed ex parte declarations (including a detailed ex parte Hardy declaration and later a Bender declaration) that supplied particulars about the investigation. A court may properly rely on sealed, ex parte declarations where disclosure of the details would defeat the very secrecy the agency seeks to protect; the D.C. Circuit has recognized this practice as sometimes necessary in FOIA suits (see Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. VA, 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C. Cir. 2023)). But ex parte submissions must still be sufficiently specific to permit judicial evaluation; if they are not, a court may require more.

In Farahi the court treated the public declarations, the sealed ex parte detail, and the updated confirmation from Bender together—crediting the initial detailed ex parte showing and treating Bender’s short public declaration as an update rather than a standalone justification.

## Ongoing criminal investigations as qualifying “proceedings”
A key practical point is that “proceedings” under Exemption 7(A) include investigations that are ongoing and aimed at developing evidence for potential future prosecution. Juarez articulated the functional test: if an investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible criminal case and premature release of evidence would jeopardize that case, 7(A) applies. CREW confirmed that a government attestation to the existence of an investigation can be enough when coupled with adequate supporting detail. In Farahi the FBI’s attestations (and sealed elaboration) that the investigation continued, together with the public factual background about terrorism‑related ties, furnished the court with a sufficient factual predicate to conclude a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” existed.

## Segregability and the “inextricably intertwined” doctrine
Even when an exemption applies, FOIA requires that agencies disclose any “reasonably segregable” non‑exempt portion of a record (5 U.S.C. § 552(b); see also § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) as cited in the opinion). The D.C. Circuit permits courts to accept agency affidavits explaining why no meaningful segregable material exists, but where nonexempt portions are merely “randomly interspersed line‑by‑line” with exempt material, segregation may be impracticable and the document may be withheld in full as “inextricably intertwined” (Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260–61 (D.C. Cir. 1977); Juarez).

The opinion stresses Mead’s teaching that FOIA’s focus is information, not isolated words, and that revealing stray non‑exempt words often reveals too much. In Farahi the FBI’s sealed submission explained why releasable material was scattered throughout the records and why disclosure of those fragments would effectively reveal exempt investigative information. The district court credited that explanation and found no meaningful segregable information; the D.C. Circuit affirmed that conclusion as a permissible application of the segregability standard.

## Category-by-category withholdings and forfeiture
When an agency withholds records on a category basis, the categories themselves must be sufficiently distinct to allow a court to evaluate whether the exemptions were properly applied (Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994); CREW). A FOIA plaintiff who challenges category‑based withholding must develop specific arguments showing why the categories are overbroad or why particular exemptions were misapplied. The Farahi court rejected the plaintiff’s challenge to the FBI’s categories because Farahi offered no concrete explanation for overbreadth and therefore forfeited the argument (Khine v. DHS, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019)).

## Clarifying note on a citation slip in the opinion
The opinion at one point cites 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B) when discussing the requirement that disclosure could be expected to “interfere with enforcement proceedings.” That is a citation error: the interference provision is Exemption 7(A) (not 7(B)). The court’s analysis throughout plainly concerns and applies Exemption 7(A); the slip is clerical and does not affect the substantive Exemption 7(A) analysis.

## How these standards controlled the court’s outcome in Farahi
Put together, these rules explain the decision path in Farahi. The court reviewed the grant of summary judgment de novo and required the government to update potentially stale affidavits. It gave the FBI a measure of deference because the agency is a specialized law‑enforcement body and the records implicated national security, but it insisted that the agency provide reasonably specific factual statements. The combination of public declarations, a detailed sealed declaration, and an unopposed ex parte update satisfied both elements of Exemption 7(A): the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The court also accepted the FBI’s segregability showing that non‑exempt snippets were inextricably intertwined with exempt material. Finally, the plaintiff’s category‑based objections were forfeited for lack of specific challenge. Those rulings, grounded in the statutory text and the precedents cited above, led the panel to affirm the district court’s judgment.

# Law-Enforcement Purpose Prong Applied

## Rational nexus requirement

Exemption 7(A) covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” so the first threshold question in many FOIA disputes is whether the withheld records were assembled in furtherance of the agency’s law‑enforcement responsibilities. The D.C. Circuit treats that showing as a two-part, factual inquiry: the agency must establish a rational “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” and it must show a connection between the “individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (quoting Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982)). Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its characterization of records as investigatory is entitled to a “fair measure of deference,” particularly where national‑security concerns are implicated. But that deference does not eliminate the agency’s obligation to supply sufficient factual detail in a declaration so a court can meaningfully assess the required nexus.

## Hardy’s public declaration

In this case the FBI’s public declaration from David Hardy directly addressed the statutory requirement. Hardy stated that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” That language identifies both elements the court looks for: (1) it places the documents squarely within ongoing law‑enforcement investigations (national security and international terrorism), and (2) it links the records to the subject’s associations with persons involved in those alleged violations. A student reading the opinion should note how the court treats such explicit language: it is not enough for an agency simply to label records “investigatory”; the declaration must describe the investigation’s law‑enforcement character and explain how the records relate to the subject or incident under inquiry. Here, the public Hardy declaration did that on its face.

## Supplemental details provided in Hardy’s ex parte declaration

The opinion emphasizes that Hardy’s sealed, ex parte declaration supplied “considerable detail about the nature of that investigation.” Although the court does not disclose the sealed material, it relied on it to flesh out and corroborate the public statement. This illustrates a common FOIA practice: when national‑security or sensitive law‑enforcement facts cannot be put on the public record without harm, a court may review classified or sealed ex parte submissions to determine whether the agency has met its burden. In Farahi the ex parte Hardy declaration provided the additional factual specificity the court needed to conclude the nexus requirement was satisfied—without exposing operational details publicly.

## Context corroborating the nexus

The court did not rely on agency declarations in isolation. It pointed to corroborating context from proceedings in which Farahi himself participated. In prior immigration litigation Farahi conceded that the FBI had investigated him for ties to one or more organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations and for ties to an individual later detained as an enemy combatant and eventually convicted on terrorism‑related federal charges. The immigration record also contained public allegations that Farahi had been a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, was connected to Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and other Al Qaeda members, had links to convicted or indicted terrorists (including Jose Padilla), and had supported charities alleged to finance terrorism (e.g., the Holy Land Foundation and Global Relief Foundation). Those concessions and public allegations supplied independent evidence that the FBI’s inquiries implicated possible security risks and violations of federal law—precisely the sort of connection the Campbell/Pratt framework requires.

## Holding on this prong

Weighing the public Hardy declaration, the substantive detail in the sealed Hardy declaration, and the corroborative public record from Farahi’s immigration proceedings, the court concluded that the FBI met its burden to show the records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.” The panel stressed both the sufficiency of the factual showings and the appropriateness of deference to the FBI given the subject matter (national‑security and counterterrorism investigations). In short, the court found the required rational nexus: the records were created in the course of the FBI’s law‑enforcement duties and tied to a subject and conduct that plausibly implicated national‑security violations. For a student analyzing similar FOIA rulings, the lesson is practical: look for (1) an agency declaration that identifies the investigatory purpose and connects the records to law‑enforcement duties, (2) factual detail (which may be in sealed submissions) that supports that identification, and (3) any corroborating public record or admissions that reinforce the nexus.

# Interference with Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

This chapter explains how the D.C. Circuit applied FOIA Exemption 7(A)’s interference prong in Farahi v. FBI. The narrow question before the court was whether release of Farahi’s FBI file “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The opinion shows how courts evaluate (1) the kinds of harms Exemption 7(A) protects, (2) what counts as proof that proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated, and (3) how a sequence of public and ex parte declarations can satisfy the agency’s burden—especially when a district court requires an update because of the passage of time.

## Recognized harms: tipping off targets to the scope of an investigation

A central, commonly accepted interference harm is that disclosure can “tip off” investigative targets about the scope or direction of an investigation, enabling them to flee, destroy evidence, or otherwise frustrate the inquiry. The Farahi court relied on this rationale and cited Center for National Security Studies (CNSS) to describe the tip‑off concern as a “widely accepted justification” for invoking Exemption 7(A). In practice, courts will credit an agency’s explanation that releasing particular material about an active investigation would reveal who or what the investigation targets, the lines of inquiry, or investigative techniques—information that could reasonably be expected to compromise future enforcement efforts.

## Proof of pendency: CREW and Juarez on attesting to ongoing investigations

Exemption 7(A) covers records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes only if disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with “enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court relied on two earlier decisions to shape what the agency must show. CREW allowed a government attestation that an investigation exists to establish the pendency of proceedings, while Juarez confirmed that ongoing criminal investigations qualify as “proceedings” and that Exemption 7(A) applies so long as an investigation is still gathering evidence for a possible future case. Put simply, a government declaration that an investigation is active can suffice to show the existence of a pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceeding—subject to the concern (expressed in CREW) that a mere vague assertion that an investigation is “ongoing” may be insufficient in some circumstances.

## Hardy’s declarations: establishing an ongoing investigation and interference risk

In Farahi the FBI’s initial showing rested on declarations from David Hardy. His public declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and attested that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with those investigations. The agency also submitted an ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration that, according to the court, supplied “considerable detail” about the nature of the investigation. Those two filings together served two functions: they linked the records to law‑enforcement work and they explained why disclosure would jeopardize active investigative efforts.

## Bender’s confirmation and the district court’s staleness concern

Because several years elapsed between the FBI’s first summary‑judgment submission and the district court’s initial decision, the court worried that earlier assertions might have become stale and ordered the FBI to update whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. The FBI complied with a supplemental public declaration from Joseph Bender and an accompanying ex parte declaration. Bender’s public statement was terse—essentially reporting that RIDS had contacted the responsible FBI agent, who confirmed the investigations “remain ongoing”—while the ex parte submission provided more detail. The district court credited the confirmation and, after reviewing the sealed material, concluded the agency had shown that disclosure could interfere with ongoing proceedings.

## The court’s response to the conclusoriness objection: context matters

Farahi argued that Bender’s succinct statement was conclusory and, standing alone, ran afoul of CREW’s warning against vague “ongoing” assertions. The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that the Bender declaration, by itself, “appear[ed] surprisingly terse.” But it rejected Farahi’s challenge for two reasons grounded in context. First, the Bender declaration was intended as an update confirming that nothing had changed since Hardy’s submissions; it was not offered as the initial detailed showing. Second, the district court had earlier found that the FBI had “seemingly” satisfied its burden based on the Hardy materials. In short, the combination of the original detailed ex parte Hardy declaration, the public Hardy declaration, and Bender’s update satisfied CREW’s requirement for sufficient specificity. The opinion thus shows that courts evaluate later, shorter attestations against the backdrop of earlier, fuller submissions rather than in isolation.

## Plausibility reinforced by the investigation’s breadth, as publicly alleged

The court also emphasized that the public record rendered the FBI’s representations plausible. The opinion referenced public allegations from Farahi’s immigration proceedings—claims that he was linked to high‑level terrorists and organizations, recruited for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, associated with Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other Al Qaeda members, connected to convicted or indicted terrorists (including Jose Padilla), and linked to charities alleged to have financed terrorism. Those allegations made it reasonable to believe the FBI’s investigation was broad and ongoing, and that premature disclosure of investigative records could jeopardize concrete prospective enforcement proceedings. The court relied on this plausibility to buttress its conclusion that Exemption 7(A) applied.

## Holding on the interference prong and practical takeaways

The court held that the FBI met its burden under Exemption 7(A) to show that release of the withheld records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were pending or reasonably anticipated. The decision illustrates several practical points for reading FOIA opinions: when substantial time elapses, courts may require updated declarations; a later, brief confirmation can be adequate if it supplements earlier, detailed (including ex parte) declarations; agencies may rely on the tip‑off rationale supported by prior case law; and courts will weigh plausibility drawn from the public record when evaluating whether an asserted ongoing investigation is concrete and likely to be jeopardized by disclosure.

# Segregability, Ex Parte Reliance, and Remaining Arguments

This chapter explains the court’s treatment of segregability, its acceptance of ex parte declarations, the disposition of Farahi’s category-based challenge, and the practical implications of the court’s ruling. The core holding is straightforward: after updating what the court viewed as potentially stale declarations, the FBI showed that disclosure would interfere with ongoing enforcement proceedings and that the non‑exempt material in the voluminous file was not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit therefore affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI.

## Segregability standard

FOIA requires agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt material and mandates that “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided.” The governing tension is that, while courts generally require disclosure of nonexempt information, they also allow withholding of entire documents when the nonexempt portions are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material or when disclosure of isolated snippets would itself reveal exempt information. Mead Data and its progeny establish that a court may accept agency affidavits that explain, with reasonable specificity, why further segregation is impossible. Juarez reaffirmed that judicial reliance on such affidavits is appropriate when they justify non‑segregability with sufficient detail.

In Farahi, the court applied those principles: it recognized the statutory duty to disclose segregable material but accepted the FBI’s showing—largely through supplemental, detailed declarations—that the responsive file contained little or no meaningful, reasonably segregable information. The court framed the inquiry not by counting words but by asking whether the remaining nonexempt material, if released, would provide usable information without revealing the exempt content the FBI sought to protect.

## The district court’s reliance on Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission

The district court initially found the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden but ordered updated declarations because of the passage of time. In response, the FBI filed public and ex parte supplemental declarations from Bender (and earlier, Hardy) that both confirmed investigations remained ongoing and explained why segregation was infeasible. The district court credited those submissions, describing Bender’s sealed supplement as a “detailed justification” for withholding in full, and concluded that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.

The D.C. Circuit endorsed that approach. It treated the Bender ex parte declaration not as the sole basis for withholding but as an update and elaboration of the government’s earlier, more detailed Hardy submissions. Given the context of the prior filings and the court’s earlier view that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden, the supplemental, sealed material supplied the particularized rationale the district court needed to find non‑segregability.

## The “information, not words” principle

A central doctrinal point in the court’s analysis is Mead’s admonition that FOIA’s objective is disclosure of information, not merely isolated words. When nonexempt items are scattered in small, context‑dependent fragments throughout a document—line by line, interspersed with exempt material—extracting those fragments often produces no meaningful, standalone information and can itself reveal too much about the exempt content. The court relied on that logic to reject Farahi’s contention that sheer volume of potentially responsive pages meant there must be significant segregable material. The opinion emphasizes that while some individual words or phrases might be technically nonexempt, their release would not satisfy FOIA’s informational purpose and might compromise the agency’s legitimate law‑enforcement interests. Under Mead (and as reiterated in Perioperative Services), such randomly interspersed snippets are not “reasonably segregable.”

## Use of ex parte declarations: permissibility and forfeiture

The opinion makes two connected points about ex parte, sealed declarations. First, courts may accept and rely upon them in FOIA litigation where necessary to adjudicate disputes without publicly disclosing the very material at issue. The panel acknowledged the fairness costs to FOIA plaintiffs but endorsed ex parte submissions where they are the only practicable way to permit meaningful judicial review while protecting sensitive law‑enforcement or national‑security information. The court cited this principle in upholding the district court’s sealed consideration of Bender’s supplement.

Second, procedural posture matters: Farahi did not oppose the FBI’s request to file supplemental declarations under seal and ex parte in the district court. The D.C. Circuit therefore held he could not challenge the district court’s reliance on those declarations on appeal. In short, a party that affirmatively allows or fails to preserve an objection to ex parte handling below forfeits the right to contest reliance on appeal.

## The category‑by‑category withholding challenge and forfeiture

When an agency withholds records by category rather than document‑by‑document, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” Gallant and CREW articulate that requirement. But the D.C. Circuit stressed that a challenger must explain why particular categories are overbroad or improperly applied; a bare assertion of overbreadth is not enough. Farahi offered no developed argument explaining why the FBI’s categories were deficient. Applying Khine, the court treated that failure as forfeiture and declined to reverse on that ground.

## Disposition and practical takeaways

The court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for the FBI. The decision has several practical lessons for FOIA practitioners. For agencies, it underscores the value of up‑to‑date, particularized declarations—sealed if necessary—to show both the pendency of enforcement proceedings and why nonexempt material cannot be usefully segregated, especially in national‑security and counterterrorism investigations where courts afford the executive particular deference. For requesters, the opinion highlights procedural traps: challenge ex parte submissions below if you intend to contest them on appeal; develop specific, document‑level arguments when attacking category‑based withholdings; and attempt to rebut agency affidavits with targeted evidence or legal argument showing particular segregable records exist. Finally, substantively, the chapter illustrates the court’s approach to segregability—focusing on whether released material would produce meaningful information rather than on whether isolated words or phrases could technically be excised.
