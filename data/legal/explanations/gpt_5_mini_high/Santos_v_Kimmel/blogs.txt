Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# From Cameo to Comment: The Second Circuit’s Objective Turn on Transformative Use

## Why this case matters

Santos v. Kimmel is a short, sharp application of the Supreme Court’s post‑Warhol view of transformative use to a very modern fact pattern: user‑generated, paid‑for celebrity videos solicited through a platform (Cameo) and then clipped into late‑night satire. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of George Santos’s copyright claims at the pleading stage, holding that Jimmy Kimmel’s use was transformative as a matter of law under the “reasonable observer” test articulated by the Supreme Court. For media defendants who repackage or comment on content—especially material that creators themselves publish or make to order—this decision is a clear and helpful signal that fair use can win early when the context of re‑use sharply changes the work’s communicative purpose.

## What the court held

The panel affirmed the district court’s 12(b)(6) dismissal. Applying the four fair‑use factors, the court found the first factor—purpose and character of the use—strongly favored fair use because Kimmel’s segments added a new purpose (satirical commentary about Santos’s willingness to say extravagant things for money) that a reasonable observer would see as distinct from the original Cameo videos (which Santos’s own complaint described as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity”). The court rejected Santos’s contention that Kimmel’s use was not transformative because Kimmel had “instigated” creation of the videos or was their “designer.” Following Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith, the Second Circuit emphasized that transformativness turns on how a reasonable observer perceives the works, not the parties’ subjective intent. The court also found the other fair‑use factors did not overcome that conclusion—particularly the lack of market usurpation—and, relying on precedents that allow fair‑use resolution at the pleading stage, concluded dismissal was proper. The court also affirmed dismissals of Santos’s contract and fraud claims because he was not a party to the platform’s Terms of Service, failed to plead an enforceable implied contract, and alleged no out‑of‑pocket loss for fraud.

## How the court applied Warhol’s “reasonable observer” test

This opinion is notable for how closely the Second Circuit hews to the Supreme Court’s Warhol framing. Warhol moved the focus away from what the secondary user intended and toward how the work’s communicative purpose will be perceived. The Second Circuit applies that principle literally: it reads Santos’s own complaint and the incorporated Cameo videos and asks what a reasonable viewer would take away. Santos’s videos, on their face, were uplifting messages. Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segments reframed those messages as evidence of Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for hire. That difference in purpose—message of encouragement versus ridicule/commentary—was enough to make the use transformative in the court’s view.

The opinion also addresses (and minimizes) the relevance of deceptive conduct in procuring the videos. Santos argued Kimmel acted in bad faith by using fake names to solicit the Cameos, invoking Harper & Row’s language that fair use presupposes good faith. The court notes that the Supreme Court has since tempered that absolutist formulation and, in any event, found nothing in the complaint showing a purpose to supplant Santos’s market for the videos. Where the complaint itself paints defendants as motivated by criticism or mockery rather than market substitution, bad‑faith allegations did not survive to defeat fair use.

## Why the court resolved fair use at the pleading stage

The dismissal rests on a well‑established—but important—procedural point: fair use is sometimes so clear from the complaint and incorporated materials that a court can resolve it on a 12(b)(6) motion. The panel relied on Second Circuit precedents (TCA Television, Cariou) holding that when the original work and the allegedly infringing use are before the court and their relationship plainly shows fair use, dismissal is appropriate. Santos’s complaint incorporated the videos themselves and the registration records; those materials made the transformativeness and lack of market harm apparent without discovery.

That procedural posture matters for litigants. Defendants who can attach or otherwise incorporate the allegedly infringing pair of works into their motion to dismiss preserve an efficient path to end cases early when the context of use is plainly transformative.

## Practical implications for creators, platforms, and media

For late‑night shows, news outlets, and other commentary programmers, Santos is a favorable ruling: repurposing user‑created or commissioned content as part of critical commentary can be fair use even if a defendant solicited the material and even if the solicitation involved theatrics or false names. The key is presentation—if the broadcast changes the message and frames it critically, courts will look to perception, not procurement.

For creators and platforms, the decision is a reminder that copyright is not the only lever to control downstream use. Santos’s state‑law contract and fraud claims failed because he was not a party to Cameo’s Terms of Service, he failed to plead an enforceable implied agreement with Kimmel/ABC, and he could not allege out‑of‑pocket damages for fraud under New York law. If creators wish to preserve control over how paid‑for, platform‑mediated content is reused, express contractual terms or assignment arrangements are necessary—platform terms that omit clear third‑party‑beneficiary language will not rescue non‑contracting creators. Platforms that want to limit reuse should make those restrictions express and consider enforcement pathways beyond a generic Terms of Service.

## Doctrinal implications and open questions

Santos shows the Second Circuit continuing to build Warhol into its fair‑use toolkit: objective, perception‑based analysis, with willingness to decide the issue early where the record supports it. That approach will likely encourage more motions to dismiss in media‑heavy cases and push plaintiffs to plead more granular facts about original purpose, audience perception, and market harm. Two open questions loom. First, how courts will define the “reasonable observer” across different media ecologies—should that hypothetical viewer be assumed ignorant of behind‑the‑scenes prompting, or may knowledge that a defendant solicited the work affect perception? Second, when does solicitation or commissioning cross a line into making the secondary user the true author or “designer” of the original work for purposes of factor one? Santos answers those questions narrowly: the mere fact of solicitation, especially without supporting allegations about how the solicitation altered the work’s meaning, does not defeat a finding of transformativness. But future litigants who can show that solicitation produced materially different content intended for reuse may find a different result.

Finally, Santos underscores a doctrinal reality: fair use jurisprudence remains intensely fact‑dependent. The Second Circuit’s clarity about the legal test does not eliminate close calls; it does, however, give defendants a clear early‑stage strategy when repurposing published or user‑made content for commentary.

## Bottom line

Santos v. Kimmel is the Second Circuit’s tidy application of Warhol to the world of Cameo clips and late‑night satire. By focusing on how a reasonable observer would perceive the two works and by resolving fair use on the pleadings where the materials permit, the court offers a practical playbook for media defendants and a cautionary note to creators who hope to control downstream re‑use without express contractual protections.



# Fair Use Without Good Faith? Deception, Satire, and the Shrinking Role of ‘Bad Faith’

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel is a compact but important addition to a steady stream of fair‑use decisions that treat deception by a secondary user as legally unavailing when the ensuing use is plainly critical or non‑substitutive. The facts are familiar and headline‑grabbing: a late‑night host used fictitious names to obtain personalized Cameo videos from former Congressman George Santos, then aired them in a mocking segment. Santos sued for copyright infringement (and a host of state‑law claims). The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal at the pleading stage, holding that the broadcasts were a paradigmatic fair use and rejecting Santos’s attempt to make Kimmel’s alleged deception dispositive.

Why that matters beyond the tabloids is twofold. First, the court reiterates that fair use analysis focuses on transformation and market substitution as judged by a reasonable observer, not on a moral narrative about the user’s conduct. Second, it follows recent Second Circuit and Supreme Court guidance that the Harper & Row dictum that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing” is not a talisman that defeats fair use whenever a defendant has acted deceptively. That doctrinal posture has practical consequences for journalists, satirists, platforms, and content creators—altering which factual disputes are likely to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

## What the Second Circuit said (in short)

The court reviewed the fair‑use factors de novo and concluded the first factor—purpose and character—weighed strongly for defendants because the JKL segments added a new purpose: sarcastic commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Drawing on the Supreme Court’s instruction in the Warhol case that transformativeness is assessed from the perspective of a reasonable observer rather than the subjective intent of either party, the panel rejected Santos’s insistence that Kimmel “instigated” the videos and so could not claim their use was transformative. The court also refused to treat Kimmel’s use as designed to supplant any licensing market for the videos. Consistent with recent Second Circuit precedent (and Campbell’s market‑substitution focus), it asked not whether Kimmel’s pieces “devalued” Santos’s originals but whether they offered a competing substitute for them—and found no plausible substitution. Because the complaint and attached registrations made those conclusions apparent, the court affirmed dismissal on fair use grounds at the pleading stage.

The court then disposed of Santos’s state‑law claims on ordinary contract and fraud principles: Santos was not a party to Cameo’s terms and alleged no plausible third‑party beneficiary or implied‑in‑fact contract, and he failed to plead the out‑of‑pocket loss required for a New York fraudulent inducement claim.

## How Santos fits the recent trajectory of fair‑use doctrine

Santos slots neatly into a line of decisions that have pushed fair use away from moralizing inquiries about a defendant’s conduct and toward an emphasis on transformation and market effect. Think of Authors Guild v. Google (library scanning as transformative, no market substitution), Swatch v. Bloomberg (use for reporting/commentary weighed heavily), Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive (careful market and use analysis), and the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision emphasizing the reasonable‑observer, transformation‑focused test. The Second Circuit here echoes those themes.

Two doctrinal points are worth underscoring. First, the court repeats that the transformativeness inquiry is objective. Whether Santos or Kimmel subjectively intended a particular purpose does not control; a reasonable observer’s perception of the works’ communicative content does. That makes sense doctrinally: fair use is about whether the secondary work performs a new purpose in the cultural economy, a question ill‑suited to probing messy, self‑serving intent allegations.

Second, the court treats bad faith or deception as relevant only insofar as it bears on the statutory factors—especially market effect—not as an independent trump. The opinion acknowledges Harper & Row’s language that fair use “presupposes good faith,” but it follows more recent authority in declining to give that dictum independent weight. Instead, deceptive procurement will defeat a fair‑use defense only when it helps prove that the secondary use is non‑transformative or that it usurps a licensing market. That is precisely what the Second Circuit held here: Kimmel’s deception did not transform the segment into a competing substitute for Santos’s originals, and the complaint itself undercut any claim that the broadcasts were an effort to replace Santos’s market.

## Limits the opinion preserves

Santos is not a blanket green light for deception. The court’s approach makes two limits plain. First, deception remains relevant when it bears directly on the statutory factors—especially market substitution. If a secondary user obtains a work by deceit and then tries to exploit it as a commercial substitute (for example, by repackaging and selling the content in place of the original), courts will view the market‑effect factor differently. Second, deceptive procurement may give rise to non‑copyright claims—fraud, torts, or contract remedies—or may be relevant to equitable relief or damages—even if it does not, by itself, defeat a fair‑use defense. In Santos the state claims failed on independent pleading grounds; that is not to say a stronger fraud claim could never coexist with a viable copyright action.

Put differently: deception is not per se fatal to fair use, but it is not irrelevant either. Its legal importance turns on how deception affects transformation, substitution, and the plaintiff’s other legal remedies.

## Practical and doctrinal consequences

For litigants, Santos reinforces two practical lessons. Plaintiffs alleging infringement should plead facts showing plausible market harm or that the secondary use serves as a market substitute; naked allegations of bad faith will not fill that gap. Defendants should know that an objectively obvious critical or parodic repurposing can be resolved early on the pleadings if the record (including registrations) supports non‑substitutive transformation.

For platforms and content creators, the case raises predictable policy responses. Platforms like Cameo will likely revisit terms of service, consent mechanisms, and disclosures so that creators know how their content might be reused and to limit ambiguous third‑party claims. Creators who want to preserve licensing leverage should think about affirmative license terms, exclusivity, and express contractual language about reuse and third‑party beneficiaries.

Doctrinally, Santos continues the narrowing of Harper & Row’s moralistic contours and cements the market‑substitution/transformative axis as the central organizing principle for fair use in the Second Circuit. That makes fair‑use doctrine more predictable in many commercial and journalistic contexts, but also raises normative tensions: if courts stop treating bad faith as a weighty, standalone factor, will parties have incentives to engage in questionable tactics to procure content for satire and criticism? The court implicitly answers that worry by pointing to other bodies of law, but that creates a distributional effect: copyright law will generally not protect creators against reputational or purely moral harms that do not translate into market substitution.

## Bottom line

Santos v. Kimmel is an incremental but telling reaffirmation of the direction in which modern fair‑use law has been heading—away from moralizing about the defendant’s character and toward an objective, market‑oriented assessment of transformation and substitution as perceived by a reasonable observer. Deception can matter, but only when it changes the statutory calculus—by converting an ostensibly critical use into a market substitute or by giving rise to independent legal relief. For satirists and broadcasters the ruling is reassuring; for content creators who do not have clear licensing markets, it is a reminder that copyright will not always vindicate every wrong that feels wrongful. Expect the lawyers and platforms on both sides to adjust accordingly, and expect further litigation where deception and commercial exploitation sit closer together on the facts.



# No Third-Party Beneficiary, No Case: Platform ToS and the Limits of Creator Enforcement

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters
The Second Circuit’s October 6, 2025 decision in Santos v. Kimmel is a useful reminder that the path from an unpleasant broadcast to a contract remedy runs through two narrow gates: (1) being a party to the contract (or an expressly intended third‑party beneficiary), and (2) pleading the essential terms and a meeting of minds if you instead rely on an implied agreement. The case shows how courts treat mass-market platform terms as contracts between the platform and its users, not as a vehicle for outsiders to police each other’s conduct, and it reinforces familiar pleading hurdles for implied contracts and fraud claims. For creators who want contractual control over downstream uses of content obtained through platforms, the opinion signals both the practical steps they should take and an unresolved doctrinal risk—whether contract-based restrictions might, in some formulations, collide with federal copyright preemption.

## What the court held
The Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal. It agreed that Kimmel’s airing of the Cameo videos was protected fair use and therefore foreclosed the copyright claims. Turning to Santos’s state-law claims—breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement—the panel did not decide whether the Copyright Act would have preempted those claims. Instead it held that Santos failed to state those claims on ordinary state‑law grounds. Applying Illinois law to the Cameo Terms of Service, the court held that Santos was not a contracting party and that Illinois presumes against conferring benefits on noncontracting third parties: “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Under New York law the panel held Santos also failed to plead an implied‑in‑fact contract because he did not allege the essential terms or a meeting of the parties’ minds, and his fraudulent‑inducement claim failed for lack of pleaded out‑of‑pocket loss.

## The court’s reasoning — what to take away doctrinally
Two doctrinal threads deserve emphasis.

First, the Third‑Party Beneficiary rule as applied to clickwrap/ToS contexts is unforgiving. The panel treated the Cameo Terms as a classic user–platform agreement governed by Illinois law; Santos, who never contracted with Cameo as the opposing party, could not step into the parties’ shoes absent an express or overwhelming implication of intent to benefit third parties. The opinion relied on what many courts do when interpreting mass contracts: when the agreement contains express third‑party‑beneficiary language in some places but not the provision at issue, that choice counsels against reading in implicit third‑party enforcement rights. Practically, that means a platform that wants nonusers (for example, creators) to have standing to enforce particular provisions must include clear, express language to that effect.

Second, implied‑in‑fact contracts are not an easy workaround. The Second Circuit emphasized that pleading an implied contract requires non‑conclusory allegations of the essential terms and an indication that the parties reached a meeting of the minds. Santos’s allegations that he produced videos in response to requests and that defendants later used those videos were insufficient; the complaint lacked allegations showing Kimmel agreed to any particular use restriction or other enforceable term. Likewise, the court reiterated the classic New York requirement that a fraud claim plead actual, pecuniary (out‑of‑pocket) loss, which Santos did not do.

Taken together, these holdings close off the most straightforward contract-based routes for a creator who is not a contracting party and who did not secure explicit promises from the requester.

## Practical takeaways for platforms and creators
The opinion maps out several practical responses that are already intuitive but now carry added force.

For platforms: if you want creators to be able to enforce certain permissions or restrictions, put it in writing—and put it expressly into the relevant clause of the contract. The presence elsewhere in the agreement of third‑party‑beneficiary language will be parsed as deliberate drafting; omission will be read as deliberate too. Platforms that do not want creators suing in their own name should continue to draft in a way that avoids conferring third‑party rights, make enforcement a platform function, and be explicit about that allocation.

For creators: do not rely on a platform’s user agreement to give you third‑party enforcement rights unless the agreement says so plainly. If you want to control downstream uses, get an express license or release from the person requesting content (or from the platform), in writing and with clear terms on permitted uses, compensation, remedies, and enforcement. Preserve communications that show assent and, when possible, use explicit language in the request interface (e.g., a checkbox or a “terms agreed” acknowledgement that includes the requester’s promises about use). If you believe a requester misled you, document any economic loss—out‑of‑pocket expense, lost licensing fees, canceled engagements—because some jurisdictions require such a showing for fraud claims.

For broadcasters, producers and intermediaries: the decision also underscores the continuing force of copyright defenses (fair use in this case). Contractual limitations that are not expressly bargained for by the requester may not be available to third parties, so broadcasters should be mindful of platform and creator practices but can take some comfort that the law does not automatically give creators a contractual veto.

## The open question left on preemption
The panel expressly declined to reach the Copyright Act preemption question because Santos’s state‑law claims failed on ordinary pleading grounds. That leaves open a live doctrinal issue: even if a creator could establish third‑party‑beneficiary status or an enforceable contract with a requester, would state-law claims that functionally restrict reproduction or public performance be preempted as equivalent to the exclusive rights enumerated in 17 U.S.C. § 106? The Second Circuit cited Melendez v. Sirius XM for the baseline test: state claims survive only if they are “qualitatively different” from copyright claims. How that test applies to an express contract that, for example, grants a creator a right to prevent broadcasting of a video is unsettled. Platforms and drafters who think they can give creators a private contractual right to block otherwise lawful uses should understand there may be a separate federal preemption hurdle, depending on how the claim is framed and the remedy sought.

## Bottom line
Santos v. Kimmel is a cautionary tale for creators who hope to enforce platform terms against third parties: absent an express contractual hook or well‑pled direct agreement, courts will not infer third‑party enforcement rights from generic ToS, and they will expect concrete allegations of essential terms and damages if you try to plead around that deficiency. Platforms that want to empower creators—or to prevent creator suits—should draft their terms accordingly. And because the panel left preemption unresolved, counsel and drafters should also think about whether the remedies they create by contract are susceptible to federal preemption as overlapping with copyright’s exclusive rights.



# Markets for Lampoon, Not Licensing: Factor Four (and Three) in the Age of Cameo

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel (Oct. 6, 2025) is a useful, practical ruling for anyone who makes, licenses, aggregates, or comments on bite‑sized, paid‑for content — think Cameo clips, influencer shout‑outs, and similar personalized productions. Former Congressman George Santos sued after late‑night host Jimmy Kimmel placed orders through Cameo, received Santos’s paid shout‑outs, and then aired them as part of a mocking segment on Jimmy Kimmel Live!. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal on the pleadings, holding that the broadcasts were fair use and that Santos’s related state‑law claims failed.

The case matters because it reiterates a now‑familiar but still consequential framing of the copyright fair‑use inquiry: factor four is about usurpation of a cognizable market, not mere devaluation or embarrassment, and factor three (amount copied) can be neutralized when wholesale copying is integral to a piece’s critical or commentary purpose. For media lawyers and content platforms, Santos is a reminder that televised lampooning of paid‑for clips ordinarily won’t look like a market substitute for the original one‑off services those clips were sold to provide.

## How the court applied the fair‑use factors

The court walked through the four statutory factors in a way that will be recognizable to Second Circuit practice. On factor one the court emphasized transformativeness: Kimmel’s re‑use had a different communicative purpose — to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money — and the “reasonable observer” test (as refined by the Supreme Court in Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith) controls over any asserted subjective intent of the parties. The complaint itself, the court said, described Santos’s originals as conveying hope and encouragement; a reasonable viewer watching JKL would see something different. That transformed purpose pushed factor one squarely toward fair use.

Factor two received a short but important factual point: registrations in the record showed the Cameo videos were published the same day they were created. Published status reduces the special protection courts afford unpublished works under Harper & Row, so factor two did not help Santos much.

The court disposed of factor four in language that will be quoted frequently by defenders of commentary: courts look to whether the secondary use “usurps the market for the [original] by offering a competing substitute,” not whether the secondary use merely devalues or embarrasses the original. Borrowing the Hachette/ Campbell line, the court concluded that creators of imaginative, commissioned, or personal works are unlikely to have a licensing market for critical reviews or lampoons of those works — and so JKL’s ridicule did not plausibly supplant any cognizable market for Santos’s Cameo clips.

Factor three — JKL’s use of the entire clips — might have weighed against fair use on its own. But the court agreed with the district court that the remaining factors, particularly the clear transformative purpose and lack of a licensing market, overcame that negative weight. Put another way: copying the whole clip mattered less where the defendant’s use was commentary‑driven and not a market substitute.

Pulling those factors together, the Second Circuit was comfortable deciding fair use at the pleading stage because the relevant facts were before the court (the complaint and the clips themselves), and those facts demonstrated fair use as a matter of law.

## Markets for lampoon, not licensing

The decision is an emphatic application of the “usurpation versus devaluation” lens that the Second Circuit has been cultivating. That lens matters in two related ways.

First, it protects the expressive space for criticism, satire, and newsworthy lampoon. Personalized videos sold through a platform like Cameo are purchased for the moment: a buyer asks for a bespoke address, that buyer receives a clip, and the clip’s economic value lies in that private performance. The court reasonably treated that transactional market as distinct from a market for licensing third‑party broadcasts or compilations of such clips as critical fodder. Courts have long been skeptical that a licensing market exists for the right to lampoon one’s own work; Santos’s claim that Kimmel’s airing “devalued” his work was therefore not the kind of economic harm the fourth factor targets.

Second, the court’s framing signals what will flip the analysis: if a defendant’s use actually functions as a practical market substitute — for example, repackaging clips for sale, building a competing service that monetizes the same customers, or otherwise exploiting the same licensing channels the rightsholder uses — factor four will cut the other way. The opinion doesn’t abolish factor four; it clarifies that mere embarrassment, reputational harm, or a dip in perceived value from criticism is not, without more, the sort of usurpation the statute forbids.

That distinction is especially salient for political figures and other public personalities who sell short‑form content. If airing a Cameo clip as part of a joke is not a substitute for the Cameo market, the safe harbor for commentary is broad — but not limitless. Volume, packaging, or a commercial re‑use strategy aimed at the same customers could transform devaluation into usurpation.

## The quiet power of factor three (and why publication matters here)

It is tempting to read Santos as an invitation to treat wholesale copying as a nonissue. That would be a mistake. Factor three still matters; what Santos does is remind us how factors interact.

Using an entire work typically counts against fair use. But courts also recognize that commentary and criticism often require enough of the original to “conjure up” what is being commented upon. The Second Circuit has been willing to tolerate whole‑work copying where the copying is necessary for the new work’s critical purpose and where, crucially, the use isn’t a market substitute. That is the posture in Santos: whole clips were used, but the use was plainly aimed at commentary and not at displacing a licensing market.

Publication status bridged factors two and four in a helpful way for the defendants. The registrations in the record showed the clips were published contemporaneously with creation, which reduced the protective weight of factor two (unpublished works get special protection). Publication also makes it harder to argue that the content was shielded from broadcast use by virtue of being private and unpublished. Where the original content has already entered the public realm, the need to allow commentary on it is stronger.

For media lawyers, the upshot is practical: if your segment’s point requires audience exposure to a clip in order to make a critical point, courts accept that need as a justification for substantial copying so long as the broader fair‑use calculus is favorable. But that justification is fact‑sensitive and self‑limiting; it does not license wholesale repurposing or commercial exploitation that targets the same customer base as the original market.

## Practical takeaways for media lawyers and platforms

For producers and broadcasters who rely on short, user‑created clips, Santos yields several implementable lessons. First, document the commentary purpose: editorial notes, pre‑air scripts, and segment narrations that make clear the use is critical or satirical help frame factor one. Second, tailor the clips used to the smallest amount necessary to make the point; whole‑clip uses can be defended, but restraint reduces risk on factor three. Third, avoid packaging or re‑monetizing the clips in a way that looks like a substitute for the content’s original market; if you plan to create a product or a library that repurposes paid-for clips for sale, license instead of relying on fair use. Fourth, platform operators and talent who want to preserve enforcement options should draft explicit terms of service and licenses that identify permissible third‑party uses and, if they wish, create an express third‑party‑beneficiary or licensing scheme that governs broadcasts.

Finally, attorneys for rights‑holders seeking to police reuse should bear in mind Santos’s procedural posture: the Second Circuit was willing to resolve fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) record because the complaint and the clips themselves made the defensive case obvious. A well‑pleaded complaint that alleges facts plausibly showing a market substitution could put fair use back into genuine dispute; the safe route for rights‑holders is to plead and, where appropriate, to adduce evidence showing that a defendant’s use targets the same market as the original.

## Doctrinal implications and what to watch next

Santos squarely tracks the Second Circuit’s recent fair‑use trajectory — Hachette, Cariou, TCA — and responds to the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision by applying the reasonable‑observer test to determine the secondary use’s purpose. The opinion reinforces two developing principles: (1) factor four should be focused on usurpation of markets the rightsholder actually exploits or plausibly could exploit, and (2) factor three’s weight depends on whether whole copying is necessary for the transformative aim.

We should watch for two developments. First, how other circuits apply the same framing to short‑form, paid‑for content created on platforms. Second, how platforms and creators respond contractually: the easiest way to change the outcome here would be contractual language that creates enforceable reuse restrictions or grants a licensing market to rights‑holders. The Second Circuit’s disposition of Santos’s state‑law claims — that contract‑based and fraud theories failed on the pleadings — suggests those remedies are not automatic and must be carefully pleaded and drafted.

## Bottom line

Santos v. Kimmel reinforces a simple rule of thumb for the modern media environment: ridicule and commentary are not the same thing as competition. When a broadcaster uses a paid‑for, one‑off shout‑out in order to lampoon the subject, that use will often be transformative and unlikely to usurp the underlying market for personalized messages. But wholesale copying and commercial repurposing aimed at the same customers remains risky. For content creators, platforms, and lawyers, the decision is a call to marry editorial clarity with careful licensing strategy.
