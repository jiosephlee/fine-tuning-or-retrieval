Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Does deception in acquiring a work (fake names on Cameo) defeat a fair use defense?
Question:
Santos argued Kimmel’s false identities on Cameo show bad faith that should negate fair use, pointing to Harper & Row’s statement that fair use presupposes good faith. The panel responds that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from that statement and emphasizes the critical/commentary purpose instead. What is the current role of "good faith" in fair use after Harper & Row and Campbell? Can deceptive acquisition weigh against fair use under factor one, or is it largely irrelevant unless it ties to market substitution or another factor? Are there cases where a defendant lied to obtain a copy but still prevailed (or lost) on fair use that help clarify how courts treat bad faith in the analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
No — deception in obtaining a work is not a per se bar to fair use. After Harper & Row the courts (and the Supreme Court) have moved away from treating “good faith” as a standalone, dispositive requirement. Deceptive acquisition can be evidence against fair use, but only to the extent it bears on the Section 107 factors (most commonly factor 1 — purpose/character — and factor 4 — market effect). Santos v. Kimmel is the recent, on-point example: the court held Kimmel’s use was transformative and not market‑supplanting, and therefore the alleged use of false identities did not defeat fair use.

Why (doctrinally) — what Santos says
- Harper & Row contained the oft‑quoted language that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but Santos explains that the Supreme Court has “since distanced itself from this statement.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. at § I. In short, “good faith” is not a freestanding element that automatically defeats fair use.
- Instead courts continue to analyze the statutory factors, with heavy emphasis on transformation under factor 1 and on market substitution under factor 4. Santos reiterates that factor 1 “focuses chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative,” quoting Andy Warhol, and that factor 4 asks whether the secondary use “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute,” quoting Campbell as Santos does. Santos v. Kimmel, § I (collecting authorities).
- Santos applied those principles: even accepting the complaint’s allegation that Kimmel used fictitious names to obtain Cameo videos, the complaint itself showed the JKL segments were sarcastic criticism (a transformative purpose) and did not plead market usurpation; therefore the deception did not negate fair use.

How deception can (and cannot) matter in practice
- It can matter, but only as evidence relevant to the statutory factors:
  - Factor 1 (purpose/character): deception can support an inference the secondary user’s purpose was to appropriate the original for the original’s purpose (non‑transformative) rather than to add new expression, but that inference is assessed by the objective reasonable‑observer test (Santos cites Andy Warhol on objective assessment), not by a purely subjective label of “bad faith.”
  - Factor 4 (market effect): deception can be probative of market harm if the way the defendant obtained the work shows intent to create a competing product or to exploit a licensing market (Santos cites Campbell on the licensing‑market inquiry).
  - Factors 2 and 3: deception is generally less directly probative, though contextual facts can matter.
- It is not dispositive on its own. As Santos shows, courts will not automatically allow a plaintiff to defeat fair use simply by pleading that the defendant lied to get a copy. The key question remains whether the secondary use is transformative and whether it usurps a potential market for the original.

Illustrative cases (from the Santos opinion)
- Santos v. Kimmel (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025): Defendant used fake Cameo identities; court held fair use at pleading stage because the complaint itself showed a transformative, critical purpose and no market usurpation — so the deception did not nullify fair use.
- Harper & Row v. Nation (quoted in Santos): the Supreme Court once said “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” language plaintiffs still invoke; Santos notes the Court has since distanced itself from treating that statement as a categorical rule.
- Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose (quoted in Santos): emphasizes that market substitution/licensing concerns — not a blanket good‑faith rule — are central to factor 4 analysis (Campbell language quoted in Santos about licensing markets for parodies).

Practical takeaway for pleading/defending
- If you represent a plaintiff: pleading that a defendant lied is useful, but you must connect that deceit to one of the Section 107 factors — most effectively by alleging (with factual detail) that the deceit shows a non‑transformative purpose or resulted in direct market usurpation/harms.
- If you represent a defendant: be prepared to show transformation (what a reasonable observer would see) and explain why the secondary use does not act as a market substitute; deception allegations will be treated as one piece of contextual evidence, not an automatic knockout.

Bottom line
Santos confirms the modern approach: bad faith/deception can be relevant evidence, but it is not a freestanding trump to fair use. Courts will fold such allegations into the Section 107 factor analysis — especially factors 1 and 4 — and will sustain fair use where the use is objectively transformative and not market‑supplanting even if the defendant obtained the material deceptively.
