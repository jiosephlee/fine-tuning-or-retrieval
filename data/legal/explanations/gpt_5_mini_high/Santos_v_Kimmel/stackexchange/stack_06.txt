Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why didn’t the court resolve Copyright Act preemption of the contract claims, and when are contract/fraud claims preempted under § 301?
Question:
The district court dismissed the breach-of-contract and implied-contract claims as preempted, but the Second Circuit didn’t reach preemption and instead held the claims fail under state law. Doctrinally, when would claims like breach of a platform’s Terms of Service or implied-in-fact promises be preempted by § 301? What is the Second Circuit’s two-prong test (subject matter + equivalence), and how do "extra elements" (like a promise or misrepresentation) affect preemption for contract and fraud claims? If Santos had adequately alleged an implied promise restricting broadcast use, would that kind of undertaking normally avoid preemption as qualitatively different from a copyright claim?
Answer:
Short answer
The Second Circuit declined to decide Copyright Act preemption because it could (and did) dispose of Santos’s state-law claims on straightforward state‑law pleading grounds. Doctrinally, the court’s reference to Melendez signals the Second Circuit’s § 301 framework: a state claim is preempted only if (1) its subject matter falls within the subject matter of copyright law and (2) the state‑law right is equivalent to one of the exclusive rights of copyright (i.e., the state cause of action does no more than grant rights that § 106 already supplies). Claims that add “extra elements” (a promise, misrepresentation, reliance, out‑of‑pocket loss, etc.) can be “qualitatively different” and therefore avoid § 301 preemption — but whether they do is fact‑specific.

How that plays out in Santos v. Kimmel
- Why the court didn’t reach preemption: The panel said Santos argued his state claims were “qualitatively different” and therefore not preempted (citing Melendez), but “we need not resolve this issue . . . because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law.” In short, the court applied judicial restraint: it disposed of the case on state‑law pleading defects (third‑party beneficiary and implied‑contract pleading failures; and failure to plead out‑of‑pocket loss on fraud) instead of deciding the § 301 preemption question. (Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II.)

The Second Circuit’s § 301 (two‑prong) framework (as the opinion frames it)
- The opinion cites the Second Circuit line that treats preemption as a two‑part inquiry (the panel cites Melendez for the “qualitatively different” formulation). In practice the test requires asking:
  1. Subject‑matter: Does the state claim concern works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium (i.e., does it fall within the subject matter of the Copyright Act)?
  2. Equivalence: Is the state claim seeking to vindicate the same exclusive rights that the Copyright Act protects (reproduction, distribution, public performance/display, preparation of derivative works)? If yes, the claim is equivalent and preempted; if the state claim contains an “extra element” that makes it qualitatively different, it normally survives preemption.
- The Santos opinion invokes this “qualitatively different” concept (Santos’s brief relied on it via Melendez), and then declines to resolve whether Santos’s contract/fraud claims met that threshold because they failed on their own terms.

“Extra elements” and how they affect contract/fraud claims
- Contract claims: A state contract claim will avoid preemption when it rests on contractual obligations that are not merely a restatement of the Copyright Act’s exclusive rights — e.g., formation, mutual assent, consideration, specific contractual promises, third‑party beneficiary status. The opinion rejects Santos’s direct breach theory because he is not a signatory and he pleaded nothing approaching the “express declaration” needed (Illinois law) that the Cameo Terms were meant to benefit non‑parties; and his implied‑in‑fact theory failed because he did not plead the essential terms or a meeting of the minds under New York law. Those are the kinds of “extra” contract elements a court considers when deciding whether a claim is qualitatively different. (See Santos § II, discussing Illinois and New York law.)
- Fraud claims: Fraud adds elements (a false representation, scienter, reliance, damages). Under New York law, for example, fraudulent inducement requires an actual out‑of‑pocket loss; Santos’s complaint failed to allege any such loss and thus failed on state‑law grounds. A properly pleaded fraud claim that alleges these elements can be qualitatively different from a pure copyright claim and may survive § 301 preemption — but again that is fact‑dependent. (Santos § II, discussing Lama/Connaughton.)

Would an adequately pleaded implied promise not to broadcast normally avoid preemption?
- Possible, but not automatic. If Santos had plausibly alleged an implied contract that imposed an independent promise (an “extra element”) — e.g., a clear undertaking by a specific requester or platform that the cameo would not be broadcast, plus the essential terms and a meeting of the minds — that could make the claim “qualitatively different” from a copyright action and therefore avoid § 301 preemption (the Second Circuit’s “qualitatively different” language is the hook for that argument, as cited in the Santos opinion).
- The caveat the Santos panel signals (and other Second Circuit decisions have emphasized): the substance matters. If the alleged promise simply duplicates an exclusive § 106 right (for example, a promise that simply says “you may not publicly perform or distribute the work”), courts may treat the state promise as equivalent to copyright and preempt it. By contrast, a promise that gives rise to an independent contractual obligation (confidentiality, limitation on specified downstream uses, reliance‑based injury, etc.) is more likely to be treated as an “extra element” and survive preemption — but the Santos court did not decide this factual question because Santos’s pleading failed on state‑law grounds.

Practical takeaways for pleading to avoid § 301 preemption (rooted in the Santos opinion)
- If you rely on contract: plead that you are an intended third‑party beneficiary or that there was an enforceable implied‑in‑fact contract with specific, nonconclusory allegations of the essential terms and a meeting of the minds. Santos lost because he alleged neither.
- If you rely on fraud: plead the fraud elements required by the governing state law (misrepresentation, reliance, and the particular damages that state law requires — e.g., out‑of‑pocket loss in New York). Santos’s fraud claim failed for lack of such loss.
- If your state claim genuinely adds obligations or injuries beyond the exclusive bundle of copyright rights (not merely a different legal label for the same harm), emphasize those “extra elements” in the complaint — that is the route the Second Circuit recognizes (Melendez, cited in Santos) to escape § 301 preemption.

Bottom line: Santos lost not because the panel held § 301 would necessarily preempt claims like his, but because his contract and fraud counts failed on their own state‑law pleading requirements; whether a properly pleaded implied promise restricting broadcast would avoid preemption is a fact‑dependent question the panel expressly declined to decide.
