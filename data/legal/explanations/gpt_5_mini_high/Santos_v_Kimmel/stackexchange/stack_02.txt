Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the "reasonable observer" test for transformative purpose fit with the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision?
Question:
The panel says the transformative analysis turns on what a reasonable observer would think, not the subjective intent of either party, and cites Warhol (2023) for the proposition that subjective intent doesn’t control. But Warhol also emphasized analyzing the specific use’s purpose in relation to the relevant licensing market rather than relying solely on new meaning or message. Here, the court frames Kimmel’s use as commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. How does that square with Warhol’s market-focused approach to factor one? In practice, after Warhol, should courts also assess whether the defendant’s use serves the same or different licensing markets even when the message is clearly critical/commentary? What kinds of allegations or evidence would matter at 12(b)(6)?
Answer:
Short answer
They’re complementary, not contradictory. Santos applied Warhol’s core point — that transformativeness under factor one is an objective, reasonable‑observer inquiry, not a probe into subjective intent — and then (as the statute and Warhol permit) separately assessed market impact under factor four. So after Warhol courts still ask both: (1) what a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use to be (transformativeness), and (2) whether the defendant’s use usurps a licensable market for the original. When the message is plainly critical, factor one often favors fair use; but factor four can still defeat fair use if the plaintiff plausibly shows the defendant’s use occupies the same licensing market as the original.

Why that is the right read (grounded in Santos)
- Warhol’s “no‑subjective‑intent” rule was the only Warhol proposition the Santos panel invoked for factor one: “whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. at __ (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (quoting Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023)). The panel used that objective test to conclude a reasonable observer would perceive Kimmel’s segments as commentary about Santos, not as a re‑use of Santos’s original purpose.
- Santos then addressed the market effect separately under factor four, using the familiar “usurpation” formulation: courts “ask not whether the second work would damage the market … but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Santos, slip op. at __ (quoting Hachette/Campbell). The panel found no plausible market usurpation because creators of imaginative works “are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons,” so that kind of commentary is ordinarily outside the relevant licensing market.

Putting the two together
- Factor 1 (purpose/character) = objective, reasonable‑observer assessment of whether the secondary work adds a new meaning/purpose or merely supersedes the original. Per Santos/Warhol, courts look to what a reasonable observer would think, not the parties’ unspoken motives.
- Factor 4 (market effect) = a distinct inquiry into whether the secondary use functions as a market substitute in a licensable market for the original. Even a work that communicates new meaning (commentary/criticism) can lose on factor four if it plausibly displaces a market that the copyright owner would normally exploit or license.
- Santos illustrates both steps: the court found the reasonable‑observer test established transformation (commentary) and then concluded on the pleadings that there was no plausible allegation of usurpation of any licensable market.

Practical rule after Warhol (and how courts will apply it)
- Yes — courts should and will assess whether the defendant’s use serves the same or different licensing markets even when the message is clearly critical. But the usual outcome is that critical commentary is not the sort of use the original author would license, so factor four often favors fair use. The critical question is factual: is there (or plausibly was) a licensing market for the copyrighted material in the particular manner the defendant used it?
- If the plaintiff can plausibly allege that the defendant’s use occupies a licensable market the plaintiff exploits (or could reasonably exploit), factor four can outweigh a finding of transformation.

What helps (and hurts) at the 12(b)(6) stage
Santos is instructive about pleading. Fair use can sometimes be decided on the face of the complaint and attached exhibits, but dismissal is appropriate only when the complaint and documents plainly establish fair use. To survive a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff should plead non‑conclusory, factual allegations that make either (A) the reasonable‑observer outcome or (B) market usurpation plausible:

Examples of allegations/evidence that would matter at 12(b)(6)
- Evidence of an actual or established licensing practice: prior license agreements, invoices, communications showing the plaintiff licensed the same or similar works for broadcast/compilation use.
- Allegations that the defendant used the work in the same functional role and distribution channels as licensed uses (e.g., the exact clips rebroadcast in monetized programming or sold/licensed to third parties).
- Allegations that the defendant sought to acquire a license, was refused, and then used the material anyway (shows a plausible intent to supplant the licensing market).
- Allegations that the plaintiff actively monetized the works (subscriptions, sublicensing, exclusive deals) and lost or would have lost licensing revenue because of the defendant’s use.
- Attachment of objective materials (the defendant’s segments, the original works, registration materials, any license offers) that support a non‑transformative reading or market‑substitution claim.

What won’t help (or will hurt) at pleading stage
- Conclusory assertions that defendants acted in bad faith or intended to “supplant” without factual support. Santos rejected Santos’s bad‑faith argument where the complaint’s own allegations (and exhibits) portrayed Kimmel’s motive as sarcastic commentary rather than market supplanting.
- Merely alleging that the defendant “instigated” creation or that the defendant had commercial motives, without facts tying that conduct to a plausible licensing market displacement.

Bottom line for litigants and students
- Read Warhol and Santos as prescribing two connected but distinct inquiries: an objective reasonable‑observer test for transformation (factor 1) and a market‑usurpation inquiry (factor 4). Courts should do both; a clear critical message often carries the day on factor 1, but if the plaintiff plausibly pleads that the defendant’s use substitutes for a licensable market the plaintiff actually exploits, factor 4 can defeat fair use — and to survive a 12(b)(6) the complaint must plead those market facts, not just labels or conclusory assertions.
