Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Under Illinois law, what contract language is needed to make a non-user creator like Santos a third-party beneficiary of Cameo’s Terms of Service?
Question:
The panel applies Illinois law (per the ToS) and emphasizes the strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries absent language “practically an express declaration.” If the ToS contains provisions that appear designed to protect creators (e.g., restrictions on impersonation or redistribution), but don’t explicitly confer enforcement rights on creators, is that ever enough under Illinois law? How explicit does the ToS need to be to let creators sue users as intended beneficiaries, and do courts draw a negative inference when some clauses expressly name third-party beneficiaries but the clauses at issue do not? Any Illinois cases where a platform’s non-signatory service provider was deemed an intended beneficiary of a user agreement?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Illinois law as applied in Santos v. Kimmel, a platform’s Terms of Service must contain language that is essentially an “express declaration” that the non‑signatory (here, creators) are intended third‑party beneficiaries and have the right to enforce the contract. Mere protective provisions (no‑impersonation, no‑redistribution, etc.) that do not explicitly name creators as beneficiaries or grant them enforcement rights will not suffice. The Second Circuit pointedly relied on the absence of such an express declaration and on the fact that other ToS provisions did use explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language — a negative inference that undermined Santos’s claim.

Relevant doctrine from the opinion
- Strong presumption against third‑party beneficiaries: the court quotes Illinois authority that “there is a ‘strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.’” Santos v. Kimmel, No. 24‑2196‑cv, slip op. § II (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (quoting Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur).
- How strong that implication must be: “[t]he implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quoting Barry).
- Negative inference from express language elsewhere: the court relied on the ToS’s use of explicit third‑party beneficiary language in other provisions to show that “when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Id.

What that means in practice
- What is required: To satisfy Illinois law (per Santos), the ToS should do more than show that certain conduct is prohibited for the benefit of creators. It should plainly state that creators (either by name or a readily identifiable class) are intended third‑party beneficiaries and that they have the right to enforce the relevant provisions. That is what the court meant by an implication “practically an express declaration.”
- What is not enough: Broad protective wording alone (e.g., “Users may not impersonate third parties” or “You may not redistribute content”) that does not identify creators as beneficiaries or grant them enforcement rights will not overcome the presumption against third‑party enforcement. Santos failed because he “identifie[d] nothing close to an ‘express declaration’” that the provisions applied to him. Id.
- Negative inference: Yes — if the agreement uses explicit third‑party beneficiary language in some clauses but omits it in the clauses a non‑signatory seeks to enforce, courts will treat that omission as evidence the parties did not intend third‑party enforcement for the omitted clauses. The Second Circuit relied on that point in rejecting Santos’s claim.

Practical drafting guidance (illustrative only, grounded in Santos)
To create enforceable third‑party rights under the standard the court applied, a platform should include clear language such as:
- “The parties expressly intend that Creators who submit content to the Platform are third‑party beneficiaries of Sections X–Y of this Agreement and may enforce those provisions directly against Users and against the Platform.”
- Or: “This Agreement is for the benefit of [Platform], its Users, and Creators (defined as persons who submit content). Creators shall have the independent right to enforce any provision that protects Creators’ interests.”

(These examples illustrate the kind of “practically an express declaration” the Santos court required; the court rejected claims where the ToS lacked anything approaching that expressness.)

On Illinois case law about platforms / non‑signatory service providers
Santos cites the Illinois standard (Barry) and applies it to the Cameo ToS, but the Second Circuit opinion does not identify an Illinois decision that treats a platform’s non‑signatory service provider (or similar third party) as an intended beneficiary of a user agreement. The upshot of Santos is that, under Illinois law as applied there, such a result is unlikely without the explicit, express beneficiary language discussed above.

Bottom line
If you want non‑signatory creators to be able to sue users under a platform’s ToS under the Illinois standard quoted in Santos, put an express, unambiguous third‑party‑beneficiary clause in the agreement that (1) identifies the class or named beneficiaries and (2) expressly grants them enforcement rights. Absent that sort of “practically an express declaration,” courts will deny third‑party enforcement and may draw a negative inference where the ToS uses express beneficiary language elsewhere but not in the clauses at issue.
