Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Fair Use Without Good Faith? Deception, Satire, and the Shrinking Role of ‘Bad Faith’

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel is a compact but important addition to a steady stream of fair‑use decisions that treat deception by a secondary user as legally unavailing when the ensuing use is plainly critical or non‑substitutive. The facts are familiar and headline‑grabbing: a late‑night host used fictitious names to obtain personalized Cameo videos from former Congressman George Santos, then aired them in a mocking segment. Santos sued for copyright infringement (and a host of state‑law claims). The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal at the pleading stage, holding that the broadcasts were a paradigmatic fair use and rejecting Santos’s attempt to make Kimmel’s alleged deception dispositive.

Why that matters beyond the tabloids is twofold. First, the court reiterates that fair use analysis focuses on transformation and market substitution as judged by a reasonable observer, not on a moral narrative about the user’s conduct. Second, it follows recent Second Circuit and Supreme Court guidance that the Harper & Row dictum that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing” is not a talisman that defeats fair use whenever a defendant has acted deceptively. That doctrinal posture has practical consequences for journalists, satirists, platforms, and content creators—altering which factual disputes are likely to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

## What the Second Circuit said (in short)

The court reviewed the fair‑use factors de novo and concluded the first factor—purpose and character—weighed strongly for defendants because the JKL segments added a new purpose: sarcastic commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Drawing on the Supreme Court’s instruction in the Warhol case that transformativeness is assessed from the perspective of a reasonable observer rather than the subjective intent of either party, the panel rejected Santos’s insistence that Kimmel “instigated” the videos and so could not claim their use was transformative. The court also refused to treat Kimmel’s use as designed to supplant any licensing market for the videos. Consistent with recent Second Circuit precedent (and Campbell’s market‑substitution focus), it asked not whether Kimmel’s pieces “devalued” Santos’s originals but whether they offered a competing substitute for them—and found no plausible substitution. Because the complaint and attached registrations made those conclusions apparent, the court affirmed dismissal on fair use grounds at the pleading stage.

The court then disposed of Santos’s state‑law claims on ordinary contract and fraud principles: Santos was not a party to Cameo’s terms and alleged no plausible third‑party beneficiary or implied‑in‑fact contract, and he failed to plead the out‑of‑pocket loss required for a New York fraudulent inducement claim.

## How Santos fits the recent trajectory of fair‑use doctrine

Santos slots neatly into a line of decisions that have pushed fair use away from moralizing inquiries about a defendant’s conduct and toward an emphasis on transformation and market effect. Think of Authors Guild v. Google (library scanning as transformative, no market substitution), Swatch v. Bloomberg (use for reporting/commentary weighed heavily), Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive (careful market and use analysis), and the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision emphasizing the reasonable‑observer, transformation‑focused test. The Second Circuit here echoes those themes.

Two doctrinal points are worth underscoring. First, the court repeats that the transformativeness inquiry is objective. Whether Santos or Kimmel subjectively intended a particular purpose does not control; a reasonable observer’s perception of the works’ communicative content does. That makes sense doctrinally: fair use is about whether the secondary work performs a new purpose in the cultural economy, a question ill‑suited to probing messy, self‑serving intent allegations.

Second, the court treats bad faith or deception as relevant only insofar as it bears on the statutory factors—especially market effect—not as an independent trump. The opinion acknowledges Harper & Row’s language that fair use “presupposes good faith,” but it follows more recent authority in declining to give that dictum independent weight. Instead, deceptive procurement will defeat a fair‑use defense only when it helps prove that the secondary use is non‑transformative or that it usurps a licensing market. That is precisely what the Second Circuit held here: Kimmel’s deception did not transform the segment into a competing substitute for Santos’s originals, and the complaint itself undercut any claim that the broadcasts were an effort to replace Santos’s market.

## Limits the opinion preserves

Santos is not a blanket green light for deception. The court’s approach makes two limits plain. First, deception remains relevant when it bears directly on the statutory factors—especially market substitution. If a secondary user obtains a work by deceit and then tries to exploit it as a commercial substitute (for example, by repackaging and selling the content in place of the original), courts will view the market‑effect factor differently. Second, deceptive procurement may give rise to non‑copyright claims—fraud, torts, or contract remedies—or may be relevant to equitable relief or damages—even if it does not, by itself, defeat a fair‑use defense. In Santos the state claims failed on independent pleading grounds; that is not to say a stronger fraud claim could never coexist with a viable copyright action.

Put differently: deception is not per se fatal to fair use, but it is not irrelevant either. Its legal importance turns on how deception affects transformation, substitution, and the plaintiff’s other legal remedies.

## Practical and doctrinal consequences

For litigants, Santos reinforces two practical lessons. Plaintiffs alleging infringement should plead facts showing plausible market harm or that the secondary use serves as a market substitute; naked allegations of bad faith will not fill that gap. Defendants should know that an objectively obvious critical or parodic repurposing can be resolved early on the pleadings if the record (including registrations) supports non‑substitutive transformation.

For platforms and content creators, the case raises predictable policy responses. Platforms like Cameo will likely revisit terms of service, consent mechanisms, and disclosures so that creators know how their content might be reused and to limit ambiguous third‑party claims. Creators who want to preserve licensing leverage should think about affirmative license terms, exclusivity, and express contractual language about reuse and third‑party beneficiaries.

Doctrinally, Santos continues the narrowing of Harper & Row’s moralistic contours and cements the market‑substitution/transformative axis as the central organizing principle for fair use in the Second Circuit. That makes fair‑use doctrine more predictable in many commercial and journalistic contexts, but also raises normative tensions: if courts stop treating bad faith as a weighty, standalone factor, will parties have incentives to engage in questionable tactics to procure content for satire and criticism? The court implicitly answers that worry by pointing to other bodies of law, but that creates a distributional effect: copyright law will generally not protect creators against reputational or purely moral harms that do not translate into market substitution.

## Bottom line

Santos v. Kimmel is an incremental but telling reaffirmation of the direction in which modern fair‑use law has been heading—away from moralizing about the defendant’s character and toward an objective, market‑oriented assessment of transformation and substitution as perceived by a reasonable observer. Deception can matter, but only when it changes the statutory calculus—by converting an ostensibly critical use into a market substitute or by giving rise to independent legal relief. For satirists and broadcasters the ruling is reassuring; for content creators who do not have clear licensing markets, it is a reminder that copyright will not always vindicate every wrong that feels wrongful. Expect the lawyers and platforms on both sides to adjust accordingly, and expect further litigation where deception and commercial exploitation sit closer together on the facts.
