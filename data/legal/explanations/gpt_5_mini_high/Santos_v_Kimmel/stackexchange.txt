Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a court decide fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, and what materials can it consider?
Question:
The panel affirms dismissal at the pleading stage based on fair use, citing TCA Television and noting it need only consider "the original version of the videos and the JKL segments," plus it takes judicial notice of copyright registrations. At Rule 12(b)(6), I thought the court is limited to the complaint, its exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference (DiFolco). Are courts allowed to watch and compare videos that aren’t attached to the complaint if they are central to the claims? Does the "incorporation by reference" or "integral to the complaint" doctrine typically cover both the original and the allegedly infringing works in copyright cases? And are there limits (e.g., plaintiff disputes authenticity/completeness) that would push this beyond 12(b)(6) into summary judgment territory?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — a court can decide fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, but only when the pleading and materials properly before the court make fair use “so clearly established” that dismissal is appropriate. Santos v. Kimmel (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) is a good illustration of that rule and of what materials a court may consider.

What the Santos opinion says about the standard and materials
- Rule 12(b)(6) review in the Second Circuit is confined to “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” Santos (quoting DiFolco).  
- The court may also take judicial notice of copyright registrations in the Copyright Office’s registry. Santos (citing Island Software).  
- Fair use can be decided at the pleading stage where the complaint (and those materials) make it “so clearly established” that dismissal is proper. Santos (citing TCA Television).  
- Concretely, the panel said it “need[ed] only consider ‘the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue’ to decide ‘the question of fair use.’” Santos (quoting Cariou).

How to read that practically
- If both the original works and the alleged infringing works are before the court (because they are attached as exhibits, are incorporated by reference, or are otherwise integral to and relied upon by the complaint), the court may watch/compare them and resolve fair use on a motion to dismiss. That is what the Second Circuit did in Santos: the complaint + works/registrations permitted a fair-use determination at the pleading stage.  
- Judicial notice of registrations can supply documentary facts (e.g., publication dates) that the court used in Santos; it is not a substitute for the works themselves but is a permissible adjunct.

Limits you asked about
- The crucial limitation is whether the court actually has an undisputed, properly before-it record of the relevant works and attendant facts. If the plaintiff pleads that the version provided is not authentic or is incomplete, or if material factual disputes about how the works were used (edits, context, audience, extent of copying, etc.) exist, then fair use will typically not be “so clearly established by the complaint,” and dismissal at 12(b)(6) would be inappropriate. Santos itself rests on the availability of the works/registrations and the complaint’s own allegations; it does not resolve situations in which authenticity or material facts are contested.

Practical takeaways
- Defendants seeking a 12(b)(6) fair-use dismissal should put the competing works and any registrations into the record and show that the complaint’s own allegations make fair use clear.  
- Plaintiffs who want to avoid early dismissal should (a) attach or incorporate the relevant works or allege why versions before the court are incomplete/inauthentic, and (b) plead facts that make the degree of copying, transformation, and market effect plausibly contested.

Bottom line: Santos confirms that fair use can be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion in the Second Circuit, but only when the complaint plus documents properly considered (exhibits, incorporated materials, and judicially-noticed registrations) present the works and facts sufficiently plainly that fair use is “so clearly established” that dismissal is warranted.

### How does the "reasonable observer" test for transformative purpose fit with the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision?
Question:
The panel says the transformative analysis turns on what a reasonable observer would think, not the subjective intent of either party, and cites Warhol (2023) for the proposition that subjective intent doesn’t control. But Warhol also emphasized analyzing the specific use’s purpose in relation to the relevant licensing market rather than relying solely on new meaning or message. Here, the court frames Kimmel’s use as commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. How does that square with Warhol’s market-focused approach to factor one? In practice, after Warhol, should courts also assess whether the defendant’s use serves the same or different licensing markets even when the message is clearly critical/commentary? What kinds of allegations or evidence would matter at 12(b)(6)?
Answer:
Short answer
They’re complementary, not contradictory. Santos applied Warhol’s core point — that transformativeness under factor one is an objective, reasonable‑observer inquiry, not a probe into subjective intent — and then (as the statute and Warhol permit) separately assessed market impact under factor four. So after Warhol courts still ask both: (1) what a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use to be (transformativeness), and (2) whether the defendant’s use usurps a licensable market for the original. When the message is plainly critical, factor one often favors fair use; but factor four can still defeat fair use if the plaintiff plausibly shows the defendant’s use occupies the same licensing market as the original.

Why that is the right read (grounded in Santos)
- Warhol’s “no‑subjective‑intent” rule was the only Warhol proposition the Santos panel invoked for factor one: “whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. at __ (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (quoting Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023)). The panel used that objective test to conclude a reasonable observer would perceive Kimmel’s segments as commentary about Santos, not as a re‑use of Santos’s original purpose.
- Santos then addressed the market effect separately under factor four, using the familiar “usurpation” formulation: courts “ask not whether the second work would damage the market … but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Santos, slip op. at __ (quoting Hachette/Campbell). The panel found no plausible market usurpation because creators of imaginative works “are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons,” so that kind of commentary is ordinarily outside the relevant licensing market.

Putting the two together
- Factor 1 (purpose/character) = objective, reasonable‑observer assessment of whether the secondary work adds a new meaning/purpose or merely supersedes the original. Per Santos/Warhol, courts look to what a reasonable observer would think, not the parties’ unspoken motives.
- Factor 4 (market effect) = a distinct inquiry into whether the secondary use functions as a market substitute in a licensable market for the original. Even a work that communicates new meaning (commentary/criticism) can lose on factor four if it plausibly displaces a market that the copyright owner would normally exploit or license.
- Santos illustrates both steps: the court found the reasonable‑observer test established transformation (commentary) and then concluded on the pleadings that there was no plausible allegation of usurpation of any licensable market.

Practical rule after Warhol (and how courts will apply it)
- Yes — courts should and will assess whether the defendant’s use serves the same or different licensing markets even when the message is clearly critical. But the usual outcome is that critical commentary is not the sort of use the original author would license, so factor four often favors fair use. The critical question is factual: is there (or plausibly was) a licensing market for the copyrighted material in the particular manner the defendant used it?
- If the plaintiff can plausibly allege that the defendant’s use occupies a licensable market the plaintiff exploits (or could reasonably exploit), factor four can outweigh a finding of transformation.

What helps (and hurts) at the 12(b)(6) stage
Santos is instructive about pleading. Fair use can sometimes be decided on the face of the complaint and attached exhibits, but dismissal is appropriate only when the complaint and documents plainly establish fair use. To survive a motion to dismiss, a plaintiff should plead non‑conclusory, factual allegations that make either (A) the reasonable‑observer outcome or (B) market usurpation plausible:

Examples of allegations/evidence that would matter at 12(b)(6)
- Evidence of an actual or established licensing practice: prior license agreements, invoices, communications showing the plaintiff licensed the same or similar works for broadcast/compilation use.
- Allegations that the defendant used the work in the same functional role and distribution channels as licensed uses (e.g., the exact clips rebroadcast in monetized programming or sold/licensed to third parties).
- Allegations that the defendant sought to acquire a license, was refused, and then used the material anyway (shows a plausible intent to supplant the licensing market).
- Allegations that the plaintiff actively monetized the works (subscriptions, sublicensing, exclusive deals) and lost or would have lost licensing revenue because of the defendant’s use.
- Attachment of objective materials (the defendant’s segments, the original works, registration materials, any license offers) that support a non‑transformative reading or market‑substitution claim.

What won’t help (or will hurt) at pleading stage
- Conclusory assertions that defendants acted in bad faith or intended to “supplant” without factual support. Santos rejected Santos’s bad‑faith argument where the complaint’s own allegations (and exhibits) portrayed Kimmel’s motive as sarcastic commentary rather than market supplanting.
- Merely alleging that the defendant “instigated” creation or that the defendant had commercial motives, without facts tying that conduct to a plausible licensing market displacement.

Bottom line for litigants and students
- Read Warhol and Santos as prescribing two connected but distinct inquiries: an objective reasonable‑observer test for transformation (factor 1) and a market‑usurpation inquiry (factor 4). Courts should do both; a clear critical message often carries the day on factor 1, but if the plaintiff plausibly pleads that the defendant’s use substitutes for a licensable market the plaintiff actually exploits, factor 4 can defeat fair use — and to survive a 12(b)(6) the complaint must plead those market facts, not just labels or conclusory assertions.

### Does deception in acquiring a work (fake names on Cameo) defeat a fair use defense?
Question:
Santos argued Kimmel’s false identities on Cameo show bad faith that should negate fair use, pointing to Harper & Row’s statement that fair use presupposes good faith. The panel responds that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from that statement and emphasizes the critical/commentary purpose instead. What is the current role of "good faith" in fair use after Harper & Row and Campbell? Can deceptive acquisition weigh against fair use under factor one, or is it largely irrelevant unless it ties to market substitution or another factor? Are there cases where a defendant lied to obtain a copy but still prevailed (or lost) on fair use that help clarify how courts treat bad faith in the analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
No — deception in obtaining a work is not a per se bar to fair use. After Harper & Row the courts (and the Supreme Court) have moved away from treating “good faith” as a standalone, dispositive requirement. Deceptive acquisition can be evidence against fair use, but only to the extent it bears on the Section 107 factors (most commonly factor 1 — purpose/character — and factor 4 — market effect). Santos v. Kimmel is the recent, on-point example: the court held Kimmel’s use was transformative and not market‑supplanting, and therefore the alleged use of false identities did not defeat fair use.

Why (doctrinally) — what Santos says
- Harper & Row contained the oft‑quoted language that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” but Santos explains that the Supreme Court has “since distanced itself from this statement.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. at § I. In short, “good faith” is not a freestanding element that automatically defeats fair use.
- Instead courts continue to analyze the statutory factors, with heavy emphasis on transformation under factor 1 and on market substitution under factor 4. Santos reiterates that factor 1 “focuses chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative,” quoting Andy Warhol, and that factor 4 asks whether the secondary use “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute,” quoting Campbell as Santos does. Santos v. Kimmel, § I (collecting authorities).
- Santos applied those principles: even accepting the complaint’s allegation that Kimmel used fictitious names to obtain Cameo videos, the complaint itself showed the JKL segments were sarcastic criticism (a transformative purpose) and did not plead market usurpation; therefore the deception did not negate fair use.

How deception can (and cannot) matter in practice
- It can matter, but only as evidence relevant to the statutory factors:
  - Factor 1 (purpose/character): deception can support an inference the secondary user’s purpose was to appropriate the original for the original’s purpose (non‑transformative) rather than to add new expression, but that inference is assessed by the objective reasonable‑observer test (Santos cites Andy Warhol on objective assessment), not by a purely subjective label of “bad faith.”
  - Factor 4 (market effect): deception can be probative of market harm if the way the defendant obtained the work shows intent to create a competing product or to exploit a licensing market (Santos cites Campbell on the licensing‑market inquiry).
  - Factors 2 and 3: deception is generally less directly probative, though contextual facts can matter.
- It is not dispositive on its own. As Santos shows, courts will not automatically allow a plaintiff to defeat fair use simply by pleading that the defendant lied to get a copy. The key question remains whether the secondary use is transformative and whether it usurps a potential market for the original.

Illustrative cases (from the Santos opinion)
- Santos v. Kimmel (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025): Defendant used fake Cameo identities; court held fair use at pleading stage because the complaint itself showed a transformative, critical purpose and no market usurpation — so the deception did not nullify fair use.
- Harper & Row v. Nation (quoted in Santos): the Supreme Court once said “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” language plaintiffs still invoke; Santos notes the Court has since distanced itself from treating that statement as a categorical rule.
- Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose (quoted in Santos): emphasizes that market substitution/licensing concerns — not a blanket good‑faith rule — are central to factor 4 analysis (Campbell language quoted in Santos about licensing markets for parodies).

Practical takeaway for pleading/defending
- If you represent a plaintiff: pleading that a defendant lied is useful, but you must connect that deceit to one of the Section 107 factors — most effectively by alleging (with factual detail) that the deceit shows a non‑transformative purpose or resulted in direct market usurpation/harms.
- If you represent a defendant: be prepared to show transformation (what a reasonable observer would see) and explain why the secondary use does not act as a market substitute; deception allegations will be treated as one piece of contextual evidence, not an automatic knockout.

Bottom line
Santos confirms the modern approach: bad faith/deception can be relevant evidence, but it is not a freestanding trump to fair use. Courts will fold such allegations into the Section 107 factor analysis — especially factors 1 and 4 — and will sustain fair use where the use is objectively transformative and not market‑supplanting even if the defendant obtained the material deceptively.

### Why did the fraudulent inducement claim fail for lack of "out-of-pocket" loss under New York law?
Question:
The panel (citing Lama Holding and Connaughton) says Santos did not allege any out-of-pocket loss, so his fraudulent inducement claim fails. I’m struggling with what counts here: Santos spent time recording the videos, claims reputational harm, and says the broadcast deprived him of licensing value. Why aren’t those cognizable damages? Is the problem that time spent and reputational/expectancy harms are "benefit-of-the-bargain" or consequential rather than out-of-pocket? Could production costs, opportunity costs, or foregone alternative Cameo bookings ever qualify as out-of-pocket losses for a fraud claim in this context? What would a sufficient damages pleading look like under NY law?
Answer:
Short answer
Because New York law requires a fraud plaintiff to plead an actual pecuniary (an “out‑of‑pocket”) loss and Santos did not allege any concrete monetary loss tied to the defendants’ misrepresentations. The panel relied on Lama Holding and Connaughton and concluded Santos “failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.” Santos’s allegations about time spent, reputational injury, and abstract diminution of “licensing value” were not pleaded as specific, actual money losses and so did not satisfy the New York out‑of‑pocket requirement.

Relevant doctrine and standard (as the opinion states)
- The Second Circuit summarised the controlling point: dismissal was required because Santos “failed to allege any out‑of‑pocket loss as required under New York law” (citing Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney, 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996) and Connaughton v. Chipotle, 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017)). The court therefore affirmed dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim for lack of pleaded actual out‑of‑pocket loss.
- Put simply (and as the opinion applies Lama/Connaughton): New York fraud damages must be alleged as an actual pecuniary loss the plaintiff suffered as a result of the misrepresentation — not merely non‑monetary harms, not vague future “value” losses, and not unconcretized expectancy harms.

Why Santos’s pleaded harms failed
- Time spent recording videos: Time or effort alone is an intangible loss unless converted into a concrete monetary expense (e.g., unpaid, unreimbursed production costs that plaintiff actually incurred). The complaint as pleaded did not allege such specific, unreimbursed expenditures.
- Reputational harm: Reputation injury is an intangible (and often consequential) injury. To meet the out‑of‑pocket test it must be tied to a concrete economic loss (for example, a lost contract or a quantifiable lost booking) pleaded with specificity. General allegations of reputational harm or diminished marketability are not the same as alleging an actual dollar loss.
- “Deprived of licensing value”: A claim that the broadcast reduced the “licensing value” of the videos is an expectancy or market‑value allegation unless tied to identified, concrete lost licensing deals or offers (with amounts and dates). The court treated Santos’s allegations as not alleging an actual, accounted loss.

Could production costs, opportunity costs, or foregone Cameo bookings qualify?
- Yes — but only if pled as concrete pecuniary losses causally linked to the alleged fraud. Examples that would plausibly qualify:
  - Production costs: allege specific unreimbursed expenditures (rent, crew, materials) incurred to produce the requested videos and state how much was spent.
  - Foregone/alternative Cameo bookings: allege that Santos turned down confirmed paying bookings (identify the bookings, dates, and dollar amounts) because he accepted the requests from the defendants and therefore lost that revenue.
  - Opportunity costs in the form of lost, realizable revenue: allege specific, imminent deals or offers that were canceled or could not be performed because of reliance on the defendants’ misrepresentations and quantify the losses.
- The critical requirements (as the panel’s citation to Lama/Connaughton reflects) are concreteness (actual money lost), causation (the loss resulted from the misrepresentation), and non‑speculativeness. Broad assertions that a broadcast “devalued” future licensing revenue are likely too speculative unless tied to concrete deals/offers or historical fee evidence.

What a sufficient damages pleading would (in practice) need to allege under New York law
- Identify the misrepresentation(s) and reliance (who said what when and why plaintiff relied).
- Tie each alleged misrepresentation to a specific monetary loss — e.g.:
  - “On [date], Defendant X (using alias Y) requested a video and I accepted. As a result I cancelled/declined a confirmed Cameo booking from [name or anonymous user ID], scheduled for [date], that would have paid $2,500; that fee was lost because I performed for defendants instead.”
  - “On [date] I incurred $1,200 in unreimbursed production costs for the video (camera rental $500; makeup/crew $700), which defendants’ misrepresentation caused me to expend and which I have not recovered.”
- State amounts, dates, and a causal connection: allege the dollar figures, why those losses were incurred, and why they flowed from the defendants’ deceit rather than some other cause.
- Allege that the damages are not speculative (e.g., show prior bookings/pricing to establish market value or identify concrete offers that were lost).

Bottom line
The panel did not say these categories of loss (production costs or foregone bookings) are never recoverable; it said Santos’s complaint failed because he did not allege any actual out‑of‑pocket monetary loss. If Santos had alleged specific, quantifiable expenditures or lost revenues causally linked to Kimmel’s misrepresentations, those allegations would have been the kind of out‑of‑pocket losses New York law requires.

### How did the court evaluate market harm when there might be a market to license these exact Cameo videos to television shows?
Question:
On factor four, the panel quotes Hachette and Campbell to say the question is usurpation of the market (offering a competing substitute) rather than devaluation by criticism, and notes creators typically won’t license lampoons. But here, Kimmel aired the videos themselves on national TV—couldn’t there be a market to license Santos’s Cameo videos (or similar personalized videos) to broadcasters, apart from a market for critical commentary? How do courts distinguish usurpation from mere devaluation when the secondary user shows the complete work to a mass audience? Does the fact that Santos likely wouldn’t have licensed the videos for mocking use end the inquiry, or does a broader market for non-critical broadcast uses matter under factor four?
Answer:
Short answer
The Second Circuit treated factor four as an “usurpation” inquiry — not a question whether Kimmel’s broadcasts merely devalued Santos’s videos — and held that broadcasting the Cameo clips on JKL did not usurp any cognizable market that Santos had plausibly alleged. The court relied on the rule that fair‑use liability turns on whether the secondary use is a competing substitute in a licensing market the copyright owner actually (or would reasonably) exploit; creators of imaginative, personalized works ordinarily do not license lampoons, so showing the whole clip on TV did not itself prove market harm. (Santos v. Kimmel, No. 24‑2196‑cv (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025)). 

Why — the doctrine the court applied
- Legal standard. The court quoted Hachette and Campbell: factor four asks “not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” The court therefore looks for a plausible market that the secondary use replaces. (Opinion quoting Hachette; citing Campbell). 

- Lampooning is generally not a cognizable licensing market. Quoting Campbell, the court stressed that “the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.” The panel used that principle to reject Santos’s market‑harm theory. 

How that applied to the facts here
- Function matters more than exposure. The court compared the original Cameo videos and the JKL segments (citing Cariou) and concluded a reasonable observer would see different functions: Santos’s videos conveyed “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while Kimmel’s use was sarcasm/criticism. Because the broadcast served a different function (transformative commentary), it was not a substitute for the original commercial market for personalized Cameos. 

- Showing the complete work doesn’t automatically equal usurpation. The opinion makes clear that broadcasting the full clip to a mass audience does not by itself establish that the secondary use is a competing substitute; the relevant question is whether the broadcast serves as a market replacement for the particular licensing markets the plaintiff actually exploits or would reasonably exploit. The complaint here did not plead a plausible licensing market in which Santos would have sold rights to Kimmel‑style mocking broadcasts.

Does Santos’s likely refusal to license mocking uses end the inquiry?
- For lampooning/commentary uses, yes — usually dispositive. The court treated the improbability that Santos would license his videos for mocking use as removing that particular licensing market from consideration (per Campbell). That was enough to dispose of Santos’s factor‑four claim as pleaded. 

- But a broader, non‑critical licensing market could matter only if plausibly alleged. The opinion confined the inquiry to the original videos and the JKL segments “at issue,” and relied on the face of the complaint. If a plaintiff pleaded facts showing a distinct, real market in which the copyright owner actually licenses complete videos to broadcasters for non‑critical uses, a court would have to consider whether the secondary use usurped that market. Santos did not plead any such market, so the court did not treat a hypothetical non‑critical broadcast market as relevant.

Bottom line
The court treated factor four as an inquiry about substitution into real licensing markets. Broadcasting the full Cameo videos on JKL did not, on the face of Santos’s complaint, substitute for any market Santos plausibly exploited (and courts generally exclude markets for lampoons). That is why factor four weighed for fair use here: the use was transformative commentary and not a competing substitute in a cognizable licensing market.

### Why didn’t the court resolve Copyright Act preemption of the contract claims, and when are contract/fraud claims preempted under § 301?
Question:
The district court dismissed the breach-of-contract and implied-contract claims as preempted, but the Second Circuit didn’t reach preemption and instead held the claims fail under state law. Doctrinally, when would claims like breach of a platform’s Terms of Service or implied-in-fact promises be preempted by § 301? What is the Second Circuit’s two-prong test (subject matter + equivalence), and how do "extra elements" (like a promise or misrepresentation) affect preemption for contract and fraud claims? If Santos had adequately alleged an implied promise restricting broadcast use, would that kind of undertaking normally avoid preemption as qualitatively different from a copyright claim?
Answer:
Short answer
The Second Circuit declined to decide Copyright Act preemption because it could (and did) dispose of Santos’s state-law claims on straightforward state‑law pleading grounds. Doctrinally, the court’s reference to Melendez signals the Second Circuit’s § 301 framework: a state claim is preempted only if (1) its subject matter falls within the subject matter of copyright law and (2) the state‑law right is equivalent to one of the exclusive rights of copyright (i.e., the state cause of action does no more than grant rights that § 106 already supplies). Claims that add “extra elements” (a promise, misrepresentation, reliance, out‑of‑pocket loss, etc.) can be “qualitatively different” and therefore avoid § 301 preemption — but whether they do is fact‑specific.

How that plays out in Santos v. Kimmel
- Why the court didn’t reach preemption: The panel said Santos argued his state claims were “qualitatively different” and therefore not preempted (citing Melendez), but “we need not resolve this issue . . . because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law.” In short, the court applied judicial restraint: it disposed of the case on state‑law pleading defects (third‑party beneficiary and implied‑contract pleading failures; and failure to plead out‑of‑pocket loss on fraud) instead of deciding the § 301 preemption question. (Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II.)

The Second Circuit’s § 301 (two‑prong) framework (as the opinion frames it)
- The opinion cites the Second Circuit line that treats preemption as a two‑part inquiry (the panel cites Melendez for the “qualitatively different” formulation). In practice the test requires asking:
  1. Subject‑matter: Does the state claim concern works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium (i.e., does it fall within the subject matter of the Copyright Act)?
  2. Equivalence: Is the state claim seeking to vindicate the same exclusive rights that the Copyright Act protects (reproduction, distribution, public performance/display, preparation of derivative works)? If yes, the claim is equivalent and preempted; if the state claim contains an “extra element” that makes it qualitatively different, it normally survives preemption.
- The Santos opinion invokes this “qualitatively different” concept (Santos’s brief relied on it via Melendez), and then declines to resolve whether Santos’s contract/fraud claims met that threshold because they failed on their own terms.

“Extra elements” and how they affect contract/fraud claims
- Contract claims: A state contract claim will avoid preemption when it rests on contractual obligations that are not merely a restatement of the Copyright Act’s exclusive rights — e.g., formation, mutual assent, consideration, specific contractual promises, third‑party beneficiary status. The opinion rejects Santos’s direct breach theory because he is not a signatory and he pleaded nothing approaching the “express declaration” needed (Illinois law) that the Cameo Terms were meant to benefit non‑parties; and his implied‑in‑fact theory failed because he did not plead the essential terms or a meeting of the minds under New York law. Those are the kinds of “extra” contract elements a court considers when deciding whether a claim is qualitatively different. (See Santos § II, discussing Illinois and New York law.)
- Fraud claims: Fraud adds elements (a false representation, scienter, reliance, damages). Under New York law, for example, fraudulent inducement requires an actual out‑of‑pocket loss; Santos’s complaint failed to allege any such loss and thus failed on state‑law grounds. A properly pleaded fraud claim that alleges these elements can be qualitatively different from a pure copyright claim and may survive § 301 preemption — but again that is fact‑dependent. (Santos § II, discussing Lama/Connaughton.)

Would an adequately pleaded implied promise not to broadcast normally avoid preemption?
- Possible, but not automatic. If Santos had plausibly alleged an implied contract that imposed an independent promise (an “extra element”) — e.g., a clear undertaking by a specific requester or platform that the cameo would not be broadcast, plus the essential terms and a meeting of the minds — that could make the claim “qualitatively different” from a copyright action and therefore avoid § 301 preemption (the Second Circuit’s “qualitatively different” language is the hook for that argument, as cited in the Santos opinion).
- The caveat the Santos panel signals (and other Second Circuit decisions have emphasized): the substance matters. If the alleged promise simply duplicates an exclusive § 106 right (for example, a promise that simply says “you may not publicly perform or distribute the work”), courts may treat the state promise as equivalent to copyright and preempt it. By contrast, a promise that gives rise to an independent contractual obligation (confidentiality, limitation on specified downstream uses, reliance‑based injury, etc.) is more likely to be treated as an “extra element” and survive preemption — but the Santos court did not decide this factual question because Santos’s pleading failed on state‑law grounds.

Practical takeaways for pleading to avoid § 301 preemption (rooted in the Santos opinion)
- If you rely on contract: plead that you are an intended third‑party beneficiary or that there was an enforceable implied‑in‑fact contract with specific, nonconclusory allegations of the essential terms and a meeting of the minds. Santos lost because he alleged neither.
- If you rely on fraud: plead the fraud elements required by the governing state law (misrepresentation, reliance, and the particular damages that state law requires — e.g., out‑of‑pocket loss in New York). Santos’s fraud claim failed for lack of such loss.
- If your state claim genuinely adds obligations or injuries beyond the exclusive bundle of copyright rights (not merely a different legal label for the same harm), emphasize those “extra elements” in the complaint — that is the route the Second Circuit recognizes (Melendez, cited in Santos) to escape § 301 preemption.

Bottom line: Santos lost not because the panel held § 301 would necessarily preempt claims like his, but because his contract and fraud counts failed on their own state‑law pleading requirements; whether a properly pleaded implied promise restricting broadcast would avoid preemption is a fact‑dependent question the panel expressly declined to decide.

### What would Santos need to plead to state an implied-in-fact contract under New York law in this scenario?
Question:
The panel says Santos didn’t allege the essential terms or a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service or otherwise limit use of the videos. In the context of a buyer requesting a personalized video and the creator supplying it, what facts would plausibly show an implied-in-fact agreement between those two parties (separate from the platform’s user agreement) restricting downstream broadcast or redistribution (e.g., express messages between the parties, standardized creator terms displayed at purchase, industry customs)? Are there New York cases recognizing implied limits on use in similar creative-services transactions that could guide how to plead the essential terms and mutual assent?
Answer:
Short answer
To plead an implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law in this setting, Santos must do more than allege in boilerplate that “an implied contract existed.” He must plead, in non‑conclusory detail, the essential terms of the bilateral agreement (the precise restriction on downstream use and related terms) and factual allegations showing a meeting of the minds between Santos and the purchaser (Kimmel)—i.e., how and when Kimmel manifested assent to those terms. The Second Circuit in Santos v. Kimmel relied on New York authorities requiring (1) allegation of the “essential terms” and (2) an “indication of a meeting of minds,” and rejected the complaint because it failed on both points. See opinion (citing Caniglia, Rocky Point, DG & A, Tjoa).

What the opinion requires (doctrine / standard)
- Essential terms: New York law requires pleading “in nonconclusory language . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., quoted in the opinion.  
- Meeting of the minds / surrounding circumstances: Courts look to “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to determine whether an implied‑in‑fact contract formed and the extent of its terms; there must be an “indication of a meeting of minds.” Rocky Point Props.; DG & A; Tjoa, quoted in the opinion.  
- The panel rejected Santos’s complaint because it “does not ‘allege, in nonconclusory language’ the essential terms” and “does not plausibly allege . . . a meeting of minds . . . that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service.” Santos v. Kimmel, Op.

Plausible facts that, if pleaded, would satisfy the opinion’s standards
(These are illustrative categories of factual allegations that would be necessary to show an independent private agreement between buyer and creator restricting broadcast/redistribution.)

1. Direct communications showing the restriction and assent
- Quote or attach the exact messages/emails/Cameo chats in which the buyer (Kimmel) requested the personalized video and expressly stated (or accepted) a restriction — e.g., “this is for private use only; please do not broadcast,” or “I will pay $X provided you do not permit public broadcast.”  
- Show Santos’s contemporaneous response accepting that condition (or otherwise performing in reliance on the condition). Dates, times, sender/recipient, and screenshots are critical. The opinion emphasizes that pleading must be tied to specific provisions and manifestations of assent.

2. A conditional payment or express price term tied to the restriction (consideration)
- Plead that the buyer paid (or promised to pay) a stated amount in exchange for a video provided on the condition that it not be broadcast; attach the payment receipt or transaction record. This supplies the bargained‑for exchange the implied contract rests on.

3. Creator‑level terms shown to and accepted by the purchaser (separate from platform ToS)
- If the creator (Santos) had standardized terms posted on his profile or shown at checkout (e.g., “personal use only; no redistribution”), plead that those terms were displayed to Kimmel and that Kimmel accepted them (screenshot of the checkout page, evidence of click‑through or explicit acknowledgement). That would support an agreement between the two parties distinct from the platform’s global Terms of Service.

4. Course of dealing / industry custom (as supporting surrounding circumstances)
- Plead concrete facts showing that personalized videos on the platform are commonly understood and sold for private use only (specific examples, other creators’ posted policies, prior transactions showing the same practice). Use such facts as context, not as a substitute for an express or reasonably clear implied term.

5. Reliance / inducement tied to the restriction
- Plead that Santos supplied the videos because he understood the buyer would limit use (and would not have supplied them, or would have charged more, absent that promise). This helps show the parties’ mutual intent and that Santos performed in reliance on the alleged promise.

What will likely be insufficient (per the opinion)
- Conclusory allegations that “an implied contract existed” or that defendants “must have assent” without factual detail. The panel rejected such conclusory pleading as deficient (Caniglia).  
- Relying solely on the platform’s Terms of Service when the plaintiff is not a signatory and has not pleaded that the contracting party manifested assent to apply those Terms to the plaintiff; the opinion notes Santos was not party to Cameo’s ToS and that nonparty enforcement requires something close to an express declaration (the Court relied on that problem when analyzing the ToS theory).

Practical pleading checklist (to keep the claim within the opinion’s requirements)
- Identify the specific communications or conduct that created the agreement (attach screenshots, messages, receipts).  
- State the essential terms with specificity: scope of the restriction (no broadcast/redistribution), duration, any territorial limits, whether the license was exclusive/nonexclusive, and the price/consideration.  
- Plead how the buyer manifested assent (e.g., “on X date, Kimmel sent: ‘…’; Santos replied: ‘I agree not to allow broadcast’; Kimmel paid $Y on X date (receipt attached)”).  
- Describe surrounding circumstances that corroborate intent (payment, industry practice, seller profile language, course of dealing).  
- If relying on the platform or a third‑party contract, plead with particularity why a nonparty should be entitled to enforce (but note the opinion’s skepticism where the plaintiff was not a signatory).

On New York precedents specific to creative‑services use limits
- The panel did not cite New York decisions applying implied limits to similar creative‑services transactions; instead it applied the general New York pleading rules for implied‑in‑fact contracts (Rocky Point, Caniglia, DG & A, Tjoa). Thus the safest course—consistent with the opinion—is to frame the allegation under those general pleading rules and support it with the specific communications, terms, and surrounding facts described above.

Bottom line
Under Santos v. Kimmel, an implied‑in‑fact contract claim will survive a 12(b)(6) only if the complaint supplies non‑conclusory factual allegations identifying the specific term(s) restricting downstream use and concrete facts showing that Kimmel manifested assent to those term(s) in exchange for consideration. Allegations of vague expectations, reliance on the platform’s ToS without showing a direct bilateral agreement, or general statements about industry practice will not suffice without the kind of fact‑specific pleading the opinion requires.

### Under Illinois law, what contract language is needed to make a non-user creator like Santos a third-party beneficiary of Cameo’s Terms of Service?
Question:
The panel applies Illinois law (per the ToS) and emphasizes the strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries absent language “practically an express declaration.” If the ToS contains provisions that appear designed to protect creators (e.g., restrictions on impersonation or redistribution), but don’t explicitly confer enforcement rights on creators, is that ever enough under Illinois law? How explicit does the ToS need to be to let creators sue users as intended beneficiaries, and do courts draw a negative inference when some clauses expressly name third-party beneficiaries but the clauses at issue do not? Any Illinois cases where a platform’s non-signatory service provider was deemed an intended beneficiary of a user agreement?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Illinois law as applied in Santos v. Kimmel, a platform’s Terms of Service must contain language that is essentially an “express declaration” that the non‑signatory (here, creators) are intended third‑party beneficiaries and have the right to enforce the contract. Mere protective provisions (no‑impersonation, no‑redistribution, etc.) that do not explicitly name creators as beneficiaries or grant them enforcement rights will not suffice. The Second Circuit pointedly relied on the absence of such an express declaration and on the fact that other ToS provisions did use explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language — a negative inference that undermined Santos’s claim.

Relevant doctrine from the opinion
- Strong presumption against third‑party beneficiaries: the court quotes Illinois authority that “there is a ‘strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.’” Santos v. Kimmel, No. 24‑2196‑cv, slip op. § II (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (quoting Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur).
- How strong that implication must be: “[t]he implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quoting Barry).
- Negative inference from express language elsewhere: the court relied on the ToS’s use of explicit third‑party beneficiary language in other provisions to show that “when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Id.

What that means in practice
- What is required: To satisfy Illinois law (per Santos), the ToS should do more than show that certain conduct is prohibited for the benefit of creators. It should plainly state that creators (either by name or a readily identifiable class) are intended third‑party beneficiaries and that they have the right to enforce the relevant provisions. That is what the court meant by an implication “practically an express declaration.”
- What is not enough: Broad protective wording alone (e.g., “Users may not impersonate third parties” or “You may not redistribute content”) that does not identify creators as beneficiaries or grant them enforcement rights will not overcome the presumption against third‑party enforcement. Santos failed because he “identifie[d] nothing close to an ‘express declaration’” that the provisions applied to him. Id.
- Negative inference: Yes — if the agreement uses explicit third‑party beneficiary language in some clauses but omits it in the clauses a non‑signatory seeks to enforce, courts will treat that omission as evidence the parties did not intend third‑party enforcement for the omitted clauses. The Second Circuit relied on that point in rejecting Santos’s claim.

Practical drafting guidance (illustrative only, grounded in Santos)
To create enforceable third‑party rights under the standard the court applied, a platform should include clear language such as:
- “The parties expressly intend that Creators who submit content to the Platform are third‑party beneficiaries of Sections X–Y of this Agreement and may enforce those provisions directly against Users and against the Platform.”
- Or: “This Agreement is for the benefit of [Platform], its Users, and Creators (defined as persons who submit content). Creators shall have the independent right to enforce any provision that protects Creators’ interests.”

(These examples illustrate the kind of “practically an express declaration” the Santos court required; the court rejected claims where the ToS lacked anything approaching that expressness.)

On Illinois case law about platforms / non‑signatory service providers
Santos cites the Illinois standard (Barry) and applies it to the Cameo ToS, but the Second Circuit opinion does not identify an Illinois decision that treats a platform’s non‑signatory service provider (or similar third party) as an intended beneficiary of a user agreement. The upshot of Santos is that, under Illinois law as applied there, such a result is unlikely without the explicit, express beneficiary language discussed above.

Bottom line
If you want non‑signatory creators to be able to sue users under a platform’s ToS under the Illinois standard quoted in Santos, put an express, unambiguous third‑party‑beneficiary clause in the agreement that (1) identifies the class or named beneficiaries and (2) expressly grants them enforcement rights. Absent that sort of “practically an express declaration,” courts will deny third‑party enforcement and may draw a negative inference where the ToS uses express beneficiary language elsewhere but not in the clauses at issue.
