Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # Applying the Fair Use Factors to Santos’s Videos and JKL

The core dispute the Second Circuit resolved was whether Kimmel’s broadcast of Santos’s Cameo videos on Jimmy Kimmel Live! (JKL) was barred by the fair use doctrine. The court applied the four statutory factors from 17 U.S.C. § 107 and concluded that, as a matter of law on the pleadings, fair use barred Santos’s copyright claims. The following sections walk through the court’s factor-by-factor reasoning and the procedural basis for deciding fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage.

## Factor 1 (purpose and character): JKL’s new purpose—commenting on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—was different from the videos’ original uplifting messages

The opinion treats the first factor—the “purpose and character of the use”—as decisive. Under binding Second Circuit and Supreme Court precedent, the inquiry focuses “chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative,” that is, whether the secondary use “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character,” rather than merely superseding the original. The District Court found, and the Second Circuit accepted, that Kimmel’s segments used the Cameo videos to comment on and mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money; Santos’s complaint itself described the original videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Those two purposes are different in kind. The court therefore concluded that a reasonable observer would see Kimmel’s broadcasts as repurposing Santos’s videos for satirical criticism rather than merely republishing them for the same uplifting ends. Because that difference in purpose is pronounced, the first factor “strongly supports” fair use.

This application illustrates two practical points for reading fair-use holdings. First, courts look at how the secondary use frames and employs the original material—whether it creates new meaning or message—not merely at whether the secondary user copied. Second, the court did not require a small change or partial use to call something transformative; repackaging the videos as evidence in a critical, satirical segment was enough to change the use’s character.

## Rejecting the ‘instigator’ argument: even if Kimmel prompted the content, the inquiry is objective; subjective purposes of either party are not controlling

Santos argued that Kimmel should not be able to claim a transformative purpose because Kimmel “instigated” or solicited the original Cameo videos and therefore was the “designer” of their original purpose. The Second Circuit rejected that line of attack. The court relied on the objective standard for determining purpose: what a reasonable observer would perceive, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the later user. Thus, even if Kimmel prompted Santos to create the videos, that fact does not erase the change in communicative purpose when JKL aired the videos as part of a mocking commentary. In short, alleged instigation does not automatically convert later critical reuse into non-transformative copying; the transformation analysis looks to the use’s observable meaning and function.

## Bad faith contention: pseudonymous requests did not show an intent to supplant Santos’s market; the use was for sarcastic criticism/commentary

Santos further argued that Kimmel’s use was in bad faith because he submitted requests under fictitious names, implying deception. Santos relied on a statement in Harper & Row that fair use presupposes good faith. The court declined to treat asserted deception as dispositive. It observed that the Supreme Court has moved away from treating good faith as a categorical prerequisite and emphasized that the complaint itself did not plausibly allege an intent to supplant Santos’s commercial market for the videos. Instead, the complaint portrayed defendants’ motive as sarcastic criticism—classic fair-use conduct. Even where a defendant’s behavior might look misleading, the key fairness inquiry remains whether the secondary use is aimed at criticism/commentary and whether it would supplant the plaintiff’s market; mere subterfuge in obtaining a work does not automatically defeat a fair-use claim where the other factors favor transformation.

## Factor 2 (nature of the work): the videos were published the day they were created; publication status weakens any claim to heightened protection for unpublished works

The second factor asks whether the copyrighted material is closer to the core of copyright protection—creative, unpublished works receive greater weight. Here the court relied on Santos’s copyright registrations, which (as the District Court judicially noticed) established that the videos were published the same day they were created. Publication under the statute reduces the weight of this factor in favor of the copyright owner because unpublished status traditionally commands stronger protection. The court treated the timing of publication as a straightforward, documentary fact that cut against giving the second factor substantial force for Santos.

## Factor 3 (amount and substantiality): commentary and criticism may require extensive copying; the use was tied to the critical purpose

The third factor considers both the quantitative and qualitative scope of what was copied. The opinion recognizes the basic principle that copying the whole work can count against fair use, but it also reiterates the important limitation from precedent: when criticism or commentary is the purpose, reproducing substantial portions—or even the whole—may be necessary to make the critical point. In Santos’s case, the JKL segments used the videos for the very purpose of showing what Santos said on camera; to critique whether he would “say it,” Kimmel needed to show the actual statements. Because the allegedly infringing use was functionally tied to that critical purpose, the court treated the third factor as not dispositively favoring Santos. The opinion therefore accepted that extensive copying in service of commentary can be reasonable under § 107.

## Factor 4 (market effect): no usurpation of Santos’s market; criticism/lampooning is not a cognizable licensing market to be protected; the segments were not substitutes for Cameo videos

The fourth factor—effect on the potential market—was analyzed under the familiar substitution test: the inquiry is whether the secondary work usurps the market for the original by serving as a market substitute, not whether it merely harms the original’s goodwill or devalues it through criticism. The court concluded on the pleadings that Kimmel’s broadcasts did not and could not plausibly be a substitute for Santos’s paid Cameo videos: a late-night comedy segment mocking a public figure is not a competing market for personalized Cameo greetings. The court also relied on the established proposition that creators of imaginative works are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions, so the existence of a cognizable licensing market for this kind of use is doubtful. Because Santos did not allege a plausible market usurpation, the fourth factor favored fair use.

## Bottom line: comparing the videos and the JKL segments sufficed to find fair use on the pleadings

Procedurally important, the Second Circuit stressed that fair use can be resolved at the motion-to-dismiss stage when the complaint and the works themselves make the factors clear. The court relied on the complaint, the videos and segments attached or incorporated into the record, and Santos’s copyright registrations (which the court may judicially notice) to perform the statutory balancing. Because those materials plainly showed a transformative critical use, publication of the originals, copying needed for criticism, and no plausible market usurpation, the court concluded that fair use barred Santos’s copyright claims as a matter of law. For a student reading the opinion, the lesson is that where the record itself reveals an objective change in purpose and no plausible market substitution, a court may find fair use without reaching disputed factual development.
