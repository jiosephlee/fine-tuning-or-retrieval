Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Background, Platform Mechanics, and Procedural Posture

## Parties and roles
This case pits former Congressman George Santos, who filed suit as the owner of copyrights in a set of short, personalized videos, against Jimmy Kimmel (the late‑night host who obtained and aired those videos) and two corporate defendants: American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (ABC), which broadcasts Jimmy Kimmel Live!, and The Walt Disney Company (ABC’s parent). Santos is the plaintiff and claims to hold the copyrights in the videos at issue; Kimmel is the individual who, according to the complaint, submitted requests through the Cameo platform and then incorporated the resulting recordings into televised segments; ABC and Disney are sued for producing and distributing those televised segments.

## How Cameo works and Kimmel’s pseudonymous requests for personalized paid videos
Cameo is an online marketplace that connects payors and on‑camera talent: a user requests and pays for a short, personalized recorded message and the creator (here, Santos) produces and delivers that video through the platform. In Santos’s complaint Kimmel—using various fictitious names—submitted paid requests through Cameo, Santos accepted those requests and recorded the personalized messages, and the messages were delivered through the platform. Cameo’s Terms of Service governed the relationships among users, creators, and the platform; Santos’s complaint asserted a direct breach of those terms (and related contract and fraud claims) based on Kimmel’s pseudonymous requests and the later broadcast of the recordings. A key factual posture for the courts was that the videos were created and delivered through that platform before they were used on television.

## JKL’s “Will Santos Say It?” segments using the resulting videos for mockery/commentary
Kimmel aired the videos on Jimmy Kimmel Live! as part of a recurring, mocking segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The segments framed and repurposed the personalized messages as material for satire and criticism—commenting on Santos’s willingness to say odd or absurd things for money. That recontextualization (from a short paid greeting to a televised comedic critique) is central to the parties’ dispute: Santos contends the television use infringed his copyrights; defendants and the courts treated the on‑air use as critical commentary and asked whether that use qualified as fair use under the Copyright Act.

## Santos’s registrations in the videos and their publication timing
Santos registered copyrights in the videos at issue, and those registrations appear in the Copyright Office’s public records. The registry entries (which the courts treated as judicially noticeable) indicate the videos were “published” the same day they were created. The existence and dates of those registrations were part of the materials the courts relied on at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage because they are public records that help establish timing and public availability—facts relevant to assessing the copyright claim and, in the district court’s view, to analyzing fair use.

## Claims asserted
Santos brought a federal copyright‑infringement claim (asserting unauthorized exploitation of his copyrighted videos) and three state‑law claims: breach of contract (based on the Cameo Terms of Service), breach of implied‑in‑fact contract, and fraudulent inducement. The contract claim rested on the theory that the Cameo agreement’s terms were violated by Kimmel’s conduct (despite Santos not being a signatory); the implied‑contract claim alleged an informal bargain or understanding with the defendants; and the fraud claim alleged that Kimmel’s use of false names and representations induced Santos to create the videos.

## District Court ruling: Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal based on fair use and failure of state‑law claims (with preemption noted below)
The Southern District of New York (Judge Cote) dismissed Santos’s complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim. The district court concluded that the Defendants’ use of the videos on JKL was protected by the fair use defense, and it dismissed Santos’s state‑law claims either as preempted by the Copyright Act or as otherwise failing to state a claim. That dismissal was a disposition on the pleadings—no discovery had occurred—and it turned largely on comparing the original videos, the alleged infringing segments, and the documentary record (including the Cameo Terms of Service and the Copyright Office registrations).

## Appellate review: de novo review of 12(b)(6) dismissal; materials courts may consider on a motion to dismiss
On appeal the Second Circuit reviewed the 12(b)(6) dismissal de novo: it asked whether, accepting the complaint’s well‑pleaded allegations and considering any documents attached to or incorporated into the complaint, the plaintiff had stated plausible claims. The panel followed settled Second Circuit practice (DiFolco v. MSNBC) that a court deciding a Rule 12(b)(6) motion may consider the complaint itself, exhibits attached to the complaint, and documents the complaint incorporates by reference. The court also took judicial notice of the Copyright Office registration records (Island Software & Microsoft; TCA Television), which are public government records not subject to reasonable dispute. Two practical consequences followed: (1) because the videos and the television segments were before the court (either attached or incorporated), the court could compare them and determine whether the defendants’ use was fair as a matter of law; and (2) because the registrations were judicially noticeable, their dates and contents could inform the timing and market‑harm inquiries embedded in a fair use analysis.

The Second Circuit relied on precedent holding that fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage where the complaint and incorporated materials establish the defense “so clearly” that dismissal is appropriate. Applying that standard, the court affirmed the dismissal of the copyright claim on fair‑use grounds and affirmed dismissal of the state‑law claims, although the panel resolved the state claims on state‑law pleading failures (third‑party beneficiary and implied‑contract pleading requirements under the governing state laws; fraudulent inducement’s requirement of out‑of‑pocket loss), rather than resting solely on federal preemption.

Concluding note for readers: when you read the opinion keep two procedural points in mind. First, a dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) is judged by the Twombly/Iqbal plausibility standard and by what the complaint and its incorporated exhibits actually show. Second, public records (like Copyright Office registrations) and the substantive works themselves (when before the court) can be decisive at the pleadings stage because they allow a court to resolve affirmative defenses—such as fair use—without developing facts through discovery.

# Fair Use Framework and Procedural Gatekeeping

## Fair use as an affirmative defense designed to avoid rigid application that would chill creativity

Fair use is an affirmative defense that operates as a safety valve in the Copyright Act: it “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” (Opinion citing Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179.) That descriptive purpose explains why courts treat fair use as a flexible, context‑sensitive inquiry rather than a bright‑line rule. In Santos v. Kimmel the Second Circuit relied on that flexibility to affirm dismissal: the district court concluded that the late‑night segments were a paradigmatic exercise of commentary/criticism, and the appeals court agreed that the complaint and the works themselves showed fair use as a matter of law.

Because fair use is an affirmative defense, a defendant normally bears the burden of proving it. Practically, however, a defendant may seek dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) when the complaint and the materials properly before the court make fair use “so clearly established” that no further factual development is necessary. The Santos court followed that path: it treated fair use as a defense that defendants could establish on the pleadings where the face of the complaint and the attached or referenced works resolve the issue.

## The four nonexclusive statutory factors under 17 U.S.C. § 107

The statutory inquiry is governed by 17 U.S.C. § 107, which directs courts to weigh four non‑exclusive factors. The Second Circuit’s opinion in Santos walks through these factors in the familiar order and shows how they interact.

First, the “purpose and character of the use” is often the most important factor because it asks whether the secondary use is transformative — whether it “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” rather than merely superseding the original. The Santos court found the JKL segments’ purpose (to comment on and satirize Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money) to be different in character from the original videos, and it treated that difference as central to the fair‑use determination.

Second, the “nature of the copyrighted work” asks whether the original is more factual or more creative and whether it was published. Creative, unpublished works receive stronger protection; published works and more factual works tend to weigh in favor of fair use. In Santos the district‑court record included copyright registrations showing the videos were published the day they were created, a fact the appeals court noted and treated as relevant to weighing this factor.

Third, the “amount and substantiality” factor looks at both quantitative and qualitative aspects of what was copied. Using the heart of a work or copying in full generally points against fair use, but that factor must be read against the backdrop of transformation: a secondary work that is truly transformative may permissibly incorporate substantial portions of the original.

Fourth, the “effect on the potential market” asks whether the secondary use usurps the market for the original or its derivatives. The Second Circuit emphasizes that courts do not ask whether the second work merely devalues the original by critique or parody; rather, the core question is whether the secondary work is a market substitute that would supplant the original’s licensing market. Santos’s complaint, the court said, did not plausibly allege that the JKL segments competed with Santos’s market for licensing the videos, and the nature of critical or satirical uses makes such licensing usurpation unlikely.

These four factors are non‑exclusive and must be balanced. Santos illustrates how a strong showing on the purpose/character and market‑effect axes—together with the record on publication—can support dismissal on fair use grounds even where other factors might be less clear.

## Transformative use: focus on new purpose or character as judged by a reasonable observer

Transformation is the analytical linchpin of the first factor. The Second Circuit (quoting prior precedent) frames the inquiry as whether the secondary work “merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” That inquiry is objective: it turns on what a reasonable observer would perceive, not on the subjective intent of either the original author or the secondary user.

Santos claimed that Kimmel had “instigated the creation of the original Works” and therefore that Kimmel could not claim a transformative purpose because he shaped the originals’ intended use. The court rejected that argument because transformation depends on public perception rather than the creator’s private design. The opinion explicitly invokes the Supreme Court’s view that the subjective intent of the user (or of a court) does not determine the purpose of the secondary use. Applied to the record here, the court concluded that a reasonable observer would read Santos’s videos as conveying hope and encouragement, whereas the JKL segments repurposed those same videos to ridicule and comment on Santos’s behavior—a materially different character that supports fair use.

For students, the takeaway is twofold: (1) focus on whether the secondary work repurposes the original to serve a new communicative function (commentary, criticism, satire, news reporting, etc.), and (2) evaluate that question through an objective “reasonable observer” lens rather than through the parties’ subjective statements of intent.

## Pleading‑stage adjudication: when fair use can be decided on a motion to dismiss by comparing the works and the complaint

Fair‑use adjudication is fact‑intensive, but the Second Circuit has repeatedly held that fair use can be decided at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage when the complaint and the materials properly before the court resolve the necessary factual and comparative questions. The governing procedural rules the court applied are familiar: on de novo review of a 12(b)(6) dismissal, the court considers the complaint, exhibits attached to the complaint, and documents incorporated by reference; it may also take judicial notice of copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry.

TCA Television v. McCollum and Cariou v. Prince are the touchstones the Santos court relied on: if the only evidentiary materials needed to decide fair use are the original work and the allegedly infringing work—which can be attached or incorporated into the complaint—then the question can be resolved as a matter of law without discovery. That is exactly what happened in Santos: the videos, the JKL segments, and the registration materials were before the court, and those items sufficed to evaluate the four statutory factors. Because the complaint’s own allegations and the attached works demonstrated fair use, dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) was appropriate.

This procedural route is important for practitioners: defendants may press a 12(b)(6) dismissal when the works themselves are in the record and the complaint’s allegations do not create factual disputes material to the four‑factor analysis. Conversely, if resolution requires extrinsic evidence—about market harm, intent to supplant a licensing market, or other contested factual matters—courts should and do deny dismissal and allow development of the record.

## Alleged deception or bad faith after Harper & Row: why it does not, by itself, defeat otherwise fair commentary or criticism

Santos argued that Kimmel’s deceptive conduct in procuring the videos (using fictitious names) demonstrated bad faith and therefore foreclosed fair use, citing Harper & Row, which contained language that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” The Second Circuit acknowledged Harper & Row’s language but explained that later decisions have (as the opinion put it) “distanced” that presupposition and treated bad faith as a relevant but non‑dispositive consideration.

In practice, allegations of deception or bad faith can inform the first‑factor analysis—they help illuminate the secondary user’s purpose—but they do not automatically nullify fair use. To defeat fair use, bad faith must be tied to an objective that undermines the first factor or to conduct that indicates an intent to supplant the original’s market—i.e., to engage in the kind of commercial displacement the fourth factor protects against. In Santos the court found the complaint itself undercut any inference that defendants intended to supplant Santos’s licensing market; instead, the complaint described a motive of sarcastic commentary. Thus, the alleged deception did not overcome the other fair‑use considerations.

For students, the rule to remember is that bad faith may weigh against fair use, but courts treat it as one piece of the statutory mosaic rather than as an independent trumping rule. When faced with allegations of deceptive procurement, ask whether the alleged bad faith bears on the core fair‑use concerns (transformative purpose and market usurpation); without that link, bad faith alone will not defeat a well‑supported fair‑use defense.

Conclusion

Santos v. Kimmel illustrates several doctrinal and procedural points that students should extract when reading fair‑use opinions: fair use is an affirmative but flexible defense aimed at protecting derivative creativity; the four § 107 factors—especially the transformative inquiry—are the controlling analytical framework; transformation is judged objectively by a reasonable observer; courts may resolve fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint and the works themselves make the statutory balancing clear; and allegations of deception or bad faith inform but do not automatically negate a fair‑use defense. The Second Circuit applied those principles to hold that the late‑night segments were a quintessentially transformative form of commentary and that the record before the court supported dismissal on fair‑use grounds.

# Applying the Fair Use Factors to Santos’s Videos and JKL

The core dispute the Second Circuit resolved was whether Kimmel’s broadcast of Santos’s Cameo videos on Jimmy Kimmel Live! (JKL) was barred by the fair use doctrine. The court applied the four statutory factors from 17 U.S.C. § 107 and concluded that, as a matter of law on the pleadings, fair use barred Santos’s copyright claims. The following sections walk through the court’s factor-by-factor reasoning and the procedural basis for deciding fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage.

## Factor 1 (purpose and character): JKL’s new purpose—commenting on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—was different from the videos’ original uplifting messages

The opinion treats the first factor—the “purpose and character of the use”—as decisive. Under binding Second Circuit and Supreme Court precedent, the inquiry focuses “chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative,” that is, whether the secondary use “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character,” rather than merely superseding the original. The District Court found, and the Second Circuit accepted, that Kimmel’s segments used the Cameo videos to comment on and mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money; Santos’s complaint itself described the original videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Those two purposes are different in kind. The court therefore concluded that a reasonable observer would see Kimmel’s broadcasts as repurposing Santos’s videos for satirical criticism rather than merely republishing them for the same uplifting ends. Because that difference in purpose is pronounced, the first factor “strongly supports” fair use.

This application illustrates two practical points for reading fair-use holdings. First, courts look at how the secondary use frames and employs the original material—whether it creates new meaning or message—not merely at whether the secondary user copied. Second, the court did not require a small change or partial use to call something transformative; repackaging the videos as evidence in a critical, satirical segment was enough to change the use’s character.

## Rejecting the ‘instigator’ argument: even if Kimmel prompted the content, the inquiry is objective; subjective purposes of either party are not controlling

Santos argued that Kimmel should not be able to claim a transformative purpose because Kimmel “instigated” or solicited the original Cameo videos and therefore was the “designer” of their original purpose. The Second Circuit rejected that line of attack. The court relied on the objective standard for determining purpose: what a reasonable observer would perceive, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the later user. Thus, even if Kimmel prompted Santos to create the videos, that fact does not erase the change in communicative purpose when JKL aired the videos as part of a mocking commentary. In short, alleged instigation does not automatically convert later critical reuse into non-transformative copying; the transformation analysis looks to the use’s observable meaning and function.

## Bad faith contention: pseudonymous requests did not show an intent to supplant Santos’s market; the use was for sarcastic criticism/commentary

Santos further argued that Kimmel’s use was in bad faith because he submitted requests under fictitious names, implying deception. Santos relied on a statement in Harper & Row that fair use presupposes good faith. The court declined to treat asserted deception as dispositive. It observed that the Supreme Court has moved away from treating good faith as a categorical prerequisite and emphasized that the complaint itself did not plausibly allege an intent to supplant Santos’s commercial market for the videos. Instead, the complaint portrayed defendants’ motive as sarcastic criticism—classic fair-use conduct. Even where a defendant’s behavior might look misleading, the key fairness inquiry remains whether the secondary use is aimed at criticism/commentary and whether it would supplant the plaintiff’s market; mere subterfuge in obtaining a work does not automatically defeat a fair-use claim where the other factors favor transformation.

## Factor 2 (nature of the work): the videos were published the day they were created; publication status weakens any claim to heightened protection for unpublished works

The second factor asks whether the copyrighted material is closer to the core of copyright protection—creative, unpublished works receive greater weight. Here the court relied on Santos’s copyright registrations, which (as the District Court judicially noticed) established that the videos were published the same day they were created. Publication under the statute reduces the weight of this factor in favor of the copyright owner because unpublished status traditionally commands stronger protection. The court treated the timing of publication as a straightforward, documentary fact that cut against giving the second factor substantial force for Santos.

## Factor 3 (amount and substantiality): commentary and criticism may require extensive copying; the use was tied to the critical purpose

The third factor considers both the quantitative and qualitative scope of what was copied. The opinion recognizes the basic principle that copying the whole work can count against fair use, but it also reiterates the important limitation from precedent: when criticism or commentary is the purpose, reproducing substantial portions—or even the whole—may be necessary to make the critical point. In Santos’s case, the JKL segments used the videos for the very purpose of showing what Santos said on camera; to critique whether he would “say it,” Kimmel needed to show the actual statements. Because the allegedly infringing use was functionally tied to that critical purpose, the court treated the third factor as not dispositively favoring Santos. The opinion therefore accepted that extensive copying in service of commentary can be reasonable under § 107.

## Factor 4 (market effect): no usurpation of Santos’s market; criticism/lampooning is not a cognizable licensing market to be protected; the segments were not substitutes for Cameo videos

The fourth factor—effect on the potential market—was analyzed under the familiar substitution test: the inquiry is whether the secondary work usurps the market for the original by serving as a market substitute, not whether it merely harms the original’s goodwill or devalues it through criticism. The court concluded on the pleadings that Kimmel’s broadcasts did not and could not plausibly be a substitute for Santos’s paid Cameo videos: a late-night comedy segment mocking a public figure is not a competing market for personalized Cameo greetings. The court also relied on the established proposition that creators of imaginative works are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions, so the existence of a cognizable licensing market for this kind of use is doubtful. Because Santos did not allege a plausible market usurpation, the fourth factor favored fair use.

## Bottom line: comparing the videos and the JKL segments sufficed to find fair use on the pleadings

Procedurally important, the Second Circuit stressed that fair use can be resolved at the motion-to-dismiss stage when the complaint and the works themselves make the factors clear. The court relied on the complaint, the videos and segments attached or incorporated into the record, and Santos’s copyright registrations (which the court may judicially notice) to perform the statutory balancing. Because those materials plainly showed a transformative critical use, publication of the originals, copying needed for criticism, and no plausible market usurpation, the court concluded that fair use barred Santos’s copyright claims as a matter of law. For a student reading the opinion, the lesson is that where the record itself reveals an objective change in purpose and no plausible market substitution, a court may find fair use without reaching disputed factual development.

# Contract-Based State Claims: Third-Party Beneficiary and Implied Contract Theories

## Preemption posture: court declines to decide; resolves on failure to state a claim
The Second Circuit began this portion of the opinion by saying it need not resolve whether Santos’s contract-based claims are preempted by the Copyright Act. Although the District Court had dismissed some state claims on preemption grounds, the appellate court declined to reach that question because it could affirm on an independent, dispositive ground: Santos’s contract theories failed to state a claim under state law. That approach is important to keep in mind when reading the opinion: the court rests its decision on traditional state-law pleading rules rather than resolving a potentially broader federal preemption issue.

## Direct breach claim under Cameo Terms (Illinois law): strong presumption against third‑party beneficiaries
Santos’s “direct breach” theory depended on his status as an intended third‑party beneficiary of Cameo’s Terms of Service (the contract between Cameo and the user who ordered a video). The Terms of Service were governed by Illinois law, and under that law there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” As the court quotes, any implication that a contract applies to third parties “must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” In other words, a non‑party like Santos cannot enforce a contract unless the contract itself shows a clear, near‑explicit intent by the contracting parties to confer an enforceable right on that non‑party.

The practical upshot for Santos was straightforward: because he was not a signatory to Cameo’s agreement with the user, he needed the Terms of Service to manifest a clear, express (or practically express) intent to permit third‑party enforcement. The complaint did not identify any such express provision that would make Santos an intended beneficiary entitled to sue for breach.

## No express third‑party‑beneficiary language conferring enforcement rights on Santos; contrast with provisions that do expressly benefit third parties
The court emphasized a textual point in the Terms of Service that works against Santos. The Terms contained some provisions that expressly mention third‑party rights, which demonstrates that the drafter knew how to create enforceable third‑party benefits when intended. That fact undercuts any inference that other, non‑express provisions were meant to benefit noncontracting persons: if the parties intended third‑party enforcement, they said so expressly in the document. Santos pointed to no comparable, express language in the provisions he relied on; he therefore identified “nothing close to an ‘express declaration’” that the Terms were meant to be enforceable by persons like him. Under Illinois law, that absence is fatal to a direct breach claim premised on third‑party beneficiary status.

## Implied‑in‑fact contract (New York law): must plead essential terms and a meeting of the minds
Santos’s alternative theory was that an implied‑in‑fact contract existed between him and the defendants (or between the defendants and him) that bound Kimmel to the Terms of Service or otherwise protected Santos. The court analyzed that claim under New York law, which requires courts to look to “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to decide whether an implied contract was formed and what its terms were.

New York law requires more than a conclusory allegation that “an implied contract existed.” The plaintiff must plead the essential terms of the alleged agreement in nonconclusory language — including the specific provisions that form the basis of liability — and must allege facts showing a meeting of the minds. Put differently, a plaintiff must do more than recite the label “implied contract”; the complaint must identify what promises were made, who made them, how acceptance occurred, what consideration (if relevant) supported the agreement, and circumstances showing the parties intended to be bound.

## Complaint’s deficiencies: no specific nonconclusory terms or plausible assent that Kimmel agreed to abide by Cameo’s Terms
Applying those rules to Santos’s complaint, the court found two critical pleading failures. First, Santos did not allege in nonconclusory fashion the essential terms of the putative implied contract. The opinion points to the complaint paragraphs that purport to plead an implied contract (App’x 22 ¶¶ 78–79) and concludes they fail to identify the specific contract provisions upon which liability is predicated. Merely alleging that defendants “breached” unspecified terms or generally invoking the Terms of Service falls short of the Caniglia standard the court cited: the complaint must recite the specific provisions and the operative terms that the plaintiff says were breached.

Second, Santos failed plausibly to allege a meeting of the minds — i.e., facts showing mutual assent by the relevant parties that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service or otherwise be bound in a way that benefited Santos. The court relied on New York authorities requiring an “indication of a meeting of minds” and concluded the complaint did not furnish factual allegations making that inference plausible. The complaint did not allege, for example, how or when Kimmel manifested assent to the Terms in a manner that could support an implied contract with Santos, nor did it allege factual circumstances from which the court could infer an agreement between Kimmel and Santos distinct from the Cameo–user contract.

Because the complaint lacked these basic factual predicates — specific nonconclusory terms and plausible mutual assent — the implied‑in‑fact contract claim failed to state a claim under New York law.

Conclusion
Taken together, these analyses explain why the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of Santos’s contract‑based claims without reaching preemption. Under Illinois law, Santos could not show the near‑express contractual intent required to be an enforceable third‑party beneficiary of Cameo’s Terms. Under New York law, he failed to plead the essential terms and a meeting of the minds necessary to sustain an implied‑in‑fact contract. The opinion therefore disposes of the contract theories on conventional state‑law pleading grounds.

# Fraudulent Inducement and the Out-of-Pocket Rule

## Damages requirement: New York’s out-of-pocket rule for fraud

Under New York law, a plaintiff bringing a claim for fraudulent inducement must plead and ultimately prove an actual pecuniary injury caused by reliance on the defendant’s misrepresentation. The Second Circuit in Santos v. Kimmel relied on this settled rule, citing Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), for the proposition that fraud damages must be measured by the plaintiff’s out-of-pocket loss. In other words, fraud is not a vehicle for recovering purely non-monetary harms; the plaintiff must allege a concrete, financial detriment that flowed from the alleged deceit. This requirement is an essential element of a New York fraud claim and is considered at the pleading stage: if the complaint contains no plausible allegation of monetary loss tied to the misrepresentation, the fraud claim fails as a matter of law.

Two features of this rule are worth emphasizing for reading opinions. First, out-of-pocket damages focus on what the victim actually lost because of reliance (for example, money paid or other quantifiable expenditures or diminution in the value of property). Second, the plaintiff must connect that monetary loss causally to the defendant’s misrepresentation—mere coincidence or speculative future harms are not enough.

## What counts as out-of-pocket loss versus what does not

Out-of-pocket loss typically means money or property the plaintiff actually parted with or a measurable decline in the value of something the plaintiff owned due to reliance on the misrepresentation. Examples that fit comfortably within the rule include payments the plaintiff made in reliance on the false statement, expenses incurred because of the reliance, or a specific asset that was diminished in value as a direct result.

By contrast, many harms that feel real but are non-pecuniary do not, standing alone, satisfy New York’s fraud-damages requirement. Reputational injury, humiliation, emotional distress, or generalized damage to goodwill do not qualify as out-of-pocket losses unless they can be translated into a concrete pecuniary injury (for example, lost fees from a specific contract that was actually terminated because of the misrepresentation and can be shown with reasonable particularity). Similarly, speculative or hypothetical lost profits—future earnings that are uncertain or not tied to a specific, provable transaction—are generally insufficient unless the plaintiff can plead them with the particularity and causal connection required by law. The Second Circuit relied on Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017), to reinforce that New York requires an actual out-of-pocket loss for fraud claims to proceed.

## Application to these facts: no actual monetary loss alleged from making or delivering the videos

Applying that rule to Santos’s complaint, the Court found the pleading conspicuously barren of any allegation that Santos suffered a monetary loss resulting from Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations. Santos’s theory was that defendants induced him to create and deliver personalized videos through false representations, but his complaint did not allege that he paid money, expended quantifiable resources in reliance, lost specific licensing fees, or otherwise experienced a tangible pecuniary injury attributable to those inducements. The District Court concluded, and the Second Circuit agreed, that Santos had failed to plead an “actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.” In short, the harms alleged in the complaint (public mockery, reputational injury, the broadcast of the videos) were not presented as concrete monetary losses tied to the alleged deceit, and therefore did not meet New York’s damages requirement for fraud.

It is also instructive to see how this factual defect interacts with the rest of the opinion. The Court’s copyright analysis separately concluded there was no market usurpation by the defendants; that same absence of market or licensing harm undercuts any theory that Santos suffered an identifiable pecuniary loss from the broadcast or reuse of the videos.

## Result: dismissal for failure to plead legally cognizable fraud damages

Because Santos did not plead the necessary element of pecuniary loss causally linked to the alleged misrepresentations, his fraudulent inducement claim failed at the pleading stage and was properly dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6). The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal, citing Lama and Connaughton for the damages rule. For students reading the opinion, the key takeaway is practical: under New York law a fraud plaintiff must do more than allege deception and hurt feelings—she must allege a specific, measurable out-of-pocket loss caused by the deception. Absent that allegation (and the plausible facts to support it), a fraudulent-inducement claim will not survive a motion to dismiss.

# Holding, Rationale, and Takeaways

## Disposition: Affirmance of dismissal—copyright claims barred by fair use; state-law claims fail to state a claim
The Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6). It held that Santos’s copyright-infringement claims were barred by the fair use doctrine and that each of his state-law claims (breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement) failed to state a claim under applicable state law. The court applied de novo review of the dismissal, considering the allegations in the complaint and documents incorporated into it (including copyright registrations, which the court may judicially notice). The panel agreed with the district court that, on the face of the complaint and supporting documents, the JKL segments were a paradigmatic example of a transformative, critical use and that Santos’s contract and fraud claims lacked the factual specificity required under Illinois and New York law.

## Key fair use lessons
This opinion is a compact demonstration of how the four statutory fair use factors—(1) purpose and character, (2) nature of the copyrighted work, (3) amount and substantiality used, and (4) effect on the market—are applied at the pleading stage when the complaint and attached materials make the analysis plain.

First, the court placed primary weight on the first factor: purpose and character. Consistent with Second Circuit precedent and Supreme Court guidance, the court focused on whether the secondary use is transformative—whether it “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character,” rather than merely superseding the original. The panel emphasized that this is an objective inquiry: what a reasonable observer would perceive the videos to convey, not the subjective intent of the creator or the secondary user. Santos’s argument that Kimmel “instigated” the creation of the videos and thus shared the original purpose failed because the complaint itself described the videos’ original tone (messages of hope and encouragement), and a reasonable observer would see Kimmel’s broadcasts as sarcastic commentary targeting Santos. The opinion cites the Andy Warhol/Goldsmith line of authority for the point that subjective intent is not dispositive.

Second, the court treated allegations of deception (Kimmel’s use of fictitious names to obtain videos) as insufficient, by themselves, to defeat fair use. The panel recognized Harper & Row’s language that fair use presupposes good faith, but noted that later authority has soured on treating good faith as a rigid prerequisite. More concretely, the court looked at the complaint for signs that defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s market for the videos; finding none, and seeing instead a use motivated by criticism and ridicule, it concluded bad faith allegations did not undermine fair use. The key point is that deception in acquisition, without more (for example, evidence that the secondary use usurped a licensing market), will not automatically turn a transformative use into infringement.

Third, the court’s treatment of the fourth factor—market effect—recapitulates a familiar principle: the relevant inquiry is whether the secondary work offers a competing substitute for the original, not merely whether it reduces the original’s value by criticism or parody. Courts ask whether the secondary use usurps the licensing market for the originals. Here, the panel relied on the complaint and registrations (which showed the videos were published contemporaneously) to conclude Santos did not plausibly allege market usurpation. The opinion cites Campbell and more recent Second Circuit rulings to explain that critical or parodic uses typically do not fall within the licensing market for the originals.

Finally, and practically important for litigators, the court reaffirmed that fair use can be resolved at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage when the complaint (and attached/integrated materials) makes the fair use defense clear. The panel invoked precedent holding that fair use, like other affirmative defenses, can be so plainly established by the complaint that dismissal for failure to state a claim is appropriate.

## State-law lessons
Although the district court had also dismissed Santos’s contract claims on preemption grounds, the Second Circuit did not need to resolve preemption; it affirmed because Santos’s state-law claims independently failed to state viable causes of action.

On the direct breach-of-contract theory, Santos sought to enforce Cameo’s Terms of Service against defendants even though he was not a party to that agreement. Applying Illinois law (which governed the Terms), the court explained the strong presumption against conferring contractual benefits on nonparties: for a nonparty to enforce a contract, there must be clear evidence that the contracting parties intended to confer an enforceable benefit—essentially an “express declaration.” The opinion relied on the presence, elsewhere in the Terms, of explicit third‑party beneficiary language to show that the drafter knew how to create such rights when intended; in the absence of such language the complaint provided no basis to treat Santos as an intended beneficiary.

On Santos’s implied-in-fact contract claim (governed by New York law), the court emphasized the requirement to plead the essential terms of the alleged contract and factual allegations showing a meeting of the minds. That means plaintiffs must identify, in nonconclusory terms, what the parties agreed to and how the agreement was formed. The complaint here failed to allege the essential provisions that defendants supposedly breached or any facts showing mutual assent between Santos and the defendants. The Second Circuit therefore affirmed dismissal of the implied-contract claim.

Finally, the fraudulent-inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege the kind of damages New York law requires for fraud: actual out-of-pocket loss. Under New York precedent, a fraud plaintiff must plead measurable pecuniary loss traceable to the misrepresentation; Santos’s complaint did not allege such a loss and so his fraud claim could not survive.

## Practical implications
For media practitioners and content creators, the opinion carries several practical lessons. First, when a broadcaster obtains personalized or user-produced content and uses it in a clearly critical, satirical, or parodying context, that re-use may be strongly transformative and fall within fair use—even if the secondary user played a role in procuring the content. The objective test of transformation means courts will look to how the reused clips function in the new work and how a reasonable audience will perceive them. Second, courts will not treat acquisition tactics (like using fictitious names) as dispositive against fair use; such tactics are relevant only to the extent they bear on the statutory factors—most importantly market effect and whether the secondary use is a market substitute for the originals.

For litigators, the case reinforces pleading discipline. A plaintiff pressing contract claims based on another party’s Terms of Service should (a) identify the precise contractual provision allegedly breached, (b) plead facts showing the contracting parties intended to benefit the plaintiff (or that the plaintiff was otherwise a party), and (c) provide facts showing offer, acceptance, and consideration or a meeting of the minds for an implied contract. For fraud claims in New York, plaintiffs must plead concrete out‑of‑pocket damages causally tied to the alleged misrepresentation.

Finally, the decision is a reminder that when fair use is apparent from the face of the complaint and attached documents (including registrations), defendants can—and courts may—seek early dismissal. Likewise, plaintiffs bringing state-law claims tied to third-party terms must anticipate high hurdles for third-party beneficiary theories and precise pleading requirements for implied-contract and fraud claims.

In short: the Santos opinion reinforces an objective, context-centered fair use inquiry focused on transformation and market substitution, places limited weight on alleged deception absent other factors, and underscores the demanding pleading standards for contract and fraud claims against non-contracting parties.
