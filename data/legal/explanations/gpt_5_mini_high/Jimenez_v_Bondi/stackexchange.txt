Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does substantial-evidence review work when the BIA issues a brief, single-member decision?
Question:
The panel says it reviews the BIA’s decision but can consult the IJ’s "more complete explanation" (citing Uanreroro). I’m confused about what that actually means in practice. Does the court treat the IJ’s reasoning as part of the BIA’s rationale, or only as background? When is remand required because the BIA’s decision is too cursory to permit meaningful review, versus when can the court lean on the IJ’s findings to uphold the BIA?
Answer:
Short answer
In the Tenth Circuit (as the panel explains in Jimenez), the court still reviews the BIA’s order under the substantial‑evidence standard, but when the BIA issues a terse single‑member affirmance the court may consult the IJ’s “more complete explanation” to identify the factual and legal bases the BIA must have relied on. If the IJ’s opinion actually explains the same grounds the BIA affirmed and supplies adequate findings, the court will use that explanation to perform substantial‑evidence review and can affirm the BIA on that basis. If neither the BIA nor the IJ gives a sufficient explanation on a dispositive issue so that meaningful review is impossible (or if the IJ’s reasoning doesn’t square with the BIA’s terse ruling), remand will be required.

Why — the doctrinal hook (from Jimenez)
Jimenez states the controlling approach directly: “Because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, we review the BIA’s decision, but ‘we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.’” (quoting Uanreroro). The court also reminds that it applies the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings: the BIA’s findings “are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” (Rivera‑Barrientos, quoted in Jimenez).

What that means in practice
- The BIA remains the decision under review; the IJ’s opinion is treated as the administrative record’s fuller statement of the grounds the BIA affirmed. The IJ’s reasoning therefore serves as the explanatory surrogate the court can consult to see whether the BIA’s conclusions are supported by substantial evidence. (Jimenez expressly permits consulting the IJ’s “more complete explanation.”)

- When the IJ’s opinion is detailed and addresses the same issues the BIA affirmed (credibility, persecution, nexus, protection, relocation, etc.), the court will read the IJ’s findings against the record and apply the substantial‑evidence test. That is precisely what the Tenth Circuit did in Jimenez: the IJ had written findings (including that petitioners were credible but that their threats and psychological harms did not amount to persecution); the court consulted those findings and concluded there was substantial evidence to support the BIA’s denial.

- By contrast, if the BIA’s short order and the IJ’s writeup leave open the dispositive legal or factual basis for the outcome (for example, if the IJ didn’t explain an adverse credibility finding, didn’t address nexus or government‑protection on the contested theory, or the IJ’s rationale conflicts with what the BIA appears to have affirmed), the appellate court cannot conduct a meaningful substantial‑evidence review and will ordinarily remand so the agency can provide a reasoned explanation.

Practical checklist for briefing/argument
1. Read the BIA order to identify the grounds it affirmed.  
2. Read the IJ opinion to see whether it explains those same grounds in sufficient detail.  
3. If the IJ supplies a clear, reasoned explanation on the contested issues, expect the court to rely on it for substantial‑evidence review (as Jimenez did).  
4. If the IJ is silent, conclusory, inconsistent with the BIA’s terse order, or fails to explain a dispositive finding, press for remand because the court will lack a record it can meaningfully review.

Bottom line
Jimenez illustrates the Tenth Circuit’s practical approach: the court reviews the BIA’s decision under the substantial‑evidence standard but may (and will) consult the IJ’s fuller opinion to perform that review. The IJ’s reasoning functions as the explanatory basis the court uses to test the BIA’s findings — sufficient IJ explanations permit affirmance on appeal; insufficient or inconsistent explanations make remand appropriate.

### Why didn’t the armed threats and the incident with the child count as past persecution?
Question:
The court repeats that "threats alone" are insufficient, but here there were two armed confrontations (guns brandished, orders to leave, death threats), an assault on a factory employee while searching for petitioners, and a man physically grabbing the son with a hand over his mouth before the child ran away. The IJ found petitioners credible. How does the Tenth Circuit distinguish these facts from cases where cumulative threats and intimidation cross the line into persecution, especially where there is some physical contact (even if brief) and weapons involved? Also, does harm to a third party (the assaulted employee) ever count toward the ‘cumulative’ persecution analysis?
Answer:
Short answer: Because under Tenth Circuit law the incidents here—armed threats, a brief grab of the child’s mouth, and an assault on a factory employee—were treated as episodic threats and intimidation without the sort of sustained, offensive suffering courts call “persecution.” The court accepted the family’s credibility but applied the settled legal test: threats (even with weapons or brief contact) can be part of a cumulative showing, but by themselves and in this record they did not amount to persecution. Third‑party injuries (the murdered colleague and the assaulted employee) are relevant background and may bolster a claim of danger, but they cannot substitute for the requirement that the applicant himself suffer harm that is “more than… threats or sporadic restrictions.”

Why: the legal standard the court applied
- Persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm … and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” (Jimenez v. Bondi, C.A.10 Oct. 7, 2025) (quoting Wiransane).
- The court “consider[s] incidents collectively” (Ritonga), but it also follows precedent that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution” (Karki). Physical harm is not strictly required, but the harm must be “offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats” (Brandy).
- The court observed that it has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” It also emphasized that petitioners conceded they suffered no physical injury or lasting physical harm.

How those principles controlled the result here
- The record contained two gunpoint encounters, a factory employee’s assault while searching for petitioners, and a brief grabbing incident with the son. The court explicitly considered those events as part of the cumulative picture, but concluded they were episodic, did not produce physical injury to the petitioners, and the resulting emotional distress (panic, sleeplessness) was legally insufficient to cross the persecution line. The opinion contrasts this outcome with other cases where even more extreme patterns nevertheless failed—illustrating that severity, duration, and actual infliction matter as much as the presence of weapons or brief contact.
- The court also noted motive: the escalation began after Jimenez’s property dispute with Don Rafa and appears tied to a private criminal/business dispute (and accusations of being a “snitch”), not primarily to a protected political opinion. Even if the events were more severe, a lack of protected‑ground nexus would defeat asylum.

What about harm to third parties?
- Jimenez treated those incidents (the murdered colleague; the employee assaulted during the search) as relevant background evidence of the persecutors’ severity and methods. But the court did not treat third‑party harm as a substitute for the statutory requirement that the applicant himself suffered persecution. In short: third‑party harm can strengthen the record about danger or the persecutor’s capacity, but it will not by itself convert episodic threats against the applicant into legally cognizable past persecution when the applicant’s own harm remains limited.

Procedural/deferential point
- The IJ found the family credible, but under the substantial‑evidence standard the BIA’s and IJ’s legal conclusion that the cumulative incidents did not amount to persecution was binding unless the record compelled a contrary conclusion. The Tenth Circuit found that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s decision.

Bottom line: weapons and brief physical contact matter, but under the court’s precedents the incidents here—viewed together, with no physical injury to petitioners, primarily private‑motive attacks, and only psychological harms—fell short of the “more than threats” threshold required to prove past persecution.

### In a mixed-motive case, how did the court conclude politics was only ‘tangential’ given the explicit references to the Liberal Party?
Question:
The opinion acknowledges a brochure saying "death to snitches from Liberal Party" and that the men called petitioners "snitches" who were "in politics." Yet the court treated the campaign-related threats as not tied to Don Rafa and emphasized the later property-dispute motive as predominant. What evidentiary showing is needed to establish that political opinion was at least one central reason when some threats are political in content but the persecutor may also be retaliating for anti-crime or personal-conflict reasons? Do applicants have to identify the same persecutor behind all incidents to link earlier political threats into the nexus analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
The Tenth Circuit treated the politics material as “tangential” because the record tied the actual, escalated threats to a property‑dispute/retaliatory motive and did not tie those threats to the earlier campaign‑related warnings. Under the governing test the applicant must show the persecutor’s motive included the applicant’s political opinion as “at least one central reason” — mere political language in a threat (or an unattended brochure) is not enough unless it can be connected to the persecutor and shown to be a central, not subordinate, motive.

Relevant legal standard (as the opinion states)
- To obtain asylum on political‑opinion grounds the petitioner must prove the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. Political motives cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” or play “a minor role.” (Jimenez v. Bondi, citing Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti; 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)).  
- Mixed motives are permitted, but the protected ground must be a central reason (the opinion cites O.C.V.).

How the court applied that test in Jimenez
- Timing and escalation: the court emphasized that petitioners “only began receiving escalated threats after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa,” which pointed to retaliation for interfering with Don Rafa’s property/operations rather than pure political targeting.  
- Lack of linkage to the persecutor: the court repeatedly noted petitioners “do not point to any evidence in the record that suggests Don Rafa was behind those initial politically affiliated threats.” The brochure (“death to snitches from Liberal Party”) and earlier calls therefore were not sufficient to show Don Rafa’s motive.  
- Persecutor’s statements pointed to a non‑political motive: the men who confronted petitioners accused Jimenez of being a “snitch for the prosecutor’s office,” blamed him for searches/seizures at the property, and told an employee the order “came from Don Rafa.” The court read those statements as evidence of a predominantly personal/financial/anti‑crime motive.  
- Conclusion: because the record showed the property dispute motive predominated and lacked evidence tying the political threats to Don Rafa, the BIA’s finding that political opinion was “merely tangential and subordinate” was supported by substantial evidence.

What evidentiary showing is needed (practical translation of the opinion)
The court’s analysis shows the kinds of proof a petitioner needs to make political opinion a central reason when some threats are political in content:
- Evidence tying the specific persecutor (or the group that later harmed you) to the political threats or showing the persecutor knew of and targeted your political activity (e.g., explicit statements by the persecutor linking their actions to your party activity). Jimenez failed this link for Don Rafa.  
- A clear temporal/causal relationship between the political activity and the inflicted harm (persecution that begins or escalates because of the political activity). The court stressed the escalation occurred after the property dispute.  
- Corroboration that the persecutor has targeted others for the same political activity (a pattern) or documentation showing the persecutor was acting against party members generally — otherwise a single politically‑tinged item (like a brochure) may be ambiguous.  
- Evidence excluding or minimizing an alternative, dominant motive (here, evidence showing the property dispute was merely coincident with political activity rather than the real cause). The court relied on evidence that the men blamed Jimenez’s law/prosecutor work and the searches/seizures.

Are you required to identify the same persecutor behind all incidents?
- Not strictly required that every incident be committed by the exact same individual, but you must link the incidents you rely on to the persecutor(s) whose motive you assert. If earlier politically‑worded threats came from different, unconnected actors and there is no evidence those actors were coordinated with or acting at the direction of the later persecutor, the earlier incidents will not help establish that the later persecutor was motivated by political opinion. The court rejected petitioners’ reliance on earlier political threats because they did not show Don Rafa was responsible for them; absent that linkage, those threats could not establish that Don Rafa’s motive was political.

Takeaway
Jimenez shows that courts will look beyond political language to motive and linkage: political content in a flyer or in isolated comments is relevant but insufficient unless you can connect that content to the actor who actually persecuted you and show the political reason was a central (not subordinate) cause of the harm.

### What counts as adequate ‘reporting’ to prove the government is unable or unwilling to protect?
Question:
Petitioners told at least one patrol officer, who allegedly said officers were part of the problem and told them to leave, but they didn’t file formal complaints or escalate beyond the neighborhood. The panel says that’s insufficient and points to prior police search attempts at the property as evidence of state willingness. Practically, what steps satisfy the notice-and-opportunity requirement in the Tenth Circuit—does verbally informing a patrol officer count? When is failure to report excused as dangerous or futile, and what kind of record evidence (e.g., country reports, prior retaliation against complainants) is needed to meet that exception?
Answer:
Short answer
In Jimenez the Tenth Circuit made clear that telling one patrol officer what happened can be evidence of “notice,” but by itself is often insufficient to show the government was “unable or unwilling” to protect. The court treats reporting as a key fact: an applicant should either (1) show the government had a real chance to act (formal reports, escalation, documented follow-up, offers of protection) or (2) put solid record evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerously counter‑productive. Jimenez rejected the petitioners’ reliance on a single, informal officer comment because the record showed the state had tried to act (searches/seizures at the property) and the petitioners did not use other channels or document further attempts. Jimenez v. Bondi, No. 24‑9573 (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).

Governing standard (as the opinion states)
- The court emphasizes two categories of evidence that “weigh heavily”: (1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether the government offered protection (e.g., protective custody). The inquiry is “intertwined” with whether the government had notice and an opportunity to respond—“a key fact … is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.” (Jimenez, §II.E (quoting Singh).)
- Failure to report “can undercut” a claim, but it is not necessarily fatal: an applicant can avoid that problem by proving that filing a police report would have been “futile or dangerous.” (Jimenez, §II.E.)

What Jimenez actually did with these principles
- Petitioners told a patrol officer that they had been threatened; the officer said police were “part of the problem” and advised them to leave. The court held that evidence of those one‑off conversations “does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect,” particularly where (i) there was nothing showing that the officer’s remark reflected official policy and (ii) the record independently showed the government had attempted “several searches and seizures” at the relevant property. The court also stressed petitioners never sought help outside their neighborhood or through known law‑enforcement contacts. (Jimenez, §II.E.)

Practical takeaways for building the record in the Tenth Circuit
- A solitary verbal report to a patrol officer may help prove notice, but don’t rely on it alone. Jimenez treats a single unhelpful conversation as weak evidence unless it is connected to systemic refusal or danger.
- To show the government had notice and a genuine opportunity to act, put into the record:
  - formal police reports (copies or incident numbers) or contemporaneous written complaints;
  - the names, dates, and content of contacts with officers and any officer responses (preferably in writing or corroborated by witnesses);
  - evidence you escalated (reports to higher police levels, prosecutors, internal affairs or known government contacts) and the responses you received;
  - documentation whether any protective measures were offered or refused.
  Jimenez faulted the petitioners for not producing such escalation or documentary proof and for having government records (searches/seizures) that contradicted their futility claim.

- To sustain the futility/danger exception, the record must show more than a feared outcome or a single hostile remark. Jimenez points toward the need for evidence of systemic failure or credible risk from reporting—e.g., multiple instances of police complicity or retaliation against complainants, repeated official non‑response in the same context, or other documentary proof that reporting would have exposed the applicant to immediate danger. Absent that, the court will credit evidence that the state tried to act (as it did here).

Bottom line
Jimenez: (1) makes reporting a central fact, (2) treats a single verbal report as weak unless tied to systemic unwillingness/inability, and (3) allows the futility/danger exception—but requires record proof of futility/danger. If you’re preparing a claim in the Tenth Circuit, document reporting carefully, escalate if the first response is inadequate, and put into the record whatever concrete evidence you have showing that reporting would have been futile or would have put you in danger.

### Who bears the burden on internal relocation absent past persecution, and did the panel apply the right factors?
Question:
My understanding is that without a past-persecution finding, the applicant bears the burden to show relocation would be unreasonable under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3). Here, the court relied on facts like family remaining unharmed in Colombia, three weeks safely spent in the countryside, and threats that said to leave the neighborhood (not the country). Are those facts alone typically sufficient to find relocation reasonable? How should factors like the applicants’ children, economic disruption, and alleged nationwide reach of the persecutors be weighed, and did the court treat any of those as forfeited due to inadequate briefing?
Answer:
Short answer
- Burden: If an applicant shows past persecution, a rebuttable presumption of future persecution arises and the government then must prove by a preponderance that internal relocation is reasonable (or that circumstances have fundamentally changed). Absent a past‑persecution finding (so no presumption), the applicant bears the burden of proving a well‑founded fear of future persecution — and that, on the record, internal relocation would be unreasonable. Jimenez applies that framework (citing Rivera‑Barrientos and 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).
- Did the panel apply the right factors here? Yes: the panel relied on the standard and the customary, fact‑specific factors — government protection, whether the applicant sought help, family ties and prior safe stays elsewhere in the country, and the scope of threats — and found, on substantial evidence, that relocation was reasonable. The court also declined to entertain several asserted hardships (children’s trauma, economic/administrative burdens, nationwide reach of persecutors) because those arguments were inadequately developed.

Why — short doctrinal recap tied to Jimenez
- The opinion repeats the settled mechanics: past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption of future persecution; the government may rebut that presumption by showing either a fundamental change or that internal relocation would avoid persecution (Rivera‑Barrientos; 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), quoted in the opinion). Because the IJ and BIA found no past persecution here, Petitioners did not get that presumption and therefore had to carry the showing of a well‑founded fear and of the unreasonableness of relocation. The panel reviewed those factual findings under the substantial‑evidence standard and deferred to the agency where the record supported its conclusions.

What the panel relied on in this case
- The panel identified specific record facts supporting reasonableness of relocation: family/relatives still in Colombia and unharmed; Jimenez’s father still working in the criminal‑justice system; the family’s three‑week, safe stay with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogotá countryside; and that the men’s warnings told Petitioners to leave the neighborhood (not the country). The court also noted evidence that the government knew about the criminal property and had attempted searches and seizures — undercutting the claim that police protection would be futile. On that record the Tenth Circuit held there was “substantial evidence” that Petitioners could “safely and reasonably relocate within Colombia.”

Are those facts “typically” sufficient?
- Answer: they can be sufficient, but the relocation inquiry is intensely fact‑specific. In Jimenez those facts — family safety, a prior safe relocation, limited scope of the threats, and evidence of government action — were enough under the substantial‑evidence standard. But other tribunals might reach a different result on a different record if, for example, the threats were nationwide or explicitly targeted the family wherever they went, the applicant showed credible, severe medical or educational harms to children that relocation would exacerbate, relocation would create insurmountable economic or housing hardship, or there was strong evidence of systemic state collusion with the persecutor. The Jimenez panel emphasized the same point indirectly by contrasting the record here with the kinds of evidence the Petitioners failed to produce.

How the court treated children, economic hardship, and alleged nationwide reach
- The opinion does address those categories, but it did not weigh them on the merits because Petitioners did not develop them. The court observed Petitioners’ claims about children’s panic attacks and other hardships but reiterated that emotional/psychological harms and threats alone rarely suffice to establish past persecution. More importantly, the court said Petitioners “only haphazardly brief” arguments about administrative/economic burdens, judicial infrastructure, and the persecutors’ ties to the president, and therefore those arguments “do not pass muster.” In other words, the panel treated them as inadequately developed (effectively forfeited) and refused to grant relief on that basis.

Practical takeaway for briefing/record development
- If you want to defeat a relocation finding (especially when you don’t have a past‑persecution presumption), you must develop the record on the precise relocation factors the agency and courts examine: competent evidence of nationwide or persistent reach of persecutors, concrete proof that relocation would cause unavoidable economic/educational/medical hardship for the family (with documentation), evidence of systemic government inability or unwillingness to protect (not just one unhelpful officer), and corroboration of children’s special needs. Jimenez illustrates that absent that development the court will treat generalized assertions as insufficient and will uphold a relocation finding supported by family ties, prior safe movement, limited scope threats, and evidence of government action.

Bottom line
- Jimenez applied the correct Tenth Circuit framework. On the record before it — family unharmed in Colombia, a prior three‑week safe stay, threats limited to “leave the neighborhood,” and some evidence the state had acted — the panel reasonably concluded relocation was available. The panel did not ignore children, economic, or national‑reach arguments; it treated them as forfeited or unproven because Petitioners failed to develop the record.

### How does alleged ‘alignment’ between private persecutors and the ruling party factor into nexus and the state-action analysis?
Question:
The opinion notes allegations that Don Rafa is a narco-guerrilla leader "politically aligned" with President Petro, but concludes the record doesn’t show persecutors were closely associated with the government, pointing to prior law-enforcement searches of the property. What kind of proof would establish a sufficient connection—ideological sympathy, selective non-enforcement, bribery, documented collusion, or something else—to (a) help satisfy nexus to political opinion, and/or (b) show the government is unwilling or unable to control private actors?
Answer:
Short answer
Ideological “alignment” by itself is weak. To satisfy (a) nexus and (b) the “unable or unwilling” protection inquiry the applicant needs evidence tying (i) the persecutors’ motive to the victim’s political opinion (not merely shared ideology), and/or (ii) the State’s practical protection or enforcement choices to the persecutors (not just isolated police apathy). Jimenez makes clear that the court asks whether political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the harm, and whether the government (with notice) tried to stop the attackers or offered protection. Absent direct or systemic proof of motive or collusion, courts will not infer state alignment from bare allegations of political sympathy. (Jimenez v. Bondi, 10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025 (discussing Rivera‑Barrientos and Singh)).

Relevant legal standards (from Jimenez)
- Nexus: the applicant must show the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution; mixed motives are allowed only if a protected ground is central. (Jimenez ¶ II.D (citing Rivera‑Barrientos).)
- Government protection: courts weigh (1) whether the government tried to stop the persecutors and (2) whether it offered protection (e.g., protective custody); the applicant must show the government had notice and an opportunity to respond. Isolated officer apathy is usually not enough—systemic failures or evidence of collusion are far more probative. (Jimenez ¶ II.E (citing Singh; Osorio‑Morales).)

What kinds of proof would help on nexus (political opinion as a central reason)
- Explicit statements by the persecutors linking the harm to the victim’s politics (e.g., “we’re attacking you because you work for [rival party/candidate]” or contemporaneous threats that single out party membership). The opinion treats such statements as direct evidence of motive.
- Contemporaneous political targeting (timing and pattern): threats or attacks that coincide with political activity and not primarily with unrelated conduct (here, the court emphasized the escalation followed the property dispute, which undermined nexus).
- Pattern evidence that the perpetrators single out members of the victim’s party or campaign (multiple victims with the same political profile, internal lists, repeated acts against the same political group).
- Documents or intercepted communications showing orders or planning to target opponents for political reasons.
- Corroborating third‑party evidence (witnesses, news reports, NGO reports) showing the persecutor’s political targeting across victims.

Why some things are weak for nexus
- Mere ideological sympathy or public support for a political actor, standing alone, does not prove the persecutor targeted this particular victim for political reasons. In Jimenez the court treated allegations of “political alignment” as insufficient because the record showed law‑enforcement activity at the property and the escalation followed a property dispute.

What kinds of proof would help to show the government is unwilling or unable
- Evidence the State had notice and nevertheless failed to act in a systematic way: formal complaints, repeated reports from multiple victims that were ignored, or official logs showing no follow‑up on serious complaints.
- Evidence of documented collusion or direction from officials (emails, text messages, meeting notes, orders directing inaction, payments or transfers from officials to perpetrators).
- Proof of selective non‑enforcement that is systemic (patterns of police standing down or declining to investigate crimes by the perpetrators while policing others), not only a single unhelpful officer. Jimenez relies on the distinction: a lone officer’s “leave” comment is not dispositive (citing Osorio‑Morales).
- Records showing prosecutions were blocked, cases deliberately dropped, or arrests not made despite available evidence—particularly if such failures are traceable to government actors.
- Direct participation or protection by official agents (police working with the group, official seizures that are performative rather than substantive)—conduct showing the State effectively shields the perpetrators.

Why the court rejected the alignment claim in Jimenez
- The court pointed to affirmative government action against the property (several searches and seizures) and the absence of a record showing systemic governmental protection of Don Rafa; that evidence “counters Petitioners’ argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government.” The petitioners also did not show they reported the threats in a way that would allow the government a meaningful chance to protect them; a single officer’s unhelpful comment did not establish systemic unwillingness. (Jimenez ¶ II.E.)

Practical takeaways for assembling proof
- File and keep police complaints; get incident numbers and written responses (notice + opportunity to respond is critical).
- Preserve contemporaneous evidence of threats (brochures, texts, recordings) and obtain witness statements from others similarly targeted.
- Collect documentary proof of any contact between perpetrators and officials (communications, travel/meeting logs, bank transfers).
- Use independent corroboration (local press, NGO or UN reports, prosecution records showing dropped or blocked investigations) to show patterns rather than isolated lapses.

Bottom line
- For nexus you need evidence that the attackers acted (at least centrally) because of the claimant’s political opinion—not merely that the attackers share political sympathies with the ruling party. For the “unable or unwilling” inquiry you need proof that the State, despite notice, systematically failed to protect, shielded or colluded with the perpetrators, or otherwise rendered enforcement ineffectual. Jimenez refused to infer such connections from bare allegations of “alignment,” prior brief police apathy, or a handful of threats when the record showed active law‑enforcement steps against the property and a plausible nonpolitical motive (the property dispute).

### Does the difference in nexus standards between asylum and withholding matter here?
Question:
The panel says petitioners necessarily fail withholding because they failed to establish asylum eligibility. I know asylum requires a ‘well-founded fear’ and ‘one central reason’ for nexus, while some circuits apply an ‘a reason’ nexus standard for withholding. What is the Tenth Circuit’s current rule on withholding nexus? Did the panel’s conclusion rest on the lack of a well-founded fear (which would automatically defeat the higher ‘more likely than not’ standard for withholding), or did its nexus holding for asylum also control the withholding analysis?
Answer:
Short answer: It didn’t matter in Jimenez. The panel denied withholding because the petitioners failed the asylum showing (no past persecution or well‑founded fear and no “central reason” nexus), and it expressly concluded that failing the asylum standard dooms withholding because withholding requires an even higher showing. The court did not adopt or apply a separate Tenth Circuit “a reason” nexus rule for withholding in this opinion.

Why (grounded in the opinion)
- Asylum nexus rule applied: the opinion reiterates the asylum nexus requirement: to show persecution “on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.” (II.D.)
- The court found that requirement unmet: the panel held Petitioners’ political opinion was “merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm” (their interference with Don Rafa’s property/revenue), so the asylum nexus failed. (II.D.)
- Withholding was disposed on the asylum failure: in the conclusion the court says in plain terms that “because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.” (III.)

What the opinion does not do
- The panel did not state or resolve a separate Tenth Circuit rule that withholding uses a different (e.g., “a reason”) nexus test. It simply relied on the asylum merits finding and the relative burdens of proof to dispose of the withholding claim.

Takeaway
- In Jimenez the nexus difference between asylum and withholding was academic: the petitioners’ evidence failed the asylum “central reason” test and produced no well‑founded fear, so the court concluded withholding (which requires a more‑likely‑than‑not showing) could not succeed. The Tenth Circuit did not use this opinion to announce whether a distinct, more favorable withholding nexus standard applies.
