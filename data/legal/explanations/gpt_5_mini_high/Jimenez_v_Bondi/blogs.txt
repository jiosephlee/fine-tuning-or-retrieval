Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Do Threats Become ‘Persecution’? The Tenth Circuit Draws a Hard Line in Jimenez v. Bondi

## Why this case matters
Threats, intimidation, and psychological harm are the central facts in a great many asylum claims — especially those arising from gang or organized-crime violence. In Jimenez v. Bondi, the Tenth Circuit again drew a clear line: repeated, serious threats (including a gunpoint confrontation, menacing follow-ups at a workplace, an assault on an employee seeking the family, and a frightening encounter with a child at school) plus psychological trauma did not, on this record, meet the statutory standard for past persecution. For practitioners and adjudicators, the decision is a useful reminder of two durable themes in the Tenth Circuit’s asylum jurisprudence: (1) threats and fear, even when cumulative and frightening, often fall short of “persecution” unless accompanied by greater objective harm or restraints; and (2) the combination of substantial-evidence review and a court’s acceptance of IJ credibility findings makes reversal a steep climb absent a record that clearly compels a contrary finding.

## The facts in brief
Wilson Jimenez and his family are Colombian nationals who had been active in local politics with the Colombian Liberal Party and who, as a law intern, intervened in a property dispute that implicated a narco-trafficking leader known as “Don Rafa.” In 2022 they received repeated threats: threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, a gunpoint confrontation telling Jimenez to leave the neighborhood, an assault on a factory employee who was asked where the family was, and a frightening school‑yard encounter in which a man placed his hand over the couple’s younger son’s mouth and urged him to “run to your dad.” The family briefly relocated within Colombia, reported an incident to a police patrol officer who told them to leave and that police could do nothing, and ultimately fled to the United States. An IJ found the family credible but denied asylum and withholding; the BIA affirmed; a Tenth Circuit panel denied review.

## How the court treated “past persecution”
The Tenth Circuit’s analysis rests on settled (and oft-repeated) points: asylum applicants must show persecution that is more than “restrictions or threats to life and liberty,” and courts assess incidents cumulatively, not in isolation. The opinion relied on prior Tenth Circuit authorities such as Wiransane and Karki to state that threats alone are generally insufficient. Although the panel acknowledged the seriousness of the Jimenez family’s experiences and the psychological harm they described, it concluded — consistently with earlier Tenth Circuit decisions — that the record did not show the sort of physical infliction, sustained severe abuse, or objective restraints on liberty necessary to classify the harms as persecution. The court expressly noted the absence of lasting physical injury and emphasized precedent refusing asylum where frightening or stressful incidents did not reach the statutory threshold.

That cumulative approach matters: the court did not dismiss the incidents as trivial. Rather, having considered them together — the gunpoint incident, the assault on an employee, the child encounter, and the murder of a colleague — it still found the totality insufficient. The opinion underscores a practical point for adjudication: credibility alone, even when the IJ accepts it, does not convert threatening conduct and resulting fear into the legal category of persecution without additional indicia of severe or forcible harm.

## Nexus and motive: why political opinion didn’t carry the day
Jimenez sought asylum principally on political‑opinion grounds. The panel applied the familiar “one central reason” test from the statute and its precedents: a protected ground must be at least one central reason for the mistreatment. The court concluded the record supported the IJ’s (and BIA’s) finding that the attackers were primarily motivated by the property dispute and its threat to Don Rafa’s revenue and operations, not by the family’s political campaigning. The escalation of threats tracks the timing of Jimenez’s confrontation with the tenant and the property’s role as a criminal base; the initial politically flavored threats predated the escalation but lacked evidence tying them to the narco‑leader. In short, the court treated the political‑opinion theory as tangential to the principal motive as supported by the record.

This highlights another practical point: in mixed‑motive contexts courts will scrutinize whether protected activity was a central driver of the targeting, and a defendant’s non‑political interactions (professional disputes, economic threats, retaliation tied to criminal enterprise) can reframe harm as non‑protected.

## Government protection and relocation
On the government‑protection and internal relocation questions, the Tenth Circuit again hewed to established tests. The panel noted that applicants should report persecution to authorities unless doing so would be futile or dangerous, and that evidence of governmental attempts to stop persecutors (or systemic failure to do so) is weighted heavily. Jimenez had limited contact with police (and one patrol officer who advised them to leave), but the record also showed law‑enforcement activity against the property tied to Don Rafa. The court concluded the record did not establish that Colombian authorities were unwilling or unable to protect the family, and that safe relocation within Colombia was feasible — the family had safely stayed with relatives for several weeks and had relatives who remained in Colombia unharmed.

## How this sits with other circuits, and what it means doctrinally
The Tenth Circuit’s result is a reaffirmation of a relatively restrictive approach: fearful, repeated threats and significant emotional distress will not automatically qualify as persecution. Other courts have sometimes reached asylum‑granting outcomes in threat‑based claims where the threats were uniquely specific, sustained, accompanied by physical attacks or attempts at abduction, or where the record showed clear and systemic government inability or unwillingness to protect. Jimenez does not create new law on that score; rather, it reinforces the Tenth Circuit’s line that the threshold for “persecution” requires more than credible fear and harrowing incidents unless the totality of facts points unmistakably to severe, targeted harm or an absence of state protection.

Doctrinally, the decision sharpens two recurring difficulties for asylum claimants: the “persecution” threshold as a qualitative test (not a mere tally of threats), and the high bar the “one central reason” standard sets in mixed‑motive cases. Because the court applied the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s fact findings, Jimenez also illustrates how deferential review compounds the challenge for applicants seeking appellate reversal.

## Takeaways for practitioners
Jimenez should produce several practical lessons for counsel preparing threat‑based asylum claims in the Tenth Circuit (and, by persuasive force, beyond):

First, document objective indicia of severity and imminence: medical records, photographs, contemporaneous communications from persecutors, police reports, hospital admissions, forensic evidence, or physical restraints that show more than menacing words.

Second, establish motive clearly and early: when alleging persecution on account of political opinion, preserve evidence that the persecutor linked the victim’s political activity to the targeting — written threats referencing politics, public statements, or contemporaneous comments by attackers help bridge the nexus.

Third, show government incapacity or futility in seeking protection: if reporting to police was dangerous or obviously futile, develop evidence (official reports, news accounts, affidavits, patterns of complaints demonstrating systemic failure, or credible testimony about collusion) to make that case; a single discouraging officer’s comment is often insufficient.

Fourth, if internal relocation is genuinely infeasible, build a record on why moving within the country would not work — specific threats that travel, lack of family/funds, or geographic control by the persecutor can be decisive.

Finally, where threats are the core of the claim, seek corroboration that transforms psychological suffering into objective harm: documented panic attacks, psychiatric treatment records, school reports about a child’s safety, and third‑party corroboration, including evidence that others in the petitioner’s position were actually attacked or killed.

## Bottom line
Jimenez v. Bondi is not a novel departure but a clear restatement: in the Tenth Circuit, frightening and persistent threats — even at gunpoint and even including a frightening encounter with a child — do not automatically equal persecution. The ruling should push advocates to develop stronger, more concrete records of physical harm, targeted intent, or government failure when pressing threat‑based asylum claims, and it underscores how substantial‑evidence review constrains appellate relief where the IJ and BIA have accepted credibility but found the legal threshold unmet.



# Politics or Policing? Parsing Nexus and the ‘Snitch’ Motive in Mixed-Motive Asylum Claims

## Why Jimenez matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful, if sobering, lesson for asylum practitioners about how courts parse motive in mixed‑motive cases where private criminal actors target people who also have political identities. The opinion denies relief to a Colombian family that had campaigned for the Liberal Party and who received a series of threats, but whose situation escalated after the husband intervened in a property dispute tied to a local narco‑trafficker. The panel concluded that the persecutors’ explicit references to the family as “snitches for the prosecutor” and the timing of the escalation pointed to an anti‑informant/economic motive rather than persecution “on account of” political opinion. For advocates, the case underscores that timing, the persecutor’s own statements, and a fully developed record on alternative nexus theories and government protection can be dispositive.

## The court’s ruling, in brief

The panel affirmed the BIA and IJ, denying the petition for review under the substantial‑evidence standard. It held that the threats and psychological harms here did not rise to past persecution; that the persecutors’ dominant motive was to protect their criminal enterprise after a property dispute rather than to punish political activity; and that Petitioners had not shown the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them or that internal relocation was unreasonable. The court relied on settled Tenth Circuit precedent that threats alone (and non‑lasting psychological harms) rarely suffice as persecution, and that when a private persecutor has mixed motives the applicant must prove a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm.

## How timing and statements shaped the nexus analysis

At the heart of Jimenez is classic motive parsing. The family had earlier received politically tinged threats during their 2022 campaign work and a colleague’s murder; but the IJ and BIA—and the Tenth Circuit on review—emphasized that the concrete escalation of threats and the violent incidents came only after Jimenez intervened in a property matter involving “Don Rafa,” a local narco‑trafficker whose property had been subject to searches. The men who later accosted Jimenez explicitly accused him of being a “snitch for the prosecutor” and blamed him for searches and seizures; they also described an order coming from Don Rafa. The court treated those statements, together with the timing, as strong evidence that the persecutors’ primary motive was to neutralize a perceived informant who threatened their revenue and security, not to punish political opinion.

That approach tracks the circuit’s mixed‑motive precedents: courts accept that persecutors can have multiple motives, but protected grounds must be a central reason, not merely incidental or tangential. Jimenez therefore illustrates how a respondent’s earlier political activity can be blunted as a basis for asylum if the record shows a separate, contemporaneous motive that explains the conduct more directly.

## Government protection and internal relocation — a record problem

The decision also highlights the familiar evidentiary pitfalls in the government‑protection and relocation inquiries. Petitioners reported limited contact with police and did not press complaints beyond a couple of officers who gave unhelpful responses. The court relied on evidence that law enforcement had previously attempted searches of the implicated property and discounted the claim that police were broadly complicit. Under the court’s framework, a failure to exhaust or document efforts to obtain protection—or to show why reporting would have been futile or dangerous—can decisively weaken an asylum claim. Likewise, short, safe stays with family outside the immediate neighborhood and the fact that relatives remain unharmed weighed against the impossibility of internal relocation.

## What to take from Jimenez — practice implications

Jimenez furnishes several practical reminders for asylum counsel facing similar mixed‑motive fact patterns. First, preserve and develop alternative nexus theories early. If a persecutor’s rhetoric can be read as anti‑informant, counsel should simultaneously press a particular‑social‑group (PSG) theory based on “those perceived to be informants” or on political‑actor formulations (e.g., local Liberal Party activists), and fully brief the PSG elements. The Tenth Circuit here faulted Petitioners for not meaningfully advancing a PSG argument; that omission cost them a route to relief that might have fit these facts.

Second, contemporaneous documentation matters. Courts give weight to the sequence of events and to the persecutors’ own statements; capturing and preserving threat letters/brochures, contemporaneous complaints to police (with case numbers and dates), witness affidavits, phone records, and any on‑the‑ground reporting that ties earlier and later threats to the same actors can make the difference. Where applicants reasonably fear reporting, develop evidence to prove futility: prior complaints by others that were ignored, pattern evidence that law enforcement colludes with or fears criminals, or media and NGO reporting showing systemic failures.

Third, marshal country‑condition and expert evidence to show patterns in how criminal groups interact with politics in the relevant locality. In Jimenez the record included a factual note that the tenant was “politically aligned” with the president and that police attempted searches—facts the court found undercut a sweeping claim of state collusion. But in other contexts, credible evidence of paramilitary‑political alignment or documented targeting of party activists by criminal groups could support a political nexus or a claim that reporting would be futile.

Finally, think about strategy on relief claims beyond asylum. Because the asylum standard requires a preponderance showing and a protected‑ground nexus, thoroughly preserving a PSG and factual nexus, together with CAT or withholding claims (when appropriate), can provide alternative pathways or preserve issues for appeal.

## Doctrinal and downstream effects

Jimenez reinforces a pattern in the circuits: judges are reluctant to extend asylum protection where private actors have plausible non‑political motives and where the record lacks contemporaneous proof tying protected activity to the harm. That is defensible as a matter of administrative deference and the statutory text, but it also creates hard cases for activists and local civic actors whose political identities and other roles (for example, working with prosecutors or helping victims) are entangled. Expect removal hearings involving private criminals to turn on fine points of sequencing, available documentary proof, and the skill with which counsel frames alternate nexus theories and demonstrates futility of protection.

## Bottom line

For advocates, Jimenez is a cautionary tale about the evidentiary work that mixed‑motive cases demand. Courts will look for concrete links—timing, statements, repeatable patterns, and proof of state impotence or complicity—before finding a protected‑ground motive was central. Where a persecutor can plausibly be read as acting to protect a criminal enterprise, asylum seekers must be prepared to show why that reading is incomplete: preserve PSG arguments, collect contemporaneous evidence, document futile efforts to obtain protection, and develop country‑condition proof that places the individual’s case in a broader pattern.



# Reporting as Gatekeeping: The ‘Unable or Unwilling’ Test After Singh v. Bondi

## Why this case matters

Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a clear signal about how the Tenth Circuit will treat the “unable or unwilling” component of asylum law going forward. The court, citing and applying its recent framework from Singh v. Bondi, reaffirmed that an applicant’s efforts to put the government on notice and the government’s opportunity to respond are central to showing that domestic authorities cannot or will not protect a person from persecution. The practical effect is straightforward: absent persuasive evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, failure to seek state protection is a major obstacle to relief.

The underlying story is familiar—Colombian campaigners and a provisional lawyer who received threats and escalating intimidation after a property dispute with a local criminal leader known as “Don Rafa.” The immigration judge found the family credible but denied asylum and withholding of removal; the BIA affirmed; the Tenth Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Tymkovich, denied review. The court concluded the record did not show past persecution, did not establish political‑opinion nexus, and did not prove the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect the petitioners—or that internal relocation was infeasible.

## The court’s reasoning

The opinion is structured around familiar doctrinal waypoints: (1) past persecution; (2) nexus to a protected ground; (3) government protection (the “unable or unwilling” inquiry); and (4) internal relocation. Several points are worth underscoring for practitioners.

On past persecution the panel reiterated settled Tenth Circuit law that threats and psychological harm can be part of a persecution claim but that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” The court emphasized the petitioners’ concession that they suffered no lasting physical harm and relied on precedent holding that cumulative threats and stress, absent more, rarely meet the statutory standard. Even though the IJ credited the family’s testimony, credibility did not convert the cumulative threats and emotional distress here into legally cognizable persecution.

On nexus, the court applied the mixed‑motive test: a protected ground must be “at least one central reason” for the persecutor’s conduct. The record showed escalation only after Jimenez confronted the tenant—identified as a criminal leader—about the rented property. Threats that explicitly tied the family to disrupting that leader’s revenue stream and a man’s accusation that Jimenez was a “snitch for the prosecutor’s office” led the panel to conclude the predominant motive was the property dispute and the threat to the criminal enterprise, not political opinion. The court therefore found the petitioners’ political‑opinion theory “tangential and subordinate” to the primary motive.

The core of the opinion—and the source of the blog title—concerns government protection. Reiterating Singh, the court said two types of evidence carry special weight: whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors and whether the government offered protection to the applicant. Interwoven with those inquiries is whether the government had notice and an opportunity to respond; without notice, the court reasoned, there is no way to know how authorities would have acted. The petitioners here had limited contact with police—two discouraging roadside conversations and not much else—and did not meaningfully report the pattern of threats through available channels, despite having family and professional ties to the Colombian justice system. The record also showed police efforts to search the problematic property, which the court read as evidence the state was not complicit. The panel therefore concluded the petitioners failed to show the futility exception to the reporting rule and, on the record presented, could not demonstrate the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

Finally, on internal relocation the court noted family ties in other parts of Colombia, the family’s temporary relocation to the mother’s country home, and that the threats were neighborhood‑specific; together, these facts supported the conclusion that reasonable relocation within Colombia was available.

## An analytic perspective

The Tenth Circuit’s approach is defensible as a matter of administrative practicality: it prevents asylum adjudications from resting on speculative future harms when domestic authorities have not been given a chance to act. The “notice and opportunity to respond” principle respects state sovereignty and fits with the statute’s structure, which presumes a state is the primary protector of its residents.

But the doctrine operates as a form of procedural gatekeeping that can be difficult to surmount in situations where reporting is genuinely dangerous or when government collusion is deep but hard to document. The opinion recognizes the futility exception, but in practice a petitioner who fears making a report must produce fairly concrete evidence showing why reporting would be futile or unsafe—more than a single unhelpful officer or generalized fears. That burden can create a cruel choice for victims: risk a dangerous report or forego relief down the line.

Jimenez is illustrative: the record included a colleague who was reportedly kidnapped and murdered despite having reported threats, yet the court still found the petitioners’ own outreach inadequate to trigger the futility exception. That suggests future litigants will need to link such background incidents more directly to their own efforts and to systemic evidence showing police impotence or collusion.

## What this means for practitioners

The practical takeaways are immediate. If it is feasible and safe to do so, counsel should advise clients to report threats, document the report (police report numbers, dates, names of officers, copies of filings), and preserve contemporaneous evidence of those attempts. If reporting is genuinely unsafe or likely futile, counsel must build a robust record explaining why: contemporaneous statements from witnesses or victims who reported and suffered retaliation, repeated failures to investigate (no arrests, no follow‑up despite reports), press and NGO reporting of policing failures or collusion, and expert declarations on local law‑enforcement practices can all help show futility.

Beyond government reporting, counsel should develop the record on nexus (showing persecutors targeted political activity rather than private disputes), preserve and brief alternate grounds (e.g., particular social group claims), and collect corroborating documentary evidence—brochures, threatening messages, medical or psychological evaluations (while recognizing that emotional harm alone is unlikely to carry the day), and witness affidavits about threats and any governmental responses.

Finally, because the Tenth Circuit reviews BIA and IJ fact findings under the substantial‑evidence standard, litigators should focus on assembling a record that compels—not merely suggests—an opposite finding.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Jimenez cements the Tenth Circuit’s posture that reporting (or a contemporaneous, credibly explained avoidance of reporting) is central to the “unable or unwilling” analysis. The opinion will push asylum petitioners to front‑load evidence of attempts to obtain state protection or to front‑load a convincing futility narrative from the earliest stage of proceedings. Expect more reliance on country‑condition material and expert testimony to demonstrate systemic police failure or state complicity where appropriate.

The decision may also generate friction in cases where reporting is dangerous but documentation is thin; whether other circuits will adopt the same exacting posture, or whether this will produce circuit splits on how courts treat single‑officer apathy and the sufficiency of futility evidence, is an issue to watch.

## Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is not a novel rule‑making opinion, but it is a clarifying one. The Tenth Circuit doubled down on Singh’s emphasis that notice to authorities and evidence that the state failed to respond are central to the unable‑or‑unwilling inquiry. For practitioners, the ruling turns ordinary steps—filing a police report, getting and preserving the report number, documenting the government’s response—into critical evidentiary building blocks. Where those steps are impossible, counsel must be prepared to show, with specificity and corroboration, why reporting would have been dangerous or futile. The lesson is practical and stark: on the Tenth Circuit’s current course, reporting (or a persuasive explanation for not reporting) is indeed gatekeeping.



# Internal Relocation by Anecdote: Three Weeks in the Countryside and the Reasonableness Inquiry

## Why Jimenez v. Bondi matters

Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a useful, if modest, template for how the Tenth Circuit will treat internal-relocation arguments in asylum cases involving private persecutors. The opinion reinforces two practical points immigration practitioners already know: (1) when an applicant cannot establish past persecution or a protected-ground nexus, the feasibility of internal relocation often becomes dispositive; and (2) an applicant who offers only thin or “haphazard” briefing and limited documentary support faces an uphill climb under the BIA’s and the circuit’s deferential substantial‑evidence review. What makes Jimenez instructive is how the court treats short-term, on-the-ground indicia of safety—three weeks staying with a relative in the countryside, family members remaining in Colombia, and threats that told the family to “move out of the neighborhood” rather than “leave the country”—as persuasive evidence that internal relocation is reasonable.

Practitioners should read Jimenez as a reminder that the relocation inquiry is intensely fact-specific and that anecdotal facts, if uncontradicted and supported by other record evidence, can be fatal to an asylum claim unless the applicant builds a contemporaneous, granular record showing why relocation would be unreasonable or impossible.

## What the court did

The Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review after concluding that the Board of Immigration Appeals (and the immigration judge) had substantial evidence to find the petitioners had not suffered past persecution, had not shown a protected‑ground nexus, and had not demonstrated the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. Because petitioners could not satisfy those elements, the court also accepted the BIA’s alternative finding that internal relocation within Colombia was reasonable.

On the relocation point the opinion emphasizes several practical facts the IJ/BIA relied on: relatives and other family members still live in Colombia unharmed; Jimenez’s father still works in the criminal justice system; the family stayed safely with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before leaving for the United States; and the men who threatened the family had told them to leave the neighborhood rather than the country. The court also criticized the petitioners’ briefing as “haphazard” on arguments about administrative and economic burdens, judicial infrastructure, and alleged ties between the persecutors and the president—arguments the regulations specifically identify as relevant to a reasonableness analysis (see 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3)). Under the governing standard, the government may rebut a presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution by showing, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating.

## A closer look: should a three‑week, uneventful stay carry outsized weight?

Jimenez illustrates the tension at the heart of the relocation inquiry. On one hand, decision‑makers must be realistic. A short, documented episode of safety—especially when combined with evidence of family presence and active governmental efforts (evidence here that authorities had executed searches and seizures at the property associated with the persecutor)—permits a reasonable adjudicator to infer that the persecutor’s reach or motive is localized. The BIA and the Tenth Circuit are entitled to rely on such inferences under the substantial‑evidence standard.

On the other hand, treating a brief, temporary period of safety as dispositive risks flattening a complex, time‑dependent question: does a few weeks without incident show a sustainable, long‑term option to relocate safely? It may not, especially if the persecutor is a criminal organization with the capacity to pursue victims beyond a single neighborhood, or if relocation would meaningfully disrupt a family's ability to earn a living, obtain identity documents, enroll children in school, or access medical care. Jimenez does not reject those concerns; it simply finds them unproven here. In other words, the court’s approach is not doctrinally novel, but it underscores how thin evidence can be decisive when the other side of the ledger—robust proof of persecutor reach, state complicity, or the economic and administrative impossibility of moving—remains empty.

## What a winning relocation record looks like

Jimenez is a practical lesson for counsel: don’t rely on generalized assertions about “economic burdens” or “judicial infrastructure.” The regulation the court cited, 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3), contemplates a non‑exhaustive set of facts that may make relocation unreasonable. To preserve and win the argument an applicant should, where applicable, develop and submit record evidence addressing those concrete factors.

At a minimum, counsel should assemble: contemporaneous police reports or complaints showing attempts to seek protection (and any official responses); affidavits from family, neighbors, or employers describing the reach or identity of the persecutors and why relocation would not hide the applicant; country‑conditions or expert declarations explaining how the persecutor network operates across regions or how local institutions are incapable of providing protection; documentary proof of economic hardship (inability to obtain work, licensing, housing, or identity documents in another part of the country); medical or psychological records demonstrating special needs that impede relocation; and specific briefing tying those facts to the regulatory factors. If reporting to police would likely be futile or dangerous, that factual predicate should be spelled out and supported by record evidence. The absence of that detail is what the court labeled “haphazard” briefing—and it proved costly.

## Doctrinal and practical downstream effects

Jimenez reinforces the BIA’s latitude to assess relocation on a case‑by‑case basis and the difficulty of overturning such findings on appeal. Under substantial‑evidence review, anecdote backed by corroborating facts (family still in country, prior government action against the persecutor, a recorded temporary, safe stay) will often defeat generalized migration‑impossibility claims. That dynamic will likely produce predictable downstream effects: petitioners must be proactive in curating a textured administrative record; IJs and the BIA will feel justified in giving weight to pragmatic indicia of safety when there is no strong evidence to the contrary; and cases involving private persecutors will turn even more than before on detailed proof of persecutor reach and the real costs of internal migration.

There is also a policy angle. The court’s pragmatic approach makes sense in a system with limited fact‑finding resources: adjudicators must weigh what is actually in the record. But it underlines an asymmetry: applicants with counsel and access to experts can produce the evidence necessary to defeat a relocation defense, while less‑resourced applicants cannot. The opinion thereby reiterates an old—yet persistent—truth in asylum law: the quality of the administrative record often determines the outcome.

## Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is not a landmark changing asylum doctrine, but it is a clear reminder of how the reasonableness-of‑relocation inquiry plays out in practice. For advocates, the message is plain: when an applicant cannot show past persecution or state inability to protect, don’t rely on high‑level assertions about how relocation would be impossible. Build the record. Document attempts (and failures) to seek protection, assemble country‑conditions and expert evidence on persecutor reach and local institutional capacity, and brief the regulatory factors in 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3) with specificity. A short, peaceful stay in the countryside—three weeks in this case—may be enough to sink a claim unless the applicant can convincingly demonstrate why that snapshot does not reflect a viable, long‑term option to avoid persecution.
