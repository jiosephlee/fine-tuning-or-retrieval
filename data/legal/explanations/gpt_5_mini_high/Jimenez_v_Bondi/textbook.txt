Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Parties, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## Petitioners’ identities and background
The petitioners are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. They are natives and citizens of Colombia from Bogotá who owned and operated a furniture factory there before relocating to the United States in December 2022. At the time of the relevant events Jimenez was a law student with a provisional law license and was working as an intern for the Colombian National Prosecutor’s Office, assisting victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. Jimenez’s professional role figures prominently in the factual record because alleged persecutors repeatedly labeled him a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office.

## Political activity
The family were long‑time members of the Colombian Liberal Party (since 2012) and actively campaigned for Federico Gutiérrez in the 2022 presidential election. Their campaign work included door‑to‑door canvassing in local neighborhoods. Beginning around the end of May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls about his party involvement—threats that other local Liberal Party members and colleagues also received. In early June 2022, a colleague who had reported similar threats was kidnapped and murdered; despite that violence, Petitioners continued to campaign.

## Property‑dispute entanglement
In June 2022 a woman from San Bernardo asked Jimenez, in his capacity as a provisional lawyer, to help with a rent and possession dispute involving a tenant who had not paid rent for over a year. When Jimenez visited the property to negotiate, he discovered the tenant was the woman’s ex‑husband—identified in the record as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa.” The administrative record describes Don Rafa as a leader of a narco‑trafficking guerrilla group who was politically aligned with Colombia’s president. Jimenez also observed that a third floor of the building had been sealed by the health department and that the premises had been used as a day‑rental, a base for trafficking and prostitution, and had been associated with reports of kidnapping and murder. Law enforcement had attempted searches and seizures at the property; a local police officer told Jimenez to stay away and not to “look for trouble.”

## Escalating incidents
After the property visit the incidents against Petitioners escalated in both frequency and severity. Later in June 2022 Jimenez found a threatening brochure at the factory reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” The next month, while working at night, two men held Jimenez at gunpoint in the factory and ordered him to leave the neighborhood, accusing him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blaming him for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. About two months after the brochure threat those same men returned while the family was out of town, assaulted a factory employee, brandished a gun, demanded to know the petitioners’ whereabouts, and stated that the order came from Don Rafa. Days later a man grabbed Jimenez’s younger son as he left school, put his hand over the child’s mouth, and told him to run to his father; Jimenez intervened, pushed the man, and the child escaped. Following the school incident the family moved to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogotá countryside for three weeks, and there decided they would leave Colombia.

## Limited police contact and decision to flee
Petitioners made only limited contacts with police before leaving Colombia. They told a police patrol officer about the school incident and other threats; that officer responded that nothing could be done, said police officers were contributing to the problem, and advised the family to leave. Petitioners also reported conversations with two other local officers who told them to “stay away” from the property and avoid trouble. The record contains no evidence that Petitioners pursued formal complaints or sought protection beyond these neighborhood‑level contacts (and they did not press the family’s broader governmental contacts); shortly after the three‑week stay with Jimenez’s mother the family departed for the United States in December 2022.

## Removal proceedings
Three months after arriving in the United States without being admitted or paroled, the Department of Homeland Security served each petitioner with a Notice to Appear charging removability. In August 2023 Petitioners admitted the truth of DHS’s allegations and thus conceded removability; the immigration judge (IJ) accordingly directed removal to Colombia. Petitioners applied for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture; the two minor children proceeded as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application (i.e., their claims depended on his). At the merits hearing the IJ expressly found Petitioners credible but denied relief. The IJ concluded Petitioners failed to prove that the harm they suffered rose to past persecution, that it was on account of a statutorily protected ground, that internal relocation was unavailable, or that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The IJ also denied their CAT claim.

## BIA affirmance and issues preserved on appeal
A single member of the Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also rejected Petitioners’ due process arguments that the IJ had violated their right to counsel. On further review in this Court, Petitioners limited their challenge to the BIA’s denial of asylum and withholding of removal; they did not appeal the denial of CAT or the BIA’s resolution of their due process claim. Before the Tenth Circuit Petitioners argued that the BIA erred in finding (1) they had not suffered past persecution, (2) their mistreatment was not on account of their political opinion, and (3) they did not have a well‑founded fear of future persecution because the Colombian government is unable or unwilling to protect them. Because they conceded removability and the IJ found them credible, the appellate contest focused on the legal sufficiency of the IJ/BIA factual findings about persecution, nexus, and government protection in the administrative record.

# Appellate Scope, Burdens, and Governing Doctrines

This chapter explains how the Tenth Circuit framed and resolved the asylum and withholding claims in Jimenez v. Bondi. The court’s decision is organized around (1) the limits of its appellate review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, (2) the substantial-evidence standard that governs challenges to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), and (3) the substantive elements the BIA must find for asylum or withholding—past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, government involvement or inability/unwillingness to protect, future fear, and the feasibility of internal relocation. Read together, these procedural and substantive rules explain why the petitioners’ record of threats, limited reporting to authorities, and brief, localized relocation did not compel relief.

## Jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and record-based review

The Tenth Circuit opens by asserting its jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 to review a final order of removal that resolves collateral asylum claims. That statutory framework also limits appellate review to the administrative record on which the order of removal is based (see §1252(b)(4)(A)), a point the court reiterates through citation to Singh v. Bondi. Practically, this means the court evaluates only the evidence the immigration court and BIA had before them; it does not receive or consider new evidence. When an appeal raises only asylum-related questions, the court conducts a consolidated review of those claims within the bounds of the existing administrative record.

## Standard of review: substantial evidence and deference to the BIA (with reference to the IJ)

The Tenth Circuit applies the substantial-evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings. Under that standard, BIA findings are “conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” Thus the appellate court does not reweigh evidence or substitute its judgment for the agency’s; it asks whether the record, taken as a whole, would force a different factual conclusion. Where the BIA issues a short order that adopts or affirms an Immigration Judge’s (IJ’s) reasoning, the court routinely reviews the BIA’s decision but consults the IJ’s more detailed findings to discern the agency’s rationale (see Uanreroro). That structure was central in Jimenez: a single BIA member affirmed the IJ, and the Tenth Circuit deferred to the agency’s factual determinations so long as substantial evidence supported them.

## Asylum eligibility and the applicant’s burden

Asylum is discretionary, and an applicant bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she is a “refugee” as defined in the Immigration and Nationality Act. The statutory refugee definition requires that the applicant be outside the country of nationality and be “unable or unwilling” to return because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion (see §1101(a)(42)(A); §1158(b)(1)). The court in Jimenez quotes this statutory framework and treats asylum eligibility as a three-part factual inquiry when the applicant relies on past persecution: (1) whether the applicant suffered incidents rising to the level of persecution, (2) whether the persecution was on account of a protected ground, and (3) whether the government committed the persecution or is unable or unwilling to control the persecutors (see Rivera-Barrientos). These elements structure the agency’s fact-finding, and under appellate review the agency’s conclusions on each element are subject to substantial-evidence review.

## Past persecution: severity, cumulative effect, and the limits of threats/emotional harm

“Persecution” is not defined in the INA, so courts rely on precedent to set a threshold. The Tenth Circuit describes persecution as suffering or harm inflicted on those who differ in a protected characteristic in a way that is “regarded as offensive,” and it must be more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats to life or liberty (citing Wiransane). Importantly, the agency considers incidents cumulatively; a series of events may, taken together, amount to persecution even if single episodes would not (Ritonga). But the court repeatedly emphasizes that threats—standing alone—are generally insufficient to show persecution (Karki), and emotional or psychological harms without more rarely suffice.

In Jimenez the IJ found the petitioners credible but concluded the factual record—threatening phone calls, brochures, two armed confrontations at the factory, an assault on an employee, and an unsettling episode involving the couple’s child—did not add up to persecution. The Tenth Circuit sustained that conclusion under substantial-evidence review. The court stressed that petitioners conceded they had not suffered lasting physical injury, and that their psychological distress, although real, did not meet the court’s precedent for persecution. The lesson for practitioners and students is that credible testimony alone is not dispositive; severity and type of injury matter, and courts frequently decline asylum relief where the record shows threats and fear but no sustained physical injury or other indicia of persecution beyond the threshold.

## Nexus doctrine: “at least one central reason” and mixed-motive cases

A petitioner must show that the harm was inflicted “on account of” a protected ground. The Tenth Circuit applies the statutory test that the protected characteristic must be “at least one central reason” for the persecutor’s conduct (citing Rivera-Barrientos and the statutory language). Mixed motives are permitted, but the protected-ground motive cannot be incidental, tangential, or subordinate—if it plays a minor role, nexus is not satisfied (Dallakoti). Jimenez illustrates how the agency examines motive in the record: the petitioners argued their political campaigning was the reason they were targeted, but the BIA and IJ found that the threats and violence escalated only after the lead petitioner became involved in a property dispute with a local criminal leader (Don Rafa) and began interfering with that leader’s illicit operations. The record contained statements by the perpetrators accusing the petitioner of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and reference to the property conflict as the cause of retaliation. The Tenth Circuit therefore concluded that the political activity was at best tangential and that substantial evidence supported the agency’s finding that the persecutor’s predominant motive was retaliation for the petitioner’s personal and professional interference with criminal revenue—not a protected political opinion. The court refused to reweigh motives where the agency’s mixed‑motive finding was supported by the record.

## Government protection: notice/reporting, efforts to stop persecutors, and futility exceptions

A key asylum element is whether the government is unable or unwilling to protect the applicant from private persecutors. The Tenth Circuit cites Singh for the idea that two kinds of evidence weigh heavily in this analysis: whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and whether the government offered the applicant protection (for example, by placing the applicant in protective custody). Tied to that is whether the applicant gave the government notice—reporting the threats or attacks—so authorities had an opportunity to respond. Failure to report can undercut a claim because without notice it is impossible to assess how the police would have reacted, though the court recognizes a futility or danger exception where reporting would have been ineffective or would have exposed the applicant to greater risk (Aviles‑Gonzalez).

Applying these principles, the Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence that petitioners did little to seek help beyond two brief interactions with individual police officers who advised them to avoid trouble. The record also showed law enforcement had attempted searches at the criminal property, suggesting governmental awareness and action against the site associated with the persecutors. The court relied on precedent (including Osorio‑Morales and Chhetri) to explain that isolated instances of police apathy do not establish a systemic unwillingness to protect. Because the petitioners had available contacts and did not exhaust or document efforts to secure protection beyond their immediate neighborhood, the agency reasonably concluded the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to offer protection.

## Presumption of future fear from past persecution and how it may be rebutted

If an applicant establishes past persecution, courts treat that showing as giving rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution. The government can rebut that presumption by demonstrating either a fundamental change in country conditions or that the applicant could reasonably avoid future persecution by relocating internally (see Rivera‑Barrientos and 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)). In Jimenez the court first sustained the agency’s determination that there was no past persecution; hence no presumption arose. Even if a presumption had arisen, the government’s ability to point to internal relocation and evidence of state efforts at the criminal property would have been relevant to rebuttal.

## Internal relocation: feasibility and reasonableness

When the government attempts to rebut a well‑founded fear by showing a safe alternative within the applicant’s country, the agency must demonstrate by a preponderance that internal relocation is reasonable and feasible. The Tenth Circuit looks at practical factors: whether the applicant has friends or family elsewhere who can help, whether the applicant has safely lived elsewhere in the country, whether the persecutor’s reach extends nationally, and whether the relocation would pose undue administrative or economic hardship. In Jimenez the record showed family members remained in Colombia without incident, the petitioner’s father continued to work in the criminal justice system, and the family had safely stayed at the petitioner’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside for several weeks. The threats allegedly directed them to leave the neighborhood—not the country. On these facts the BIA reasonably concluded that internal relocation was a workable alternative to asylum, and the Tenth Circuit deferred to that finding.

## Withholding of removal and its higher standard

Withholding of removal requires a higher showing than asylum: the applicant must demonstrate a clear probability (more likely than not) of persecution on a protected ground if returned. Because that standard is stricter, a failure to meet the asylum requirements usually forecloses withholding as well. The Tenth Circuit therefore treated the denial of asylum as dispositive for withholding: once the BIA’s findings on past persecution, nexus, government protection, and relocation were sustained under substantial‑evidence review, the petitioners could not meet the higher withholding standard.

## How the court applied these doctrines to the Jimenez record: a short synthesis

Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful model of how appellate deference and the specific doctrinal tests interact. Procedurally, §1252 limits review to the administrative record and directs deference to the BIA’s factual findings unless the record would compel a contrary conclusion. Substantively, the court required petitioners to prove either past persecution or a well‑founded future fear on a protected ground, and to show that the state was unable or unwilling to protect them or that internal relocation was impossible. On the administrative record—repeated threats and two armed confrontations, but no lasting physical injury, limited reporting to police, evidence the persecutors were retaliating for a property dispute, and evidence of possible safe relocation—the agency’s negative findings were supported by substantial evidence. Accordingly, the Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review.

Takeaway for students: when reading asylum opinions, first identify the standard of appellate review and the scope of the record; then follow how the agency’s factual findings map onto the three statutory elements (persecution, nexus, government protection) and the alternative relief rules (presumption from past persecution, rebuttal via internal relocation, and the higher withholding standard). Jimenez exemplifies the importance of documenting severity, motive, reporting to authorities, and the infeasibility of internal relocation when an asylum claim rests largely on threats and psychological harm.

# Past Persecution: Threats and Psychological Harm Do Not Suffice Here

## Persecution concept: must entail more than restrictions or threats to life and liberty; cumulative assessment of incidents
The opinion begins this section by anchoring the analysis in the Tenth Circuit’s settled definition of “persecution.” Because the Immigration and Nationality Act does not define that term, the court relies on prior precedent: persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm that is “regarded as offensive” and is more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty. The court also reiterates the idea that incidents are evaluated cumulatively—individual episodes are not analyzed in isolation; the totality of mistreatment may together amount to persecution. At the same time, whether private actors’ conduct rises to persecution is a fact question, subject on appeal to the substantial‑evidence standard. Thus the court’s role is not to reweigh credibility but to decide whether the record compels a different factual conclusion than the BIA and IJ reached.

## Record evidence: multiple threats, armed confrontations, employee assault, school incident; petitioners’ concession of no lasting physical harm
The court then lays out the record facts it must assess under that standard. Petitioners were active in the Colombian Liberal Party and campaigned for a presidential candidate. Beginning in late May 2022, Jimenez received repeated threatening phone calls; a colleague who reported threats was later kidnapped and murdered. Jimenez became involved in a property dispute with a man identified as a local narco‑trafficker and leader known as “Don Rafa,” whose property had been the subject of police searches. The harassment escalated: a threatening brochure arrived at Petitioners’ factory; two men held Jimenez at gunpoint and told him to leave the neighborhood; those men later assaulted a factory employee, brandished a gun while demanding Petitioners’ whereabouts, and said the order came from Don Rafa; and a man accosted Jimenez’s younger son as he left school. Petitioners relocated temporarily to a relative’s home and ultimately left Colombia for the United States. Importantly for the legal analysis, Petitioners conceded at their merits hearing (and in briefing) that they suffered no physical injury and no lasting physical harm.

## Threats alone are insufficient; physical injury not required but harm must be severe and offensive
Applying the governing test, the court emphasizes two controlling points from Tenth Circuit precedent. First, threats by themselves generally do not amount to persecution. Second, while physical injury is not absolutely required to show persecution, the harm an applicant alleges must be sufficiently severe and offensive—more than routine harassment, sporadic threats, or ordinary criminal extortion. The court evaluates Petitioners’ incidents in aggregate but concludes that, even collectively, the record presents threats and menacing conduct that fall short of the degree of suffering the term “persecution” demands under circuit law. The IJ and BIA had found Petitioners credible, but credibility alone cannot convert episodic threats and intimidation into persecution if the objective level of harm does not meet the circuit’s threshold.

## Psychological and emotional effects (insomnia, panic attacks) considered but found inadequate without qualifying severity
Petitioners pressed their lasting emotional and psychological harm—Duarte’s insomnia and their son’s panic attacks—as evidence that the harassment produced persecution. The court acknowledges these effects and treats them as part of the cumulative picture, but it follows the line of authority holding that psychological or emotional distress coupled with threats, in the absence of sufficiently severe attendant conduct, rarely satisfies the persecution requirement. The opinion explicitly notes that the Tenth Circuit has “rarely, if ever,” granted asylum on the basis of threats plus emotional harm alone, and it finds that the present symptoms, while regrettable, do not raise the objective level of suffering required.

## Comparator cases illustrating higher‑severity fact patterns still found insufficient
To give context for the severity threshold, the court cites several circuit decisions in which more pronounced or prolonged mistreatment also failed to constitute persecution. Those precedents include cases involving arrest and detention with threats, repeated robberies and threats involving weapons, multiple telephone threats and a vague in‑person threat, and other sustained harassment—each of which the Tenth Circuit treated as falling short. By invoking those decisions, the court signals that Petitioners’ facts are not distinguishable in a way that would warrant a different outcome: counsel itself conceded at oral argument that the court had rejected asylum claims in even more extreme factual scenarios.

## Conclusion: substantial evidence supports BIA/IJ finding that aggregate harm does not constitute past persecution
Putting the legal standard and the record together under the substantial‑evidence review, the court concludes that the BIA’s and IJ’s factual determination—that Petitioners’ aggregate mistreatment does not rise to past persecution—is supported by the record and therefore must stand. Because the applicants failed to establish past persecution, they are not entitled to the rebuttable presumption of future persecution that such a showing creates, and their asylum claim founders at this first essential element. For students reading the opinion, the key lessons are (1) the distinction between credibility and sufficiency of harm, (2) the importance of objective severity for a persecution finding, and (3) how the court uses precedent to calibrate that severity threshold when reviewing cumulative threats and psychological harm.

# Nexus to a Protected Ground: Criminal Retaliation, Not Political Opinion

## Protected grounds asserted; particular social group forfeited
The petitioners in Jimenez v. Bondi advanced two routes to show asylum eligibility: persecution on account of political opinion and persecution as members of a particular social group (PSG). The Tenth Circuit, however, confined its review to the political‑opinion claim because the petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group.” In appellate practice, an argument that is not meaningfully briefed or developed is forfeited; the court therefore declined to consider the PSG theory and focused only on whether political opinion was at least one central reason for the harm.

## Legal standard for nexus to political opinion
To qualify for asylum based on political opinion, an applicant must show that the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. The court repeated the familiar Tenth Circuit gloss that the protected ground cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” to another motive. Mixed‑motive targeting is possible — a persecutor can have multiple motives — but when motives are mixed the protected ground must still be a central reason for the harm. The asylum applicant bears the burden to prove this element, and appellate review of the BIA’s factual determinations is deferential under the substantial‑evidence standard.

## Temporal and causal sequence: escalation after the property dispute
A key factual thread in the opinion is timing. Petitioners had political activity long before the critical escalation: they were Liberal Party members who campaigned in 2022 and received threatening phone calls beginning around late May. Crucially, however, the more violent and specific targeting began only after Jimenez intervened in a June property dispute involving the tenant identified as Rafael “Don Rafa,” a local leader tied to narco‑trafficking and prostitution. The sequence in the record—initial, more generalized political threats, then Jimenez’s investigation of the property, then intensified threats and direct confrontations—permitted the BIA and the court to infer a causal connection between Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s enterprise and the later, escalated retaliatory conduct.

## Perpetrators’ statements as evidence of motive
The adjudicative weight of contemporaneous statements by the attackers figures prominently in the court’s analysis. On several occasions the men accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches and seizures at the property; they also identified Don Rafa as the source of the threats. Those statements directly tie the motivation for the attackers’ conduct to Jimenez’s professional and private interference with a criminal operation, not to his political campaigning. In asylum adjudications, such admissions or allegations by perpetrators are probative of motive because they reveal why the victim was targeted; here the BIA permissibly treated those statements as supporting a non‑political, criminal‑retaliation motive.

## Lack of evidence linking early political threats to Don Rafa; continued campaigning
The opinion also stresses that there is no record evidence showing Don Rafa instigated the earlier politically framed threats. Petitioners continued to campaign after the initial threats and after a colleague’s murder, which undercuts an inference that their political activity was the driving cause of the later, more direct attacks. The BIA reasonably concluded that because the escalation coincided with Jimenez’s involvement in the property matter and because Petitioners produced no evidence tying Don Rafa to the earlier political threats, the political‑opinion explanation was weak compared to the criminal‑retaliation explanation.

## Mixed‑motive framework applied to these facts
Applying the mixed‑motive framework, the court acknowledged that an attacker might have several motivations, but reiterated the governing principle that protected grounds must be a central reason for persecution. Given the timing, the attackers’ own statements, and the absence of evidence connecting Don Rafa to the earlier political threats, the BIA’s conclusion that the predominant motive was retaliation for interference with an illicit enterprise — and not Petitioners’ political opinion — was supported by substantial evidence. Because appellate review does not permit reweighing the evidence, the court refused to overturn that factual finding.

## Holding on nexus and its consequences
The Tenth Circuit held that petitioners failed to establish the required nexus between their political opinion and the harm they suffered. Substantial evidence supported the BIA’s finding that criminal retaliation for Jimenez’s involvement with Don Rafa’s property was the predominant motive and that any political motive was merely tangential. That failure of proof on the nexus element doomed both the asylum claim and the higher‑standard withholding claim that depends on the same nexus showing.

## Practical takeaways for litigating nexus claims
Jimenez emphasizes several practical points for asylum advocacy. First, preserve and brief every theory you rely upon (e.g., particular social group) and establish membership and contours in the record; otherwise the argument can be forfeited. Second, establish temporal and causal connections: show why the persecutor’s motive ties to the protected ground rather than to a personal, criminal, or economic motive. Third, collect and highlight contemporaneous statements by perpetrators, and documentary evidence that links early threats to identified actors when arguing a political nexus. Finally, remember that the BIA and courts give weight to the sequence of events and direct statements of attackers, and that appellate courts will sustain credibility and nexus findings as long as substantial evidence supports them.

# Government Protection, Internal Relocation, and Disposition

This chapter explains how the Tenth Circuit applied the “unable or unwilling” government‑protection inquiry and the internal‑relocation framework in Jimenez v. Bondi, and why those conclusions compelled denial of the petition for review. The court viewed the protection and relocation questions through the settled lenses announced in Singh and Rivera‑Barrientos: (1) whether the government had notice and an opportunity to respond and (2) whether an applicant could reasonably avoid future persecution by relocating inside the country. Under the substantial‑evidence standard the court deferred to the BIA’s factual findings because the record here supported the BIA’s conclusion that the Colombian government was not unable or unwilling to protect Petitioners and that safe internal relocation was feasible. Because Petitioners could not show a well‑founded fear of future persecution for asylum purposes, the court also held that withholding of removal necessarily failed.

## Reporting and notice: minimal outreach

A central factual predicate for finding a government unwilling or unable to protect an asylum applicant is whether the government had notice of the persecution and a realistic opportunity to respond. The court relied on Singh’s articulation that two types of evidence weigh heavily in this inquiry: whether authorities stopped or tried to stop the persecutors and whether the government offered protective measures (for example, protective custody). Intertwined with those inquiries is whether the applicant reported the persecution. As the opinion emphasizes, without reporting there is no way to know how authorities would have reacted.

Applying those principles, the court noted that Petitioners’ outreach to Colombian authorities was minimal. Jimenez testified that he told a police patrol officer about the incident involving his son and that he and his wife spoke to two individual officers who advised them not to “seek trouble” and to leave. But they did not file formal complaints or seek protection beyond those limited conversations, nor did they exhaust other available channels—even though Jimenez and his father had government or law‑enforcement connections. The IJ and BIA reasonably treated that absence of formal reporting and escalation as probative against the claim that the government was unable or unwilling to intervene.

## Isolated officer comments versus systemic failure

The court drew a critical distinction between isolated, unhelpful comments by individual officers and evidence of systemic inability or unwillingness by the state to protect its citizens. Relying on Osorio‑Morales as authority, the court explained that a one‑off conversation with an apathetic or discouraging officer does not necessarily show a government‑wide failure. To prove inability or unwillingness a petitioner ordinarily needs something more concrete than an offhand remark—evidence of systemic dysfunction, a pattern of official collusion, or multiple official refusals to act.

In Jimenez, the record contained only the officers’ discouraging responses and no evidence that those responses reflected official policy or widespread government inaction. The court therefore gave those isolated comments limited probative value and concluded they did not overcome the presumption that the state could and would protect Petitioners if given formal notice and an opportunity to act.

## Countervailing evidence of enforcement: searches and seizures

The record contained affirmative evidence that Colombian authorities had investigated the very property at the center of Petitioners’ danger: the premises had been sealed by the health department, and law‑enforcement officers had attempted several searches and seizures related to narcotrafficking, prostitution, and reports of kidnapping and murder. The court treated that enforcement activity as countervailing evidence undermining Petitioners’ thesis that the persecutors were effectively protected by, or colluding with, the government.

That enforcement history mattered for two related reasons. First, it showed the government knew about ongoing criminal activity tied to the persecutor and had taken at least some steps to address it. Second, it undercut Petitioners’ claim that contacting authorities would have been futile because the persecutor and state were aligned. On the record, the court found substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion that government agents were not simply passive instruments of the persecutors.

## The futility/danger exception: not established here

The law recognizes a futility or danger exception to the reporting requirement: an applicant need not report persecution if doing so would have been futile or would have exposed the applicant to greater danger. The Jimenez opinion acknowledged that exception, but required evidentiary support for it. The court found Petitioners’ assertion of futility to be conclusory and unsupported. Petitioners argued generally that the persecutors were closely aligned with the government, but the record showed active government attention to the property and contained no clear evidence of pervasive or systemic collusion.

The court also relied on precedent (including Chhetri) in observing that a mere belief that persecutors are “part of the government” is insufficient when the record lacks corroboration. Here, Petitioners had available avenues to report (including professional contacts) and did not show that using them would have been either futile or unduly dangerous. On that basis, the futility exception did not excuse the lack of formal reporting.

## Internal relocation indicators: feasibility and reasonableness

The government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution by showing by a preponderance of the evidence that relocation within the applicant’s country would avoid the predicted harm. Rivera‑Barrientos and the governing regulation frame this as an alternative means of avoiding persecution (short of protection by foreign authorities).

The court identified several record facts supporting the BIA’s conclusion that internal relocation to another part of Colombia was reasonable for Petitioners. Family members and relatives remained in Colombia unharmed; Jimenez’s father continued to work in the criminal‑justice system; the family had safely stayed with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks; and the men who threatened Petitioners told them only to leave the neighborhood, not the country. Although Petitioners claimed that relocation would be administratively or economically burdensome and argued in a conclusory way that persecutors had ties to the president, the court observed that those arguments were haphazardly briefed and were not supported by the record. Accordingly, the court concluded relocation was a feasible safety option.

## Alternative holding: relocation rebuts future‑fear even if some fear exists

Even setting aside Petitioners’ inability to show past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground, the court addressed relocation as an alternative ground for denying asylum. It explained that, had the court assumed Petitioners had a well‑founded fear, the government still carried its burden to show that Petitioners could avoid future persecution by reasonably relocating. The combination of family ties in Colombia, the safe temporary stay in the countryside, and the lack of evidence that threats would follow them elsewhere sufficed as substantial evidence that internal relocation was a viable option.

## Disposition: denial of the petition and the withholding consequence

The court denied the petition for review. Because Petitioners failed to establish the elements necessary for asylum—both the government‑protection prong and internal‑relocation prong—their asylum claim failed under the preponderance standard. The court then noted the corollary for withholding of removal: withholding requires the higher “more likely than not” standard, and an applicant who cannot meet asylum’s threshold a fortiori cannot satisfy withholding’s heightened burden. Citing Carias‑Mejia, the court therefore affirmed denial of withholding as well.

## Practice takeaways

Jimenez illustrates several practical lessons for counsel preparing asylum claims that rest in whole or part on a claim of inadequate government protection or impossibility of internal relocation. First, document any and all reports to authorities: file formal police reports when possible, retain copies, and create contemporaneous records of who was contacted, when, and what was said. Second, if reporting is purportedly futile or dangerous, develop concrete evidence to support that claim—multiple instances of official collusion, corroborating country‑condition reports, affidavits showing a pattern of government inaction, or credible threats that reporting would invite immediate harm. Third, when the government has taken any enforcement action against the persecutors, gather those documents (police reports, health‑department orders, seizure records) because they can be dispositive in rebutting a claim of state collusion. Fourth, for internal‑relocation disputes, produce evidence explaining why relocation is impracticable (economic obstacles, administrative barriers, proof that threats are nationwide or that persecutors have reach beyond a locality); absent that showing, courts will often find relocation reasonable where family ties and prior safe stays exist. Finally, when a claim depends on political‑motivation nexus or severity of harm, assemble corroborating evidence to show the mistreatment was more than threats and psychological stress; courts have repeatedly held threats plus stress alone are often insufficient to establish persecution.

In short, Jimenez underscores that claims about government inability or unwillingness and about the infeasibility of internal relocation turn on the record. Concrete, contemporaneous evidence of reporting, systemic official failure, or the impracticability of relocation is crucial to prevail under the substantial‑evidence standard that governs judicial review.
