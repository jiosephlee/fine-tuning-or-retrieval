Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Reporting as Gatekeeping: The ‘Unable or Unwilling’ Test After Singh v. Bondi

## Why this case matters

Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a clear signal about how the Tenth Circuit will treat the “unable or unwilling” component of asylum law going forward. The court, citing and applying its recent framework from Singh v. Bondi, reaffirmed that an applicant’s efforts to put the government on notice and the government’s opportunity to respond are central to showing that domestic authorities cannot or will not protect a person from persecution. The practical effect is straightforward: absent persuasive evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, failure to seek state protection is a major obstacle to relief.

The underlying story is familiar—Colombian campaigners and a provisional lawyer who received threats and escalating intimidation after a property dispute with a local criminal leader known as “Don Rafa.” The immigration judge found the family credible but denied asylum and withholding of removal; the BIA affirmed; the Tenth Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Tymkovich, denied review. The court concluded the record did not show past persecution, did not establish political‑opinion nexus, and did not prove the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect the petitioners—or that internal relocation was infeasible.

## The court’s reasoning

The opinion is structured around familiar doctrinal waypoints: (1) past persecution; (2) nexus to a protected ground; (3) government protection (the “unable or unwilling” inquiry); and (4) internal relocation. Several points are worth underscoring for practitioners.

On past persecution the panel reiterated settled Tenth Circuit law that threats and psychological harm can be part of a persecution claim but that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” The court emphasized the petitioners’ concession that they suffered no lasting physical harm and relied on precedent holding that cumulative threats and stress, absent more, rarely meet the statutory standard. Even though the IJ credited the family’s testimony, credibility did not convert the cumulative threats and emotional distress here into legally cognizable persecution.

On nexus, the court applied the mixed‑motive test: a protected ground must be “at least one central reason” for the persecutor’s conduct. The record showed escalation only after Jimenez confronted the tenant—identified as a criminal leader—about the rented property. Threats that explicitly tied the family to disrupting that leader’s revenue stream and a man’s accusation that Jimenez was a “snitch for the prosecutor’s office” led the panel to conclude the predominant motive was the property dispute and the threat to the criminal enterprise, not political opinion. The court therefore found the petitioners’ political‑opinion theory “tangential and subordinate” to the primary motive.

The core of the opinion—and the source of the blog title—concerns government protection. Reiterating Singh, the court said two types of evidence carry special weight: whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors and whether the government offered protection to the applicant. Interwoven with those inquiries is whether the government had notice and an opportunity to respond; without notice, the court reasoned, there is no way to know how authorities would have acted. The petitioners here had limited contact with police—two discouraging roadside conversations and not much else—and did not meaningfully report the pattern of threats through available channels, despite having family and professional ties to the Colombian justice system. The record also showed police efforts to search the problematic property, which the court read as evidence the state was not complicit. The panel therefore concluded the petitioners failed to show the futility exception to the reporting rule and, on the record presented, could not demonstrate the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

Finally, on internal relocation the court noted family ties in other parts of Colombia, the family’s temporary relocation to the mother’s country home, and that the threats were neighborhood‑specific; together, these facts supported the conclusion that reasonable relocation within Colombia was available.

## An analytic perspective

The Tenth Circuit’s approach is defensible as a matter of administrative practicality: it prevents asylum adjudications from resting on speculative future harms when domestic authorities have not been given a chance to act. The “notice and opportunity to respond” principle respects state sovereignty and fits with the statute’s structure, which presumes a state is the primary protector of its residents.

But the doctrine operates as a form of procedural gatekeeping that can be difficult to surmount in situations where reporting is genuinely dangerous or when government collusion is deep but hard to document. The opinion recognizes the futility exception, but in practice a petitioner who fears making a report must produce fairly concrete evidence showing why reporting would be futile or unsafe—more than a single unhelpful officer or generalized fears. That burden can create a cruel choice for victims: risk a dangerous report or forego relief down the line.

Jimenez is illustrative: the record included a colleague who was reportedly kidnapped and murdered despite having reported threats, yet the court still found the petitioners’ own outreach inadequate to trigger the futility exception. That suggests future litigants will need to link such background incidents more directly to their own efforts and to systemic evidence showing police impotence or collusion.

## What this means for practitioners

The practical takeaways are immediate. If it is feasible and safe to do so, counsel should advise clients to report threats, document the report (police report numbers, dates, names of officers, copies of filings), and preserve contemporaneous evidence of those attempts. If reporting is genuinely unsafe or likely futile, counsel must build a robust record explaining why: contemporaneous statements from witnesses or victims who reported and suffered retaliation, repeated failures to investigate (no arrests, no follow‑up despite reports), press and NGO reporting of policing failures or collusion, and expert declarations on local law‑enforcement practices can all help show futility.

Beyond government reporting, counsel should develop the record on nexus (showing persecutors targeted political activity rather than private disputes), preserve and brief alternate grounds (e.g., particular social group claims), and collect corroborating documentary evidence—brochures, threatening messages, medical or psychological evaluations (while recognizing that emotional harm alone is unlikely to carry the day), and witness affidavits about threats and any governmental responses.

Finally, because the Tenth Circuit reviews BIA and IJ fact findings under the substantial‑evidence standard, litigators should focus on assembling a record that compels—not merely suggests—an opposite finding.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Jimenez cements the Tenth Circuit’s posture that reporting (or a contemporaneous, credibly explained avoidance of reporting) is central to the “unable or unwilling” analysis. The opinion will push asylum petitioners to front‑load evidence of attempts to obtain state protection or to front‑load a convincing futility narrative from the earliest stage of proceedings. Expect more reliance on country‑condition material and expert testimony to demonstrate systemic police failure or state complicity where appropriate.

The decision may also generate friction in cases where reporting is dangerous but documentation is thin; whether other circuits will adopt the same exacting posture, or whether this will produce circuit splits on how courts treat single‑officer apathy and the sufficiency of futility evidence, is an issue to watch.

## Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is not a novel rule‑making opinion, but it is a clarifying one. The Tenth Circuit doubled down on Singh’s emphasis that notice to authorities and evidence that the state failed to respond are central to the unable‑or‑unwilling inquiry. For practitioners, the ruling turns ordinary steps—filing a police report, getting and preserving the report number, documenting the government’s response—into critical evidentiary building blocks. Where those steps are impossible, counsel must be prepared to show, with specificity and corroboration, why reporting would have been dangerous or futile. The lesson is practical and stark: on the Tenth Circuit’s current course, reporting (or a persuasive explanation for not reporting) is indeed gatekeeping.
