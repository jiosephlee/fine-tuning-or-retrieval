Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Do Threats Become ‘Persecution’? The Tenth Circuit Draws a Hard Line in Jimenez v. Bondi

## Why this case matters
Threats, intimidation, and psychological harm are the central facts in a great many asylum claims — especially those arising from gang or organized-crime violence. In Jimenez v. Bondi, the Tenth Circuit again drew a clear line: repeated, serious threats (including a gunpoint confrontation, menacing follow-ups at a workplace, an assault on an employee seeking the family, and a frightening encounter with a child at school) plus psychological trauma did not, on this record, meet the statutory standard for past persecution. For practitioners and adjudicators, the decision is a useful reminder of two durable themes in the Tenth Circuit’s asylum jurisprudence: (1) threats and fear, even when cumulative and frightening, often fall short of “persecution” unless accompanied by greater objective harm or restraints; and (2) the combination of substantial-evidence review and a court’s acceptance of IJ credibility findings makes reversal a steep climb absent a record that clearly compels a contrary finding.

## The facts in brief
Wilson Jimenez and his family are Colombian nationals who had been active in local politics with the Colombian Liberal Party and who, as a law intern, intervened in a property dispute that implicated a narco-trafficking leader known as “Don Rafa.” In 2022 they received repeated threats: threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, a gunpoint confrontation telling Jimenez to leave the neighborhood, an assault on a factory employee who was asked where the family was, and a frightening school‑yard encounter in which a man placed his hand over the couple’s younger son’s mouth and urged him to “run to your dad.” The family briefly relocated within Colombia, reported an incident to a police patrol officer who told them to leave and that police could do nothing, and ultimately fled to the United States. An IJ found the family credible but denied asylum and withholding; the BIA affirmed; a Tenth Circuit panel denied review.

## How the court treated “past persecution”
The Tenth Circuit’s analysis rests on settled (and oft-repeated) points: asylum applicants must show persecution that is more than “restrictions or threats to life and liberty,” and courts assess incidents cumulatively, not in isolation. The opinion relied on prior Tenth Circuit authorities such as Wiransane and Karki to state that threats alone are generally insufficient. Although the panel acknowledged the seriousness of the Jimenez family’s experiences and the psychological harm they described, it concluded — consistently with earlier Tenth Circuit decisions — that the record did not show the sort of physical infliction, sustained severe abuse, or objective restraints on liberty necessary to classify the harms as persecution. The court expressly noted the absence of lasting physical injury and emphasized precedent refusing asylum where frightening or stressful incidents did not reach the statutory threshold.

That cumulative approach matters: the court did not dismiss the incidents as trivial. Rather, having considered them together — the gunpoint incident, the assault on an employee, the child encounter, and the murder of a colleague — it still found the totality insufficient. The opinion underscores a practical point for adjudication: credibility alone, even when the IJ accepts it, does not convert threatening conduct and resulting fear into the legal category of persecution without additional indicia of severe or forcible harm.

## Nexus and motive: why political opinion didn’t carry the day
Jimenez sought asylum principally on political‑opinion grounds. The panel applied the familiar “one central reason” test from the statute and its precedents: a protected ground must be at least one central reason for the mistreatment. The court concluded the record supported the IJ’s (and BIA’s) finding that the attackers were primarily motivated by the property dispute and its threat to Don Rafa’s revenue and operations, not by the family’s political campaigning. The escalation of threats tracks the timing of Jimenez’s confrontation with the tenant and the property’s role as a criminal base; the initial politically flavored threats predated the escalation but lacked evidence tying them to the narco‑leader. In short, the court treated the political‑opinion theory as tangential to the principal motive as supported by the record.

This highlights another practical point: in mixed‑motive contexts courts will scrutinize whether protected activity was a central driver of the targeting, and a defendant’s non‑political interactions (professional disputes, economic threats, retaliation tied to criminal enterprise) can reframe harm as non‑protected.

## Government protection and relocation
On the government‑protection and internal relocation questions, the Tenth Circuit again hewed to established tests. The panel noted that applicants should report persecution to authorities unless doing so would be futile or dangerous, and that evidence of governmental attempts to stop persecutors (or systemic failure to do so) is weighted heavily. Jimenez had limited contact with police (and one patrol officer who advised them to leave), but the record also showed law‑enforcement activity against the property tied to Don Rafa. The court concluded the record did not establish that Colombian authorities were unwilling or unable to protect the family, and that safe relocation within Colombia was feasible — the family had safely stayed with relatives for several weeks and had relatives who remained in Colombia unharmed.

## How this sits with other circuits, and what it means doctrinally
The Tenth Circuit’s result is a reaffirmation of a relatively restrictive approach: fearful, repeated threats and significant emotional distress will not automatically qualify as persecution. Other courts have sometimes reached asylum‑granting outcomes in threat‑based claims where the threats were uniquely specific, sustained, accompanied by physical attacks or attempts at abduction, or where the record showed clear and systemic government inability or unwillingness to protect. Jimenez does not create new law on that score; rather, it reinforces the Tenth Circuit’s line that the threshold for “persecution” requires more than credible fear and harrowing incidents unless the totality of facts points unmistakably to severe, targeted harm or an absence of state protection.

Doctrinally, the decision sharpens two recurring difficulties for asylum claimants: the “persecution” threshold as a qualitative test (not a mere tally of threats), and the high bar the “one central reason” standard sets in mixed‑motive cases. Because the court applied the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s fact findings, Jimenez also illustrates how deferential review compounds the challenge for applicants seeking appellate reversal.

## Takeaways for practitioners
Jimenez should produce several practical lessons for counsel preparing threat‑based asylum claims in the Tenth Circuit (and, by persuasive force, beyond):

First, document objective indicia of severity and imminence: medical records, photographs, contemporaneous communications from persecutors, police reports, hospital admissions, forensic evidence, or physical restraints that show more than menacing words.

Second, establish motive clearly and early: when alleging persecution on account of political opinion, preserve evidence that the persecutor linked the victim’s political activity to the targeting — written threats referencing politics, public statements, or contemporaneous comments by attackers help bridge the nexus.

Third, show government incapacity or futility in seeking protection: if reporting to police was dangerous or obviously futile, develop evidence (official reports, news accounts, affidavits, patterns of complaints demonstrating systemic failure, or credible testimony about collusion) to make that case; a single discouraging officer’s comment is often insufficient.

Fourth, if internal relocation is genuinely infeasible, build a record on why moving within the country would not work — specific threats that travel, lack of family/funds, or geographic control by the persecutor can be decisive.

Finally, where threats are the core of the claim, seek corroboration that transforms psychological suffering into objective harm: documented panic attacks, psychiatric treatment records, school reports about a child’s safety, and third‑party corroboration, including evidence that others in the petitioner’s position were actually attacked or killed.

## Bottom line
Jimenez v. Bondi is not a novel departure but a clear restatement: in the Tenth Circuit, frightening and persistent threats — even at gunpoint and even including a frightening encounter with a child — do not automatically equal persecution. The ruling should push advocates to develop stronger, more concrete records of physical harm, targeted intent, or government failure when pressing threat‑based asylum claims, and it underscores how substantial‑evidence review constrains appellate relief where the IJ and BIA have accepted credibility but found the legal threshold unmet.
