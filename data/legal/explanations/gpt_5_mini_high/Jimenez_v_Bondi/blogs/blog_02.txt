Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Politics or Policing? Parsing Nexus and the ‘Snitch’ Motive in Mixed-Motive Asylum Claims

## Why Jimenez matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful, if sobering, lesson for asylum practitioners about how courts parse motive in mixed‑motive cases where private criminal actors target people who also have political identities. The opinion denies relief to a Colombian family that had campaigned for the Liberal Party and who received a series of threats, but whose situation escalated after the husband intervened in a property dispute tied to a local narco‑trafficker. The panel concluded that the persecutors’ explicit references to the family as “snitches for the prosecutor” and the timing of the escalation pointed to an anti‑informant/economic motive rather than persecution “on account of” political opinion. For advocates, the case underscores that timing, the persecutor’s own statements, and a fully developed record on alternative nexus theories and government protection can be dispositive.

## The court’s ruling, in brief

The panel affirmed the BIA and IJ, denying the petition for review under the substantial‑evidence standard. It held that the threats and psychological harms here did not rise to past persecution; that the persecutors’ dominant motive was to protect their criminal enterprise after a property dispute rather than to punish political activity; and that Petitioners had not shown the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them or that internal relocation was unreasonable. The court relied on settled Tenth Circuit precedent that threats alone (and non‑lasting psychological harms) rarely suffice as persecution, and that when a private persecutor has mixed motives the applicant must prove a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm.

## How timing and statements shaped the nexus analysis

At the heart of Jimenez is classic motive parsing. The family had earlier received politically tinged threats during their 2022 campaign work and a colleague’s murder; but the IJ and BIA—and the Tenth Circuit on review—emphasized that the concrete escalation of threats and the violent incidents came only after Jimenez intervened in a property matter involving “Don Rafa,” a local narco‑trafficker whose property had been subject to searches. The men who later accosted Jimenez explicitly accused him of being a “snitch for the prosecutor” and blamed him for searches and seizures; they also described an order coming from Don Rafa. The court treated those statements, together with the timing, as strong evidence that the persecutors’ primary motive was to neutralize a perceived informant who threatened their revenue and security, not to punish political opinion.

That approach tracks the circuit’s mixed‑motive precedents: courts accept that persecutors can have multiple motives, but protected grounds must be a central reason, not merely incidental or tangential. Jimenez therefore illustrates how a respondent’s earlier political activity can be blunted as a basis for asylum if the record shows a separate, contemporaneous motive that explains the conduct more directly.

## Government protection and internal relocation — a record problem

The decision also highlights the familiar evidentiary pitfalls in the government‑protection and relocation inquiries. Petitioners reported limited contact with police and did not press complaints beyond a couple of officers who gave unhelpful responses. The court relied on evidence that law enforcement had previously attempted searches of the implicated property and discounted the claim that police were broadly complicit. Under the court’s framework, a failure to exhaust or document efforts to obtain protection—or to show why reporting would have been futile or dangerous—can decisively weaken an asylum claim. Likewise, short, safe stays with family outside the immediate neighborhood and the fact that relatives remain unharmed weighed against the impossibility of internal relocation.

## What to take from Jimenez — practice implications

Jimenez furnishes several practical reminders for asylum counsel facing similar mixed‑motive fact patterns. First, preserve and develop alternative nexus theories early. If a persecutor’s rhetoric can be read as anti‑informant, counsel should simultaneously press a particular‑social‑group (PSG) theory based on “those perceived to be informants” or on political‑actor formulations (e.g., local Liberal Party activists), and fully brief the PSG elements. The Tenth Circuit here faulted Petitioners for not meaningfully advancing a PSG argument; that omission cost them a route to relief that might have fit these facts.

Second, contemporaneous documentation matters. Courts give weight to the sequence of events and to the persecutors’ own statements; capturing and preserving threat letters/brochures, contemporaneous complaints to police (with case numbers and dates), witness affidavits, phone records, and any on‑the‑ground reporting that ties earlier and later threats to the same actors can make the difference. Where applicants reasonably fear reporting, develop evidence to prove futility: prior complaints by others that were ignored, pattern evidence that law enforcement colludes with or fears criminals, or media and NGO reporting showing systemic failures.

Third, marshal country‑condition and expert evidence to show patterns in how criminal groups interact with politics in the relevant locality. In Jimenez the record included a factual note that the tenant was “politically aligned” with the president and that police attempted searches—facts the court found undercut a sweeping claim of state collusion. But in other contexts, credible evidence of paramilitary‑political alignment or documented targeting of party activists by criminal groups could support a political nexus or a claim that reporting would be futile.

Finally, think about strategy on relief claims beyond asylum. Because the asylum standard requires a preponderance showing and a protected‑ground nexus, thoroughly preserving a PSG and factual nexus, together with CAT or withholding claims (when appropriate), can provide alternative pathways or preserve issues for appeal.

## Doctrinal and downstream effects

Jimenez reinforces a pattern in the circuits: judges are reluctant to extend asylum protection where private actors have plausible non‑political motives and where the record lacks contemporaneous proof tying protected activity to the harm. That is defensible as a matter of administrative deference and the statutory text, but it also creates hard cases for activists and local civic actors whose political identities and other roles (for example, working with prosecutors or helping victims) are entangled. Expect removal hearings involving private criminals to turn on fine points of sequencing, available documentary proof, and the skill with which counsel frames alternate nexus theories and demonstrates futility of protection.

## Bottom line

For advocates, Jimenez is a cautionary tale about the evidentiary work that mixed‑motive cases demand. Courts will look for concrete links—timing, statements, repeatable patterns, and proof of state impotence or complicity—before finding a protected‑ground motive was central. Where a persecutor can plausibly be read as acting to protect a criminal enterprise, asylum seekers must be prepared to show why that reading is incomplete: preserve PSG arguments, collect contemporaneous evidence, document futile efforts to obtain protection, and develop country‑condition proof that places the individual’s case in a broader pattern.
