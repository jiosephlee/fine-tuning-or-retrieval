Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Internal Relocation by Anecdote: Three Weeks in the Countryside and the Reasonableness Inquiry

## Why Jimenez v. Bondi matters

Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a useful, if modest, template for how the Tenth Circuit will treat internal-relocation arguments in asylum cases involving private persecutors. The opinion reinforces two practical points immigration practitioners already know: (1) when an applicant cannot establish past persecution or a protected-ground nexus, the feasibility of internal relocation often becomes dispositive; and (2) an applicant who offers only thin or “haphazard” briefing and limited documentary support faces an uphill climb under the BIA’s and the circuit’s deferential substantial‑evidence review. What makes Jimenez instructive is how the court treats short-term, on-the-ground indicia of safety—three weeks staying with a relative in the countryside, family members remaining in Colombia, and threats that told the family to “move out of the neighborhood” rather than “leave the country”—as persuasive evidence that internal relocation is reasonable.

Practitioners should read Jimenez as a reminder that the relocation inquiry is intensely fact-specific and that anecdotal facts, if uncontradicted and supported by other record evidence, can be fatal to an asylum claim unless the applicant builds a contemporaneous, granular record showing why relocation would be unreasonable or impossible.

## What the court did

The Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review after concluding that the Board of Immigration Appeals (and the immigration judge) had substantial evidence to find the petitioners had not suffered past persecution, had not shown a protected‑ground nexus, and had not demonstrated the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. Because petitioners could not satisfy those elements, the court also accepted the BIA’s alternative finding that internal relocation within Colombia was reasonable.

On the relocation point the opinion emphasizes several practical facts the IJ/BIA relied on: relatives and other family members still live in Colombia unharmed; Jimenez’s father still works in the criminal justice system; the family stayed safely with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before leaving for the United States; and the men who threatened the family had told them to leave the neighborhood rather than the country. The court also criticized the petitioners’ briefing as “haphazard” on arguments about administrative and economic burdens, judicial infrastructure, and alleged ties between the persecutors and the president—arguments the regulations specifically identify as relevant to a reasonableness analysis (see 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3)). Under the governing standard, the government may rebut a presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution by showing, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating.

## A closer look: should a three‑week, uneventful stay carry outsized weight?

Jimenez illustrates the tension at the heart of the relocation inquiry. On one hand, decision‑makers must be realistic. A short, documented episode of safety—especially when combined with evidence of family presence and active governmental efforts (evidence here that authorities had executed searches and seizures at the property associated with the persecutor)—permits a reasonable adjudicator to infer that the persecutor’s reach or motive is localized. The BIA and the Tenth Circuit are entitled to rely on such inferences under the substantial‑evidence standard.

On the other hand, treating a brief, temporary period of safety as dispositive risks flattening a complex, time‑dependent question: does a few weeks without incident show a sustainable, long‑term option to relocate safely? It may not, especially if the persecutor is a criminal organization with the capacity to pursue victims beyond a single neighborhood, or if relocation would meaningfully disrupt a family's ability to earn a living, obtain identity documents, enroll children in school, or access medical care. Jimenez does not reject those concerns; it simply finds them unproven here. In other words, the court’s approach is not doctrinally novel, but it underscores how thin evidence can be decisive when the other side of the ledger—robust proof of persecutor reach, state complicity, or the economic and administrative impossibility of moving—remains empty.

## What a winning relocation record looks like

Jimenez is a practical lesson for counsel: don’t rely on generalized assertions about “economic burdens” or “judicial infrastructure.” The regulation the court cited, 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3), contemplates a non‑exhaustive set of facts that may make relocation unreasonable. To preserve and win the argument an applicant should, where applicable, develop and submit record evidence addressing those concrete factors.

At a minimum, counsel should assemble: contemporaneous police reports or complaints showing attempts to seek protection (and any official responses); affidavits from family, neighbors, or employers describing the reach or identity of the persecutors and why relocation would not hide the applicant; country‑conditions or expert declarations explaining how the persecutor network operates across regions or how local institutions are incapable of providing protection; documentary proof of economic hardship (inability to obtain work, licensing, housing, or identity documents in another part of the country); medical or psychological records demonstrating special needs that impede relocation; and specific briefing tying those facts to the regulatory factors. If reporting to police would likely be futile or dangerous, that factual predicate should be spelled out and supported by record evidence. The absence of that detail is what the court labeled “haphazard” briefing—and it proved costly.

## Doctrinal and practical downstream effects

Jimenez reinforces the BIA’s latitude to assess relocation on a case‑by‑case basis and the difficulty of overturning such findings on appeal. Under substantial‑evidence review, anecdote backed by corroborating facts (family still in country, prior government action against the persecutor, a recorded temporary, safe stay) will often defeat generalized migration‑impossibility claims. That dynamic will likely produce predictable downstream effects: petitioners must be proactive in curating a textured administrative record; IJs and the BIA will feel justified in giving weight to pragmatic indicia of safety when there is no strong evidence to the contrary; and cases involving private persecutors will turn even more than before on detailed proof of persecutor reach and the real costs of internal migration.

There is also a policy angle. The court’s pragmatic approach makes sense in a system with limited fact‑finding resources: adjudicators must weigh what is actually in the record. But it underlines an asymmetry: applicants with counsel and access to experts can produce the evidence necessary to defeat a relocation defense, while less‑resourced applicants cannot. The opinion thereby reiterates an old—yet persistent—truth in asylum law: the quality of the administrative record often determines the outcome.

## Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is not a landmark changing asylum doctrine, but it is a clear reminder of how the reasonableness-of‑relocation inquiry plays out in practice. For advocates, the message is plain: when an applicant cannot show past persecution or state inability to protect, don’t rely on high‑level assertions about how relocation would be impossible. Build the record. Document attempts (and failures) to seek protection, assemble country‑conditions and expert evidence on persecutor reach and local institutional capacity, and brief the regulatory factors in 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3) with specificity. A short, peaceful stay in the countryside—three weeks in this case—may be enough to sink a claim unless the applicant can convincingly demonstrate why that snapshot does not reflect a viable, long‑term option to avoid persecution.
