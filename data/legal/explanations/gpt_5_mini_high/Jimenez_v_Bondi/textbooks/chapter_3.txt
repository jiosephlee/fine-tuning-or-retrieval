Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Past Persecution: Threats and Psychological Harm Do Not Suffice Here

## Persecution concept: must entail more than restrictions or threats to life and liberty; cumulative assessment of incidents
The opinion begins this section by anchoring the analysis in the Tenth Circuit’s settled definition of “persecution.” Because the Immigration and Nationality Act does not define that term, the court relies on prior precedent: persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm that is “regarded as offensive” and is more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty. The court also reiterates the idea that incidents are evaluated cumulatively—individual episodes are not analyzed in isolation; the totality of mistreatment may together amount to persecution. At the same time, whether private actors’ conduct rises to persecution is a fact question, subject on appeal to the substantial‑evidence standard. Thus the court’s role is not to reweigh credibility but to decide whether the record compels a different factual conclusion than the BIA and IJ reached.

## Record evidence: multiple threats, armed confrontations, employee assault, school incident; petitioners’ concession of no lasting physical harm
The court then lays out the record facts it must assess under that standard. Petitioners were active in the Colombian Liberal Party and campaigned for a presidential candidate. Beginning in late May 2022, Jimenez received repeated threatening phone calls; a colleague who reported threats was later kidnapped and murdered. Jimenez became involved in a property dispute with a man identified as a local narco‑trafficker and leader known as “Don Rafa,” whose property had been the subject of police searches. The harassment escalated: a threatening brochure arrived at Petitioners’ factory; two men held Jimenez at gunpoint and told him to leave the neighborhood; those men later assaulted a factory employee, brandished a gun while demanding Petitioners’ whereabouts, and said the order came from Don Rafa; and a man accosted Jimenez’s younger son as he left school. Petitioners relocated temporarily to a relative’s home and ultimately left Colombia for the United States. Importantly for the legal analysis, Petitioners conceded at their merits hearing (and in briefing) that they suffered no physical injury and no lasting physical harm.

## Threats alone are insufficient; physical injury not required but harm must be severe and offensive
Applying the governing test, the court emphasizes two controlling points from Tenth Circuit precedent. First, threats by themselves generally do not amount to persecution. Second, while physical injury is not absolutely required to show persecution, the harm an applicant alleges must be sufficiently severe and offensive—more than routine harassment, sporadic threats, or ordinary criminal extortion. The court evaluates Petitioners’ incidents in aggregate but concludes that, even collectively, the record presents threats and menacing conduct that fall short of the degree of suffering the term “persecution” demands under circuit law. The IJ and BIA had found Petitioners credible, but credibility alone cannot convert episodic threats and intimidation into persecution if the objective level of harm does not meet the circuit’s threshold.

## Psychological and emotional effects (insomnia, panic attacks) considered but found inadequate without qualifying severity
Petitioners pressed their lasting emotional and psychological harm—Duarte’s insomnia and their son’s panic attacks—as evidence that the harassment produced persecution. The court acknowledges these effects and treats them as part of the cumulative picture, but it follows the line of authority holding that psychological or emotional distress coupled with threats, in the absence of sufficiently severe attendant conduct, rarely satisfies the persecution requirement. The opinion explicitly notes that the Tenth Circuit has “rarely, if ever,” granted asylum on the basis of threats plus emotional harm alone, and it finds that the present symptoms, while regrettable, do not raise the objective level of suffering required.

## Comparator cases illustrating higher‑severity fact patterns still found insufficient
To give context for the severity threshold, the court cites several circuit decisions in which more pronounced or prolonged mistreatment also failed to constitute persecution. Those precedents include cases involving arrest and detention with threats, repeated robberies and threats involving weapons, multiple telephone threats and a vague in‑person threat, and other sustained harassment—each of which the Tenth Circuit treated as falling short. By invoking those decisions, the court signals that Petitioners’ facts are not distinguishable in a way that would warrant a different outcome: counsel itself conceded at oral argument that the court had rejected asylum claims in even more extreme factual scenarios.

## Conclusion: substantial evidence supports BIA/IJ finding that aggregate harm does not constitute past persecution
Putting the legal standard and the record together under the substantial‑evidence review, the court concludes that the BIA’s and IJ’s factual determination—that Petitioners’ aggregate mistreatment does not rise to past persecution—is supported by the record and therefore must stand. Because the applicants failed to establish past persecution, they are not entitled to the rebuttable presumption of future persecution that such a showing creates, and their asylum claim founders at this first essential element. For students reading the opinion, the key lessons are (1) the distinction between credibility and sufficiency of harm, (2) the importance of objective severity for a persecution finding, and (3) how the court uses precedent to calibrate that severity threshold when reviewing cumulative threats and psychological harm.
