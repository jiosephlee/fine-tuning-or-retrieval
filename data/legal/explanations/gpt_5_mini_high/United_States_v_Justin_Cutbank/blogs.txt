Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic or 404(b)? The Eighth Circuit Reaffirms Prior Gun Sightings as Proof of Possession

## Why this matters

Proving knowing possession is the central evidentiary hurdle in most § 922(g) prosecutions. When the firearm is recovered away from the charged scene, eyewitness testimony that the defendant had been seen with the same gun before the offense can be decisive. In United States v. Cutbank, the Eighth Circuit again treated such testimony as intrinsic — not Rule 404(b) — evidence, clearing the way for prosecutors to introduce witnesses’ “prior sightings” of the very weapon recovered. The decision is a reminder that in the Eighth Circuit the identity and continuity of possession are powerful engines for admissibility, and that defendants must deploy focused pretrial and trial tactics if they want to keep prior-sighting testimony out of the jury’s hands.

## What the court did

The panel affirmed Cutbank’s conviction and sentence. Factually, the case involved a sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle recovered along a likely path between a victim’s home and where Cutbank later barricaded himself; DNA on the gun matched him. Witnesses testified that they had seen Cutbank with that gun “a handful of times” or “once or twice” before the night of the offense. Cutbank argued pretrial and at trial that the witnesses’ statements about earlier sightings were barred as propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), or otherwise prejudicial under Rule 403. The district court admitted the testimony as intrinsic evidence of continuous possession and ownership; the Eighth Circuit affirmed, relying on United States v. Battle and emphasizing that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

The court also rebuffed related evidentiary claims: it found no Confrontation Clause or hearsay problem because the declarant testified, it upheld the trial court’s authentication requirement for police reports used in impeachment, and it declined to reverse based on cumulative error given overwhelming government proof.

## The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning — and its limits

The Eighth Circuit’s treatment is doctrinally orthodox as applied to these facts. The intrinsic/extrinsic distinction turns on whether the evidence is necessary to complete the story of the charged offense or is not independent of it. When a recovered firearm can be tied to the defendant through prior consumptive acts — multiple eyewitness identifications of the same weapon, unique physical features (here, a sawed‑off barrel and stock), and forensic links — prior sightings do more than show a general proclivity to carry guns; they tend to prove ownership or control and therefore fit comfortably within the “intrinsic” category. The Battle line, expressly relied on by the panel, captures that logic.

That said, the opinion does not create a bright‑line rule that all testimony that a defendant was seen with guns is intrinsic. The controlling feature in Cutbank was the nexus: the witnesses pointed to (1) the same, identifiable weapon, (2) in a factual narrative that supplied continuity between earlier possession and the time of the offense, and (3) corroborating physical and forensic evidence. Absent those elements — for example, where prior sightings are of different guns, are vaguely described, are temporally remote, or where multiple people had equal access to the weapon — an “intrinsic” label will be harder to justify.

## What this means for prosecutors and defense counsel

For prosecutors, Cutbank reinforces that a careful proffer and foundation can place prior-sighting testimony beyond the reach of Rule 404(b). If the government can show identity of the weapon (distinctive markings, sawed‑off parts, serial‑number issues) and factual continuity, the testimony will likely be admitted, and Rule 404(b) notice is not required for true intrinsic evidence. Prosecutors should therefore develop witnesses’ descriptions of the weapon, preserve corroborating physical or forensic links, and be prepared to explain how the prior sightings complete the story of possession rather than simply show a propensity for guns.

For defenders, Cutbank is a cautionary tale: the Eighth Circuit will defer to district courts’ evidentiary judgments, and appellate courts will sustain rulings unless the admission is an abuse of discretion or produces substantial prejudice. But the decision is not the last word — it identifies where litigators should fight.

## Practical defense strategies

Defense counsel should attack prior‑sighting testimony at multiple points. First, press a focused Rule 403 motion in limine: emphasize the risk that testimony about “seeing him with a gun” will invite the jury to infer criminal propensity, and highlight any inflammatory collateral facts (domestic violence, presence of children, mention of a prior homicide in the family, etc.) that increase the risk of unfair prejudice. Ask the court to limit the number of witnesses allowed to testify about prior sightings or to limit the scope of their testimony to narrowly tailored identification evidence.

Second, narrow the temporal and descriptive window. Challenge the admissibility unless the government can show a sufficiently close temporal relationship and a reliable basis for identity — e.g., distinctive features or markings that connect the earlier sightings to the recovered weapon. If the prior sightings are years old, imprecise, or describe different guns, press that they are extrinsic and require 404(b) notice and balancing.

Third, insist on strict authentication. Where the government wants to use police reports, investigators’ notes, or third‑party statements to impeach, require proper foundation so the court cannot admit unsworn narrative summaries without authentication (the Cumbie line the panel cited). Likewise, probe and exploit weaknesses in forensic links (ambiguous DNA, lack of fingerprints, porous chain of custody) to undermine the government’s continuity narrative.

Fourth, seek contemporaneous limiting instructions and, if necessary, a curative instruction when collateral, inflammatory testimony slips in. While the Eighth Circuit observed that a court need not give a limiting instruction at the moment of admission, many judges will do so if asked, and an early caution can blunt juror misuse.

Finally, at trial use cross‑examination to emphasize alternative explanations and the unreliability of memory: show differences in witnesses’ descriptions, the conditions under which they “saw” the weapon, and other persons who had access to the firearm. If the defendant’s case is that he lacked possession at the relevant time, marshal proof of shared access, transfer, or absence from the scene.

## Doctrinal fault lines and downstream effects

Cutbank sits in the current stream of appellate deference to district courts on intrinsic evidence disputes, but it also flags a continuing doctrinal tension. Treating prior‑sighting testimony as intrinsic gives prosecutors an efficient route into identity and continuity without 404(b) disclosures, but if applied too expansively it risks swallowing Rule 404(b)’s protections. That tension will surface in close cases: when descriptions are vague, when multiple people had access, or when the count of prior sightings is effectively an allegation of character (e.g., “he always carried a gun”). Those situations are where circuit courts will have to refine the intrinsic/extrinsic line.

Practically, expect more instances where prosecutors will develop witness testimony about prior sightings early in investigations to buttress possession cases, and expect defense lawyers to litigate early and often about foundation, temporal proximity, and prejudice. Appellate outcomes will continue to turn on the factual texture of each case and the district court’s careful (or careless) gatekeeping.

## Bottom line

Cutbank reaffirms a predictable but important point in the Eighth Circuit: eyewitness testimony that the defendant was seen with the same, identifiable firearm before an offense is often admissible as intrinsic evidence of possession. That is a powerful tool for prosecutors, but it is not license to introduce every reference to prior gun ownership or use. Defense counsel must press for narrow, concrete foundations, insist on strict authentication, and wage targeted Rule 403 fights to prevent prior‑sighting testimony from functioning as improper propensity evidence.



# Beyond ACCA: How § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and Unobjected-to PSR Facts Drove a 292-Month Sentence

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank is a useful, practical reminder for defense lawyers and sentencing judges: ACCA’s mandatory minimum and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline are not a ceiling on how high a judge can set an advisory Guidelines range. Even where the jury has found the ACCA predicates, district courts can — and will — rely on uncharged or otherwise unadjudicated conduct recited in an uncontested PSR to trigger a §2K2.1 enhancement that produces a higher Guidelines range than the ACCA calculation. The Eighth Circuit affirmed a within‑Guidelines 292‑month sentence based on that route, highlighting three points that practitioners should take seriously: (1) Application Note 14(C) to §2K2.1 permits consideration of uncharged conduct for the enhancement; (2) adopted PSR facts that go unobjected to are extremely difficult to overturn on appeal; and (3) courts remain deferential to within‑Guidelines sentences.

## What the court did (short tour of the opinion)

The facts are stark: after using methamphetamine, Cutbank terrorized his girlfriend at gunpoint, ordered others awakened, struck the victim with a gun, fled with the victim’s phone and the rifle, barricaded himself later and fought officers, and was tied to the sawed‑off Marlin by DNA and eyewitness testimony. A jury convicted him under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) and found three or more prior violent felonies — triggering ACCA.

At sentencing the PSR produced a base offense level (itself reflecting the sawed‑off firearm) and several increases: a serial‑number alteration enhancement, a two‑level increase for creating a risk while fleeing police, and a four‑level §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for possession “in connection with” another felony. The PSR, and the enhancements it recommended, were adopted by the district court without objection. The result: an offense level (35 in the PSR) that exceeded the ACCA‑based Guidelines calculation (34), and an advisory range of 292–365 months. The district court imposed 292 months. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed, rejecting Cutbank’s scattered evidentiary claims (largely unpreserved) and sustaining the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on the PSR facts, relying on Application Note 14(C) and its usual clear‑error review when a PSR is adopted without objection. The court also noted its presumption of reasonableness for within‑Guidelines sentences.

## Why the result follows established law — and where the tension lies

There is nothing novel in the Eighth Circuit’s legal framework here. Three precedents (each invoked or alluded to by the court) form the spine of the result:

- Booker made the Guidelines advisory and left judges room to find sentencing facts by a preponderance of the evidence when those facts do not increase the statutory maximum or a mandatory minimum set by Congress.
- Watts and later cases allow a sentencing judge to consider uncharged or even acquitted conduct in sentencing so long as the relevant constitutional limits are respected.
- The Sentencing Commission’s own commentary — Application Note 14(C) to §2K2.1 — expressly allows the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) “in connection with another felony” enhancement to be applied “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.”

Pulling those strands together, the Eighth Circuit had straightforward authority to (1) accept PSR factual recitations that were not objected to, (2) permit the district judge to find the necessary nexus between the gun and other felony conduct by a preponderance, and (3) apply the §2K2.1 enhancement even though the conduct giving rise to it did not result in a separate conviction.

That legal posture creates a practical tension. The ACCA imposes a statutory floor (a mandatory minimum) and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline is often the dominant relevant Guidelines provision, but the Guidelines themselves provide a mechanism — USSG §4B1.4(b) — that tells courts to apply the greater offense level, whether it comes from §2K2.1 calculations or from the career‑offender/ACCA path. Thus a defendant can be subject to ACCA’s statutory consequences while still receiving a higher advisory Guidelines range because the judge relied on additional conduct to build §2K2.1 up past the ACCA figure. That tension is not doctrinally unsound under present precedent, but it is why Cutbank’s result will be rhetorically and strategically significant in practice.

## The preservation lesson — adopt the PSR at your peril

Cutbank illustrates the classic sentencing‑practice trap: if the PSR contains narrative facts that support enhancements and defense counsel does not lodge timely, specific objections (and does not demand an evidentiary hearing), the district court’s adoption of the PSR converts those recitations into findings subject only to clear‑error review on appeal. The Eighth Circuit applied that deferential standard, citing Dunn’s review rule and concluding that the PSR facts were not clearly erroneous.

That is the critical, avoidable vulnerability here. A sentencing challenge to a §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on uncharged conduct can succeed only if the defendant (a) preserves the objection, (b) insists on an evidentiary vehicle adequate to test the government’s factual showing, and (c) makes a reasoned argument that the preponderance standard was not met. Absent those steps, appellate courts treat the matter as a factual record problem rather than a legal defect.

## Acquitted/uncharged‑conduct limits — what matters and what doesn’t

A frequent instinct is to object on Sixth Amendment grounds when a judge uses uncharged or acquitted conduct to increase a sentence. But the constitutional constraints identified in Apprendi and Alleyne bite only where judicial fact‑finding increases the statutory maximum or raises a statutory mandatory minimum that the jury has not found. In Cutbank the jury already found the ACCA predicates (and prior‑conviction issues occupy a discrete Almendarez‑Torres/Apprendi exception). The §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement affected only the advisory Guidelines range; the facts underpinning it therefore did not implicate the Apprendi/Alleyne jury‑trial rule.

That is the key doctrinal point: unless the judge’s factual findings change a statutory floor or ceiling to which Apprendi/Alleyne applies, judges retain authority, within Booker’s framework, to find sentencing facts by a preponderance and to consider uncharged conduct under Application Note 14(C). Debates over the desirability of that practice are legitimate, but Cutbank is entirely consistent with controlling precedent.

## Practical takeaways and downstream effects

For defense counsel: Cutbank is a reminder that sentencing is a battle that begins with the PSR. Timely, specific objections to narrative PSR statements are essential; when the government intends to rely on uncharged conduct to seek enhancements, demand a live hearing, require the government to introduce admissible proof, and press the standard for nexus (for §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the nexus between firearm and felony). Silence or non‑specific objections are likely to be fatal on appeal.

For prosecutors: Cutbank signals that careful drafting of the PSR and production of corroborating evidence for enhancements will be rewarded. The explicit cross‑reference in the Guidelines and Application Note 14(C) makes clear that a robust PSR narrative can do the heavy lifting at sentencing even if no separate charge is brought.

For courts and policymakers: the case highlights the cumulative power of guideline enhancements. When a defendant has violent conduct that is not separately charged, the sentencing machinery can still stack significant enhancements — and defenders will rightly protest that those enhancements can be grounded in untested allegations. The result is likely to keep alive the policy debates over whether Application Note 14(C) and the broader acquitted/uncharged‑conduct practice should be reined in.

## A small editorial note — and a reminder to check the math

The opinion refers to a three‑level increase for an altered or obliterated serial number where §2K2.1(b)(4)(B) normally provides a four‑level increase. The Eighth Circuit called this a likely typographical slip; whatever the intended number, the PSR’s arithmetic produced an offense level that exceeded the ACCA calculation, and the outcome did not turn on that stray digit. Still, Cutbank is a practical reminder to litigators and judges to double‑check Guidelines math and to make the record reflect agreed, contested, and judicially resolved facts with precision.

## Bottom line

Cutbank does not announce any new doctrine, but it is a crisp, practical lesson about how the Guidelines, Application Note 14(C), and PSR practices combine in the real world to produce long sentences even where ACCA is already in play. For defendants, the moral is straightforward: fight the PSR hard; for prosecutors, the lesson is equally plain: assemble the proof and the PSR narrative. Until (and unless) the Supreme Court or Congress narrows the use of uncharged conduct in sentencing, cases like Cutbank will remain the operative battlefield for high‑stakes sentencing disputes.



# The Perils of “Background” Hearsay: Course-of-Investigation Testimony Survives Crawford—Again

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank is a useful, if not novel, tour of the tensions that Bishop v. Crawford (Crawford v. Washington) and its progeny left in their wake. The Eighth Circuit, in a brisk opinion by Judge Loken, affirmed a conviction in which police officers described statements a victim made to them during the investigation, the district court declined a defense request for a contemporaneous limiting instruction, and the victim later testified at trial. The panel endorsed two familiar propositions: a trial court may admit such statements for non‑hearsay, contextual purposes (the so‑called “course‑of‑investigation” testimony), and the Confrontation Clause concern that animated Crawford is mooted if the declarant actually testifies and is subject to cross‑examination. For defense lawyers and trial judges, the decision spotlights the practical problem at the heart of many criminal trials — when does explanatory repetition by police become a backdoor way to get testimonial accusations to the jury where they may do the most harm?

## What the Eighth Circuit did

Cutbank asked the district court, before trial, to instruct the jury that it could not consider statements D.F. made to police as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until she testified herself. The government answered that the officers’ recounting of D.F.’s statements was admissible to explain why police took the actions they did, and that a later limiting instruction (if needed) would suffice. The district court refused the contemporaneous instruction; D.F. later testified at trial; and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The panel applied the familiar abuse‑of‑discretion standard for limiting‑instruction rulings, citing United States v. Bennett for the proposition that “the court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” It also relied on United States v. Charboneau for the straightforward Confrontation Clause point: the Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Because the record, in the court’s view, “demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial” and D.F. testified, there was no abuse of discretion.

## The Crawford tension remains

Cutbank exemplifies a recurring, doctrinally familiar problem. Crawford and its follow‑ups (not least Davis and Melendez‑Diaz) established that testimonial statements to law enforcement ordinarily cannot be admitted against a criminal defendant unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross‑examination. But Crawford did not eliminate the long‑standing practice of allowing officers to recount to juries what complaining witnesses said to them when those recitals are offered to explain the course of the investigation, the officers’ state of mind, or why police acted as they did. Courts often treat such repetition as non‑hearsay (or as admissible for a non‑hearsay purpose) and allow it in the interest of narrative coherence.

That accommodation raises two persistent worries. First, whether a particular statement to police is “testimonial” can be a close call; if it is testimonial and the declarant does not testify, the Confrontation Clause blocks its use. Second, even where the Confrontation Clause is satisfied because the declarant later testifies, or where the statement is admitted only to explain police behavior, the jury may — intentionally or not — treat the officer’s recital as evidence of the truth of the matter asserted. The Eighth Circuit acknowledged the first worry and disposed of it simply: D.F. testified, so Crawford’s prophylaxis was not implicated. On the second, the panel leaned on Rule 105/Bennett’s deferential standard and the district court’s discretion about timing of instructions, concluding that forcing contemporaneous limiting instructions is unnecessary so long as a proper instruction can be given later.

That posture prioritizes courtroom manageability and the judge’s discretion to control the trial narrative, but it tends to understate the cognitive realities of how jurors process testimony. Allowing raw, accusatory statements to be replayed by officers early in the case and telling the jurors later to ignore their truth — particularly after the same statements have been rehearsed in the jury’s mind — is not a neutral choice about process. It is a decision that can materially affect how jurors allocate credibility and memory during the long arc of a trial.

## Practical guidance for trial judges and counsel

Cutbank is an important reminder that, in practice, the safest course for a defendant is to make the objection and build the record at trial; for judges, the safest course is to manage the timing and wording of investigations‑as‑narrative with an eye to prejudice.

Defense counsel should, at a minimum, (1) raise pretrial Crawford objections when statements to police are plainly testimonial; (2) seek a contemporaneous limiting instruction whenever officers are likely to repeat highly incriminating accusations before the declarant testifies; (3) preserve objections on the record at the point the testimony is elicited and, if necessary, move to strike and for a curative instruction or mistrial; and (4) insist that the government call a key declarant early in its case‑in‑chief when feasible. Cross‑examination of officers should focus on what the officer personally observed and on whether the officer is recounting words verbatim — a line of questioning that can limit the officer’s ability to repeat the speaker’s vivid accusations in the jury’s ear.

Trial judges, for their part, should not reflexively defer all limiting instructions to the end of trial. A brief, carefully tailored admonition at the time of admission can do real work: a contemporaneous instruction framed in plain language — for example, that the officer’s testimony about what the victim said is being admitted only to explain why the officer acted and not for the truth of the matter asserted — is both simple and likely to reduce the risk that jurors will treat the repetition as substantive proof. When statements are particularly inflammatory, Rule 403 remains a viable tool: the probative value of the officer’s repetition may be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. Judges also have docket management tools; when the government elects a narrative that depends on a declarant’s out‑of‑court statements, the court should consider whether the declarant can and should be called early.

Finally, appellate counsel must avoid the mistake the court observed in Cutbank: bare assertions of error without supportive authority or developed analysis rarely persuade an appellate court to disturb a district judge’s discretionary rulings.

## Where this decision leaves the law

Cutbank does not break new doctrinal ground; it reaffirms a pragmatic line that many circuits already follow — officers may recount what witnesses told them to explain police action, courts review refusals to give contemporaneous limiting instructions for abuse of discretion, and the Confrontation Clause concern vanishes if the declarant later testifies. But its practical effect is notable. By tolerating delayed limiting instructions and characterizing “background” statements as non‑fatal so long as the declarant testifies, the Eighth Circuit’s approach makes it harder in many cases for defendants to show reversible error on appeal. That will matter most in cases where the declarant is reluctant or unavailable to testify, where the officer’s recital is unusually vivid or detailed, or where the government builds its narrative around repeated out‑of‑court accusations.

If there is a takeoff point for doctrinal challenge, it is not in Crawford’s text but in the continuing empirical and normative question whether late instructions actually neutralize the prejudicial effect of earlier officer recitals. That is the space where trial judges can do the most to protect the fairness of the factfinding process: by tailoring limiting instructions to the moment, policing the form of officer testimony, and refusing to allow police narratives to become the principal vehicle for the prosecution’s substantive proof.

## Conclusion

Cutbank is a reminder that the law tolerates course‑of‑investigation testimony, and that Crawford’s Confrontation Clause protections are easily satisfied if the declarant testifies. But tolerance is not endorsement of complacency. Courts that value both coherent trials and fair trials should think hard at the moment such testimony is offered about whether a prompt, plain limiting instruction, selective redaction, or an early calling of the declarant is necessary to prevent the jury’s natural tendency to conflate context with truth. Defense counsel should press those choices aggressively; absent a strong record, appellate relief will be an uphill climb.



# Impeachment, Authentication, and an Appellate Nudge: Using Police Reports the Right Way

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a compact but useful reminder about a few prosaic — and frequently litigated — trial problems: how to use third‑party police reports to impeach a witness, what foundations courts will require before letting defense counsel read those reports into the record, and how appellate courts view litigants that broaden the scope of an appeal. The decision doesn’t break new doctrinal ground, but it applies settled Eighth Circuit precedent (notably United States v. Cumbie) in a way that should sharpen trial planning for both defense and government counsel. It also contains an unusually plainspoken rebuke to appellees who press arguments that diverge from the appellant’s brief — a small procedural lesson that can affect appellate briefing strategy.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The facts are stark: after a violent home incident, police later found a sawed‑off Marlin .22 with DNA matching Cutbank, a victim who identified the rifle, and other corroborating evidence. At trial the defense tried to impeach a government witness (C.F.) with statements reflected in a police report. The district court allowed questions about what C.F. told the officer but sustained objections when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the police report into the record without calling the officer or otherwise authenticating the report. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The court relied on its precedent that extrinsic impeachment evidence — including prior inconsistent statements memorialized in a third‑party report — must be authenticated before being admitted (citing United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022)). In short: asking a witness on cross whether she said X to an officer is one thing; reading a police report written by someone else into the record without foundation is another. The court also upheld other evidentiary rulings (including a brief reference by a witness that her sister had been killed by a gun, allowed as responsive explanation to prior inconsistent statements) under Rule 403, and concluded any errors were harmless in light of compelling physical and testimonial proof.

Finally, the opinion included an explicit curbstone to the government: “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” That comment followed the government’s attempt to address a defense claim that Cutbank did not himself press on appeal.

## What the decision means in practice

The Cutbank panel’s treatment of the attempted impeachment with a police report is an object lesson in foundation and authentication. Courts will generally allow counsel to probe a witness about prior statements to police. But if you intend to use the officer’s report — or any document prepared by someone other than the witness — to take the sting out of a witness’s testimony, you should be ready to authenticate the report.

There are a few reliable paths to get that foundation at trial. First, on cross‑examination try to get the witness to acknowledge the substance of the statement recorded in the report. If the witness admits saying the thing recorded, the report’s contents can often be proved through the witness’ admission or through the document as a prior inconsistent statement under Rule 613 and, depending on the circumstances, under Rule 801(d)(1)(A). If the witness disavows the statement, plan to call the officer who prepared the report to authenticate the document and testify about what the witness told the officer. Calling the report’s drafter resolves both authentication and hearsay concerns for impeachment purposes. If the officer is unavailable, consider earlier preservation steps (declarations, depositions where permitted, or ensuring agents are on your witness list and subpoenaed).

Second, do not assume exceptions like the public‑records hearsay exception will rescue you in a criminal case. Many courts treat police investigative reports differently (and some parts of Rule 803(8) explicitly carve out law‑enforcement observations in criminal matters), so the safest route is authentication by testimony rather than reliance on hearsay exceptions.

Third, if you anticipate a recorded‑recollection argument, remember that the recorded‑recollection exception (Rule 803(5)) is available where the witness once had knowledge, now lacks memory, and the writing accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge. That exception is typically used for the author’s own writing; it does not eliminate the need to authenticate a third‑party police report unless the foundation for recorded recollection is laid through the report’s author or the witness’s own adoption.

Cutbank also reminds trial lawyers to prepare the people who can authenticate and explain documents. If you want to make the police report work for impeachment, put the report’s author on call, or secure stipulations or pretrial rulings admitting the necessary portions with proper foundation.

## Appellate practice: a brief, pointed nudge

The court’s line about appellees expanding issues is worth pausing over. The government argued a variant claim about how the district court handled cross‑examination that Cutbank had not itself pressed on appeal. The panel’s curt rebuke — “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal” — is not just an affectation. It signals judicial impatience with briefs that broaden the contest beyond the appellant’s framing and invites appellate counsel to stay focused on the preserved, properly briefed issues.

Two practical lessons flow from that tone. Defense counsel should preserve objections and develop arguments at trial (and brief them on appeal) rather than burying them in perfunctory assertions on appeal. Government counsel should avoid expansive counter‑claims that go beyond the appellant’s actual arguments; doing so may irritate the court and can lead to the argument receiving short shrift.

## Rule 403, harmlessness, and cumulative‑error

Cutbank also illustrates the interplay between discretionary evidentiary rulings and appellate harmlessness review. The testimony about D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun came in as an explanatory response to cross‑examination about inconsistent statements. The district court declined to strike and the Eighth Circuit saw no Rule 403 problem — probative context for credibility and motive outweighed any potential prejudice. Where the record also contains strong physical corroboration (here, DNA on the rifle, the victim’s phone found along the likely path, eyewitness identifications, and resisting‑officer injuries), the court was comfortable finding any asserted errors harmless and rejecting a cumulative‑error claim. The practical point is simple: when challenging evidentiary rulings on appeal, the presence (or absence) of independent, powerful proof on the central elements of the offense will heavily influence outcomes.

## Bottom line

Cutbank won’t change the law of impeachment or authentication, but it is a useful reminder to litigators of old but often overlooked truths. If you plan to impeach with statements memorialized in a police report, build your foundation: get the witness to adopt the statement, or bring the report’s author to authenticate it. Don’t assume hearsay exceptions will carry you in a criminal case. Preserve and brief the issues you want decided, and avoid the temptation to let appellate argument drift into unpreserved or collateral claims — courts notice. And finally, when the government has strong physical evidence, appellate courts will be unsympathetic to arguments that rest on disputed evidentiary quibbles that did not prejudice the verdict.
