Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What exactly qualified as the “other felony offense” for § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and can the court rely on unobjected-to PSR facts to apply it?
Question:
The PSR recommended a 4-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony, and the court adopted the PSR’s facts without objection. The opinion then says the court adopted "factual statements regarding [Cutbank’s] prior felony convictions" and "thus" found the gun was used in connection with another felony—this seems like a possible typo because prior convictions wouldn’t prove the nexus for the instant offense. Which felony was the "other" one here (e.g., kidnapping/false imprisonment, felony assault, terroristic threats, or resisting with injury)? What is the Eighth Circuit’s required nexus under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (facilitated or had the potential to facilitate)? Is a preponderance standard applied, and is it proper to rely on unobjected-to PSR factual recitations alone to support the enhancement?
Answer:
Short answer
- The “other felony” the court relied on was the violent, contemporaneous conduct described in the PSR — i.e., the incident conduct (pointing the gun at D.F., keeping her in a closet at gunpoint, threatening and striking her, the assault/domestic‑abuse/kidnaping facts and the injury to an officer while resisting arrest). The opinion does not single out one label but affirms the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on the PSR narrative describing those felonies.  
- The opinion applies the Guidelines language that the enhancement attaches where the firearm was “used … in connection with another … felony” (USSG §2K2.1, comment. n.14(C)), but it does not in this decision spell out a separate doctrinal test (e.g., “facilitated” vs. merely “had the potential to facilitate”) or expressly state the burden of proof. What the opinion does show is that actual use in the commission of violent felonies (not merely a prior conviction) was sufficient on these facts.  
- Yes — the district court may rely on PSR factual recitations adopted at sentencing when the defendant does not object. The Eighth Circuit upheld the enhancement because the district court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements,” and the court found no clear error on appeal.

Relevant excerpts and analysis grounded in the opinion
- What the enhancement requires (as the opinion quotes): “This enhancement applies if a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense ‘regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.’ § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)).” The opinion therefore treats an uncharged or unconvicted felony as potentially qualifying if tied to the firearm use.  
- Which felony supported the enhancement here: the opinion’s factual narrative shows that during the November 19–20, 2020 incident Cutbank “awakened his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a gun at her face, and directed her into a closet, making paranoid accusations … He kept D.F. in the closet at gunpoint … threatening her life and striking her across the face with the gun.” The PSR recommended the four‑level increase “because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense,” and at sentencing the court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements.” The court later summarizes the conduct as involving “domestic abuse, kidnaping, assault in the victim’s home … and injuring a police officer while resisting arrest.” The opinion therefore rests the enhancement on those incident felonies described in the PSR (rather than on mere prior convictions).  
- On the apparent drafting error in the opinion: the opinion says the court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Thus, the court did not err … in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.” That phrasing is confusing (and the appellant’s point is correct as a matter of logic): prior convictions by themselves do not prove nexus between the gun and the instant conduct. Read sensibly in context, the court meant that it adopted the PSR’s factual narrative about the instant offense (the facts described above), and those adopted facts established the necessary connection.  
- Burden of proof and appellate review: the opinion does not state a burden‑of‑proof (it does not say “preponderance” here) but it does treat the district court’s adoption of unobjected‑to PSR facts as dispositive and reviews the district court’s factual finding deferentially — citing Dunn for the applicable standard of review and saying the court “did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.” In short: failure to object at sentencing let the district court adopt those PSR facts, and the Eighth Circuit will reverse only for clear error.

Practical takeaways for sentencing practice
- If a defendant wants to contest a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, object at sentencing to the relevant PSR recitations and offer contrary evidence: unobjected‑to PSR facts can and will be treated as adopted and will support the enhancement on appeal absent clear error.  
- In cases like this one the court relied on the PSR’s narrative of active gun use (pointing, threats, detention, assault), so active use in the course of contemporaneous felonious conduct is plainly sufficient on these facts.

Bottom line
The Eighth Circuit affirmed the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) increase based on the PSR’s adopted narrative of the instant violent offenses (kidnaping/assault/terroristic threats and resisting with injury). The opinion applies the Guidelines phrasing “used … in connection with another … felony” but does not in this opinion elaborate a separate “facilitated vs. potential” test or state the burden of proof; procedurally, the dispositive point was that the district court adopted the PSR facts without objection and the appellate court found no clear error.
