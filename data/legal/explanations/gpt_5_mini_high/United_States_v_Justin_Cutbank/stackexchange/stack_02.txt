Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### When can officers repeat a victim’s statements “for context” without violating hearsay rules, and does later in-court testimony by the victim cure any Confrontation Clause problem?
Question:
Pretrial, the defense asked for a limiting instruction so the jury wouldn’t consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence before she testified. The court declined to give an immediate instruction, the opinion says the trial did not admit hearsay, and notes the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because D.F. later testified. How do Eighth Circuit courts distinguish permissible "context" testimony (effect on listener) from impermissible hearsay where officers recount what a victim said? Is a contemporaneous limiting instruction ever required, or is a final instruction sufficient? If some officer testimony did slip into hearsay territory, does the victim’s later testimony eliminate any Confrontation Clause issue under cases like Crawford/Charboneau?
Answer:
Short answer
- Eighth Circuit law (as applied in Cutbank) draws the line by purpose: officers may repeat a victim’s out‑of‑court statements to explain the officers’ knowledge, motive, or actions (the statement’s effect on the listener) — that is treated as non‑hearsay — but may not recount those statements to prove the truth of what the victim asserted unless the statement fits a hearsay exception or the declarant testifies. (Cutbank II(B) (government’s argument that statements “establish the context for police officer actions and their knowledge at the time”); district court found no hearsay admitted.)
- A contemporaneous limiting instruction is not automatically required; the district court may defer to a closing instruction. The Eighth Circuit reviews denial of a contemporaneous instruction for abuse of discretion and has held a close‑of‑trial instruction can be sufficient. (Cutbank citing United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014).)
- If an officer’s testimony did slip into hearsay territory but the declarant later appears and testifies, the Confrontation Clause problem is cured under Eighth Circuit precedent — Charboneau — and Cutbank applied that rule: because D.F. later testified, the panel held the Confrontation Clause was satisfied. (Cutbank II(B) (quoting United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010)).)

Short explanation and how this played out in Cutbank
- The operative evidentiary distinction is the purpose for which the out‑of‑court statement is offered. If the government offers what the victim told police to explain why officers did what they did (what they knew, why they investigated, why they secured the scene), that testimony is admitted for its effect on the listener and is not hearsay in the sense that it’s not being offered to prove the literal truth of the victim’s assertion. Cutbank records the government’s exact argument that the victim’s statements were offered to “establish the context for police officer actions and their knowledge at the time,” and the court concluded the record showed no hearsay was admitted.
- Timing of limiting instructions: the court in Cutbank applied Bennett — the district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” The trial court’s choice to decline the pretrial/ contemporaneous limiting instruction was reviewed for abuse of discretion and the Eighth Circuit found no abuse here.
- Confrontation Clause cure: the panel relied on Charboneau for the straightforward rule that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Because D.F. testified at trial, the panel concluded there was no Confrontation Clause deprivation.

Practical implications / what to watch for
- If the officer’s testimony is framed as explaining police conduct or what the officer heard (effect on listener), the Eighth Circuit treats that as permissible context testimony. If the same testimony is used to prove the facts of the victim’s account (i.e., offered for the truth), it is hearsay and must fit an exception or be excluded unless the declarant testifies.
- If you fear prejudice, preserve the issue with a timely objection and request a limiting instruction; but under Eighth Circuit law you can’t assume a contemporaneous instruction is mandatory — reversal requires showing an abuse of discretion and prejudice (harmless‑error review). Cutbank applied the harmless‑error/abuse‑of‑discretion framework and found no reversible error given the record and D.F.’s testimony.
- If an officer did improperly repeat testimonial statements and the victim later testifies, the Confrontation Clause claim typically fails on appeal under Charboneau as applied in Cutbank; evidentiary (hearsay) objections remain a separate ground and are subject to harmless‑error analysis.

Bottom line: Cutbank reflects the standard Eighth Circuit approach — officer repetition for context is generally allowed, courts have discretion about when to give limiting instructions (deferment to close of trial is permissible), and a victim’s later in‑court testimony cures a Confrontation Clause problem under the court’s precedent.
