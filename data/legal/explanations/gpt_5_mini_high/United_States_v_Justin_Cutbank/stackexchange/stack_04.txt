Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Was D.F.’s statement that her sister was killed by a gun properly admitted to explain inconsistencies, or should it have been excluded under Rule 403?
Question:
On cross, D.F. explained some inconsistent statements by saying she was unfamiliar with and disliked guns "as [her] sister was killed by a gun." The defense moved to strike but the court denied, and later a similar reference came in through C.F. without objection. How do courts weigh the probative value of this kind of explanation for inconsistency or bias against the risk of unfair prejudice under Rule 403? Did the defense "open the door" by probing D.F.’s inconsistent statements, making this explanation fair response? Would a limiting instruction have been appropriate?
Answer:
Short answer: The Eighth Circuit held the testimony was properly admitted. The court treated D.F.’s comment that her sister “was killed by a gun” as a responsive explanation for prior inconsistent statements about the gun, found it probative of her state of mind/credibility, and concluded that the district court did not abuse its broad Rule 403 discretion in admitting it (especially where C.F. later repeated the same fact without objection).

Why — the controlling legal points (as the opinion states)
- Responsive explanation/impeachment: D.F.’s remark came on cross when the defense pressed her about prior inconsistent statements about seeing the gun. The opinion says plainly that “This testimony responded to cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements,” which frames it as an explanation aimed at credibility — a highly probative purpose.  
- Rule 403 balancing and deference to the trial court: The district court must balance probative value against unfair prejudice under Fed. R. Evid. 403; the Eighth Circuit reviews that decision for abuse of discretion. The panel found that “Cutback points to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony, and none is apparent. See Fed. R. Evid. 403,” and therefore declined to disturb the ruling.  
- Tactical consequences and waiver: C.F. later testified to the same fact “without a defense objection,” which weakened any appellate claim of unfair surprise or prejudice. The court emphasized the district court’s “broad evidentiary discretion” (citing Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn) and applied harmless-error principles given the strength of the government’s evidence.

Did the defense “open the door”?
- Yes in effect. By impeaching D.F. with inconsistent statements about the gun, the defense invited an explanation; the court treated the sister’s death statement as a permissible, responsive explanation. The opinion frames the testimony as a direct response to the defense’s cross-examination, which is the classic “opening the door” dynamic courts allow: a party’s attack on credibility lets the witness explain motives/fear that account for inconsistencies.

Would a limiting instruction have been appropriate?
- It would have been a reasonable precaution: as the opinion notes (quoting United States v. Bennett), a district court need not give a limiting instruction at the moment evidence is admitted and can give an appropriate instruction at the close of trial. That said, the Eighth Circuit did not fault the court for failing to give (or for failing to give immediately) a limiting instruction here because no unfair prejudice was shown. So while a limiting instruction clarifying that the remark was for credibility/state-of-mind purposes would be prudent trial practice, its absence was not reversible error under the record the court reviewed.

Practical takeaways for trial practice
- If defense impeaches with prior inconsistent statements, expect the witness may give explanatory testimony about bias/fear and the court will likely admit it as probative.  
- If the explanatory statement raises a risk of unfair prejudice (e.g., graphic or emotionally charged collateral facts), ask for a limiting instruction when the evidence is admitted or at the close of evidence and preserve objections to later repetition by other witnesses.  
- On appeal, courts defer to the district court’s Rule 403 balancing and will reverse only for abuse of discretion or if the admission substantially swayed the jury.

Bottom line: Under the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank, the sister‑was‑killed remark was a permissible, probative explanation for a witness’s inconsistent statements; the court saw no Rule 403 problem, treated it as responsive to cross-examination (i.e., effectively opened by the defense), and found no reversible abuse of discretion.
