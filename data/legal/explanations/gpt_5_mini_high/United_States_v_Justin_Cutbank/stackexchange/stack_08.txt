Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the Eighth Circuit’s presumption of reasonableness for within-Guidelines sentences interact with the § 3553(a) “not greater than necessary” principle?
Question:
The court presumes a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable (Goodhouse) and says a district court need not mechanically recite the § 3553(a) factors (Masood, Maluoth). If a defendant argues that guideline enhancements (e.g., § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), serial number, reckless endangerment) overstate the offense and that a lower sentence is “sufficient, but not greater than necessary,” what must the district court say to show it considered and rejected those arguments? On appeal, what kinds of arguments or record gaps are sufficient to overcome the presumption of reasonableness in the Eighth Circuit?
Answer:
Short answer
- In the Eighth Circuit (as shown in Cutbank), a within‑Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal, but that presumption does not nullify § 3553(a)’s “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” duty. The presumption applies only after the district court has (1) correctly calculated the Guidelines and (2) the record shows the court considered the § 3553(a) factors and the defendant’s arguments. A district court need not “mechanically recite” each § 3553(a) factor; a clear on‑the‑record indication that it reviewed the PSR, briefs, and the § 3553(a) factors—as in Cutbank—is sufficient to generate the presumption. To overcome the presumption on appeal a defendant must show a significant procedural defect (e.g., miscalculation of the Guidelines), clear error in the facts supporting an enhancement, or an inadequate record showing the court failed to consider the defendant’s § 3553(a) arguments.

What the doctrine is (from Cutbank)
- Presumption on appeal: “a sentence within the applicable guidelines range . . . we presume is reasonable on appeal.” (Cutbank quoting United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023)).  
- Threshold for appellate review: before assessing substantive reasonableness, the court must ensure no significant procedural error (e.g., failing to calculate or miscalculating the Guidelines). (Cutbank quoting Gall and Rosales‑Mireles.)  
- No mechanical recital required: the district court “simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors.” (Cutbank quoting Maluoth.)

What a district court should put on the record (practical, grounded in Cutbank)
- At minimum, the record should show: (a) the Guidelines were calculated; (b) the court reviewed the PSR and sentencing submissions; and (c) the court considered the § 3553(a) factors and was aware of the defendant’s arguments about enhancements. In Cutbank the court “noted its review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors,” and the appellate court treated that as showing the court considered and rejected the defendant’s mitigation arguments. The court therefore need not parse every argument point‑by‑point, but it must make clear that it considered them.

How to preserve a challenge that a guideline enhancement “overstates the offense”
- Preserve a timely objection to the enhancement and develop the factual and legal record (so the district court can rule on the objection). If the defendant wants a lower sentence because an enhancement overstates the offense, ask the court to rule expressly on that contention or to state on the record why the enhancement (or the within‑Guidelines sentence) is nonetheless appropriate.

What will overcome the presumption on appeal (what succeeded or failed in Cutbank)
- Successful routes to rebut the presumption (as reflected in Cutbank’s reasoning and cited authorities):  
  1. Procedural error: show the Guidelines were not correctly calculated or a significant procedural mistake occurred (threshold from Rosales‑Mireles/Gall).  
  2. Clear factual error: show the factual basis for a guideline enhancement is clearly erroneous or unsupported (Cutbank rejected the challenge because the PSR facts were adopted and supported the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement; see Dunn for the standard).  
  3. Inadequate record of consideration: show the sentencing record does not make clear the district court considered the defendant’s § 3553(a) arguments (Masood/Maluoth). If the court never acknowledges the argument or the § 3553(a) factors, the presumption is weaker or unavailable.  
- Weak briefing or failure to develop the argument will not rebut the presumption (Cutbank criticized the defendant’s brief as giving “no cases and almost no analysis”).

Bottom line (how to think about it for practice)
- The Eighth Circuit treats a within‑Guidelines sentence as presumptively reasonable so long as the district court properly did the basics: calculate the Guidelines and show on the record that it considered the PSR, the parties’ submissions, and the § 3553(a) factors. If you want to overcome that presumption when arguing an enhancement overstates the offense, preserve objections, develop the record on the factual basis for the enhancement, and get the district court to address your § 3553(a) argument (or create a record showing a procedural or factual error). Cutbank illustrates that a brief acknowledgement on the record that the court reviewed the PSR and § 3553(a) can be enough to sustain a within‑Guidelines sentence on appeal.
