Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why did the court treat prior sightings of the same firearm as “intrinsic” rather than Rule 404(b) propensity evidence?
Question:
Both D.F. and C.F. testified they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle before the charged incident. The defense argued this was inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), but the court deemed it intrinsic and cited United States v. Battle for the idea that prior possession of the same gun shows ownership/control. I'm confused about the Eighth Circuit’s test for when evidence is “intrinsic” (as opposed to a 404(b) other act). How is this not propensity reasoning, and what limiting principles are there? Also, even if intrinsic, is there still a Rule 403 balancing the court must conduct, and what would that look like here?
Answer:
Short answer
The court treated the prior sightings as intrinsic because those sightings were offered not to prove Cutbank’s bad character but to prove an element of the charged offense — ownership/control and continuous possession of the very same sawed‑off Marlin .22. As the opinion puts it (quoting Battle), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” Because the testimony tended to prove possession/continuous possession of the particular gun, the court viewed it as intrinsic, not Rule 404(b) propensity evidence.

Why that is not (impermissible) propensity reasoning
Rule 404(b) bars proof of other crimes or acts when the government offers them solely to show a defendant’s character or propensity to commit the charged crime. The opinion shows the opposite framing here: the prior sightings were of the identical firearm and were offered to show ownership/control and continuous possession — facts that directly bear on the possession element of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g). The Eighth Circuit relied on United States v. Battle for the point that prior possession of the same weapon is “directly relevant” to later possession because it helps prove ownership/control. That is the classic distinction: evidence that proves (or makes more likely) an element of the offense is intrinsic; evidence offered only to prove bad character is a 404(b) other act.

Limiting principles (how to tell intrinsic from 404(b) evidence)
Grounded in the opinion’s reasoning, the limiting principle the court applied is functional: ask what the evidence is being used to prove. If the prior act (here, prior sightings of the same rifle) is probative of an element of the charged offense (ownership, control, identity, continuous possession of the very weapon), the court treats it as intrinsic. If the supposed “other act” does not have that direct probative link to an element but is offered to show the defendant’s propensity, it falls under Rule 404(b) and must satisfy the 404(b) framework. The opinion does not adopt a mechanical rule beyond that point; it simply applies Battle’s proposition that prior possession of the same gun directly helps to establish ownership/control.

Does Rule 403 still apply, and what would that balancing look like here?
Yes. Intrinsic evidence is not immune from Rule 403. The opinion itself cites Fed. R. Evid. 403 when rejecting the defendant’s arguments about unfair prejudice and notes that a district court’s evidentiary rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion. In practice a 403 balancing for this kind of evidence weighs:

- Probative value: high here because the testimony concerned the same sawed‑off Marlin .22 identified by multiple witnesses and because other physical evidence (the rifle was found on the likely path, DNA matched Cutbank, D.F.’s cell phone was near where the gun was found) strongly ties the weapon to the charged conduct; and
- Unfair prejudice: the risk that the jury will use the testimony as an illicit propensity inference (or be inflamed by violent-character evidence).

The district court admitted the prior‑sighting testimony and the appellate court concluded no abuse of discretion — noting also that the defendant pointed to no unfair prejudice and that the government’s testimonial and physical evidence was “substantial.” The opinion also notes that limiting instructions need not be given at the moment each item is admitted (citing Bennett) and that an erroneous evidentiary ruling will not warrant reversal unless the defendant shows the jury may have been substantially swayed (harmless‑error standard in DeMarce/Keys).

Bottom line
The Eighth Circuit treated the witnesses’ prior sightings as intrinsic because they were probative of ownership/control and continuous possession of the very gun that was central to the possession charge (the Battle principle). That classification avoids 404(b) analysis, but the district court still must (and here did) exercise Rule 403 discretion. On the facts the court found — same weapon, eyewitness testimony, DNA and other physical links — the probative value outweighed any unfair prejudice, and the appellate court found no abuse of discretion.
