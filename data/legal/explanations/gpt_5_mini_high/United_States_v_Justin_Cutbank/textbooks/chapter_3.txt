Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 3: # Other Trial Rulings: Hearsay, Confrontation, Rule 403, and Impeachment Mechanics

This chapter explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of several remaining evidentiary and procedural complaints raised by Justin Cutbank: the district court’s refusal to give an immediate limiting instruction, Confrontation Clause concerns about D.F.’s out‑of‑court statements to police, a Rule 403 challenge to brief testimony that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun, and the court’s limits on impeaching a witness with a police report. For each issue the court applied familiar standards (abuse of discretion for most evidentiary rulings, harmless‑error review where preservation or prejudice was contested) and resolved the claims against Cutbank. The following explains the doctrine and the opinion’s application in a way that helps you read similar appellate rulings.

## Limiting‑instruction timing and hearsay used to explain police conduct

Cutbank asked the court to instruct the jury that it could not treat D.F.’s statements to police as substantive proof of the events until D.F. herself testified. The district court declined to give any contemporaneous limiting instruction and instead let the evidence and later testimony stand. On appeal the court relied on United States v. Bennett to state the controlling principle: a trial judge does not abuse discretion by refusing to give a limiting instruction at the moment a particular item of evidence is admitted so long as an appropriate instruction is provided at the close of trial (or the court otherwise manages the evidence correctly). That rule recognizes the practicalities of trial procedure and gives judges leeway in sequencing instructions.

Two related legal points drove the result here. First, hearsay is an out‑of‑court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (see Fed. R. Evid. 801(c)). Not every repetition of what a victim told police is hearsay for purposes of admission; prosecutors commonly elicit those statements to explain why officers acted as they did (for example, to justify an investigative stop, a search, or the officer’s testimony about what they observed and did). When offered for that non‑truth purpose (to show the effect on the listener or the officers’ state of knowledge), the statements are not hearsay. The government argued, and the court found, that the officers’ testimony about what they were told was offered for context and did not admit hearsay to prove the truth of the victim’s account. Second, the court did not have to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction because the record showed no prejudicial hearsay problem actually occurred and D.F. later testified at trial; under Bennett the timing of instructions is a discretionary matter the district court may manage. The appellate court reviewed this decision for abuse of discretion and found none.

For a student: when you see a limiting‑instruction complaint, ask (1) whether the contested repetition was actually hearsay (i.e., offered for truth), (2) whether the declarant later testified, and (3) whether the judge gave any appropriate limiting instruction at all or otherwise controlled the record. If the court’s management of these elements is reasonable, appellate courts defer.

## Confrontation Clause: live testimony cures testimonial hearsay concerns

Cutbank also framed the requested limiting instruction as protecting his Confrontation Clause rights. That claim fails for a straightforward reason in the opinion: D.F. testified live at trial. The Confrontation Clause bars the admission of testimonial out‑of‑court statements against a defendant unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross‑examination. Conversely, when the declarant appears and testifies in court, the Clause is satisfied because the defendant can cross‑examine the declarant there. The court cited United States v. Charboneau for this proposition and held that D.F.’s in‑court testimony eliminated any Confrontation Clause problem. Thus, a live witness appearance ordinarily moots Confrontation challenges to that witness’s prior statements.

As a practical matter: if the defense’s Confrontation concern rests on a declarant’s out‑of‑court statements, check whether that declarant actually testified. If so, the Clause is usually satisfied and the issue becomes an evidentiary one (hearsay and limiting‑instruction choreography) rather than a constitutional bar.

## Rule 403 objection to testimony that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun

On cross‑examination to explain prior inconsistent statements, D.F. said she was “not familiar with guns” and that she “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The defense moved to strike that reference under Rule 403 (exclude if probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice). The district court denied the motion, and the appellate court affirmed.

The opinion applies the familiar Rule 403 balancing test and the deferential standard of review for evidentiary rulings. The key points are: the remark was responsive to cross‑examination about credibility and motive (it was offered to explain why D.F. might have made inconsistent statements about guns), it was brief and not elaborated into a graphic narrative, and the probative value of explaining the witness’s state of mind and credibility was not substantially outweighed by prejudicial impact. The court also noted that C.F. mentioned the same family history without objection, which undercuts any claim that the reference uniquely poisoned the jury. Sprint/United Management was cited for the principle that district courts have broad evidentiary discretion in these close calls.

For a student: Rule 403 exclusion requires a showing that evidence’s prejudicial effect substantially outweighs its probative value. A short, explanatory statement about motive or bias—especially when elicited on cross to explain inconsistencies—is often admissible because it bears directly on credibility and is less likely to be found so inflammatory as to warrant exclusion.

## Limits on using a police report to impeach: authentication and impeachment mechanics

Defense counsel tried to impeach C.F. by reading portions of a police investigator’s report. The court allowed cross‑examination about what C.F. told the investigator but sustained the government’s objection to counsel reading parts of the written report into the record without foundation because the report was not authored by C.F. The Eighth Circuit sustained that ruling, relying on United States v. Cumbie: while extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach with a prior inconsistent statement, the extrinsic document must be authenticated.

Two teaching points here. First, impeachment by extrinsic evidence is procedurally controlled: Fed. R. Evid. 613(b) (not named in the opinion, but the concept matters) permits use of prior inconsistent statements through extrinsic proof only after the witness is given the chance to explain or deny, and courts enforce authentication and foundation requirements before admitting documentary impeachment materials. Authentication (Rule 901) requires proof that the document is what the proponent says it is—if the report was not prepared by the witness, the proponent must lay a foundation that it accurately records the witness’s statement (for example, the witness’s acknowledgment that the report reflects what she said, testimony from the officer who prepared the report, or other indicia of reliability). Second, trial courts have discretion to protect the record’s integrity; permitting direct questions about what the witness told police preserved the defense’s impeachment theory while denying an uncontrolled reading of a third‑party document. The court again reviewed for abuse of discretion and found none.

For students: when you read an appellate ruling about impeachment with reports, look for whether counsel attempted to authenticate the document, whether the witness was first confronted with the inconsistency, and whether the court offered alternative means (such as oral questioning) that preserve the impeachment value without admitting the raw report.

## Appellate briefing, preservation, and harmless‑error context

The opinion repeatedly notes that Cutbank’s briefing was thin—“no cases and almost no analysis”—and that the government in turn tried to expand the appellate issues. That comment is itself instructive: appellate courts expect developed arguments with legal authority; failure to brief an issue adequately may lead to dismissal of the argument or a summary treatment. Preservation matters too: many evidentiary rulings were reviewed for abuse of discretion because proper objections were made at trial; but when an objection was not made or foundation not laid, the standard can be plain error or the issue can be treated as forfeited.

Finally, even if an evidentiary error occurred, the court applied harmless‑error analysis (citing DeMarce and Keys) and concluded that any mistaken rulings did not substantially sway the jury given the weight of the government’s testimonial and physical evidence (DNA on the gun, witnesses identifying the weapon, officer injuries, etc.). Thus, a defendant must show that an evidentiary error had a substantial influence on the verdict; speculative prejudice is insufficient.

Concluding takeaway: when you read an opinion resolving multiple trial rulings, identify (1) what the district court actually admitted or excluded, (2) whether the declarant testified live, (3) the party’s preservation and the standard of appellate review, (4) any authenticity or foundation problems, and (5) how the appellate court treats prejudice (harmless‑error analysis). These lenses explain why discretionary trial rulings are frequently affirmed and how to spot the narrow circumstances where reversal is possible.
