Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case arises from a violent home incident on the night of November 19–20, 2020, after which a jury convicted Justin Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm, 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The jury also found that Cutbank had three or more prior violent-felony convictions, triggering the enhanced penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). At sentencing the district court adopted the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and applied multiple Guidelines enhancements, producing an advisory range of 292–365 months; the court imposed 292 months. On appeal Cutbank challenged several pretrial and in-trial evidentiary rulings and the district court’s application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (the 4‑level enhancement for possessing a firearm “in connection with” another felony). The Eighth Circuit affirmed. The paragraphs that follow set out the facts that led to the charge, the discovery and identification of the firearm, the trial outcome including the ACCA finding, the sentencing calculation, and the issues raised on appeal.

## Offense conduct on November 19–20, 2020

The core criminal episode began when, after drinking and using methamphetamine, Cutbank awoke his girlfriend (referred to in the opinion as D.F.), pointed a gun at her face, and forced her into a closet. According to D.F.’s testimony, he accused people of “listening” and of waiting outside to harm him, threatened to kill her, and struck her across the face with the gun. During the episode he ordered D.F. to wake others in the house, including her teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. The violent, armed confinement and the physical assault on D.F. are the actus reus that put the firearm at the center of the criminal case: they show not just presence of a weapon in the house but active control and threatening use, facts the prosecution used at trial to prove possession and to support sentencing enhancement arguments tied to violent conduct.

## Subsequent events: flight, garage barricade, and resistance

After the confrontation Cutbank fled the residence with D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. Separately, police received a report about a man refusing to leave a garage roughly half a mile away. When officers arrived, Cutbank had barricaded himself in the garage, made erratic statements including claims that he had a weapon, and resisted detention. The resisting conduct was physical and violent: the opinion recounts that Cutbank bit an officer in the face and neck and grabbed the officer’s gun. These later events mattered at trial as factual background and, at sentencing, supported a PSR enhancement for creating a reckless risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police.

## Recovery and identification of physical evidence

Officers searched the path Cutbank likely took between the two locations and recovered D.F.’s phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. Forensic testing tied Cutbank to the rifle: DNA on the weapon matched him. At trial both D.F. and C.F. identified the sawed-off rifle as the gun they had seen Cutbank with prior to that night. Those identifications and the DNA link were central pieces of physical and testimonial evidence tying Cutbank to the firearm. The district court treated the witnesses’ testimony that they had observed Cutbank with the rifle previously as intrinsic — not prohibited propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) — because prior possession of the same weapon bears directly on ownership or control.

## Charges and trial proceedings

Cutbank was charged with violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) (felon in possession). The jury convicted him of that offense and also made the separate finding that he had three or more prior violent-felony convictions, a factual finding that activates ACCA’s enhanced statutory penalties. Pretrial and in-trial disputes focused on evidentiary matters: Cutbank sought to exclude testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen him with the sawed-off rifle, arguing it was improper 404(b) propensity evidence; the court allowed the testimony as intrinsic. Defense counsel requested a limiting jury instruction to prevent the jury from using D.F.’s statements to police as substantive proof before she testified; the court did not give a contemporaneous limiting instruction, but D.F. later testified in person, and the court and the Eighth Circuit relied on the settled principle that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant appears and testifies. Other evidentiary rulings included denial of a motion to strike testimony in which D.F. explained inconsistencies by referring to her sister’s death by a gun, and limiting the defense’s attempt to read portions of a police report into the record for impeachment when the report lacked proper authentication. The district court’s evidentiary rulings were reviewed on appeal under the abuse-of-discretion standard and, where applicable, any errors were evaluated for harmlessness.

## Verdict, PSR calculations, and sentence

The PSR treated the firearm as a weapon described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) (a sawed-off firearm), which set the base offense level at 26. The PSR then recommended multiple specific increases: a three-level increase for an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police, and a four-level increase under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because the rifle was used or possessed “in connection with” another felony. The PSR’s arithmetic produced a total offense level of 35; with a Criminal History Category VI, the advisory Guidelines range was 292–365 months. At sentencing the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection, reviewed the § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a within-Guidelines sentence of 292 months. The opinion notes the interaction with the Armed Career Criminal Guideline: applying USSG §4B1.4(b) would have produced an offense level of 34, but the Guidelines direct that the greater applicable offense level controls, so the higher total offense level from the PSR governed.

## Issues raised on appeal and appellate posture

On appeal Cutbank attacked a string of pretrial and in-trial evidentiary rulings, arguing they deprived him of a fair trial; he also argued the district court improperly applied the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement and that the resulting within-Guidelines sentence conflicted with § 3553(a)’s requirement that a sentence be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary.” The Eighth Circuit framed the evidentiary challenges against the applicable standards: district courts have broad discretion on Rule 404(b), hearsay, and limiting-instruction questions (reviewed for abuse of discretion), and extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated. For sentencing, the court emphasized that a correct Guidelines calculation is a procedural prerequisite to any substantive-reasonableness review (Gall; Rosales‑Mireles), and that a within-Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal. Because the district court adopted the PSR’s facts without objection, considered the § 3553(a) factors, and sentenced within the calculated range, the appellate court affirmed both conviction and sentence.

This posture — contested evidentiary rulings at trial, a PSR that drove a substantial Guidelines range, a within-Guidelines sentence, and an appeal asserting both trial and sentencing errors — frames the Eighth Circuit’s legal analysis in the remainder of the opinion.
