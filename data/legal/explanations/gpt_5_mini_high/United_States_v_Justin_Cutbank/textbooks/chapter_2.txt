Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 2: # Proving Possession: Intrinsic Evidence vs. Rule 404(b)

This chapter explains why the Eighth Circuit upheld the district court’s decision to allow testimony that witnesses had seen Justin Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle before the night of the charged offense. The court treated that testimony as intrinsic evidence—directly probative of the possession offense—rather than forbidden “other‑acts” propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b).

## Intrinsic evidence contrasted with Rule 404(b) propensity evidence

Rule 404(b) bars evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts when offered solely to prove a defendant’s character or propensity to commit the charged crime. But not every fact about past conduct is a 404(b) problem. Evidence is “intrinsic” (and therefore not governed by Rule 404(b)) when it directly proves the charged offense or is inextricably intertwined with it—e.g., when it helps complete the story of the crime, shows identity, or otherwise supplies a direct link to the offense charged. The Cutbank opinion follows this familiar line: prior acts that are directly probative of the charged possession (rather than being used to show general bad character) are not “other‑acts” for 404(b) purposes.

## Defense motion to exclude prior‑sighting testimony; government’s theory of continuous possession of the same weapon

Cutbank moved before trial to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with the sawed‑off rifle before the incident, arguing it was propensity evidence barred by Rule 404(b). The government’s response—and the theory the district court accepted—was that such testimony was intrinsic evidence of continuous possession of the same weapon. Under that theory, evidence that a defendant had the particular gun on prior occasions is directly relevant to establishing ownership or control at the time of the charged offense and to bridging any temporal gap between the earlier sightings and the criminal episode. Because the testimony tended to show that the rifle found near the scene was the same weapon the defendant had previously possessed, the government argued it was not being offered to show Cutbank’s bad character but to prove an essential element of the charged offense: possession.

## Eighth Circuit precedent: United States v. Battle—prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to later possession because it shows ownership/control

The court relied on Eighth Circuit precedent, especially United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), for the governing principle. Battle explained that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” That ruling treats prior sightings or possession of the identical weapon as probative of the later possession charged in the indictment and therefore as intrinsic evidence, not as 404(b) propensity evidence. The Cutbank court applied that holding to the facts here.

## Application in Cutbank: D.F.’s “handful of times,” C.F.’s testimony, and the district court’s rulings

On the facts, D.F. testified at trial that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off rifle a “handful of times” before the night at issue; C.F. gave similar testimony. The district court, after hearing the pretrial motion, agreed with the government that those prior sightings were intrinsic and admissible to prove continuous possession and control of the weapon. At trial the court denied Cutbank’s renewed objections and admitted the witnesses’ testimony. The Eighth Circuit affirmed those rulings: the prior‑sighting testimony was not “other‑acts” evidence under Rule 404(b) but direct proof bearing on who controlled and owned the weapon at the time of the charged offense.

## Standard of review (abuse of discretion) and the defendant’s failure to supply contrary authority on appeal

Evidentiary rulings about admissibility and whether evidence is intrinsic or subject to Rule 404(b) are reviewed on appeal for abuse of discretion. Under that deferential standard the appellate court will affirm unless the district court’s decision was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on a legal error. The Cutbank panel emphasized that Cutbank’s briefs contained almost no cases or analysis challenging the district court’s ruling; given the binding Battle precedent and the district court’s reasoned finding that the testimony bore directly on ownership/control and continuity of possession, the appellate court found no abuse of discretion. In short, admitting testimony that witnesses had earlier seen the defendant with the same firearm fit squarely into the line of Eighth Circuit authority treating prior possession of the identical weapon as intrinsic, properly admissible evidence.
