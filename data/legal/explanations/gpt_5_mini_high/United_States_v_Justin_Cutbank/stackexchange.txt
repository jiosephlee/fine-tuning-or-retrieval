Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why did the court treat prior sightings of the same firearm as “intrinsic” rather than Rule 404(b) propensity evidence?
Question:
Both D.F. and C.F. testified they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle before the charged incident. The defense argued this was inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), but the court deemed it intrinsic and cited United States v. Battle for the idea that prior possession of the same gun shows ownership/control. I'm confused about the Eighth Circuit’s test for when evidence is “intrinsic” (as opposed to a 404(b) other act). How is this not propensity reasoning, and what limiting principles are there? Also, even if intrinsic, is there still a Rule 403 balancing the court must conduct, and what would that look like here?
Answer:
Short answer
The court treated the prior sightings as intrinsic because those sightings were offered not to prove Cutbank’s bad character but to prove an element of the charged offense — ownership/control and continuous possession of the very same sawed‑off Marlin .22. As the opinion puts it (quoting Battle), “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” Because the testimony tended to prove possession/continuous possession of the particular gun, the court viewed it as intrinsic, not Rule 404(b) propensity evidence.

Why that is not (impermissible) propensity reasoning
Rule 404(b) bars proof of other crimes or acts when the government offers them solely to show a defendant’s character or propensity to commit the charged crime. The opinion shows the opposite framing here: the prior sightings were of the identical firearm and were offered to show ownership/control and continuous possession — facts that directly bear on the possession element of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g). The Eighth Circuit relied on United States v. Battle for the point that prior possession of the same weapon is “directly relevant” to later possession because it helps prove ownership/control. That is the classic distinction: evidence that proves (or makes more likely) an element of the offense is intrinsic; evidence offered only to prove bad character is a 404(b) other act.

Limiting principles (how to tell intrinsic from 404(b) evidence)
Grounded in the opinion’s reasoning, the limiting principle the court applied is functional: ask what the evidence is being used to prove. If the prior act (here, prior sightings of the same rifle) is probative of an element of the charged offense (ownership, control, identity, continuous possession of the very weapon), the court treats it as intrinsic. If the supposed “other act” does not have that direct probative link to an element but is offered to show the defendant’s propensity, it falls under Rule 404(b) and must satisfy the 404(b) framework. The opinion does not adopt a mechanical rule beyond that point; it simply applies Battle’s proposition that prior possession of the same gun directly helps to establish ownership/control.

Does Rule 403 still apply, and what would that balancing look like here?
Yes. Intrinsic evidence is not immune from Rule 403. The opinion itself cites Fed. R. Evid. 403 when rejecting the defendant’s arguments about unfair prejudice and notes that a district court’s evidentiary rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion. In practice a 403 balancing for this kind of evidence weighs:

- Probative value: high here because the testimony concerned the same sawed‑off Marlin .22 identified by multiple witnesses and because other physical evidence (the rifle was found on the likely path, DNA matched Cutbank, D.F.’s cell phone was near where the gun was found) strongly ties the weapon to the charged conduct; and
- Unfair prejudice: the risk that the jury will use the testimony as an illicit propensity inference (or be inflamed by violent-character evidence).

The district court admitted the prior‑sighting testimony and the appellate court concluded no abuse of discretion — noting also that the defendant pointed to no unfair prejudice and that the government’s testimonial and physical evidence was “substantial.” The opinion also notes that limiting instructions need not be given at the moment each item is admitted (citing Bennett) and that an erroneous evidentiary ruling will not warrant reversal unless the defendant shows the jury may have been substantially swayed (harmless‑error standard in DeMarce/Keys).

Bottom line
The Eighth Circuit treated the witnesses’ prior sightings as intrinsic because they were probative of ownership/control and continuous possession of the very gun that was central to the possession charge (the Battle principle). That classification avoids 404(b) analysis, but the district court still must (and here did) exercise Rule 403 discretion. On the facts the court found — same weapon, eyewitness testimony, DNA and other physical links — the probative value outweighed any unfair prejudice, and the appellate court found no abuse of discretion.

### When can officers repeat a victim’s statements “for context” without violating hearsay rules, and does later in-court testimony by the victim cure any Confrontation Clause problem?
Question:
Pretrial, the defense asked for a limiting instruction so the jury wouldn’t consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence before she testified. The court declined to give an immediate instruction, the opinion says the trial did not admit hearsay, and notes the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because D.F. later testified. How do Eighth Circuit courts distinguish permissible "context" testimony (effect on listener) from impermissible hearsay where officers recount what a victim said? Is a contemporaneous limiting instruction ever required, or is a final instruction sufficient? If some officer testimony did slip into hearsay territory, does the victim’s later testimony eliminate any Confrontation Clause issue under cases like Crawford/Charboneau?
Answer:
Short answer
- Eighth Circuit law (as applied in Cutbank) draws the line by purpose: officers may repeat a victim’s out‑of‑court statements to explain the officers’ knowledge, motive, or actions (the statement’s effect on the listener) — that is treated as non‑hearsay — but may not recount those statements to prove the truth of what the victim asserted unless the statement fits a hearsay exception or the declarant testifies. (Cutbank II(B) (government’s argument that statements “establish the context for police officer actions and their knowledge at the time”); district court found no hearsay admitted.)
- A contemporaneous limiting instruction is not automatically required; the district court may defer to a closing instruction. The Eighth Circuit reviews denial of a contemporaneous instruction for abuse of discretion and has held a close‑of‑trial instruction can be sufficient. (Cutbank citing United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014).)
- If an officer’s testimony did slip into hearsay territory but the declarant later appears and testifies, the Confrontation Clause problem is cured under Eighth Circuit precedent — Charboneau — and Cutbank applied that rule: because D.F. later testified, the panel held the Confrontation Clause was satisfied. (Cutbank II(B) (quoting United States v. Charboneau, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010)).)

Short explanation and how this played out in Cutbank
- The operative evidentiary distinction is the purpose for which the out‑of‑court statement is offered. If the government offers what the victim told police to explain why officers did what they did (what they knew, why they investigated, why they secured the scene), that testimony is admitted for its effect on the listener and is not hearsay in the sense that it’s not being offered to prove the literal truth of the victim’s assertion. Cutbank records the government’s exact argument that the victim’s statements were offered to “establish the context for police officer actions and their knowledge at the time,” and the court concluded the record showed no hearsay was admitted.
- Timing of limiting instructions: the court in Cutbank applied Bennett — the district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” The trial court’s choice to decline the pretrial/ contemporaneous limiting instruction was reviewed for abuse of discretion and the Eighth Circuit found no abuse here.
- Confrontation Clause cure: the panel relied on Charboneau for the straightforward rule that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Because D.F. testified at trial, the panel concluded there was no Confrontation Clause deprivation.

Practical implications / what to watch for
- If the officer’s testimony is framed as explaining police conduct or what the officer heard (effect on listener), the Eighth Circuit treats that as permissible context testimony. If the same testimony is used to prove the facts of the victim’s account (i.e., offered for the truth), it is hearsay and must fit an exception or be excluded unless the declarant testifies.
- If you fear prejudice, preserve the issue with a timely objection and request a limiting instruction; but under Eighth Circuit law you can’t assume a contemporaneous instruction is mandatory — reversal requires showing an abuse of discretion and prejudice (harmless‑error review). Cutbank applied the harmless‑error/abuse‑of‑discretion framework and found no reversible error given the record and D.F.’s testimony.
- If an officer did improperly repeat testimonial statements and the victim later testifies, the Confrontation Clause claim typically fails on appeal under Charboneau as applied in Cutbank; evidentiary (hearsay) objections remain a separate ground and are subject to harmless‑error analysis.

Bottom line: Cutbank reflects the standard Eighth Circuit approach — officer repetition for context is generally allowed, courts have discretion about when to give limiting instructions (deferment to close of trial is permissible), and a victim’s later in‑court testimony cures a Confrontation Clause problem under the court’s precedent.

### How could the defense properly impeach C.F. using a police report she didn’t write?
Question:
The defense tried to undermine C.F.’s “once or twice” prior-sighting testimony by reading portions of a police report. The court stopped counsel from reading the report into the record because C.F. did not author it and there was no proper authentication. Under FRE 613(b), what is the correct way to impeach a witness with a prior inconsistent statement memorialized in a third-party officer’s report? Do you need to call the officer to testify and authenticate the report, or get the witness to adopt the statement? Could FRE 803(5) (recorded recollection) or 612 (refreshing recollection) have been used? Why wasn’t reading the report aloud permissible as impeachment-only evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
You cannot just read aloud a police report written by an officer to impeach a witness who did not write it. Under Fed. R. Evid. 613(b) the proper sequence is: (1) confront the witness on cross about the prior statement; then (2) either get the witness to adopt/acknowledge the statement (so the inconsistency is established on the record) or authenticate the officer’s report (usually by calling the officer who prepared it). Rule 612 can be used to refresh the witness’s memory; Rule 803(5) (recorded recollection) only helps if the writing was made or adopted by the witness when the matter was fresh. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank upheld the district court’s refusal to let defense counsel read the officer’s report into evidence because the report was not authored by C.F. and lacked the required authentication. See United States v. Justin Cutbank, No. 24‑1225, II.E (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) (citing United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022): “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.”).

Why the court stopped the defense reading the report
- Authentication requirement: Extrinsic impeachment material must be authenticated under Rule 901—the court refused to admit portions of a police report that C.F. did not write because there was no proper foundation that the document was what the defense claimed it was. Cutbank II.E (district court allowed questions about what C.F. told police but sustained objections to reading the report because it “was not authored by C.F.”).
- Hearsay/substantive-use risk: A third‑party police report contains out‑of‑court statements and, absent a proper foundation or exception, is hearsay if read for the truth of what it records. Even when used only for impeachment, courts require authentication and will police the use of such documents so juries are not treated to unchecked hearsay.

How to do it properly (practical sequence)
1. Confront on cross (Rule 613(b) step). Ask the witness point‑blank: “Did you tell Officer Jones on Nov. 20 that you had seen the rifle ‘once or twice’ before?” Rule 613(b) requires the witness be given an opportunity to explain or deny prior inconsistent statements before extrinsic evidence is admitted.
2. If the witness admits the prior statement, you’re done (no need to read the report).
3. If the witness denies or claims no recollection, attempt to refresh under Rule 612: show the report to the witness to refresh memory. If the witness regains recollection and testifies consistent with the report, no extrinsic admission is needed. If she still cannot or will not recall, proceed to authentication.
4. Authenticate the report: call the officer who prepared the report to identify it and testify that it fairly records what the witness told him (FRE 901). That officer’s testimony supplies the necessary foundation to put the document before the jury as extrinsic impeachment evidence (subject to any limiting instruction). Alternatively, if the witness “adopts” the report on the stand (e.g., “Yes, that’s what I told the officer”), that adoption itself helps authenticate the writing and establishes the prior statement for impeachment purposes.
5. Recorded recollection (FRE 803(5)) is a narrow escape hatch: it applies only where the record was made or adopted by the witness when the matter was fresh and accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge. A police report written only by the officer normally will not qualify unless the witness in fact made or adopted the writing at the time.

Why FRE 803(5) and 612 were unlikely to salvage reading the report in Cutbank
- Rule 612 (refreshing recollection) was available and, in fact, the court allowed questions about what C.F. told police; but 612 is a memory‑aid procedure — it lets the witness review a writing and then testify if memory is restored. It does not by itself permit counsel to read the officer’s report to the jury.
- Rule 803(5) (recorded recollection) would permit reading a writing into evidence only if the witness made or adopted that writing when the matter was fresh and now cannot recall. The opinion shows the district court’s concern: the report was “not authored by C.F.,” so absent adoption it would not be an obvious fit for 803(5). The safer route (and what the court suggested by its ruling) was to authenticate through the officer or get the witness to adopt the content.

Bottom line (what defense counsel should have done)
- First, confront C.F. directly on cross about the inconsistent statement (Rule 613(b)); try to get her to admit she said it or to adopt the written report.
- If she denies it or won’t adopt, call the officer who prepared the report to authenticate it before offering it as extrinsic impeachment evidence (or move to admit it under a proper hearsay exception only if the factual prerequisites are satisfied).
- Don’t assume you can simply read an officer’s report into the record for impeachment—Cutbank confirms the district court was within its discretion to require authentication before admission.

### Was D.F.’s statement that her sister was killed by a gun properly admitted to explain inconsistencies, or should it have been excluded under Rule 403?
Question:
On cross, D.F. explained some inconsistent statements by saying she was unfamiliar with and disliked guns "as [her] sister was killed by a gun." The defense moved to strike but the court denied, and later a similar reference came in through C.F. without objection. How do courts weigh the probative value of this kind of explanation for inconsistency or bias against the risk of unfair prejudice under Rule 403? Did the defense "open the door" by probing D.F.’s inconsistent statements, making this explanation fair response? Would a limiting instruction have been appropriate?
Answer:
Short answer: The Eighth Circuit held the testimony was properly admitted. The court treated D.F.’s comment that her sister “was killed by a gun” as a responsive explanation for prior inconsistent statements about the gun, found it probative of her state of mind/credibility, and concluded that the district court did not abuse its broad Rule 403 discretion in admitting it (especially where C.F. later repeated the same fact without objection).

Why — the controlling legal points (as the opinion states)
- Responsive explanation/impeachment: D.F.’s remark came on cross when the defense pressed her about prior inconsistent statements about seeing the gun. The opinion says plainly that “This testimony responded to cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements,” which frames it as an explanation aimed at credibility — a highly probative purpose.  
- Rule 403 balancing and deference to the trial court: The district court must balance probative value against unfair prejudice under Fed. R. Evid. 403; the Eighth Circuit reviews that decision for abuse of discretion. The panel found that “Cutback points to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony, and none is apparent. See Fed. R. Evid. 403,” and therefore declined to disturb the ruling.  
- Tactical consequences and waiver: C.F. later testified to the same fact “without a defense objection,” which weakened any appellate claim of unfair surprise or prejudice. The court emphasized the district court’s “broad evidentiary discretion” (citing Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn) and applied harmless-error principles given the strength of the government’s evidence.

Did the defense “open the door”?
- Yes in effect. By impeaching D.F. with inconsistent statements about the gun, the defense invited an explanation; the court treated the sister’s death statement as a permissible, responsive explanation. The opinion frames the testimony as a direct response to the defense’s cross-examination, which is the classic “opening the door” dynamic courts allow: a party’s attack on credibility lets the witness explain motives/fear that account for inconsistencies.

Would a limiting instruction have been appropriate?
- It would have been a reasonable precaution: as the opinion notes (quoting United States v. Bennett), a district court need not give a limiting instruction at the moment evidence is admitted and can give an appropriate instruction at the close of trial. That said, the Eighth Circuit did not fault the court for failing to give (or for failing to give immediately) a limiting instruction here because no unfair prejudice was shown. So while a limiting instruction clarifying that the remark was for credibility/state-of-mind purposes would be prudent trial practice, its absence was not reversible error under the record the court reviewed.

Practical takeaways for trial practice
- If defense impeaches with prior inconsistent statements, expect the witness may give explanatory testimony about bias/fear and the court will likely admit it as probative.  
- If the explanatory statement raises a risk of unfair prejudice (e.g., graphic or emotionally charged collateral facts), ask for a limiting instruction when the evidence is admitted or at the close of evidence and preserve objections to later repetition by other witnesses.  
- On appeal, courts defer to the district court’s Rule 403 balancing and will reverse only for abuse of discretion or if the admission substantially swayed the jury.

Bottom line: Under the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank, the sister‑was‑killed remark was a permissible, probative explanation for a witness’s inconsistent statements; the court saw no Rule 403 problem, treated it as responsive to cross-examination (i.e., effectively opened by the defense), and found no reversible abuse of discretion.

### What exactly qualified as the “other felony offense” for § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and can the court rely on unobjected-to PSR facts to apply it?
Question:
The PSR recommended a 4-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony, and the court adopted the PSR’s facts without objection. The opinion then says the court adopted "factual statements regarding [Cutbank’s] prior felony convictions" and "thus" found the gun was used in connection with another felony—this seems like a possible typo because prior convictions wouldn’t prove the nexus for the instant offense. Which felony was the "other" one here (e.g., kidnapping/false imprisonment, felony assault, terroristic threats, or resisting with injury)? What is the Eighth Circuit’s required nexus under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (facilitated or had the potential to facilitate)? Is a preponderance standard applied, and is it proper to rely on unobjected-to PSR factual recitations alone to support the enhancement?
Answer:
Short answer
- The “other felony” the court relied on was the violent, contemporaneous conduct described in the PSR — i.e., the incident conduct (pointing the gun at D.F., keeping her in a closet at gunpoint, threatening and striking her, the assault/domestic‑abuse/kidnaping facts and the injury to an officer while resisting arrest). The opinion does not single out one label but affirms the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on the PSR narrative describing those felonies.  
- The opinion applies the Guidelines language that the enhancement attaches where the firearm was “used … in connection with another … felony” (USSG §2K2.1, comment. n.14(C)), but it does not in this decision spell out a separate doctrinal test (e.g., “facilitated” vs. merely “had the potential to facilitate”) or expressly state the burden of proof. What the opinion does show is that actual use in the commission of violent felonies (not merely a prior conviction) was sufficient on these facts.  
- Yes — the district court may rely on PSR factual recitations adopted at sentencing when the defendant does not object. The Eighth Circuit upheld the enhancement because the district court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements,” and the court found no clear error on appeal.

Relevant excerpts and analysis grounded in the opinion
- What the enhancement requires (as the opinion quotes): “This enhancement applies if a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense ‘regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.’ § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)).” The opinion therefore treats an uncharged or unconvicted felony as potentially qualifying if tied to the firearm use.  
- Which felony supported the enhancement here: the opinion’s factual narrative shows that during the November 19–20, 2020 incident Cutbank “awakened his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a gun at her face, and directed her into a closet, making paranoid accusations … He kept D.F. in the closet at gunpoint … threatening her life and striking her across the face with the gun.” The PSR recommended the four‑level increase “because he possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense,” and at sentencing the court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements.” The court later summarizes the conduct as involving “domestic abuse, kidnaping, assault in the victim’s home … and injuring a police officer while resisting arrest.” The opinion therefore rests the enhancement on those incident felonies described in the PSR (rather than on mere prior convictions).  
- On the apparent drafting error in the opinion: the opinion says the court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutback’s prior felony convictions. Thus, the court did not err … in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.” That phrasing is confusing (and the appellant’s point is correct as a matter of logic): prior convictions by themselves do not prove nexus between the gun and the instant conduct. Read sensibly in context, the court meant that it adopted the PSR’s factual narrative about the instant offense (the facts described above), and those adopted facts established the necessary connection.  
- Burden of proof and appellate review: the opinion does not state a burden‑of‑proof (it does not say “preponderance” here) but it does treat the district court’s adoption of unobjected‑to PSR facts as dispositive and reviews the district court’s factual finding deferentially — citing Dunn for the applicable standard of review and saying the court “did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.” In short: failure to object at sentencing let the district court adopt those PSR facts, and the Eighth Circuit will reverse only for clear error.

Practical takeaways for sentencing practice
- If a defendant wants to contest a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, object at sentencing to the relevant PSR recitations and offer contrary evidence: unobjected‑to PSR facts can and will be treated as adopted and will support the enhancement on appeal absent clear error.  
- In cases like this one the court relied on the PSR’s narrative of active gun use (pointing, threats, detention, assault), so active use in the course of contemporaneous felonious conduct is plainly sufficient on these facts.

Bottom line
The Eighth Circuit affirmed the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) increase based on the PSR’s adopted narrative of the instant violent offenses (kidnaping/assault/terroristic threats and resisting with injury). The opinion applies the Guidelines phrasing “used … in connection with another … felony” but does not in this opinion elaborate a separate “facilitated vs. potential” test or state the burden of proof; procedurally, the dispositive point was that the district court adopted the PSR facts without objection and the appellate court found no clear error.

### Is base offense level 26 under § 2K2.1(a) correct for a sawed-off rifle plus two prior crimes of violence?
Question:
The opinion says the PSR set a base offense level of 26 because the offense involved a § 5845(a) sawed-off firearm and Cutbank had at least two prior crimes of violence. I’m having trouble mapping that to the text of § 2K2.1(a) in the current Guidelines: some subsections hinge on the type of firearm (e.g., § 5845(a) firearms), others on number of prior crimes of violence or controlled substance offenses, and some combinations set different base levels (e.g., 22, 24, or 26). Which exact subsection(s) of § 2K2.1(a) in effect at the time support a 26 here, and why isn’t the correct base offense level 22 or 24 instead?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the PSR’s choice of base offense level 26 was correct. Under the edition of USSG §2K2.1 in effect for Cutbank, §2K2.1(a) lists alternative “base offense level” buckets and you pick the greatest one that fits the facts. One alternative gives a base level of 26 for an offense involving a firearm described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) (a short‑barreled/sawed‑off weapon); another alternative gives a base level of 26 where the defendant committed the instant offense after at least two prior felony convictions for a crime of violence or a controlled‑substance offense. Because the PSR found both: (1) the Marlin .22 had a sawed‑off barrel/stock (a § 5845(a) firearm) and (2) Cutbank had at least two prior crimes of violence, the PSR properly selected the 26 “bucket.” The district court adopted the PSR without objection, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

Why not 22 or 24? Those lower base levels are alternative buckets in §2K2.1(a) tied to different fact patterns (for example, fewer or different priors or a different kind of firearm). You do not combine or average the alternatives — you apply the single greatest alternative that matches the facts. Because one (indeed two) of the alternatives that carry a 26 base level applied here, 26 — not 22 or 24 — was correct.

Support in the opinion: the court summarizes the PSR’s reasoning: “The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended a base offense level of 26 because the offense involved a sawed‑off firearm described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) and Cutback had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence,” and then describes the other enhancements that produced the total offense level the court adopted.

### Does applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) on top of ACCA, while also considering § 4B1.4’s “greater of” rule, risk impermissible double-counting?
Question:
The panel notes the Armed Career Criminal Guideline (§ 4B1.4) would have yielded offense level 34, but the § 2K2.1 route with enhancements produced 35, so the greater applied. If the same conduct (possessing the gun "in connection with" another felony) could both trigger § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and satisfy § 4B1.4(b)(3)(A)’s 34 level, is that double-counting? Or does the "use the greater of" directive resolve any double-counting concern because only the higher path is used? Is there Eighth Circuit authority squarely addressing this interaction?
Answer:
Short answer
No—at least in Cutbank the Eighth Circuit treated this as a guidelines‑mechanics issue, not impermissible double‑counting. The court upheld the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four‑level enhancement and then applied USSG §4B1.4(b)’s “greater of” rule (compute both offense‑level routes and take the higher), and it found no error or §3553(a) violation. United States v. Cutbank, No. 24‑1225, slip op. at Section III (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).

Why (short explanation tied to the opinion)
- The PSR in Cutbank calculated a total offense level of 35 by applying the §2K2.1 enhancements (including the four‑level §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement). The Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced total offense level 34. The Guidelines themselves resolve that conflict: “The Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b).” Slip op., § III.
- The panel reasoned that the district court adopted the PSR’s facts (so the finding that the gun was “in connection with” another felony was not clearly erroneous) and that the Guidelines’ text prescribes the answer here—compute both routes and use the higher offense level. The court therefore affirmed the calculation and the within‑Guidelines sentence. Slip op., § III.

What this means for a double‑counting challenge
- “Double‑counting” challenges succeed when a sentencing court impermissibly uses the same conduct twice in a way the Guidelines do not authorize. Cutbank shows that where the Guidelines expressly provide a tie‑breaker (here §4B1.4(b)’s “greater of” instruction), applying the §2K2.1 enhancement and then selecting the higher offense level under §4B1.4(b) is not treated as impermissible double‑counting in the Eighth Circuit facts presented.
- The panel did consider (and reject) the defendant’s related §3553(a) argument that the enhancement produced an excessive sentence, but it did so on the record showing the district court considered the §3553(a) factors and imposed a within‑Guidelines term (which is presumptively reasonable on appeal). Slip op., § III.

Is there Eighth Circuit authority squarely on point?
- Yes: United States v. Cutbank itself is the controlling Eighth Circuit panel decision that addresses this interaction and affirms applying §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) when the court then uses USSG §4B1.4(b) to select the greater offense level. The opinion does not undertake a broader doctrinal analysis of “double‑counting” beyond applying the Guidelines’ own rule, but it is the on‑point Eighth Circuit treatment of the precise situation you describe.

### How does the Eighth Circuit’s presumption of reasonableness for within-Guidelines sentences interact with the § 3553(a) “not greater than necessary” principle?
Question:
The court presumes a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable (Goodhouse) and says a district court need not mechanically recite the § 3553(a) factors (Masood, Maluoth). If a defendant argues that guideline enhancements (e.g., § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), serial number, reckless endangerment) overstate the offense and that a lower sentence is “sufficient, but not greater than necessary,” what must the district court say to show it considered and rejected those arguments? On appeal, what kinds of arguments or record gaps are sufficient to overcome the presumption of reasonableness in the Eighth Circuit?
Answer:
Short answer
- In the Eighth Circuit (as shown in Cutbank), a within‑Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal, but that presumption does not nullify § 3553(a)’s “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” duty. The presumption applies only after the district court has (1) correctly calculated the Guidelines and (2) the record shows the court considered the § 3553(a) factors and the defendant’s arguments. A district court need not “mechanically recite” each § 3553(a) factor; a clear on‑the‑record indication that it reviewed the PSR, briefs, and the § 3553(a) factors—as in Cutbank—is sufficient to generate the presumption. To overcome the presumption on appeal a defendant must show a significant procedural defect (e.g., miscalculation of the Guidelines), clear error in the facts supporting an enhancement, or an inadequate record showing the court failed to consider the defendant’s § 3553(a) arguments.

What the doctrine is (from Cutbank)
- Presumption on appeal: “a sentence within the applicable guidelines range . . . we presume is reasonable on appeal.” (Cutbank quoting United States v. Goodhouse, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023)).  
- Threshold for appellate review: before assessing substantive reasonableness, the court must ensure no significant procedural error (e.g., failing to calculate or miscalculating the Guidelines). (Cutbank quoting Gall and Rosales‑Mireles.)  
- No mechanical recital required: the district court “simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors.” (Cutbank quoting Maluoth.)

What a district court should put on the record (practical, grounded in Cutbank)
- At minimum, the record should show: (a) the Guidelines were calculated; (b) the court reviewed the PSR and sentencing submissions; and (c) the court considered the § 3553(a) factors and was aware of the defendant’s arguments about enhancements. In Cutbank the court “noted its review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors,” and the appellate court treated that as showing the court considered and rejected the defendant’s mitigation arguments. The court therefore need not parse every argument point‑by‑point, but it must make clear that it considered them.

How to preserve a challenge that a guideline enhancement “overstates the offense”
- Preserve a timely objection to the enhancement and develop the factual and legal record (so the district court can rule on the objection). If the defendant wants a lower sentence because an enhancement overstates the offense, ask the court to rule expressly on that contention or to state on the record why the enhancement (or the within‑Guidelines sentence) is nonetheless appropriate.

What will overcome the presumption on appeal (what succeeded or failed in Cutbank)
- Successful routes to rebut the presumption (as reflected in Cutbank’s reasoning and cited authorities):  
  1. Procedural error: show the Guidelines were not correctly calculated or a significant procedural mistake occurred (threshold from Rosales‑Mireles/Gall).  
  2. Clear factual error: show the factual basis for a guideline enhancement is clearly erroneous or unsupported (Cutbank rejected the challenge because the PSR facts were adopted and supported the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement; see Dunn for the standard).  
  3. Inadequate record of consideration: show the sentencing record does not make clear the district court considered the defendant’s § 3553(a) arguments (Masood/Maluoth). If the court never acknowledges the argument or the § 3553(a) factors, the presumption is weaker or unavailable.  
- Weak briefing or failure to develop the argument will not rebut the presumption (Cutbank criticized the defendant’s brief as giving “no cases and almost no analysis”).

Bottom line (how to think about it for practice)
- The Eighth Circuit treats a within‑Guidelines sentence as presumptively reasonable so long as the district court properly did the basics: calculate the Guidelines and show on the record that it considered the PSR, the parties’ submissions, and the § 3553(a) factors. If you want to overcome that presumption when arguing an enhancement overstates the offense, preserve objections, develop the record on the factual basis for the enhancement, and get the district court to address your § 3553(a) argument (or create a record showing a procedural or factual error). Cutbank illustrates that a brief acknowledgement on the record that the court reviewed the PSR and § 3553(a) can be enough to sustain a within‑Guidelines sentence on appeal.
