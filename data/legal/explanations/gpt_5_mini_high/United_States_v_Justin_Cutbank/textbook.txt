Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case arises from a violent home incident on the night of November 19–20, 2020, after which a jury convicted Justin Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm, 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The jury also found that Cutbank had three or more prior violent-felony convictions, triggering the enhanced penalties of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). At sentencing the district court adopted the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and applied multiple Guidelines enhancements, producing an advisory range of 292–365 months; the court imposed 292 months. On appeal Cutbank challenged several pretrial and in-trial evidentiary rulings and the district court’s application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (the 4‑level enhancement for possessing a firearm “in connection with” another felony). The Eighth Circuit affirmed. The paragraphs that follow set out the facts that led to the charge, the discovery and identification of the firearm, the trial outcome including the ACCA finding, the sentencing calculation, and the issues raised on appeal.

## Offense conduct on November 19–20, 2020

The core criminal episode began when, after drinking and using methamphetamine, Cutbank awoke his girlfriend (referred to in the opinion as D.F.), pointed a gun at her face, and forced her into a closet. According to D.F.’s testimony, he accused people of “listening” and of waiting outside to harm him, threatened to kill her, and struck her across the face with the gun. During the episode he ordered D.F. to wake others in the house, including her teenage sons and her housemate, C.F. The violent, armed confinement and the physical assault on D.F. are the actus reus that put the firearm at the center of the criminal case: they show not just presence of a weapon in the house but active control and threatening use, facts the prosecution used at trial to prove possession and to support sentencing enhancement arguments tied to violent conduct.

## Subsequent events: flight, garage barricade, and resistance

After the confrontation Cutbank fled the residence with D.F.’s cell phone and the gun. Separately, police received a report about a man refusing to leave a garage roughly half a mile away. When officers arrived, Cutbank had barricaded himself in the garage, made erratic statements including claims that he had a weapon, and resisted detention. The resisting conduct was physical and violent: the opinion recounts that Cutbank bit an officer in the face and neck and grabbed the officer’s gun. These later events mattered at trial as factual background and, at sentencing, supported a PSR enhancement for creating a reckless risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police.

## Recovery and identification of physical evidence

Officers searched the path Cutbank likely took between the two locations and recovered D.F.’s phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. Forensic testing tied Cutbank to the rifle: DNA on the weapon matched him. At trial both D.F. and C.F. identified the sawed-off rifle as the gun they had seen Cutbank with prior to that night. Those identifications and the DNA link were central pieces of physical and testimonial evidence tying Cutbank to the firearm. The district court treated the witnesses’ testimony that they had observed Cutbank with the rifle previously as intrinsic — not prohibited propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) — because prior possession of the same weapon bears directly on ownership or control.

## Charges and trial proceedings

Cutbank was charged with violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) (felon in possession). The jury convicted him of that offense and also made the separate finding that he had three or more prior violent-felony convictions, a factual finding that activates ACCA’s enhanced statutory penalties. Pretrial and in-trial disputes focused on evidentiary matters: Cutbank sought to exclude testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen him with the sawed-off rifle, arguing it was improper 404(b) propensity evidence; the court allowed the testimony as intrinsic. Defense counsel requested a limiting jury instruction to prevent the jury from using D.F.’s statements to police as substantive proof before she testified; the court did not give a contemporaneous limiting instruction, but D.F. later testified in person, and the court and the Eighth Circuit relied on the settled principle that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant appears and testifies. Other evidentiary rulings included denial of a motion to strike testimony in which D.F. explained inconsistencies by referring to her sister’s death by a gun, and limiting the defense’s attempt to read portions of a police report into the record for impeachment when the report lacked proper authentication. The district court’s evidentiary rulings were reviewed on appeal under the abuse-of-discretion standard and, where applicable, any errors were evaluated for harmlessness.

## Verdict, PSR calculations, and sentence

The PSR treated the firearm as a weapon described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a) (a sawed-off firearm), which set the base offense level at 26. The PSR then recommended multiple specific increases: a three-level increase for an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police, and a four-level increase under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because the rifle was used or possessed “in connection with” another felony. The PSR’s arithmetic produced a total offense level of 35; with a Criminal History Category VI, the advisory Guidelines range was 292–365 months. At sentencing the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection, reviewed the § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a within-Guidelines sentence of 292 months. The opinion notes the interaction with the Armed Career Criminal Guideline: applying USSG §4B1.4(b) would have produced an offense level of 34, but the Guidelines direct that the greater applicable offense level controls, so the higher total offense level from the PSR governed.

## Issues raised on appeal and appellate posture

On appeal Cutbank attacked a string of pretrial and in-trial evidentiary rulings, arguing they deprived him of a fair trial; he also argued the district court improperly applied the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement and that the resulting within-Guidelines sentence conflicted with § 3553(a)’s requirement that a sentence be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary.” The Eighth Circuit framed the evidentiary challenges against the applicable standards: district courts have broad discretion on Rule 404(b), hearsay, and limiting-instruction questions (reviewed for abuse of discretion), and extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated. For sentencing, the court emphasized that a correct Guidelines calculation is a procedural prerequisite to any substantive-reasonableness review (Gall; Rosales‑Mireles), and that a within-Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal. Because the district court adopted the PSR’s facts without objection, considered the § 3553(a) factors, and sentenced within the calculated range, the appellate court affirmed both conviction and sentence.

This posture — contested evidentiary rulings at trial, a PSR that drove a substantial Guidelines range, a within-Guidelines sentence, and an appeal asserting both trial and sentencing errors — frames the Eighth Circuit’s legal analysis in the remainder of the opinion.

# Proving Possession: Intrinsic Evidence vs. Rule 404(b)

This chapter explains why the Eighth Circuit upheld the district court’s decision to allow testimony that witnesses had seen Justin Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle before the night of the charged offense. The court treated that testimony as intrinsic evidence—directly probative of the possession offense—rather than forbidden “other‑acts” propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b).

## Intrinsic evidence contrasted with Rule 404(b) propensity evidence

Rule 404(b) bars evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts when offered solely to prove a defendant’s character or propensity to commit the charged crime. But not every fact about past conduct is a 404(b) problem. Evidence is “intrinsic” (and therefore not governed by Rule 404(b)) when it directly proves the charged offense or is inextricably intertwined with it—e.g., when it helps complete the story of the crime, shows identity, or otherwise supplies a direct link to the offense charged. The Cutbank opinion follows this familiar line: prior acts that are directly probative of the charged possession (rather than being used to show general bad character) are not “other‑acts” for 404(b) purposes.

## Defense motion to exclude prior‑sighting testimony; government’s theory of continuous possession of the same weapon

Cutbank moved before trial to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with the sawed‑off rifle before the incident, arguing it was propensity evidence barred by Rule 404(b). The government’s response—and the theory the district court accepted—was that such testimony was intrinsic evidence of continuous possession of the same weapon. Under that theory, evidence that a defendant had the particular gun on prior occasions is directly relevant to establishing ownership or control at the time of the charged offense and to bridging any temporal gap between the earlier sightings and the criminal episode. Because the testimony tended to show that the rifle found near the scene was the same weapon the defendant had previously possessed, the government argued it was not being offered to show Cutbank’s bad character but to prove an essential element of the charged offense: possession.

## Eighth Circuit precedent: United States v. Battle—prior possession of the same firearm is directly relevant to later possession because it shows ownership/control

The court relied on Eighth Circuit precedent, especially United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), for the governing principle. Battle explained that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” That ruling treats prior sightings or possession of the identical weapon as probative of the later possession charged in the indictment and therefore as intrinsic evidence, not as 404(b) propensity evidence. The Cutbank court applied that holding to the facts here.

## Application in Cutbank: D.F.’s “handful of times,” C.F.’s testimony, and the district court’s rulings

On the facts, D.F. testified at trial that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off rifle a “handful of times” before the night at issue; C.F. gave similar testimony. The district court, after hearing the pretrial motion, agreed with the government that those prior sightings were intrinsic and admissible to prove continuous possession and control of the weapon. At trial the court denied Cutbank’s renewed objections and admitted the witnesses’ testimony. The Eighth Circuit affirmed those rulings: the prior‑sighting testimony was not “other‑acts” evidence under Rule 404(b) but direct proof bearing on who controlled and owned the weapon at the time of the charged offense.

## Standard of review (abuse of discretion) and the defendant’s failure to supply contrary authority on appeal

Evidentiary rulings about admissibility and whether evidence is intrinsic or subject to Rule 404(b) are reviewed on appeal for abuse of discretion. Under that deferential standard the appellate court will affirm unless the district court’s decision was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on a legal error. The Cutbank panel emphasized that Cutbank’s briefs contained almost no cases or analysis challenging the district court’s ruling; given the binding Battle precedent and the district court’s reasoned finding that the testimony bore directly on ownership/control and continuity of possession, the appellate court found no abuse of discretion. In short, admitting testimony that witnesses had earlier seen the defendant with the same firearm fit squarely into the line of Eighth Circuit authority treating prior possession of the identical weapon as intrinsic, properly admissible evidence.

# Other Trial Rulings: Hearsay, Confrontation, Rule 403, and Impeachment Mechanics

This chapter explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of several remaining evidentiary and procedural complaints raised by Justin Cutbank: the district court’s refusal to give an immediate limiting instruction, Confrontation Clause concerns about D.F.’s out‑of‑court statements to police, a Rule 403 challenge to brief testimony that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun, and the court’s limits on impeaching a witness with a police report. For each issue the court applied familiar standards (abuse of discretion for most evidentiary rulings, harmless‑error review where preservation or prejudice was contested) and resolved the claims against Cutbank. The following explains the doctrine and the opinion’s application in a way that helps you read similar appellate rulings.

## Limiting‑instruction timing and hearsay used to explain police conduct

Cutbank asked the court to instruct the jury that it could not treat D.F.’s statements to police as substantive proof of the events until D.F. herself testified. The district court declined to give any contemporaneous limiting instruction and instead let the evidence and later testimony stand. On appeal the court relied on United States v. Bennett to state the controlling principle: a trial judge does not abuse discretion by refusing to give a limiting instruction at the moment a particular item of evidence is admitted so long as an appropriate instruction is provided at the close of trial (or the court otherwise manages the evidence correctly). That rule recognizes the practicalities of trial procedure and gives judges leeway in sequencing instructions.

Two related legal points drove the result here. First, hearsay is an out‑of‑court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (see Fed. R. Evid. 801(c)). Not every repetition of what a victim told police is hearsay for purposes of admission; prosecutors commonly elicit those statements to explain why officers acted as they did (for example, to justify an investigative stop, a search, or the officer’s testimony about what they observed and did). When offered for that non‑truth purpose (to show the effect on the listener or the officers’ state of knowledge), the statements are not hearsay. The government argued, and the court found, that the officers’ testimony about what they were told was offered for context and did not admit hearsay to prove the truth of the victim’s account. Second, the court did not have to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction because the record showed no prejudicial hearsay problem actually occurred and D.F. later testified at trial; under Bennett the timing of instructions is a discretionary matter the district court may manage. The appellate court reviewed this decision for abuse of discretion and found none.

For a student: when you see a limiting‑instruction complaint, ask (1) whether the contested repetition was actually hearsay (i.e., offered for truth), (2) whether the declarant later testified, and (3) whether the judge gave any appropriate limiting instruction at all or otherwise controlled the record. If the court’s management of these elements is reasonable, appellate courts defer.

## Confrontation Clause: live testimony cures testimonial hearsay concerns

Cutbank also framed the requested limiting instruction as protecting his Confrontation Clause rights. That claim fails for a straightforward reason in the opinion: D.F. testified live at trial. The Confrontation Clause bars the admission of testimonial out‑of‑court statements against a defendant unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross‑examination. Conversely, when the declarant appears and testifies in court, the Clause is satisfied because the defendant can cross‑examine the declarant there. The court cited United States v. Charboneau for this proposition and held that D.F.’s in‑court testimony eliminated any Confrontation Clause problem. Thus, a live witness appearance ordinarily moots Confrontation challenges to that witness’s prior statements.

As a practical matter: if the defense’s Confrontation concern rests on a declarant’s out‑of‑court statements, check whether that declarant actually testified. If so, the Clause is usually satisfied and the issue becomes an evidentiary one (hearsay and limiting‑instruction choreography) rather than a constitutional bar.

## Rule 403 objection to testimony that D.F.’s sister was killed by a gun

On cross‑examination to explain prior inconsistent statements, D.F. said she was “not familiar with guns” and that she “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” The defense moved to strike that reference under Rule 403 (exclude if probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice). The district court denied the motion, and the appellate court affirmed.

The opinion applies the familiar Rule 403 balancing test and the deferential standard of review for evidentiary rulings. The key points are: the remark was responsive to cross‑examination about credibility and motive (it was offered to explain why D.F. might have made inconsistent statements about guns), it was brief and not elaborated into a graphic narrative, and the probative value of explaining the witness’s state of mind and credibility was not substantially outweighed by prejudicial impact. The court also noted that C.F. mentioned the same family history without objection, which undercuts any claim that the reference uniquely poisoned the jury. Sprint/United Management was cited for the principle that district courts have broad evidentiary discretion in these close calls.

For a student: Rule 403 exclusion requires a showing that evidence’s prejudicial effect substantially outweighs its probative value. A short, explanatory statement about motive or bias—especially when elicited on cross to explain inconsistencies—is often admissible because it bears directly on credibility and is less likely to be found so inflammatory as to warrant exclusion.

## Limits on using a police report to impeach: authentication and impeachment mechanics

Defense counsel tried to impeach C.F. by reading portions of a police investigator’s report. The court allowed cross‑examination about what C.F. told the investigator but sustained the government’s objection to counsel reading parts of the written report into the record without foundation because the report was not authored by C.F. The Eighth Circuit sustained that ruling, relying on United States v. Cumbie: while extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach with a prior inconsistent statement, the extrinsic document must be authenticated.

Two teaching points here. First, impeachment by extrinsic evidence is procedurally controlled: Fed. R. Evid. 613(b) (not named in the opinion, but the concept matters) permits use of prior inconsistent statements through extrinsic proof only after the witness is given the chance to explain or deny, and courts enforce authentication and foundation requirements before admitting documentary impeachment materials. Authentication (Rule 901) requires proof that the document is what the proponent says it is—if the report was not prepared by the witness, the proponent must lay a foundation that it accurately records the witness’s statement (for example, the witness’s acknowledgment that the report reflects what she said, testimony from the officer who prepared the report, or other indicia of reliability). Second, trial courts have discretion to protect the record’s integrity; permitting direct questions about what the witness told police preserved the defense’s impeachment theory while denying an uncontrolled reading of a third‑party document. The court again reviewed for abuse of discretion and found none.

For students: when you read an appellate ruling about impeachment with reports, look for whether counsel attempted to authenticate the document, whether the witness was first confronted with the inconsistency, and whether the court offered alternative means (such as oral questioning) that preserve the impeachment value without admitting the raw report.

## Appellate briefing, preservation, and harmless‑error context

The opinion repeatedly notes that Cutbank’s briefing was thin—“no cases and almost no analysis”—and that the government in turn tried to expand the appellate issues. That comment is itself instructive: appellate courts expect developed arguments with legal authority; failure to brief an issue adequately may lead to dismissal of the argument or a summary treatment. Preservation matters too: many evidentiary rulings were reviewed for abuse of discretion because proper objections were made at trial; but when an objection was not made or foundation not laid, the standard can be plain error or the issue can be treated as forfeited.

Finally, even if an evidentiary error occurred, the court applied harmless‑error analysis (citing DeMarce and Keys) and concluded that any mistaken rulings did not substantially sway the jury given the weight of the government’s testimonial and physical evidence (DNA on the gun, witnesses identifying the weapon, officer injuries, etc.). Thus, a defendant must show that an evidentiary error had a substantial influence on the verdict; speculative prejudice is insufficient.

Concluding takeaway: when you read an opinion resolving multiple trial rulings, identify (1) what the district court actually admitted or excluded, (2) whether the declarant testified live, (3) the party’s preservation and the standard of appellate review, (4) any authenticity or foundation problems, and (5) how the appellate court treats prejudice (harmless‑error analysis). These lenses explain why discretionary trial rulings are frequently affirmed and how to spot the narrow circumstances where reversal is possible.

# Harmless and Cumulative Error: Why Any Alleged Missteps Did Not Matter

## Harmless error standard (United States v. DeMarce)

When a defendant claims an evidentiary ruling at trial was erroneous, the appeal has two layers. First, the appellate court asks whether the district court abused its discretion in admitting or excluding evidence. Second, even if error occurred, the court asks whether the error was harmless. In United States v. DeMarce, the Eighth Circuit stated the controlling practical rule for non‑constitutional evidentiary errors: an appellate court will not reverse unless the improperly admitted (or excluded) evidence may have been substantially persuasive to the jury. Minor or “slight” influence is insufficient to upset a verdict. Put simply, the appellant must show a reasonable possibility that the error affected the jury’s verdict; if the rest of the record plainly supports the verdict, the error is harmless.

This standard focuses attention on the record as a whole. The reviewing court looks to the nature of the erroneous evidence, whether it was cumulative of other proof, whether it bore on a central contested issue, and how strongly the government’s other evidence pointed to guilt. The Eighth Circuit applied that DeMarce standard here: even assuming some rulings were erroneous, Cutbank could not show the jury was substantially swayed by them.

## Cumulative error limits (United States v. Keys)

Relatedly, the court applies a cumulative‑error doctrine when multiple trial rulings are challenged. United States v. Keys states the principle: multiple individually harmless errors do not automatically add up to reversible error; the defendant must demonstrate substantial prejudice from the aggregate effect of the errors. The inquiry asks whether the combined effect of the errors deprived the defendant of a fair trial, looked at against the rest of the admissible evidence and the issues actually contested at trial.

In practice the court asks whether the errors, considered together, undermined confidence in the outcome. If the same uncontested, powerful proof independently established the critical elements of the offense, the cumulative impact of peripheral mistakes is unlikely to be substantial.

## Application to this record: strong physical evidence and consistent eyewitness identifications

Applying DeMarce and Keys to Cutbank’s appeals, the Eighth Circuit treated each challenged ruling and then examined the trial record as a whole. Several rulings are at issue: the court allowed the victim and a housemate to testify that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle before the night in question (the district court had ruled that such testimony was intrinsic evidence rather than improper propensity evidence); the court refused a pretrial request for a contemporaneous limiting instruction about the victim’s out‑of‑court statements (although the victim later testified in person); the court declined to strike testimony in which the victim explained that she disliked guns because her sister had been killed by a gun; and the court prevented the defense from reading portions of an investigator’s report into the record without authentication while permitting cross‑examination about prior statements. Cutbank’s brief provided little legal analysis or authority on these points, and the district court’s discretionary rulings were largely sustainable on the merits. Even setting that aside, the harmless‑error and cumulative‑error frameworks supplied the decisive analysis.

The record contained multiple, independent pieces of strong evidence tying Cutbank to the particular firearm and to possession of it on the night charged. Officers searching the likely route between the victim’s house and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself recovered the victim’s cell phone, a butcher‑style knife, and the Marlin .22 rifle with a sawed‑off barrel and stock. DNA testing matched Cutbank to the rifle. Both the victim (D.F.) and the housemate (C.F.) identified that same weapon as one they had seen Cutbank with previously. There was also testimony about Cutbank’s behavior at the scene—barricading in the garage, claiming to have a weapon, resisting officers, biting an officer and grabbing his gun—which corroborated continuous possession and control of a gun during the relevant period.

Given that combination of physical and testimonial evidence, the court found that any arguably improper evidentiary admissions could not have substantially swayed the jury. The prior‑possession testimony that Cutbank sought to exclude was admissible as intrinsic evidence (it tended to show ownership or control of the weapon) and, even if one assumed it was erroneously admitted, it was cumulative of the DNA match, recovery of the rifle along the victim’s route, and the eyewitness identifications. The trial court’s decision not to give an immediate limiting instruction did not result in hearsay or Confrontation Clause error because the victim later testified in open court—so the jury heard the declarant’s testimony firsthand. The passing mention that the victim’s sister had been killed by a gun was elicited in response to cross‑examination about inconsistent statements; the defense did not show unfair prejudice under Rule 403, and another government witness mentioned the same fact without objection. And the court’s insistence on authentication before allowing the defense to read a police‑investigator’s report into evidence rested on established law governing extrinsic impeachment; the defense was still able to impeach the witness by asking what she told police.

Because the improperly admitted (or disputed) items were either properly admissible, cumulative, or peripheral in light of the DNA, the physical recovery of the rifle and phone along the route, and consistent eyewitness identifications, the Eighth Circuit concluded that the evidentiary rulings “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Under DeMarce and Keys, that is not reversible error.

## Conclusion: no reversible evidentiary error; any assumed missteps were harmless

For students reading the opinion, the lesson is twofold. First, appellate harmless‑error review is outcome‑oriented: a defendant must identify prejudice, not merely error. Second, when the government presents independent, persuasive physical evidence and consistent witness identifications, appellate courts are unlikely to find that marginal evidentiary mistakes—especially those not central to the prosecution’s case or that produced only cumulative proof—undermined the fairness of the trial. Applying those principles here, the Eighth Circuit affirmed because Cutbank could not show that any alleged errors, taken alone or together, substantially swayed the jury.

# Sentencing: The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement, ACCA Interplay, and Reasonableness Review

This chapter explains how the Eighth Circuit analyzed the sentencing issues in United States v. Cutbank, focusing on the four‑level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm “in connection with” another felony, why the district court’s reliance on the unobjected Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) was dispositive, how the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) interacted with the Guidelines calculation, and why the within‑range 292‑month sentence was upheld as reasonable.

## Guideline math from the PSR

The PSR’s offense‑level math is simple and controls the Guidelines range the court used. The PSR started with a base offense level of 26 (reflecting that the offense involved a sawed‑off firearm as defined in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)). It then added a 3‑level increase for an altered/obliterated serial number, a 2‑level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing police, and the 4‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for possession of the firearm “in connection with” another felony. Adding those adjustments (26 + 3 + 2 + 4) produced a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, that total offense level yielded an advisory Guidelines range of 292 to 365 months. The district court adopted the PSR and sentenced Cutbank to the bottom of that range: 292 months.

## Scope of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

The enhancement at issue applies when a defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with any other felony offense,” and the Guidelines commentary makes explicit that this applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” (USSG § 2K2.1, comment. n.14(C), quoted in the opinion.) In Cutbank the PSR and the record showed felonious conduct tied to the firearm—domestic violence and kidnapping of the victim in her home (in the presence of children), plus violent resistance and injury to an officer during the arrest sequence. The court treated those crimes and the weapon’s role in them as the factual predicate for the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. In short: the Guidelines permit the enhancement based on felony conduct involving the gun even if those felony acts were not separately prosecuted, and the district court found that factual connection here.

## Factual basis and appellate review

An important procedural point underlies the court’s analysis: the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements “without objection.” Where the PSR is adopted and the defendant does not object to its facts, a sentencing court may rely on those facts in applying Guidelines enhancements. On appeal, such factual findings are reviewed for clear error. The Eighth Circuit cited United States v. Dunn for that standard and concluded Cutbank could not show clear error because he had not preserved or substantiated such a challenge. Practically, that means a defendant who wants to contest the factual basis for an enhancement tied to the PSR must lodge timely and specific objections in the district court; failing to do so makes appellate reversal unlikely.

## ACCA interaction and the greater‑of rule

Cutbank’s conviction also triggered the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), which exposes defendants with three or more qualifying violent felonies to enhanced statutory penalties. The Guidelines have a parallel “Armed Career Criminal” guideline framework (USSG § 4B1.4). The court noted that, under the ACCA/GUIDELINES calculation, Cutbank would have had a total offense level of 34. But USSG § 4B1.4(b) instructs the sentencing court to apply the greater offense level when multiple Guidelines provisions could apply. Because the § 2K2.1‑based calculation (with the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement) yielded offense level 35—higher than the ACCA‑based 34—the district court properly used level 35. That choice is grounded in the Guidelines’ “greater‑of” rule rather than any mistrust of ACCA; the result simply follows the mechanics the Guidelines themselves prescribe.

## Procedural reasonableness: correct Guidelines calculation as starting point

The opinion reiterates the familiar two‑step structure of federal sentencing review. First, a district court must correctly calculate the applicable Guidelines range; Gall v. United States establishes that as the starting point for sentencing, and Rosales‑Mireles v. United States confirms that an appellate court must ensure no significant procedural error (for example, an incorrect Guidelines calculation) before reaching substantive reasonableness. Here, because the district court adopted the PSR facts and applied the Guidelines adjustments that followed, the court committed no procedural error in arriving at the 292–365 month range.

## Substantive reasonableness: deference to within‑range sentences

After confirming the Guidelines range, the appellate court considered substantive reasonableness. The Eighth Circuit applies deferential review and treats within‑Guidelines sentences as presumptively reasonable on appeal (citing United States v. Goodhouse). The district court need not give a rote, factor‑by‑factor recital of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) to show it considered those factors; the record need only make it clear the court actually considered them (the opinion cites United States v. Masood and United States v. Maluoth on that point). The district court had reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda and expressly considered § 3553(a) arguments, including Cutbank’s plea that the sentence be no greater than necessary. Given Cutbank’s extensive criminal history—nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms—and the aggravated facts of the instant offense (domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault in the victim’s home in the presence of children, and injuring an officer during resistance), the court concluded a within‑Guidelines sentence was appropriate. The appellate court found no abuse of the district court’s substantial sentencing discretion and affirmed.

## Holding and practical takeaways

The Eighth Circuit affirmed Cutbank’s 292‑month sentence. The court held that (1) the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was properly applied under the Guidelines and supported by the PSR’s unobjected facts; (2) the Guidelines’ greater‑of rule justified using the higher offense level rather than the ACCA‑based level; and (3) the within‑range sentence was both procedurally and substantively reasonable.

For a sentencing practitioner or student, the opinion illustrates three practical points: timely and specific objections to PSR facts are critical if a defendant hopes to avoid or challenge Guidelines enhancements; § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) can be applied based on felony conduct tied to the firearm even without separate prosecution of that felony; and within‑Guidelines sentences enjoy substantial appellate deference so long as the district court properly calculates the range and shows it considered the § 3553(a) factors.
