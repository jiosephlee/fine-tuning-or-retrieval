Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic or 404(b)? The Eighth Circuit Reaffirms Prior Gun Sightings as Proof of Possession

## Why this matters

Proving knowing possession is the central evidentiary hurdle in most § 922(g) prosecutions. When the firearm is recovered away from the charged scene, eyewitness testimony that the defendant had been seen with the same gun before the offense can be decisive. In United States v. Cutbank, the Eighth Circuit again treated such testimony as intrinsic — not Rule 404(b) — evidence, clearing the way for prosecutors to introduce witnesses’ “prior sightings” of the very weapon recovered. The decision is a reminder that in the Eighth Circuit the identity and continuity of possession are powerful engines for admissibility, and that defendants must deploy focused pretrial and trial tactics if they want to keep prior-sighting testimony out of the jury’s hands.

## What the court did

The panel affirmed Cutbank’s conviction and sentence. Factually, the case involved a sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle recovered along a likely path between a victim’s home and where Cutbank later barricaded himself; DNA on the gun matched him. Witnesses testified that they had seen Cutbank with that gun “a handful of times” or “once or twice” before the night of the offense. Cutbank argued pretrial and at trial that the witnesses’ statements about earlier sightings were barred as propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), or otherwise prejudicial under Rule 403. The district court admitted the testimony as intrinsic evidence of continuous possession and ownership; the Eighth Circuit affirmed, relying on United States v. Battle and emphasizing that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

The court also rebuffed related evidentiary claims: it found no Confrontation Clause or hearsay problem because the declarant testified, it upheld the trial court’s authentication requirement for police reports used in impeachment, and it declined to reverse based on cumulative error given overwhelming government proof.

## The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning — and its limits

The Eighth Circuit’s treatment is doctrinally orthodox as applied to these facts. The intrinsic/extrinsic distinction turns on whether the evidence is necessary to complete the story of the charged offense or is not independent of it. When a recovered firearm can be tied to the defendant through prior consumptive acts — multiple eyewitness identifications of the same weapon, unique physical features (here, a sawed‑off barrel and stock), and forensic links — prior sightings do more than show a general proclivity to carry guns; they tend to prove ownership or control and therefore fit comfortably within the “intrinsic” category. The Battle line, expressly relied on by the panel, captures that logic.

That said, the opinion does not create a bright‑line rule that all testimony that a defendant was seen with guns is intrinsic. The controlling feature in Cutbank was the nexus: the witnesses pointed to (1) the same, identifiable weapon, (2) in a factual narrative that supplied continuity between earlier possession and the time of the offense, and (3) corroborating physical and forensic evidence. Absent those elements — for example, where prior sightings are of different guns, are vaguely described, are temporally remote, or where multiple people had equal access to the weapon — an “intrinsic” label will be harder to justify.

## What this means for prosecutors and defense counsel

For prosecutors, Cutbank reinforces that a careful proffer and foundation can place prior-sighting testimony beyond the reach of Rule 404(b). If the government can show identity of the weapon (distinctive markings, sawed‑off parts, serial‑number issues) and factual continuity, the testimony will likely be admitted, and Rule 404(b) notice is not required for true intrinsic evidence. Prosecutors should therefore develop witnesses’ descriptions of the weapon, preserve corroborating physical or forensic links, and be prepared to explain how the prior sightings complete the story of possession rather than simply show a propensity for guns.

For defenders, Cutbank is a cautionary tale: the Eighth Circuit will defer to district courts’ evidentiary judgments, and appellate courts will sustain rulings unless the admission is an abuse of discretion or produces substantial prejudice. But the decision is not the last word — it identifies where litigators should fight.

## Practical defense strategies

Defense counsel should attack prior‑sighting testimony at multiple points. First, press a focused Rule 403 motion in limine: emphasize the risk that testimony about “seeing him with a gun” will invite the jury to infer criminal propensity, and highlight any inflammatory collateral facts (domestic violence, presence of children, mention of a prior homicide in the family, etc.) that increase the risk of unfair prejudice. Ask the court to limit the number of witnesses allowed to testify about prior sightings or to limit the scope of their testimony to narrowly tailored identification evidence.

Second, narrow the temporal and descriptive window. Challenge the admissibility unless the government can show a sufficiently close temporal relationship and a reliable basis for identity — e.g., distinctive features or markings that connect the earlier sightings to the recovered weapon. If the prior sightings are years old, imprecise, or describe different guns, press that they are extrinsic and require 404(b) notice and balancing.

Third, insist on strict authentication. Where the government wants to use police reports, investigators’ notes, or third‑party statements to impeach, require proper foundation so the court cannot admit unsworn narrative summaries without authentication (the Cumbie line the panel cited). Likewise, probe and exploit weaknesses in forensic links (ambiguous DNA, lack of fingerprints, porous chain of custody) to undermine the government’s continuity narrative.

Fourth, seek contemporaneous limiting instructions and, if necessary, a curative instruction when collateral, inflammatory testimony slips in. While the Eighth Circuit observed that a court need not give a limiting instruction at the moment of admission, many judges will do so if asked, and an early caution can blunt juror misuse.

Finally, at trial use cross‑examination to emphasize alternative explanations and the unreliability of memory: show differences in witnesses’ descriptions, the conditions under which they “saw” the weapon, and other persons who had access to the firearm. If the defendant’s case is that he lacked possession at the relevant time, marshal proof of shared access, transfer, or absence from the scene.

## Doctrinal fault lines and downstream effects

Cutbank sits in the current stream of appellate deference to district courts on intrinsic evidence disputes, but it also flags a continuing doctrinal tension. Treating prior‑sighting testimony as intrinsic gives prosecutors an efficient route into identity and continuity without 404(b) disclosures, but if applied too expansively it risks swallowing Rule 404(b)’s protections. That tension will surface in close cases: when descriptions are vague, when multiple people had access, or when the count of prior sightings is effectively an allegation of character (e.g., “he always carried a gun”). Those situations are where circuit courts will have to refine the intrinsic/extrinsic line.

Practically, expect more instances where prosecutors will develop witness testimony about prior sightings early in investigations to buttress possession cases, and expect defense lawyers to litigate early and often about foundation, temporal proximity, and prejudice. Appellate outcomes will continue to turn on the factual texture of each case and the district court’s careful (or careless) gatekeeping.

## Bottom line

Cutbank reaffirms a predictable but important point in the Eighth Circuit: eyewitness testimony that the defendant was seen with the same, identifiable firearm before an offense is often admissible as intrinsic evidence of possession. That is a powerful tool for prosecutors, but it is not license to introduce every reference to prior gun ownership or use. Defense counsel must press for narrow, concrete foundations, insist on strict authentication, and wage targeted Rule 403 fights to prevent prior‑sighting testimony from functioning as improper propensity evidence.
