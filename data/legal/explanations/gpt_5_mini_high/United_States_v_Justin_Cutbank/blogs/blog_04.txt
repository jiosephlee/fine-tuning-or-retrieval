Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Impeachment, Authentication, and an Appellate Nudge: Using Police Reports the Right Way

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a compact but useful reminder about a few prosaic — and frequently litigated — trial problems: how to use third‑party police reports to impeach a witness, what foundations courts will require before letting defense counsel read those reports into the record, and how appellate courts view litigants that broaden the scope of an appeal. The decision doesn’t break new doctrinal ground, but it applies settled Eighth Circuit precedent (notably United States v. Cumbie) in a way that should sharpen trial planning for both defense and government counsel. It also contains an unusually plainspoken rebuke to appellees who press arguments that diverge from the appellant’s brief — a small procedural lesson that can affect appellate briefing strategy.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The facts are stark: after a violent home incident, police later found a sawed‑off Marlin .22 with DNA matching Cutbank, a victim who identified the rifle, and other corroborating evidence. At trial the defense tried to impeach a government witness (C.F.) with statements reflected in a police report. The district court allowed questions about what C.F. told the officer but sustained objections when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the police report into the record without calling the officer or otherwise authenticating the report. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The court relied on its precedent that extrinsic impeachment evidence — including prior inconsistent statements memorialized in a third‑party report — must be authenticated before being admitted (citing United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022)). In short: asking a witness on cross whether she said X to an officer is one thing; reading a police report written by someone else into the record without foundation is another. The court also upheld other evidentiary rulings (including a brief reference by a witness that her sister had been killed by a gun, allowed as responsive explanation to prior inconsistent statements) under Rule 403, and concluded any errors were harmless in light of compelling physical and testimonial proof.

Finally, the opinion included an explicit curbstone to the government: “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” That comment followed the government’s attempt to address a defense claim that Cutbank did not himself press on appeal.

## What the decision means in practice

The Cutbank panel’s treatment of the attempted impeachment with a police report is an object lesson in foundation and authentication. Courts will generally allow counsel to probe a witness about prior statements to police. But if you intend to use the officer’s report — or any document prepared by someone other than the witness — to take the sting out of a witness’s testimony, you should be ready to authenticate the report.

There are a few reliable paths to get that foundation at trial. First, on cross‑examination try to get the witness to acknowledge the substance of the statement recorded in the report. If the witness admits saying the thing recorded, the report’s contents can often be proved through the witness’ admission or through the document as a prior inconsistent statement under Rule 613 and, depending on the circumstances, under Rule 801(d)(1)(A). If the witness disavows the statement, plan to call the officer who prepared the report to authenticate the document and testify about what the witness told the officer. Calling the report’s drafter resolves both authentication and hearsay concerns for impeachment purposes. If the officer is unavailable, consider earlier preservation steps (declarations, depositions where permitted, or ensuring agents are on your witness list and subpoenaed).

Second, do not assume exceptions like the public‑records hearsay exception will rescue you in a criminal case. Many courts treat police investigative reports differently (and some parts of Rule 803(8) explicitly carve out law‑enforcement observations in criminal matters), so the safest route is authentication by testimony rather than reliance on hearsay exceptions.

Third, if you anticipate a recorded‑recollection argument, remember that the recorded‑recollection exception (Rule 803(5)) is available where the witness once had knowledge, now lacks memory, and the writing accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge. That exception is typically used for the author’s own writing; it does not eliminate the need to authenticate a third‑party police report unless the foundation for recorded recollection is laid through the report’s author or the witness’s own adoption.

Cutbank also reminds trial lawyers to prepare the people who can authenticate and explain documents. If you want to make the police report work for impeachment, put the report’s author on call, or secure stipulations or pretrial rulings admitting the necessary portions with proper foundation.

## Appellate practice: a brief, pointed nudge

The court’s line about appellees expanding issues is worth pausing over. The government argued a variant claim about how the district court handled cross‑examination that Cutbank had not itself pressed on appeal. The panel’s curt rebuke — “We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal” — is not just an affectation. It signals judicial impatience with briefs that broaden the contest beyond the appellant’s framing and invites appellate counsel to stay focused on the preserved, properly briefed issues.

Two practical lessons flow from that tone. Defense counsel should preserve objections and develop arguments at trial (and brief them on appeal) rather than burying them in perfunctory assertions on appeal. Government counsel should avoid expansive counter‑claims that go beyond the appellant’s actual arguments; doing so may irritate the court and can lead to the argument receiving short shrift.

## Rule 403, harmlessness, and cumulative‑error

Cutbank also illustrates the interplay between discretionary evidentiary rulings and appellate harmlessness review. The testimony about D.F.’s sister being killed by a gun came in as an explanatory response to cross‑examination about inconsistent statements. The district court declined to strike and the Eighth Circuit saw no Rule 403 problem — probative context for credibility and motive outweighed any potential prejudice. Where the record also contains strong physical corroboration (here, DNA on the rifle, the victim’s phone found along the likely path, eyewitness identifications, and resisting‑officer injuries), the court was comfortable finding any asserted errors harmless and rejecting a cumulative‑error claim. The practical point is simple: when challenging evidentiary rulings on appeal, the presence (or absence) of independent, powerful proof on the central elements of the offense will heavily influence outcomes.

## Bottom line

Cutbank won’t change the law of impeachment or authentication, but it is a useful reminder to litigators of old but often overlooked truths. If you plan to impeach with statements memorialized in a police report, build your foundation: get the witness to adopt the statement, or bring the report’s author to authenticate it. Don’t assume hearsay exceptions will carry you in a criminal case. Preserve and brief the issues you want decided, and avoid the temptation to let appellate argument drift into unpreserved or collateral claims — courts notice. And finally, when the government has strong physical evidence, appellate courts will be unsympathetic to arguments that rest on disputed evidentiary quibbles that did not prejudice the verdict.
