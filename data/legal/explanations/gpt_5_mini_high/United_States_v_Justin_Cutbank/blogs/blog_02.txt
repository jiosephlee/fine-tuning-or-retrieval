Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Beyond ACCA: How § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and Unobjected-to PSR Facts Drove a 292-Month Sentence

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank is a useful, practical reminder for defense lawyers and sentencing judges: ACCA’s mandatory minimum and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline are not a ceiling on how high a judge can set an advisory Guidelines range. Even where the jury has found the ACCA predicates, district courts can — and will — rely on uncharged or otherwise unadjudicated conduct recited in an uncontested PSR to trigger a §2K2.1 enhancement that produces a higher Guidelines range than the ACCA calculation. The Eighth Circuit affirmed a within‑Guidelines 292‑month sentence based on that route, highlighting three points that practitioners should take seriously: (1) Application Note 14(C) to §2K2.1 permits consideration of uncharged conduct for the enhancement; (2) adopted PSR facts that go unobjected to are extremely difficult to overturn on appeal; and (3) courts remain deferential to within‑Guidelines sentences.

## What the court did (short tour of the opinion)

The facts are stark: after using methamphetamine, Cutbank terrorized his girlfriend at gunpoint, ordered others awakened, struck the victim with a gun, fled with the victim’s phone and the rifle, barricaded himself later and fought officers, and was tied to the sawed‑off Marlin by DNA and eyewitness testimony. A jury convicted him under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) and found three or more prior violent felonies — triggering ACCA.

At sentencing the PSR produced a base offense level (itself reflecting the sawed‑off firearm) and several increases: a serial‑number alteration enhancement, a two‑level increase for creating a risk while fleeing police, and a four‑level §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for possession “in connection with” another felony. The PSR, and the enhancements it recommended, were adopted by the district court without objection. The result: an offense level (35 in the PSR) that exceeded the ACCA‑based Guidelines calculation (34), and an advisory range of 292–365 months. The district court imposed 292 months. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed, rejecting Cutbank’s scattered evidentiary claims (largely unpreserved) and sustaining the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on the PSR facts, relying on Application Note 14(C) and its usual clear‑error review when a PSR is adopted without objection. The court also noted its presumption of reasonableness for within‑Guidelines sentences.

## Why the result follows established law — and where the tension lies

There is nothing novel in the Eighth Circuit’s legal framework here. Three precedents (each invoked or alluded to by the court) form the spine of the result:

- Booker made the Guidelines advisory and left judges room to find sentencing facts by a preponderance of the evidence when those facts do not increase the statutory maximum or a mandatory minimum set by Congress.
- Watts and later cases allow a sentencing judge to consider uncharged or even acquitted conduct in sentencing so long as the relevant constitutional limits are respected.
- The Sentencing Commission’s own commentary — Application Note 14(C) to §2K2.1 — expressly allows the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) “in connection with another felony” enhancement to be applied “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.”

Pulling those strands together, the Eighth Circuit had straightforward authority to (1) accept PSR factual recitations that were not objected to, (2) permit the district judge to find the necessary nexus between the gun and other felony conduct by a preponderance, and (3) apply the §2K2.1 enhancement even though the conduct giving rise to it did not result in a separate conviction.

That legal posture creates a practical tension. The ACCA imposes a statutory floor (a mandatory minimum) and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline is often the dominant relevant Guidelines provision, but the Guidelines themselves provide a mechanism — USSG §4B1.4(b) — that tells courts to apply the greater offense level, whether it comes from §2K2.1 calculations or from the career‑offender/ACCA path. Thus a defendant can be subject to ACCA’s statutory consequences while still receiving a higher advisory Guidelines range because the judge relied on additional conduct to build §2K2.1 up past the ACCA figure. That tension is not doctrinally unsound under present precedent, but it is why Cutbank’s result will be rhetorically and strategically significant in practice.

## The preservation lesson — adopt the PSR at your peril

Cutbank illustrates the classic sentencing‑practice trap: if the PSR contains narrative facts that support enhancements and defense counsel does not lodge timely, specific objections (and does not demand an evidentiary hearing), the district court’s adoption of the PSR converts those recitations into findings subject only to clear‑error review on appeal. The Eighth Circuit applied that deferential standard, citing Dunn’s review rule and concluding that the PSR facts were not clearly erroneous.

That is the critical, avoidable vulnerability here. A sentencing challenge to a §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on uncharged conduct can succeed only if the defendant (a) preserves the objection, (b) insists on an evidentiary vehicle adequate to test the government’s factual showing, and (c) makes a reasoned argument that the preponderance standard was not met. Absent those steps, appellate courts treat the matter as a factual record problem rather than a legal defect.

## Acquitted/uncharged‑conduct limits — what matters and what doesn’t

A frequent instinct is to object on Sixth Amendment grounds when a judge uses uncharged or acquitted conduct to increase a sentence. But the constitutional constraints identified in Apprendi and Alleyne bite only where judicial fact‑finding increases the statutory maximum or raises a statutory mandatory minimum that the jury has not found. In Cutbank the jury already found the ACCA predicates (and prior‑conviction issues occupy a discrete Almendarez‑Torres/Apprendi exception). The §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement affected only the advisory Guidelines range; the facts underpinning it therefore did not implicate the Apprendi/Alleyne jury‑trial rule.

That is the key doctrinal point: unless the judge’s factual findings change a statutory floor or ceiling to which Apprendi/Alleyne applies, judges retain authority, within Booker’s framework, to find sentencing facts by a preponderance and to consider uncharged conduct under Application Note 14(C). Debates over the desirability of that practice are legitimate, but Cutbank is entirely consistent with controlling precedent.

## Practical takeaways and downstream effects

For defense counsel: Cutbank is a reminder that sentencing is a battle that begins with the PSR. Timely, specific objections to narrative PSR statements are essential; when the government intends to rely on uncharged conduct to seek enhancements, demand a live hearing, require the government to introduce admissible proof, and press the standard for nexus (for §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the nexus between firearm and felony). Silence or non‑specific objections are likely to be fatal on appeal.

For prosecutors: Cutbank signals that careful drafting of the PSR and production of corroborating evidence for enhancements will be rewarded. The explicit cross‑reference in the Guidelines and Application Note 14(C) makes clear that a robust PSR narrative can do the heavy lifting at sentencing even if no separate charge is brought.

For courts and policymakers: the case highlights the cumulative power of guideline enhancements. When a defendant has violent conduct that is not separately charged, the sentencing machinery can still stack significant enhancements — and defenders will rightly protest that those enhancements can be grounded in untested allegations. The result is likely to keep alive the policy debates over whether Application Note 14(C) and the broader acquitted/uncharged‑conduct practice should be reined in.

## A small editorial note — and a reminder to check the math

The opinion refers to a three‑level increase for an altered or obliterated serial number where §2K2.1(b)(4)(B) normally provides a four‑level increase. The Eighth Circuit called this a likely typographical slip; whatever the intended number, the PSR’s arithmetic produced an offense level that exceeded the ACCA calculation, and the outcome did not turn on that stray digit. Still, Cutbank is a practical reminder to litigators and judges to double‑check Guidelines math and to make the record reflect agreed, contested, and judicially resolved facts with precision.

## Bottom line

Cutbank does not announce any new doctrine, but it is a crisp, practical lesson about how the Guidelines, Application Note 14(C), and PSR practices combine in the real world to produce long sentences even where ACCA is already in play. For defendants, the moral is straightforward: fight the PSR hard; for prosecutors, the lesson is equally plain: assemble the proof and the PSR narrative. Until (and unless) the Supreme Court or Congress narrows the use of uncharged conduct in sentencing, cases like Cutbank will remain the operative battlefield for high‑stakes sentencing disputes.
