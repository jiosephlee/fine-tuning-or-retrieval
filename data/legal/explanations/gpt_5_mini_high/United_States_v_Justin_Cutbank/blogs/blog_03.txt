Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# The Perils of “Background” Hearsay: Course-of-Investigation Testimony Survives Crawford—Again

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank is a useful, if not novel, tour of the tensions that Bishop v. Crawford (Crawford v. Washington) and its progeny left in their wake. The Eighth Circuit, in a brisk opinion by Judge Loken, affirmed a conviction in which police officers described statements a victim made to them during the investigation, the district court declined a defense request for a contemporaneous limiting instruction, and the victim later testified at trial. The panel endorsed two familiar propositions: a trial court may admit such statements for non‑hearsay, contextual purposes (the so‑called “course‑of‑investigation” testimony), and the Confrontation Clause concern that animated Crawford is mooted if the declarant actually testifies and is subject to cross‑examination. For defense lawyers and trial judges, the decision spotlights the practical problem at the heart of many criminal trials — when does explanatory repetition by police become a backdoor way to get testimonial accusations to the jury where they may do the most harm?

## What the Eighth Circuit did

Cutbank asked the district court, before trial, to instruct the jury that it could not consider statements D.F. made to police as substantive proof of Cutbank’s conduct until she testified herself. The government answered that the officers’ recounting of D.F.’s statements was admissible to explain why police took the actions they did, and that a later limiting instruction (if needed) would suffice. The district court refused the contemporaneous instruction; D.F. later testified at trial; and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The panel applied the familiar abuse‑of‑discretion standard for limiting‑instruction rulings, citing United States v. Bennett for the proposition that “the court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” It also relied on United States v. Charboneau for the straightforward Confrontation Clause point: the Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Because the record, in the court’s view, “demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial” and D.F. testified, there was no abuse of discretion.

## The Crawford tension remains

Cutbank exemplifies a recurring, doctrinally familiar problem. Crawford and its follow‑ups (not least Davis and Melendez‑Diaz) established that testimonial statements to law enforcement ordinarily cannot be admitted against a criminal defendant unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross‑examination. But Crawford did not eliminate the long‑standing practice of allowing officers to recount to juries what complaining witnesses said to them when those recitals are offered to explain the course of the investigation, the officers’ state of mind, or why police acted as they did. Courts often treat such repetition as non‑hearsay (or as admissible for a non‑hearsay purpose) and allow it in the interest of narrative coherence.

That accommodation raises two persistent worries. First, whether a particular statement to police is “testimonial” can be a close call; if it is testimonial and the declarant does not testify, the Confrontation Clause blocks its use. Second, even where the Confrontation Clause is satisfied because the declarant later testifies, or where the statement is admitted only to explain police behavior, the jury may — intentionally or not — treat the officer’s recital as evidence of the truth of the matter asserted. The Eighth Circuit acknowledged the first worry and disposed of it simply: D.F. testified, so Crawford’s prophylaxis was not implicated. On the second, the panel leaned on Rule 105/Bennett’s deferential standard and the district court’s discretion about timing of instructions, concluding that forcing contemporaneous limiting instructions is unnecessary so long as a proper instruction can be given later.

That posture prioritizes courtroom manageability and the judge’s discretion to control the trial narrative, but it tends to understate the cognitive realities of how jurors process testimony. Allowing raw, accusatory statements to be replayed by officers early in the case and telling the jurors later to ignore their truth — particularly after the same statements have been rehearsed in the jury’s mind — is not a neutral choice about process. It is a decision that can materially affect how jurors allocate credibility and memory during the long arc of a trial.

## Practical guidance for trial judges and counsel

Cutbank is an important reminder that, in practice, the safest course for a defendant is to make the objection and build the record at trial; for judges, the safest course is to manage the timing and wording of investigations‑as‑narrative with an eye to prejudice.

Defense counsel should, at a minimum, (1) raise pretrial Crawford objections when statements to police are plainly testimonial; (2) seek a contemporaneous limiting instruction whenever officers are likely to repeat highly incriminating accusations before the declarant testifies; (3) preserve objections on the record at the point the testimony is elicited and, if necessary, move to strike and for a curative instruction or mistrial; and (4) insist that the government call a key declarant early in its case‑in‑chief when feasible. Cross‑examination of officers should focus on what the officer personally observed and on whether the officer is recounting words verbatim — a line of questioning that can limit the officer’s ability to repeat the speaker’s vivid accusations in the jury’s ear.

Trial judges, for their part, should not reflexively defer all limiting instructions to the end of trial. A brief, carefully tailored admonition at the time of admission can do real work: a contemporaneous instruction framed in plain language — for example, that the officer’s testimony about what the victim said is being admitted only to explain why the officer acted and not for the truth of the matter asserted — is both simple and likely to reduce the risk that jurors will treat the repetition as substantive proof. When statements are particularly inflammatory, Rule 403 remains a viable tool: the probative value of the officer’s repetition may be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. Judges also have docket management tools; when the government elects a narrative that depends on a declarant’s out‑of‑court statements, the court should consider whether the declarant can and should be called early.

Finally, appellate counsel must avoid the mistake the court observed in Cutbank: bare assertions of error without supportive authority or developed analysis rarely persuade an appellate court to disturb a district judge’s discretionary rulings.

## Where this decision leaves the law

Cutbank does not break new doctrinal ground; it reaffirms a pragmatic line that many circuits already follow — officers may recount what witnesses told them to explain police action, courts review refusals to give contemporaneous limiting instructions for abuse of discretion, and the Confrontation Clause concern vanishes if the declarant later testifies. But its practical effect is notable. By tolerating delayed limiting instructions and characterizing “background” statements as non‑fatal so long as the declarant testifies, the Eighth Circuit’s approach makes it harder in many cases for defendants to show reversible error on appeal. That will matter most in cases where the declarant is reluctant or unavailable to testify, where the officer’s recital is unusually vivid or detailed, or where the government builds its narrative around repeated out‑of‑court accusations.

If there is a takeoff point for doctrinal challenge, it is not in Crawford’s text but in the continuing empirical and normative question whether late instructions actually neutralize the prejudicial effect of earlier officer recitals. That is the space where trial judges can do the most to protect the fairness of the factfinding process: by tailoring limiting instructions to the moment, policing the form of officer testimony, and refusing to allow police narratives to become the principal vehicle for the prosecution’s substantive proof.

## Conclusion

Cutbank is a reminder that the law tolerates course‑of‑investigation testimony, and that Crawford’s Confrontation Clause protections are easily satisfied if the declarant testifies. But tolerance is not endorsement of complacency. Courts that value both coherent trials and fair trials should think hard at the moment such testimony is offered about whether a prompt, plain limiting instruction, selective redaction, or an early calling of the declarant is necessary to prevent the jury’s natural tendency to conflate context with truth. Defense counsel should press those choices aggressively; absent a strong record, appellate relief will be an uphill climb.
