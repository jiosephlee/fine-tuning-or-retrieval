Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Does a patent claim for 'seven multiplications' require an exact 1:1 mapping even if the device performs more than seven?
Question:
At oral argument, Finesse claimed the accused radios actually perform ten multiplications, which is more than the seven required by the '775 patent. However, the court still found non-infringement because Finesse couldn't map a specific subset of those ten to the seven claimed multiplications. If a device does *more* than what the patent requires, why is the failure to provide a precise mapping of the subset sufficient to reverse the infringement verdict?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is **yes**. In patent infringement analysis, the patentee must prove that the accused device meets **every limitation** of the asserted claim. Performing *more* than the required number of operations does not automatically satisfy a claim limitation if the specific operations required by the patent cannot be identified within the accused device's process.

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court held that Finesse failed to prove infringement of the '775 patent because it could not demonstrate that the accused radios performed the **seven specific multiplications** required by claim 1[d].

#### The Legal Standard: Meeting Every Limitation
To establish infringement, a plaintiff must show that the accused device contains every element of the claimed invention. In this case, the '775 patent did not simply claim "seven multiplications" in a general sense; it claimed seven **specific** mathematical combinations of three signals ($S_1, S_2, S_3$):
1. $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$
2. $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$
3. $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$
4. $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_3$
5. $S_2 \times S_2 \times S_3$
6. $S_1 \times S_3 \times S_3$
7. $S_2 \times S_3 \times S_3$

#### Why "More than Seven" Was Insufficient
Finesse argued at oral argument that the accused radios performed ten multiplications, which is numerically greater than the seven required. However, the court rejected this "more is better" logic for two primary reasons:

1. **Lack of Mapping:** The court noted that "nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications... corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications." Simply proving that a device performs ten operations is irrelevant if the patentee cannot point to seven of those operations and show they are exactly the seven specified in the claim.
2. **Flawed Mapping:** The evidence presented at trial (a Nokia technical document) only showed three distinct multiplications ($x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, $x_1 \cdot x_1 \cdot x_2'$, and $x_1 \cdot |x_2| \cdot |x_2|$). Finesse's attempt to map these to the claimed seven was found to be "flawed" because it required mapping a single signal ($S_3$) to three different things simultaneously: the signal $x_2$, its complex conjugate $x_2'$, and its modulus $|x_2|$.

#### Conclusion
The court concluded that because there was no evidence that the accused radios performed the specific seven multiplications required by the claim—and no valid mapping from the ten actual multiplications to the seven claimed ones—no reasonable jury could have found the devices infringing. In essence, performing ten random multiplications does not satisfy a claim for seven specific ones.
