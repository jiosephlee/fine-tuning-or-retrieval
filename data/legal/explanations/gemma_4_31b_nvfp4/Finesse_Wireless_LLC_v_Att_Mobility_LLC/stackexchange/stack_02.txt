Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does 'sampling' work in the context of the '134 patent's non-infringement ruling?
Question:
In the '134 patent analysis, the court reverses because the 'modeled PIM path' is generated *after* the RF ADC sampling occurs. The court says signals cannot be sampled before they are generated. For a non-lawyer/student, I'm struggling to understand why this is a fatal blow to the infringement claim. If the system eventually processes these signals, why does the specific sequence of sampling vs. generation matter so much for the 'interference generating signals' limitation?
Answer:
### Answer

To understand why the sequence of sampling versus generation is "fatal" to the infringement claim, you have to look at the specific language of the claim limitation and the requirement for literal infringement.

**The Legal Standard: Meeting Every Limitation**
In patent law, for a device to literally infringe a claim, it must meet every single limitation of that claim. If even one limitation is missing, there is no literal infringement.

**The Specific Limitation at Issue**
The '134 patent’s Claim 1[a] requires:
> "over-sampling... a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, **wherein the received signals include** signals of interest and **interference generating signals**..."

The key phrase here is *"wherein the received signals include."* This means that at the exact moment and location where the sampling occurs, the signals being sampled must already exist and must include the "interference generating signals."

**The "Fatal Blow" in the Accused Device**
The court's analysis focused on the physical and temporal architecture of the accused Nokia radios. The mapping provided by Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, created a logical impossibility based on the following facts:

1.  **The Location of Sampling:** The sampling occurs at the RF ADC (Radio Frequency Analog-to-Digital Converter).
2.  **The Identity of the Signal:** Dr. Wells mapped the "interference generating signals" to the "modeled PIM path."
3.  **The Sequence of Generation:** The court found (and the expert admitted under cross-examination) that the "modeled PIM path" is generated **downstream** of the RF ADC.

Because the modeled PIM path is generated *after* the sampling occurs, it is physically impossible for the RF ADC to sample a passband that *includes* that path. As the court noted, "signals cannot be sampled before they are generated."

**Why "Eventually Processing" the Signals is Irrelevant**
You asked why it matters if the system eventually processes these signals. In a patent infringement analysis, the court is not asking "does this machine achieve the same result?" but rather "does this machine perform the specific steps described in the claim?"

The claim does not say "the system shall eventually process interference generating signals"; it says the system must **sample a passband that includes them**. 

If the accused device generates a signal digitally *after* the analog-to-digital conversion (sampling) has already happened, it is performing a fundamentally different operation than what is claimed. The "interference generating signal" in the accused device is a product of the system's internal processing, whereas the '134 patent claims a system that samples those signals as part of the *incoming* received passband.

**Conclusion**
Because Finesse's expert mapped the "interference generating signals" to a path that did not exist until after the sampling stage, the accused radios failed to meet limitation 1[a]. Since that limitation was not met, the claim was not infringed.
