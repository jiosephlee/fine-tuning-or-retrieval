Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What is the distinction between 'unique' signals and 'separately identifiable' signals in claim construction?
Question:
Regarding the '775 patent, the court held that 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' does not require the signals to be 'unique,' only 'separately identifiable.' The court then suggests that using two unique signals plus a copy of one of them satisfies this. I don't understand the legal or technical distinction here. If S3 is just a copy of S1, isn't that still just two unique signals? How can a copy be 'separately identifiable' without being 'unique'?
Answer:
### The Distinction Between "Unique" and "Separately Identifiable" Signals

The distinction the court is making centers on the difference between **distinct input sources** (uniqueness) and **distinct instances within a formula** (identifiability).

**The Legal Distinction**
In claim construction, "unique" typically refers to the nature of the source. If a claim requires three *unique* signals, the accused device must utilize three different input sources. However, the district court in this case construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" as "**separately identifiable, but not unique**." 

This means that while the claim requires three "slots" to be filled for the purpose of the mathematical operations described (the seven multiplications), those slots do not necessarily need to be filled by three different sources.

**How a Copy is "Separately Identifiable"**
To answer your specific question: Yes, if S3 is a copy of S1, you only have two *unique* signals. However, in the context of a mathematical operation (like $S1 \times S2 \times S3$), you still have three *separately identifiable* signals because there are three distinct positions in the equation.

The court grounded this interpretation in two pieces of evidence:
1.  **Intrinsic Evidence:** The court found that the patent's own text did not limit the signals to unique inputs.
2.  **Extrinsic Evidence:** The inventor testified that S1, S2, and S3 "represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals."

Therefore, if a system takes two unique input signals ($x1$ and $x2$) and uses $x1$ as S1, $x2$ as S2, and then uses a copy of $x1$ as S3, it has provided three signals that can be separately identified and mapped to the variables in the claim's formula, even though only two unique sources exist.

**Application to the Case**
The Federal Circuit agreed that Finesse's amendment to "three signals" (rather than "two or three signals") did not constitute a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of devices using only two unique input signals. The amendment merely required that three signals be *identifiable* for the multiplications to occur.

Notably, while this distinction helped the plaintiff's argument regarding the *inputs*, it did not save the infringement verdict. The court ultimately reversed because the accused radios did not actually perform the **seven specific multiplications** required by the claim, regardless of whether the signals used were unique or copies.
