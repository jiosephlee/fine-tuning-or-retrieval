Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 3: # Infringement Analysis of the '775 Patent

In analyzing the infringement of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775, the court had to distinguish between the *inputs* used in a mathematical process (the signals) and the *operations* performed on those inputs (the multiplications). This distinction is critical in patent litigation, as a product must meet every limitation of a claim to infringe.

## Claim Construction: 'Three signals S1, S2 and S3' as 'separately identifiable' vs. 'unique'

The first issue the court addressed was the construction of the phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in Limitation 1[d]. The appellants argued that for a device to infringe, it must utilize three unique input signals. However, the district court construed the phrase to mean the signals must be "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique."

This is a subtle but important pedagogical distinction. If a claim requires "unique" signals, the accused device must have three distinct sources of information. If the signals only need to be "separately identifiable," a device could potentially satisfy the limitation by using two unique signals (x1 and x2) and a copy of one of those signals to serve as the third. The district court relied on the intrinsic record and inventor testimony to determine that "S1, S2 and S3" represented signals generally, allowing them to be the same signal or different signals.

## Prosecution History: The Impact of the Filipovic Prior Art on Claim Scope

The appellants attempted to use the prosecution history—the record of the patent's application and amendment process—to argue that Finesse had surrendered the "two-signal" scope. During prosecution, the patent examiner rejected a claim that recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals" as obvious in light of a reference called *Filipovic*, which taught the use of two inputs. Finesse responded by amending the claim to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3."

The appellants argued that this amendment constituted a "disavowal" of any claim scope covering products that use only two signals. However, the Federal Circuit disagreed. For an amendment to limit the scope of a patent through disavowal, the surrender must be "clear and unambiguous." The court found that Finesse's amendment to "three signals" did not clearly exclude products with two unique input signals, especially since Finesse had also overcome the *Filipovic* rejection by arguing that *Filipovic* dealt with active intermodulation products (IMPs) rather than the passive IMPs addressed by the '775 patent.

## Analysis of Limitation 1[d]: The Requirement for Seven Specific Third-Order Multiplications

While the court decided that two unique signals could satisfy the "three signals" requirement, the accused radios still failed to meet the operational requirements of Limitation 1[d]. This limitation requires that the receiver generate an n-th order interference cancellation signal (ICS) by performing seven specific third-order multiplications:
1. $S1 \times S1 \times S2$
2. $S1 \times S2 \times S2$
3. $S1 \times S2 \times S3$
4. $S1 \times S1 \times S3$
5. $S2 \times S2 \times S3$
6. $S1 \times S3 \times S3$
7. $S2 \times S3 \times S3$

To prove infringement, the patent holder must demonstrate that the accused device performs *all seven* of these specific mathematical operations.

## Comparison Between the Nokia Technical Document and the Claimed Multiplications

Finesse relied on a Nokia technical document to prove that the accused radios performed these operations. However, the court found a significant gap between the evidence and the claim language. The Nokia document described calculations involving modulus and complex conjugate operations, listing only three distinct multiplications:
* $x1 \cdot |x1| \cdot |x1|$
* $x1 \cdot x1 \cdot x2'$
* $x1 \cdot |x2| \cdot |x2|$

The court noted that there was no evidence in the record that these operations were repeated or modified in a way that would result in the seven specific multiplications required by the patent. Because the document relied on complex conjugates (indicated by the prime symbol $x2'$) and modulus (indicated by the bars $|x2|$), it did not align with the simple multiplication of signals $S1, S2, S3$ as recited in the claim.

## Failure of the Expert to Map Three Distinct Multiplications to the Seven Claimed Operations

The final failure in Finesse's case was the expert's inability to "map" the accused device's functions to the claim's limitations. In patent law, it is not enough for an expert to testify that a device "generally" performs a function; the expert must specifically map each element of the claim to a corresponding part of the accused device.

Dr. Wells testified that the three multiplications found in the Nokia document were equivalent to the seven claimed multiplications, but he failed to explain *how* that equivalence worked. On appeal, Finesse attempted to argue that the Nokia document actually showed ten multiplications, but they could not identify which subset of those ten corresponded to the seven required by the patent. Because the expert's testimony was unsupported by the technical documents and lacked a specific mapping, the court concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement. Consequently, the court reversed the denial of the judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and vacated the damages award.
