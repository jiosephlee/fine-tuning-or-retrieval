Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Introduction and Standard of Review

## Technical Background: Intermodulation Products (IMP) and Passive Intermodulation (PIM)

To understand the dispute in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, one must first understand the technical problem the patents aim to solve: signal interference in radios. Radios typically avoid interference by operating on different frequencies. However, when transmit signals encounter physical obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors—those signals can combine to form new, unwanted frequencies. These resulting frequencies are known as "intermodulation products" (IMPs). When these IMPs are caused by the aforementioned passive obstacles, they are specifically referred to as "passive intermodulation products" (PIM). The patents at issue in this case describe methods for mitigating this specific type of interference.

## Overview of the '134 and '775 Patents

Finesse Wireless owns two patents, the ’134 and ’775 patents, both of which focus on reducing IMP interference. While they share a common goal, they approach the problem through different mechanisms. The ’134 patent describes a method of isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals" and using a computed estimate of the IMPs to cancel out the interference. The ’775 patent describes a method for performing interference cancellation in a receiver by digitally multiplying three signals through a specific series of seven multiplications to generate "intermodulation product cancellation signals" (ICSs).

## Procedural Posture: From Jury Verdict to Appeal

The litigation began when Finesse sued AT&T, alleging that AT&T infringed the asserted claims of both patents by using a "PIM cancellation" (PIM-C) feature in radios manufactured by Nokia. Nokia intervened in the lawsuit to protect its interests. Following a trial, the jury found all asserted claims of both patents to be valid and infringed, awarding Finesse a lump-sum damages award of approximately $166.3 million.

Following the verdict, AT&T and Nokia (the Appellants) moved for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of noninfringement and a new trial on damages. The district court denied these motions, maintaining that the jury's verdict was supported by the evidence. The Appellants then appealed these denials to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

## Standard of Review for Denial of JMOL

When the Federal Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of a motion for JMOL, it applies the law of the regional circuit where the district court sits. In this case, the court applied Fifth Circuit law. Under this standard, the review is *de novo*, meaning the appellate court examines the record without deference to the district court's legal conclusion. 

However, the threshold for reversing a denial of JMOL is high. The appellate court will reverse only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the jury's verdict, the evidence points so "strongly and overwhelmingly" in favor of the non-prevailing party that the court believes reasonable jurors could not have arrived at any contrary conclusion. Essentially, the court asks whether any reasonable jury could have reached the verdict based on the evidence presented.

## The 'Substantial Evidence' Standard for Infringement

In addition to reviewing the JMOL denial, the Federal Circuit reviews the jury’s factual finding of infringement under the "substantial evidence" standard. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial. 

While this may seem similar to the JMOL standard, it focuses specifically on whether there was sufficient evidence to support the jury's factual conclusion. If the evidence is self-contradictory or fails to map the accused device's functions to the specific limitations of the patent claims, the court may find that the infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence, thereby necessitating a reversal.

# Infringement Analysis of the '134 Patent

To determine whether a product infringes a patent, a court must ensure that every limitation of the asserted claim is met by the accused device. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit reversed a finding of infringement for the ’134 patent because the plaintiff failed to provide substantial evidence that the accused radios met a specific technical limitation regarding the sampling of signals.

## Analysis of Limitation 1[a]: Over-sampling 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals'

The dispute centered on limitation 1[a] of the ’134 patent, which requires a method of "over-sampling... a passband of received signals... wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals." In patent litigation, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving that the accused device meets each limitation of the claim. Here, Finesse was required to prove that the accused radios' receiver sampled a passband containing both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals."

## Dr. Wells' mapping of the 'modeled PIM path' to the claim terms

To satisfy this burden, Finesse relied on the testimony of its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. Dr. Wells utilized a Nokia technical document to map the functions of the accused radios to the language of the patent. Specifically, he mapped the "signals of interest" to the "downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference" and the "interference generating signals" to the "modeled PIM path." By equating the "modeled PIM path" with the "interference generating signals" required by limitation 1[a], Dr. Wells attempted to establish that the radio’s analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) sampled both required signals.

## The logical impossibility of sampling signals generated downstream of the ADC

During cross-examination, a critical technical flaw in Dr. Wells' mapping was revealed. The "modeled PIM path" is generated downstream of the RF ADC—meaning the signal is created *after* the sampling process has already occurred. Because a signal cannot be sampled before it exists, it is logically impossible for the RF ADC to sample the "modeled PIM path." Consequently, if the "modeled PIM path" was the only signal identified as the "interference generating signal," the accused radios could not possibly infringe limitation 1[a].

## Evaluation of the expert's 'pivot' to x1 and x2 signals

When confronted with this logical impossibility, Dr. Wells attempted to pivot his testimony, suggesting that there were two signals (labeled x1 and x2) on the red path of the technical document. However, the Federal Circuit noted that Dr. Wells never explicitly testified that x1 and x2 mapped onto the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Despite this lack of direct testimony, the district court denied the motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), concluding that Dr. Wells was "clearly referring to x1 and x2."

The Federal Circuit rejected this conclusion, finding that the district court had improperly filled in the gaps of the expert's testimony. The court observed that Finesse was attempting to create a mapping by piecing together fragmented testimony from different parts of the record. Because Dr. Wells never clearly linked x1 and x2 to the specific claim terms, this "pivot" provided no actual evidentiary support for infringement.

## The legal consequence of self-contradictory expert testimony in satisfying the burden of proof

The court's analysis culminates in the legal standard for expert testimony when a party bears the burden of proof. Even if the court had accepted that Dr. Wells meant x1 and x2, his testimony remained contradictory, as he repeatedly insisted that the "modeled PIM path" was the "interference generating signal."

The Federal Circuit held that when a plaintiff rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, such evidence is insufficient to satisfy the burden of proof. Because Dr. Wells' testimony was confusing and contradictory—and the "modeled PIM path" mapping was technically impossible—no reasonable jury could have found the infringement verdict to be supported by substantial evidence. Accordingly, the court reversed the denial of JMOL, finding that the accused radios did not infringe the ’134 patent.

# Infringement Analysis of the '775 Patent

In analyzing the infringement of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775, the court had to distinguish between the *inputs* used in a mathematical process (the signals) and the *operations* performed on those inputs (the multiplications). This distinction is critical in patent litigation, as a product must meet every limitation of a claim to infringe.

## Claim Construction: 'Three signals S1, S2 and S3' as 'separately identifiable' vs. 'unique'

The first issue the court addressed was the construction of the phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in Limitation 1[d]. The appellants argued that for a device to infringe, it must utilize three unique input signals. However, the district court construed the phrase to mean the signals must be "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique."

This is a subtle but important pedagogical distinction. If a claim requires "unique" signals, the accused device must have three distinct sources of information. If the signals only need to be "separately identifiable," a device could potentially satisfy the limitation by using two unique signals (x1 and x2) and a copy of one of those signals to serve as the third. The district court relied on the intrinsic record and inventor testimony to determine that "S1, S2 and S3" represented signals generally, allowing them to be the same signal or different signals.

## Prosecution History: The Impact of the Filipovic Prior Art on Claim Scope

The appellants attempted to use the prosecution history—the record of the patent's application and amendment process—to argue that Finesse had surrendered the "two-signal" scope. During prosecution, the patent examiner rejected a claim that recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals" as obvious in light of a reference called *Filipovic*, which taught the use of two inputs. Finesse responded by amending the claim to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3."

The appellants argued that this amendment constituted a "disavowal" of any claim scope covering products that use only two signals. However, the Federal Circuit disagreed. For an amendment to limit the scope of a patent through disavowal, the surrender must be "clear and unambiguous." The court found that Finesse's amendment to "three signals" did not clearly exclude products with two unique input signals, especially since Finesse had also overcome the *Filipovic* rejection by arguing that *Filipovic* dealt with active intermodulation products (IMPs) rather than the passive IMPs addressed by the '775 patent.

## Analysis of Limitation 1[d]: The Requirement for Seven Specific Third-Order Multiplications

While the court decided that two unique signals could satisfy the "three signals" requirement, the accused radios still failed to meet the operational requirements of Limitation 1[d]. This limitation requires that the receiver generate an n-th order interference cancellation signal (ICS) by performing seven specific third-order multiplications:
1. $S1 \times S1 \times S2$
2. $S1 \times S2 \times S2$
3. $S1 \times S2 \times S3$
4. $S1 \times S1 \times S3$
5. $S2 \times S2 \times S3$
6. $S1 \times S3 \times S3$
7. $S2 \times S3 \times S3$

To prove infringement, the patent holder must demonstrate that the accused device performs *all seven* of these specific mathematical operations.

## Comparison Between the Nokia Technical Document and the Claimed Multiplications

Finesse relied on a Nokia technical document to prove that the accused radios performed these operations. However, the court found a significant gap between the evidence and the claim language. The Nokia document described calculations involving modulus and complex conjugate operations, listing only three distinct multiplications:
* $x1 \cdot |x1| \cdot |x1|$
* $x1 \cdot x1 \cdot x2'$
* $x1 \cdot |x2| \cdot |x2|$

The court noted that there was no evidence in the record that these operations were repeated or modified in a way that would result in the seven specific multiplications required by the patent. Because the document relied on complex conjugates (indicated by the prime symbol $x2'$) and modulus (indicated by the bars $|x2|$), it did not align with the simple multiplication of signals $S1, S2, S3$ as recited in the claim.

## Failure of the Expert to Map Three Distinct Multiplications to the Seven Claimed Operations

The final failure in Finesse's case was the expert's inability to "map" the accused device's functions to the claim's limitations. In patent law, it is not enough for an expert to testify that a device "generally" performs a function; the expert must specifically map each element of the claim to a corresponding part of the accused device.

Dr. Wells testified that the three multiplications found in the Nokia document were equivalent to the seven claimed multiplications, but he failed to explain *how* that equivalence worked. On appeal, Finesse attempted to argue that the Nokia document actually showed ten multiplications, but they could not identify which subset of those ten corresponded to the seven required by the patent. Because the expert's testimony was unsupported by the technical documents and lacked a specific mapping, the court concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement. Consequently, the court reversed the denial of the judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and vacated the damages award.

# Disposition and Impact on Damages

The conclusion of a patent litigation opinion often focuses on the "disposition"—the final legal determination of the case and the resulting effect on any lower court judgments. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit's ruling on the merits of the infringement claims led to a complete reversal of the trial court's outcome.

## Reversal of the District Court's Denial of JMOL for Both Patents

The Federal Circuit focused its analysis on whether the jury's infringement verdict was supported by "substantial evidence." In both the ’134 and ’775 patents, the court determined that the evidence—specifically the testimony of Finesse’s expert and the technical documentation provided—was either contradictory, confusing, or insufficient to map onto the specific limitations of the patent claims.

Because the court found that no reasonable jury could have reached a verdict of infringement based on the evidence presented, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of the defendants' motions for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL). In practical terms, by reversing the denial of JMOL, the appellate court effectively granted the JMOL, ruling as a matter of law that the accused radios did not infringe the asserted claims of either patent.

## The Relationship Between the Infringement Finding and the Damages Award

To understand the disposition of this case, a law student must understand the sequential nature of patent liability and damages. A damages award is a remedy that follows a finding of liability (infringement). If the underlying finding of infringement is overturned, the legal basis for the damages award disappears. 

In this case, the jury had awarded Finesse a lump-sum amount of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of both patents. However, because the Federal Circuit reversed the infringement findings for all asserted claims of both the ’134 and ’775 patents, there was no longer any legal justification for the monetary award. 

## Vacating the $166 Million Lump-Sum Damages Award

While the court "reversed" the district court's ruling on the JMOL motions, it "vacated" the damages award. In legal terms, to vacate a judgment is to render it void or set it aside. 

The Appellants had originally sought a new trial on damages or JMOL on the damages amount. While the court did not need to analyze whether the damages calculation itself was flawed (such as whether the $166 million figure was excessive or improperly calculated), the total reversal of the non-infringement JMOL made such an analysis unnecessary. Since the defendants were found not to have infringed the patents, the entire $166,303,391 award was vacated.

## Final Judgment on Costs

The final step in the court's disposition was the assignment of costs. The court ordered that costs be awarded to the Defendants-Appellants (AT&T and Nokia). This is a standard procedural conclusion where the prevailing party in the appeal is entitled to recover certain litigation expenses from the losing party.
