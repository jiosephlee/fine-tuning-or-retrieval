Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

To understand the legal issues in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, one must first understand the factual context of the plaintiff’s relationship with the U.S. government and the procedural timeline of the litigation. This case centers on a tension between a citizen's right to access government records under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and the government's interest in protecting ongoing law enforcement investigations.

## Foad Farahi's Background and the Nature of the FBI's Interest in Him

Foad Farahi, an Iranian national residing in the United States since 1993, became the subject of government scrutiny in 2002 when the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him for overstaying his visa. Throughout these proceedings, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum and protection under the Convention Against Torture. 

The FBI’s interest in Farahi is rooted in national security. During the immigration proceedings, the government presented evidence and testimony from an FBI agent alleging that Farahi had connections to high-level terrorists. Specifically, the government alleged that Farahi acted as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood and had links to known Al Qaeda members—including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed—and other convicted terrorists like Jose Padilla. Additionally, the FBI alleged that Farahi supported charities used to finance terrorism. This factual backdrop is critical because the FBI’s claims of "national security" and "counterterrorism" investigations grant the agency a higher degree of deference from the courts when asserting FOIA exemptions.

## The FOIA Request and the FBI's Initial Withholding of Records

The dispute began when Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI identified a substantial volume of potentially responsive material, totaling approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Because the FBI failed to respond to the request within the statutory deadline, Farahi filed suit to compel the agency to release the records.

Over the course of several years, the FBI reviewed the responsive records, releasing some portions to Farahi while withholding the majority of the file. The agency justified these withholdings by invoking Exemption 7(A) of FOIA, which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are either pending or reasonably anticipated.

## The Initial Summary Judgment Motion and the District Court's Requirement for Updated Declarations

In October 2019, the FBI moved for summary judgment, asking the district court to uphold its decision to withhold the records. To support its position, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration asserted that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release would interfere with ongoing or anticipated proceedings. 

Notably, the FBI requested to file a supplemental Hardy declaration *ex parte* and under seal. In FOIA litigation, *ex parte* submissions allow the government to provide sensitive, secret information to the judge without disclosing it to the plaintiff, preventing the disclosure of the very secrets the government is trying to protect. The district court granted this request.

However, when the district court finally addressed the motion in November 2022, it faced a timing problem. Because three years had passed since the Hardy declaration was written, the court was concerned that the evidence regarding "pending" proceedings might be stale. While the court noted that the FBI "seemingly" met its burden, it denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice and ordered the FBI to provide updated declarations confirming that the enforcement proceedings were still active. The court also requested further information regarding "segregability"—whether the FBI could redact exempt portions and release the remaining non-exempt information.

## The Roles of the Hardy and Bender Declarations

The procedural resolution of the case depended on the transition from the Hardy declarations to the Bender declarations. The Hardy declarations provided the original, detailed evidentiary foundation for the FBI's claims, including the specific nature of the counterterrorism investigation. 

To satisfy the district court's requirement for updated information, the FBI submitted new declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender’s public declaration was brief, stating that the agent overseeing Farahi’s file confirmed the investigations remained ongoing. His *ex parte* declaration provided the deeper level of detail necessary for the court to verify the pendency of the proceedings and to determine that the records were not "reasonably segregable" (meaning the exempt and non-exempt information were too intertwined to separate).

## The District Court's Final Grant of Summary Judgment

Upon reviewing the updated Bender declarations, the district court granted the FBI's renewed motion for summary judgment. The court credited the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant enforcement proceedings remained ongoing and accepted the *ex parte* justifications for why the records could not be further segregated. 

The district court essentially concluded that the FBI had successfully demonstrated that Exemption 7(A) applied to the withheld records. Farahi subsequently appealed this decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, leading to the current opinion.
