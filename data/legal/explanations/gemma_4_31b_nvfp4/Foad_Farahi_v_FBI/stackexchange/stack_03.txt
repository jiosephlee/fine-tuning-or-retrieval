Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the 'inextricably intertwined' standard for segregability in large record sets?
Question:
The FBI withheld over 10,000 pages, claiming that non-exempt material was 'inextricably intertwined' or 'interspersed line-by-line' with exempt material. The court seems to accept that no 'meaningful information' was segregable. If a document is 10,000 pages long, is it practically possible for every single page to be so intertwined that not even a few sentences can be released? I'm trying to understand how high the bar is for the government to avoid the duty to segregate.
Answer:
***

**The "Inextricably Intertwined" Standard and Segregability under FOIA**

Under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), agencies are generally required to take "reasonable steps" to segregate and release non-exempt information even if the record as a whole is protected. However, the "inextricably intertwined" standard provides a critical exception to this duty.

### The Legal Standard
The government may withhold records in their entirety when non-exempt portions are **"inextricably intertwined"** with exempt portions. *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008). This occurs when exempt and non-exempt information is so mixed—for example, "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document"—that the non-exempt material is not "reasonably segregable." *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, No. 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. 2025) (citing *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 261).

### "Meaningful Information" vs. "Words"
Your question asks whether it is practically possible for 10,000 pages to be so intertwined that not a few sentences can be released. The court in *Farahi* addresses this by distinguishing between **words** and **information**.

The court emphasizes that "the focus of the FOIA is information... not simply words which the Government has written down." *Farahi* (quoting *Mead*). Consequently, the bar for segregability is not whether a few isolated words or phrases can be released without harm, but whether any **meaningful information** can be extracted. If the non-exempt content consists only of fragments that do not constitute meaningful information, the government has satisfied its duty to segregate.

### The Evidentiary Bar for the Government
To avoid the duty to segregate, the government does not necessarily need to provide a public, page-by-page analysis. Instead:

1.  **Specificity:** The government must provide affidavits or declarations that show "with reasonable specificity" why the documents cannot be further segregated. *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61.
2.  **Ex Parte Submissions:** In sensitive cases (such as those involving national security or ongoing law enforcement investigations), the court may rely on *ex parte* declarations. The court in *Farahi* held that such declarations are often the only way to resolve segregability disputes without disclosing the very secret material the government is trying to protect.
3.  **Detailed Justification:** In *Farahi*, the court found the government met its burden because it provided a "detailed justification" in a supplemental *ex parte* submission explaining why the records were not reasonably segregable.

**Summary for your analysis:** The "bar" is not a mathematical one based on the volume of pages. Even in a 10,000-page set, the government can avoid segregation if it provides a reasonably specific justification—even if *ex parte*—that the only releasable elements are isolated words or phrases rather than "meaningful information."
