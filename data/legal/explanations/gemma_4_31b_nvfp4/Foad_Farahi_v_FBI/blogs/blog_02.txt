Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Shadow Litigation: The Role of Ex Parte Declarations in FOIA Enforcement

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is designed to be a window into the federal government, but in cases involving national security and law enforcement, that window is often replaced by a mirrored wall. The D.C. Circuit recently reaffirmed this reality in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, a case that highlights a troubling procedural paradox: while FOIA mandates transparency, the process for determining whether the government is *justified* in withholding information is often conducted in total secrecy.

In *Farahi*, the appellant—an Iranian national with a long history of immigration proceedings—sought his FBI file. The Bureau withheld the vast majority of the records under Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with "pending or reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceedings. The district court granted summary judgment to the FBI, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed. 

The core of the dispute was not merely whether the FBI had a valid reason to withhold documents, but *how* the FBI proved it. To satisfy the court, the government relied on a combination of public declarations and ex parte, under-seal submissions. When the district court asked for an update on whether the investigations were still ongoing years after the initial request, the FBI provided a supplemental declaration from an acting section chief. Farahi argued that this update was too terse and conclusory to be credible. 

The D.C. Circuit disagreed, holding that the "terse" update did not need to stand alone; it merely supplemented previous, detailed ex parte declarations that the court had already reviewed in private. The court further ruled that the FBI had sufficiently demonstrated that the records were not "reasonably segregable," meaning that any non-exempt information was too intertwined with the secret material to be extracted.

The most striking aspect of the opinion is the court’s candid admission regarding the procedural imbalance of this process. The court acknowledged that the use of ex parte declarations "handicaps" FOIA plaintiffs. In any other area of civil litigation, a party moving for summary judgment must provide evidence that the opposing party can challenge. Here, however, the "evidence" is a secret document that the plaintiff—and the public—will never see. The court dismissed this concern as an "inherent" part of FOIA cases where national security is at stake, suggesting that the only way to protect the secret is to keep the justification for the secret, secret as well.

From an analytical perspective, this approach risks transforming judicial review into a rubber-stamp exercise. While the court argues that it is providing oversight by reviewing the ex parte declarations, that oversight is fundamentally one-sided. The plaintiff cannot point to contradictions in the government's narrative, cannot suggest alternative interpretations of the "interference" risk, and cannot argue that the "pending proceedings" are a pretext for avoiding embarrassment. When the court relies on the government's own secret narrative to decide that the government's secret narrative is true, the "adversarial" nature of the litigation vanishes.

The doctrinal implications here are significant, particularly regarding the "reasonably anticipated" standard of Exemption 7(A). By allowing the government to maintain the "ongoing" status of an investigation via periodic, vague updates to secret filings, the court provides the FBI with a powerful tool to indefinitely shield records from public view. If a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" can be maintained simply by an agency's internal attestation—which the court will then verify using other secret attestations—the burden of proof effectively shifts from the government to the plaintiff, who must prove a negative without any access to the evidence.

Ultimately, *Farahi* reinforces a high wall of deference to the executive in counterterrorism contexts. While the court may be correct that disclosing the details of an investigation would jeopardize it, the reliance on a "shadow" record creates a dangerous precedent. It suggests that in the realm of national security, the government is granted a near-blank check to define the scope of its own transparency, leaving the judiciary to act not as a check on executive power, but as a confidential auditor of it.
