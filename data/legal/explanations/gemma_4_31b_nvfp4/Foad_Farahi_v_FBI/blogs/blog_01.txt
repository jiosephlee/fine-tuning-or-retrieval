Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The "Update" Loophole: How Terse Declarations Sustain FOIA Withholdings Over Time

The tension between the Freedom of Information Act’s (FOIA) transparency mandate and the government’s need for investigative secrecy often comes down to a question of timing. Under Exemption 7(A), the government can withhold records if their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." But what happens when a FOIA lawsuit drags on for years, and the "pending" proceeding becomes a decades-long ghost? The D.C. Circuit recently addressed this in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, and in doing so, it created a potentially convenient path for agencies to maintain long-term secrecy with minimal evidentiary effort.

The case involved Foad Farahi, an Iranian national whose FBI file was subject to a massive withholding based on Exemption 7(A). The litigation spanned several years, leading the district court to correctly observe that the government’s initial justifications might be stale. The court required the FBI to update its assessment of whether the enforcement proceedings were still pending. The FBI responded with a declaration from Joseph Bender that was, by the court's own admission, "surprisingly terse." Bender essentially confirmed that the investigations remained ongoing, without providing the granular detail found in the agency's original, more comprehensive submissions.

Farahi argued that this conclusory "update" failed to meet the evidentiary standard set forth in *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington (CREW) v. DOJ*, where the D.C. Circuit warned that vague assertions of ongoing investigations may be insufficient to sustain a withholding. However, the D.C. Circuit rejected this argument. The court reasoned that the Bender declaration should not be read in isolation. Instead, it viewed the update as a bridge back to the original, detailed declarations provided by a previous official, David Hardy. Because the initial evidence was robust, the court held that the agency only needed to provide a confirmation that the status quo persisted.

This reasoning establishes a "maintenance" standard for government declarations that may frustrate FOIA practitioners. By treating the update declaration as a supplement rather than a standalone piece of evidence, the court has effectively lowered the bar for agencies to refresh stale declarations. If an agency can get a detailed declaration into the record once, it may be able to shield those records for an indefinite period by providing minimalist updates that merely attest that "nothing has changed." 

The doctrinal implication here is a narrowing of the *CREW* holding. While *CREW* suggests that "vague" assertions are insufficient, *Farahi* suggests that vagueness is acceptable if the agency is simply updating a previously detailed record. This creates a strategic incentive for agencies to front-load their litigation with exhaustive (and often ex parte) declarations, knowing that the subsequent burden of proof to maintain the withholding over the life of the litigation will be significantly diminished.

Furthermore, the court's treatment of segregability in this case reinforces the difficulty plaintiffs face when dealing with national security files. The court deferred to the FBI’s assertion that non-exempt information was "inextricably intertwined" with exempt material, noting that even if individual words could be released, the government is not required to produce "meaningless" fragments of information. When combined with the court's willingness to rely on ex parte declarations to resolve these disputes, the result is a high wall of deference.

For practitioners, *Farahi* suggests that challenging "stale" government evidence requires more than pointing to the brevity of an update. To overcome this "update loophole," plaintiffs will likely need to provide specific evidence that the underlying circumstances of the investigation have fundamentally shifted, rather than relying on the court to demand a more detailed accounting from the agency. As it stands, the D.C. Circuit has signaled that once the government establishes a "reasonably anticipated" proceeding, it can keep the door closed with very little effort.
