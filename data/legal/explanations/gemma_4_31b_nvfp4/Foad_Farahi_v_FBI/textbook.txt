Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Case Background and Procedural History

To understand the legal issues in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, one must first understand the factual context of the plaintiff’s relationship with the U.S. government and the procedural timeline of the litigation. This case centers on a tension between a citizen's right to access government records under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and the government's interest in protecting ongoing law enforcement investigations.

## Foad Farahi's Background and the Nature of the FBI's Interest in Him

Foad Farahi, an Iranian national residing in the United States since 1993, became the subject of government scrutiny in 2002 when the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him for overstaying his visa. Throughout these proceedings, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum and protection under the Convention Against Torture. 

The FBI’s interest in Farahi is rooted in national security. During the immigration proceedings, the government presented evidence and testimony from an FBI agent alleging that Farahi had connections to high-level terrorists. Specifically, the government alleged that Farahi acted as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood and had links to known Al Qaeda members—including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed—and other convicted terrorists like Jose Padilla. Additionally, the FBI alleged that Farahi supported charities used to finance terrorism. This factual backdrop is critical because the FBI’s claims of "national security" and "counterterrorism" investigations grant the agency a higher degree of deference from the courts when asserting FOIA exemptions.

## The FOIA Request and the FBI's Initial Withholding of Records

The dispute began when Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. The FBI identified a substantial volume of potentially responsive material, totaling approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Because the FBI failed to respond to the request within the statutory deadline, Farahi filed suit to compel the agency to release the records.

Over the course of several years, the FBI reviewed the responsive records, releasing some portions to Farahi while withholding the majority of the file. The agency justified these withholdings by invoking Exemption 7(A) of FOIA, which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are either pending or reasonably anticipated.

## The Initial Summary Judgment Motion and the District Court's Requirement for Updated Declarations

In October 2019, the FBI moved for summary judgment, asking the district court to uphold its decision to withhold the records. To support its position, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). The Hardy declaration asserted that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release would interfere with ongoing or anticipated proceedings. 

Notably, the FBI requested to file a supplemental Hardy declaration *ex parte* and under seal. In FOIA litigation, *ex parte* submissions allow the government to provide sensitive, secret information to the judge without disclosing it to the plaintiff, preventing the disclosure of the very secrets the government is trying to protect. The district court granted this request.

However, when the district court finally addressed the motion in November 2022, it faced a timing problem. Because three years had passed since the Hardy declaration was written, the court was concerned that the evidence regarding "pending" proceedings might be stale. While the court noted that the FBI "seemingly" met its burden, it denied the motion for summary judgment without prejudice and ordered the FBI to provide updated declarations confirming that the enforcement proceedings were still active. The court also requested further information regarding "segregability"—whether the FBI could redact exempt portions and release the remaining non-exempt information.

## The Roles of the Hardy and Bender Declarations

The procedural resolution of the case depended on the transition from the Hardy declarations to the Bender declarations. The Hardy declarations provided the original, detailed evidentiary foundation for the FBI's claims, including the specific nature of the counterterrorism investigation. 

To satisfy the district court's requirement for updated information, the FBI submitted new declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender’s public declaration was brief, stating that the agent overseeing Farahi’s file confirmed the investigations remained ongoing. His *ex parte* declaration provided the deeper level of detail necessary for the court to verify the pendency of the proceedings and to determine that the records were not "reasonably segregable" (meaning the exempt and non-exempt information were too intertwined to separate).

## The District Court's Final Grant of Summary Judgment

Upon reviewing the updated Bender declarations, the district court granted the FBI's renewed motion for summary judgment. The court credited the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant enforcement proceedings remained ongoing and accepted the *ex parte* justifications for why the records could not be further segregated. 

The district court essentially concluded that the FBI had successfully demonstrated that Exemption 7(A) applied to the withheld records. Farahi subsequently appealed this decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, leading to the current opinion.

# The Legal Framework of FOIA Exemption 7(A)

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a general presumption of transparency, requiring federal agencies to disclose records to the public unless those records fall under a specific statutory exemption. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the central dispute involves Exemption 7(A), which allows an agency to withhold records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if the agency can demonstrate that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." To successfully invoke this exemption, the government must satisfy a two-pronged test: it must prove that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release would likely interfere with a proceeding that is either pending or reasonably anticipated.

The first requirement—that records be "compiled for law enforcement purposes"—is not automatically satisfied simply because a law enforcement agency created the document. The government must establish a rational "nexus" between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties. Furthermore, there must be a demonstrable connection between the individual or incident being investigated and a possible security risk or a violation of federal law. In *Farahi*, the court found this requirement easily met. The FBI’s declarations indicated the records were compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism, and the broader context—including the plaintiff’s own admissions regarding his alleged ties to terrorist organizations—confirmed the law enforcement nature of the file.

The second requirement focuses on the potential for interference with "enforcement proceedings." Under judicial interpretation, such proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court clarifies that ongoing criminal investigations qualify as proceedings for this purpose. The government can typically satisfy this requirement by attesting to the existence of the investigation. Interference is often established by showing that the release of records would "tip off" targets about the scope and nature of an investigation, thereby jeopardizing the government's ability to gather evidence for a future criminal case. In *Farahi*, the court noted that while the government's updated declarations were terse, they should be read in the context of earlier, more detailed ex parte submissions that established the pendency of the investigation.

The procedural burden in FOIA litigation rests with the government. The agency bears the burden of proving that a requested exemption applies to the withheld records. However, this burden is modulated by the doctrine of agency deference. Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, courts grant its determinations a "fair measure of deference." This deference is significantly heightened when the records in question implicate national security or counterterrorism investigations. The court in *Farahi* emphasized that the executive branch is entitled to substantial deference in these sensitive areas, which informs how the court evaluates the sufficiency of the agency's declarations.

Finally, even when an exemption applies, the government cannot withhold a document in its entirety if the exempt information is reasonably segregable. Under FOIA, agencies must take reasonable steps to separate and release nonexempt information. However, the government may withhold a record in full if the nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with the exempt portions. As applied in *Farahi*, this means that if nonexempt material is interspersed line-by-line throughout a document, it is not considered reasonably segregable, and the agency is not required to redact the document word-by-word.

# Analysis of the Exemption 7(A) Application

To determine whether the FBI was justified in withholding records from Foad Farahi's file, the court focused on the two-pronged requirement of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) Exemption 7(A). This exemption protects records that were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" and whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Because the FBI is a specialized law enforcement agency, the court began its analysis by applying a "fair measure of deference" to the agency's determinations, a standard particularly emphasized in cases involving national security and counterterrorism.

## Establishing the Rational Nexus Between the Records and Law Enforcement Duties

The first step in the court's analysis was determining if the records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." To satisfy this requirement, the government must establish a rational nexus between the investigation and the agency's law enforcement duties, as well as a connection between the subject of the investigation and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. 

The court found that the FBI easily met this burden. The agency submitted a public declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), stating the records were compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism. This assertion was bolstered by Farahi’s own admissions in the district court, where he conceded that the FBI had investigated his ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations and an individual who had been detained as an enemy combatant. Consequently, the court concluded there was a clear nexus between the records and the FBI's law enforcement mandate.

## Evaluating the "Ongoing" Nature of the Investigation Through the Hardy and Bender Declarations

The second prong of Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court noted that ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," and the government can typically establish their existence by attesting to them in a declaration.

In this case, the government relied on a sequence of declarations. The initial public declaration by David Hardy attested that the investigation was ongoing and that releasing the information would "tip off" targets regarding the scope of the investigation—a justification the court found widely acceptable. Because years had passed since the initial request, the district court required an update. Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS, submitted a subsequent declaration confirming that the investigations remained ongoing as of March 2023.

## Addressing the Challenge to the "Terse" Nature of Updated Declarations

Farahi challenged the sufficiency of the Bender declaration, arguing it was too conclusory and "terse." He relied on the precedent in *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ (CREW)*, which suggests that vague assertions that an investigation is "ongoing" may not be enough to justify a withholding.

The court rejected this argument by emphasizing that the Bender declaration should not be read in isolation. Unlike a primary declaration used to establish a case from scratch, the Bender declaration served as a supplemental update to the more detailed Hardy declarations (including an ex parte submission containing specific details). Because the district court had already found that the FBI "seemingly" met its burden based on the Hardy evidence, the court held that a brief confirmation from Bender was sufficient to prove that the status of the investigation had not changed.

## The Impact of the Investigation's Breadth on the Plausibility of Its Continued Pendency

The court further justified its reliance on the government's assertions by looking at the factual breadth of the FBI's investigation into Farahi. While the court could not publicly disclose the contents of the ex parte declarations, it referenced public allegations made during Farahi's immigration proceedings. These allegations included ties to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, connections to Al Qaeda members and convicted terrorists like Jose Padilla, and support for charities that financed terrorism. 

The court reasoned that given the severity and scope of these allegations, it was highly plausible that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist. This factual context reinforced the court's decision to defer to the FBI's claim that the investigation remained active and that disclosure would be disruptive.

## The Court's Treatment of the Plaintiff's Forfeited Arguments Regarding Document Categories

Finally, Farahi argued that the FBI failed to properly define the categories of documents it used to justify the withholdings. Under FOIA, when an agency withholds documents by category, those categories must be "sufficiently distinct" to allow the court to determine if the exemptions were applied correctly to each.

The court dismissed this challenge on procedural grounds. Because Farahi provided no specific explanation or evidence as to why the FBI's categories were overbroad, the court ruled that he had "forfeited" the argument. This serves as a reminder that a plaintiff cannot simply object to a government's categorization in general terms; they must provide a specific basis for the challenge to avoid forfeiture.

# Segregability and the Use of Ex Parte Evidence

Once a government agency determines that certain information in a requested record is exempt from disclosure under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), its obligation is not necessarily to withhold the entire document. Instead, the agency must follow the "segregability" requirement. This chapter explores the standards for segregating non-exempt information and the procedural mechanisms courts use to review sensitive evidence without compromising national security.

## The 'Reasonably Segregable' Standard under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)

Under 5. U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b), federal agencies are required to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." In practice, this means that if a document contains both exempt and non-exempt material, the agency must redact the protected portions and provide the remaining text to the requester. 

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the appellant argued that because the FBI's responsive files were vast—comprising thousands of pages and dozens of CDs—it was highly probable that some segregable, non-exempt information existed. However, the burden remains on the agency to demonstrate that further segregation is not reasonable. The court in *Farahi* affirmed the district court's finding that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" through its declarations explaining why the withheld records could not be further segregated.

## The 'Inextricably Intertwined' Doctrine for Withholding Entire Documents

While the general rule is to redact and release, there are circumstances where the government may withhold records in their entirety. The "inextricably intertwined" doctrine allows this when non-exempt portions of a document are so closely linked with exempt portions that they cannot be separated without revealing the protected information. 

As noted in *Juarez v. DOJ*, a court may rely on government affidavits that demonstrate with reasonable specificity why the documents cannot be further segregated. In *Farahi*, the court applied this principle, concluding that the FBI's submissions sufficiently showed that the exempt material (related to ongoing law enforcement proceedings) was so integrated into the records that the documents could not be meaningfully split.

## Meaningful Information versus Individual Words or Phrases

A critical distinction in FOIA litigation is the difference between "meaningful information" and mere "words." Plaintiffs often argue that the government should be forced to release any fragment of a document that is not specifically exempt. However, the court in *Farahi* clarified that the focus of FOIA is the disclosure of *information*, not the disclosure of individual words.

Relying on *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dept. of Air Force*, the court explained that if non-exempt material is "randomly 'interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,'" it is not "reasonably segregable." The court reasoned that while individual words or phrases might technically be non-exempt, their release would not provide the requester with meaningful information and would not outweigh the government's interest in protecting the exempt content.

## Legal Validity of Relying on Ex Parte Declarations in National Security Contexts

In most civil litigation, the parties have a right to see all evidence presented to the court. However, FOIA cases involving national security often create a paradox: to prove that a record is exempt from disclosure, the government may need to show the court the very secrets it is trying to protect. To resolve this, courts allow the use of *ex parte* declarations—submissions that are provided only to the judge and kept secret from the plaintiff.

In *Farahi*, the district court relied heavily on an *ex parte* declaration from Joseph Bender to determine whether the records were segregable and whether enforcement proceedings were still pending. Farahi challenged this reliance, arguing it handicapped his ability to contest the government's claims. The Court of Appeals rejected this argument, citing *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dept. of Veterans Affs.*, noting that *ex parte* submissions are often the only way to resolve a dispute without disclosing the sensitive material at the heart of the litigation.

## The Balance Between the Plaintiff's Right to Review and Government Secrecy

The use of *ex parte* evidence represents a significant departure from the traditional adversarial process, effectively balancing the plaintiff's right to an informed challenge against the government's need for secrecy. The *Farahi* court emphasized that such a balance is permissible, especially when the plaintiff has not explicitly opposed the use of *ex parte* motions at the trial level.

Ultimately, the court held that the district court did not need to put its analysis of the *ex parte* declaration on the public record. Because the FBI's declarations provided a rational nexus between the investigation and law enforcement duties, and the *ex parte* evidence confirmed the ongoing nature of those investigations, the court deferred to the agency's expertise in national security. This highlights the high degree of deference given to the FBI in Exemption 7(A) cases, particularly when the records implicate counterterrorism.

# Conclusion and Disposition

## Summary of the DC Circuit's Findings on Exemption 7(A) and Segregability

To resolve this case, the D.C. Circuit focused on whether the FBI had successfully invoked Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and whether the agency had met its obligation to segregate non-exempt information. Exemption 7(A) allows an agency to withhold records if they were compiled for law enforcement purposes and if their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are either pending or reasonably anticipated.

The court first found that the FBI satisfied the "law enforcement purpose" prong. Because the FBI is a specialized law enforcement agency, the court applied a measure of deference to its determination. The court reasoned that there was a clear rational nexus between the records and the FBI’s duties, as the files were related to investigations of national security and international terrorism. This finding was supported not only by agency declarations but also by Farahi’s own concessions regarding his history and associations.

The court then addressed whether disclosure would interfere with "pending or reasonably anticipated" proceedings. The primary legal dispute here was whether the government's declarations were too vague or conclusory to prove that an investigation was actually ongoing. While Farahi argued that a specific update (the Bender declaration) was too terse to be credible, the court adopted a holistic approach. It ruled that the Bender declaration should not be read in isolation, but rather as an update to the more detailed Hardy declarations. Given the breadth of the underlying investigation into Farahi’s alleged ties to terrorist organizations, the court concluded that the FBI had proven a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist.

Finally, the court addressed the issue of segregability. Under FOIA, an agency cannot withhold an entire document simply because some of it is exempt; it must release any "reasonably segregable" non-exempt portions. However, the court applied the "inextricably intertwined" standard, noting that when non-exempt information is so interspersed with exempt material that it cannot be separated without compromising the exemption, the agency may withhold the records in their entirety. Based on the FBI's ex parte submissions, the court determined that no meaningful information could be segregated and released.

## The Final Holding and the Affirmation of the District Court's Judgment

The D.C. Circuit concluded that the FBI had carried its burden of proof under both the statutory requirements of Exemption 7(A) and the procedural requirements for segregability. The court highlighted that the high stakes of national security and counterterrorism investigations justify a significant degree of deference to the executive branch, particularly when the court is reviewing ex parte declarations that the public cannot see.

Because the FBI successfully demonstrated that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, that disclosure would jeopardize an ongoing investigation, and that no meaningful portions of the records were segregable, the court found no error in the lower court's ruling. Consequently, the D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of the FBI, meaning the requested records remain protected from disclosure.
