Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # Statutory Mandates for Refugee Resettlement under 8 U.S.C. § 1522

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit addressed a critical distinction in immigration law: the difference between the President’s broad authority to control the entry of aliens into the United States and the executive branch's statutory obligations to those who have already been admitted. While the court deferred to the President's power to suspend the refugee admissions program under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), it held that this authority does not exempt the government from following specific congressional mandates regarding the domestic resettlement of refugees.

## The requirements of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (Reception and Placement services)

The core of the dispute involves "reception and placement" services, which are the domestic support systems provided to refugees after they arrive in the United States. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is tasked with ensuring that refugees are integrated into American society. Because the government does not typically provide these services directly, § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) allows the government to fulfill these duties through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies.

In this case, the government terminated nearly all cooperative agreements with these nonprofit organizations. The legal question for the court was whether the government had the discretion to terminate these services entirely or whether the statute created a mandatory obligation to ensure these services remained available to refugees already within the country.

## Mandatory obligations for employment training, English language training, and cash assistance

The court's analysis centered on the language of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A), which governs the conditions of resettlement assistance. The statute states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "shall"—to the extent of available appropriations—provide several specific resources:
1. Employment training and placement to achieve economic self-sufficiency.
2. English language training.
3. Cash assistance administered in a way that does not discourage self-sufficiency.

The court emphasized the use of the word "shall," interpreting it as a mandatory command rather than a permissive authorization. Although this mandate is qualified by the availability of appropriations, the court noted that Congress had indeed appropriated billions of dollars for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025. Consequently, the court found that the government was likely to lose its challenge under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) because it could not ignore a clear statutory mandate to provide these services simply because of a policy shift regarding new admissions.

## The legal status of unaccompanied minor refugees under § 1522(d)(2)(B)

The court further identified heightened protections for unaccompanied minor refugees. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(d)(2)(B), the Director is required to assume legal and financial responsibility for an unaccompanied child if such responsibility is "necessary" during the interim period before the child is placed in a permanent setting. 

The court viewed this provision as another example of a statutory obligation that persists regardless of the President's decision to suspend the broader refugee admissions program. By using the phrase "shall assume legal responsibility," the statute creates a specific duty of care for a vulnerable population that the executive branch cannot unilaterally waive.

## The government's failure to establish a new framework for providing mandated services

A pivotal moment in the court's reasoning occurred during oral argument. The government had previously represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals" to find new agencies to provide the mandated resettlement services. However, when questioned by the panel during oral argument, the government admitted it was unaware of whether any actual progress had been made toward securing new providers.

This admission was critical to the court's decision. Because the government had terminated the existing cooperative agreements but had failed to implement a replacement framework, the court concluded that the statutory mandates of § 1522 were not being met. The government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide these services created a gap in legal compliance that the court felt compelled to close.

## The court's order to reinstate cooperative agreements to ensure statutory compliance

Because the government failed to demonstrate that it was fulfilling its duties under § 1522, the Ninth Circuit issued a specific directive: the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The court specified that these must include the "usual and customary services" provided under the prior agreements.

It is important to note the tension within the court on this point. While the majority viewed § 1522 as a mandate, Judge Lee wrote a partial dissent arguing that the statute is merely "authorizing." Judge Lee pointed to the section title ("Authorization for programs...") and the repeated use of the word "authorized" in subsections (b) through (e) to argue that Congress gave the President *permission* to fund these programs, but did not *require* the government to operate them. The majority, however, rejected this reading, holding that the "shall" in section (a) created a binding obligation that the government must fulfill.
