Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# The Persistence of the Hawaii Doctrine: The Ninth Circuit Reaffirms Broad Presidential Power to Suspend Refugee Entry

The Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* serves as a stark reminder of the enduring power of the "Hawaii Doctrine." By staying a district court's injunction against a presidential order suspending the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), the court has reaffirmed that when the President invokes 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the judiciary’s role is remarkably limited. The decision effectively separates the act of *entering* the United States from the *services* provided once inside, creating a bifurcated legal reality for refugees.

The core of the dispute rests on the tension between the Refugee Act of 1980—which established a comprehensive statutory framework for refugee admissions—and the President’s authority under § 1182(f). The latter allows the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens if he finds such entry "detrimental to the interests of the United States." In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit refused to engage in a "searching inquiry" into whether the President's finding of detriment was substantiated. Following the precedent set in *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court held that the sole prerequisite is that the President simply *makes* the finding. Once that box is checked, the court views the President's authority as nearly absolute, regardless of whether the suspension conflicts with the general goals of the Refugee Act.

This application of *Hawaii* essentially grants the Executive Branch a "blank check" regarding the borders. By treating the finding of "detriment" as a conclusory trigger rather than a factual determination subject to judicial review, the court has signaled that the administrative machinery of refugee admissions can be halted by a single executive decree. For practitioners and advocates, this confirms that challenges to entry bans based on § 1182(f) are uphill battles; the court is unwilling to second-guess the President’s national security or foreign policy justifications.

However, the court drew a significant line at the point of admission. While the President may stop refugees from arriving, the majority held that he cannot simply ignore the statutory obligations owed to those who have already been admitted. The court pointed to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, ruling that the government is legally mandated to provide "reception and placement" services—such as English training and employment assistance—to refugees already within the U.S. By ordering the government to reinstate cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations, the court asserted that while the President can close the door, he cannot starve those who already walked through it.

This distinction sparked a sharp dissent from Judge Lee, who argued that § 1522 is an "authorization," not a "mandate." Judge Lee’s reading suggests that "authorization" is permissive, meaning the Executive retains the discretion to fund or defund these services based on available appropriations. This disagreement highlights a classic statutory interpretation conflict: does a "shall" provision in a section titled "Authorization" create a mandatory duty or merely set the conditions for a discretionary program?

The doctrinal implications of *Pacito* are profound. First, it solidifies the primacy of § 1182(f) over the Refugee Act, suggesting that the general statutory scheme for refugee admissions is subordinate to the President's broad entry powers. Second, it establishes a potential "safe harbor" for refugees already admitted, asserting that domestic statutory mandates can survive even when the broader program is suspended.

Downstream, this decision may encourage future administrations to use § 1182(f) as a primary tool for systemic policy shifts, knowing that the courts will likely defer to the "detriment" finding. However, the court's insistence on the mandate of § 1522 creates a critical friction point: the government cannot unilaterally dismantle the domestic infrastructure of refugee support without risking judicial intervention. For now, the "Hawaii Doctrine" remains the dominant force at the border, but the Ninth Circuit has signaled that presidential discretion ends where specific, mandatory statutory duties to individuals begin.



# Authorization vs. Mandate: The Battle Over 'Shall' in the Refugee Act

The Ninth Circuit recently issued a critical interim order in *Pacito v. Trump*, a case that serves as a microcosm of the broader tension between presidential discretion in immigration and the statutory obligations of the administrative state. While the court’s ruling provides a significant victory for the executive branch regarding the power to suspend refugee entry, it draws a firm line at the treatment of refugees already within the United States. The core of the dispute now rests on a classic statutory interpretation conflict: does the Refugee Act authorize the government to provide resettlement services, or does it mandate them?

The "easy" part of the ruling for the government concerns the President’s authority to suspend the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Following the precedent set in *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court reaffirmed that § 1182(f) grants the President broad, deferential power to restrict the entry of any class of aliens if such entry is deemed detrimental to U.S. interests. In the court's view, the President has clearly met the statutory threshold by simply "finding" that such a suspension is necessary. For the purposes of this stay, the court accepted that the executive has the authority to close the door to new refugees.

The legal knot tightens, however, when the analysis shifts from the border to the interior. The majority held that while the President can stop refugees from entering, the government cannot unilaterally vanish the "reception and placement" services for those who have already been admitted. The court pointed to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which uses the word "shall" when describing the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement’s duties to provide employment training, English language training, and cash assistance. By interpreting these provisions as mandatory, the court ordered the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies that actually deliver these services.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent offers a sharply contrasting textualist critique. To Lee, the majority is ignoring the structural hierarchy of the statute. He argues that the very title of Section 1522—"Authorization for programs..."—signals that the entire section is permissive. In Lee's reading, the "shall" language in subsection (a) does not create a standalone mandate to provide services; rather, it creates mandatory *conditions* that must be met *if* the government chooses to exercise its authorization to fund those programs. Under this interpretation, the executive branch possesses the discretion to decide whether to operate these programs at all, regardless of whether Congress has provided the appropriations.

This disagreement exposes a significant doctrinal rift regarding the "shall/may" distinction in administrative law. The majority views "shall" as a command that creates a legal duty to the beneficiaries of the program. Judge Lee views "shall" as a set of instructions for a discretionary power. If Lee’s view were to prevail, it would suggest that almost any "authorized" government program—even those with specific appropriations—could be unilaterally terminated by the executive branch without violating the law, provided the statute does not explicitly forbid such a termination.

The downstream effects of this ruling are substantial. By requiring the government to maintain these cooperative agreements, the Ninth Circuit has established that presidential discretion under § 1182(f) does not extend to the erasure of statutory obligations to persons already lawfully admitted. However, because this is a stay pending a final opinion, the battle over whether § 1522 is an "authorization" or a "mandate" remains unresolved. The final merits decision will likely determine whether the executive branch can use a policy of "defunding" as a tool to effectively nullify statutory programs that Congress has specifically appropriated to support.



# Crossing the Threshold: Distinguishing the Power to Exclude from the Duty to Support

The Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* provides a critical roadmap for where the President’s broad immigration powers end and the government’s statutory obligations begin. In a nuanced ruling, the court drew a sharp line between the "threshold" of the United States border and the domestic reality of resettlement, ruling that while the President may effectively shut the door to refugees, he cannot unilaterally ignore the legal duties owed to those who have already walked through it.

The case centered on two executive actions: the suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the subsequent defunding of domestic resettlement services. The Ninth Circuit’s handling of these two issues demonstrates a stark contrast in judicial deference. On the issue of entry, the court leaned heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, reaffirming that § 1182(f) grants the President "ample power" to suspend the entry of any class of aliens deemed detrimental to U.S. interests. By staying the district court's injunction against the suspension of admissions, the Ninth Circuit signaled that when the President invokes § 1182(f), the judiciary will not engage in a "searching inquiry" into the wisdom or persuasiveness of that decision.

However, the court’s analysis shifted dramatically once the conversation moved from the border to the interior. The government had terminated cooperative agreements that provided "reception and placement" services—essential supports like English training and employment assistance—for refugees already admitted to the U.S. The Ninth Circuit refused to stay the injunction requiring the reinstatement of these services, finding that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a statutory mandate. By interpreting the "shall" language in § 1522 as an obligation rather than a mere suggestion, the court held that the Executive cannot use a policy of general suspension to bypass specific congressional directives regarding the care of admitted refugees.

This distinction is the core of the "threshold" approach. The court is essentially arguing that while the President has nearly plenary power to determine *who* enters the country, that power does not extend to rewriting the rules for those who have already legally entered. Once a refugee is admitted, they are no longer merely a "class of aliens" subject to the President's entry restrictions; they are individuals to whom the government owes specific, statutory duties of care.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent offers a potent textualist counter-narrative. He argues that § 1522 is an "authorization"—a permission slip from Congress—rather than a mandate. In Lee’s view, the "shall" language in the statute only triggers *if* the government chooses to fund the programs. This is a classic struggle in administrative law: whether a statute "authorizes" an agency to act or "commands" it to do so. The majority’s decision to treat these services as mandatory is bolstered by the fact that Congress had already appropriated billions for these specific purposes, suggesting that the legislative intent was for these programs to function, not to sit dormant at the whim of the Executive.

The doctrinal implications of *Pacito* are significant. If the "threshold" rule holds, it creates a potent check on the use of "defunding" as a tool of immigration policy. It suggests that the Executive cannot circumvent statutory obligations by simply cutting off the administrative machinery used to implement them. For the government, this means that a broad policy of "realignment" or "suspension" cannot be used as a blanket justification to ignore specific, non-discretionary duties imposed by Congress.

Ultimately, *Pacito* reinforces a fundamental principle of American law: the President’s power is vast, but it is not absolute. The power to exclude is a shield used at the border, but it cannot be used as a sword to sever the legal obligations the government assumes once a person is lawfully admitted to the United States.



# Administrative Vacuum: The APA and the Sudden Termination of Cooperative Agreements

The Ninth Circuit recently issued a nuanced order in *Pacito v. Trump* that draws a sharp line between the President’s broad authority to control the borders and the Executive Branch’s statutory obligations to those already within them. While the court affirmed the President’s wide latitude to suspend refugee admissions, it refused to allow the administration to create an "administrative vacuum" by summarily terminating the services mandated by Congress for refugees already admitted to the United States.

The case centers on two executive orders: one suspending the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and another defunding the program, including the "reception and placement" services provided to refugees via cooperative agreements with nonprofit organizations. The Ninth Circuit’s order stays the lower court's injunctions in part, but critically directs the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide resettlement services.

On the issue of the refugee moratorium, the court followed the well-trodden path of *Trump v. Hawaii*. The panel acknowledged that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President, granting him ample power to suspend the entry of any class of aliens deemed detrimental to U.S. interests. By ruling that the President had "undoubtedly fulfilled" the requirement of making such a finding, the court signaled that the merits of the suspension itself are largely insulated from judicial second-guessing.

However, the court’s analysis shifts dramatically when moving from the *entry* of refugees to their *resettlement*. The government's attempt to defund the domestic side of the program encountered a statutory wall in 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The court found that the government is unlikely to prevail on the argument that it can unilaterally terminate all resettlement services. The court pointed to specific mandates within § 1522 requiring the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide employment training, English language training, and cash assistance.

The most damning detail for the government was not just the statutory language, but the administrative reality. The government had terminated all cooperative agreements with nonprofit providers but admitted at oral argument that it had no viable plan or agency in place to actually deliver these mandated services. By erasing the administrative machinery without a replacement, the government moved beyond policy discretion into a violation of statutory mandates. This is where the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) becomes the primary tool for the judiciary: the court isn't necessarily challenging the President's policy goal, but rather the legality of implementing that goal by ignoring a clear congressional directive.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent highlights the central doctrinal conflict here: is § 1522 a mandate or a mere authorization? Judge Lee argues that the section's title—"Authorization for programs"—and the repeated use of the word "authorized" indicate that the funding is permissive. In Lee's view, if the Executive decides not to fund the program, it is simply exercising its discretion.

The majority, however, reads the "shall" provisions of § 1522 as creating a mandatory duty that persists as long as appropriations are available. This creates a significant downstream implication for how the Executive Branch manages "cooperative agreements." If a statutory mandate exists, the government cannot simply "defund" its way out of that obligation by terminating the third-party contracts that make the service possible, especially when it lacks an alternative means of delivery.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a reminder that while the President may have the keys to the gate, he does not have an unfettered license to dismantle the statutory infrastructure Congress has built for those who have already walked through it. The case will likely head toward the Supreme Court, where the clash between "authorization" and "mandate" will determine whether the Executive can effectively nullify congressional intent through administrative erasure.
