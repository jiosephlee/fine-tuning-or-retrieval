Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Executive Order 14163: The Suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)

The dispute in *Pacito v. Trump* began on January 20, 2025, when President Trump issued Executive Order (EO) 14163, "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." In this order, the President determined that the entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) was detrimental to the interests of the United States. Relying on the authority granted by 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President suspended the entry of refugees under the USRAP pending further findings. Under § 1182(f), the President possesses broad authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens if he finds such entry to be detrimental to the national interest.

## Executive Order 14169: The Suspension of Funding for USRAP and Domestic Resettlement

Shortly after the suspension of refugee entry, the administration moved to dismantle the financial infrastructure supporting the program. On January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for the USRAP. This action was purportedly based on a separate executive order, EO 14169, titled "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid." Notably, this funding suspension extended beyond the overseas processing of refugees; it also included the funding for domestic resettlement services provided to refugees who had already been admitted to the United States.

## The Plaintiffs: Individual Refugees and Cooperative Resettlement Organizations

The plaintiffs in this matter consist of two distinct groups. The first group is comprised of refugees—some who had already been admitted to the U.S. and others who had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country. The second group consists of three organizations that operated under cooperative agreements with the State Department. These organizations were responsible for the overseas processing of refugee applicants and the provision of resettlement services for those arriving in the U.S. Together, these plaintiffs challenged the administration's actions in the Western District of Washington, arguing that the suspension of the refugee program violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and that the defunding of the USRAP violated the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).

## The District Court's Preliminary Injunctions (February and March 2025)

The district court initially intervened on February 25, 2025, by orally issuing a preliminary injunction that prohibited the government from implementing the suspension of the refugee program. On February 28, the court issued a written order clarifying that this injunction also prohibited the government from suspending USRAP funding. 

The conflict intensified when the government terminated nearly all cooperative agreements for refugee processing and placement. In response, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24, 2025, ordering the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. The government subsequently appealed both the February 28 and March 24 orders to the Ninth Circuit.

## The Sequence of Administrative and Limited Stays Issued by the Ninth Circuit

The procedural history in the Ninth Circuit is characterized by a series of shifting stays and "carveouts." On March 25, 2025, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit partially stayed the district court’s February 28 order. While the court acknowledged the President's broad authority under § 1182(f), it created a "limited carveout," refusing to stay the injunction as it applied to individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. This carveout was further clarified by court orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025.

The tension peaked in July 2025. After the district court established a framework to enforce the "limited carveout," the government filed an emergency motion for clarification and amendment. On July 18, 2025, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court's enforcement framework. This sequence of events led to the expedited oral argument on September 3, 2025, and the subsequent September 12 order, which vacated the previous administrative stays and issued a comprehensive stay pending the final resolution of the appeal.

# The Legal Standard for Stays Pending Appeal

When a district court issues a preliminary injunction—such as the orders in *Pacito v. Trump* that prohibited the government from suspending refugee admissions and defunding resettlement services—the government often asks an appellate court to "stay" that injunction. A stay pending appeal effectively pauses the lower court's order, allowing the status quo (or the challenged government policy) to remain in place until the appellate court reaches a final decision on the merits. In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit had to determine whether the district court's injunctions should be stayed while the court reviewed the legality of the President's executive orders.

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test

To determine whether to grant a stay, the Ninth Circuit applies a well-established four-factor framework derived from the Supreme Court case *Nken v. Holder*. The court considers: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that they are likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured without a stay; (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. While all four factors are relevant, the court emphasizes that the first two—likelihood of success on the merits and the threat of irreparable injury—are the "most critical" components of the analysis.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits

The "likelihood of success on the merits" factor is a predictive analysis: the court asks whether the party seeking the stay is likely to win the case once a full decision is issued. In *Pacito*, the court found that the government's likelihood of success varied depending on which part of the challenged policy was being analyzed.

Regarding the suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under Executive Order 14163, the court found the government was likely to prevail. The court relied on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which grants the President broad authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens deemed detrimental to the interests of the United States. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court noted that this statutory authority "exudes deference" to the President. Because the President had made a finding that the entry of refugees was detrimental, the court concluded the government had fulfilled the sole prerequisite of the statute.

However, the court found the government was *not* likely to prevail on the challenge regarding the defunding of resettlement services. The court pointed to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which specifies that the government "shall" provide certain reception and placement services (such as English language training and employment resources) to refugees admitted to the United States. Because this statutory language appeared mandatory, the court reasoned that the government could not unilaterally terminate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide these services.

## Irreparable Injury to the Applicant

The second critical factor is whether the applicant (in this case, the government) will suffer "irreparable injury" if the stay is denied. Irreparable injury refers to harm that cannot be undone or adequately compensated by money damages after the case is eventually decided. In *Pacito*, the court noted that the remaining factors—including irreparable injury—favored the United States. When the government is the applicant, this often involves the argument that the inability to enforce a national security or immigration policy causes immediate and lasting harm to the country's interests.

## Substantial Injury to Other Interested Parties

The third factor requires the court to weigh the harm to the applicant against the harm to other parties, such as the refugee plaintiffs and the organizations providing resettlement services. If a stay is granted, the plaintiffs are "injured" because the lower court's protection (the injunction) is removed. In *Pacito*, the court balanced these interests by granting a partial stay: it stayed the injunction regarding the suspension of refugee entry (favoring the government) but refused to stay the injunction regarding resettlement services (protecting the refugees already in the country).

## The Role of the Public Interest in Immigration and National Security

The final factor is the "public interest." In cases involving immigration and national security, the public interest is often tied to the Executive Branch's ability to manage the nation's borders and foreign affairs. The Ninth Circuit noted that the President is conferred "broad discretion" over domestic and foreign interests.

This deference is central to the court's reasoning. By applying the *Nken* factors, the court determined that the public interest in upholding the President's broad authority to restrict entry under § 1182(f) outweighed the plaintiffs' interests. Conversely, the public interest in complying with the statutory mandates of the Refugee Act regarding those already admitted to the U.S. necessitated the reinstatement of resettlement services.

# Presidential Authority to Suspend Entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the court addressed whether the President possesses the legal authority to suspend the entry of refugees into the United States. The central legal question revolved around the scope of the President's power under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the degree of deference that federal courts must afford the executive branch when the President invokes this statutory authority to restrict immigration.

## The statutory text of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

The authority for Executive Order 14163, which suspended the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), is rooted in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). This statute provides that whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens, or any class of aliens, "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States," he may issue a proclamation to suspend such entry for a period he deems necessary. Additionally, the statute allows the President to "impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." 

In this case, the President utilized this authority to determine that the entry of refugees under the USRAP was detrimental to U.S. interests and subsequently directed that their entry be suspended. The court's analysis focused on whether this action exceeded the textual limits of the statute.

## Application of the Trump v. Hawaii precedent

To determine the validity of the suspension, the Ninth Circuit relied heavily on the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* (2018). The court noted that the authority granted by § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the executive branch. According to the *Hawaii* precedent, the statute vests the President with "ample power" to impose entry restrictions that go beyond those specifically enumerated elsewhere in the Immigration and Nationality Act.

By applying this precedent, the court established that the President's power to control the borders and determine who may enter the country is exceptionally broad. This level of deference means that the court is not looking for a detailed administrative record or a complex set of justifications, but is instead assessing whether the President acted within the broad permissions granted by Congress.

## The 'detrimental to the interests of the United States' finding

A critical component of the court's reasoning was the interpretation of the "finding" requirement in § 1182(f). The statute requires the President to find that the entry of a class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interest of the United States." The court clarified that this is the "sole prerequisite" for exercising the power to suspend entry.

In *Pacito*, the court found that the President had undoubtedly fulfilled this requirement by stating in Executive Order 14163 that the entry of refugees under the USRAP would be detrimental to the United States. Because the statute only requires that the President *make* such a finding—rather than prove it to a specific evidentiary standard—the court accepted the finding as a valid trigger for the suspension of admissions.

## Judicial restraint regarding the President's justifications for entry restrictions

The court emphasized a strict standard of judicial restraint when reviewing the President's justifications for entry restrictions. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court stated that it cannot engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness" of the President's reasons for finding an entry to be detrimental.

This means that the judiciary will not second-guess the wisdom, logic, or accuracy of the President's determination. As long as the President has issued a finding of detriment, the court will not scrutinize the evidence behind that finding or demand that the President provide a detailed explanation to the court to justify the restriction.

## The court's conclusion on the validity of the suspension of admissions

Applying these standards, the court concluded that the government was likely to prevail on the merits regarding the validity of the suspension of refugee admissions. Because the language of § 1182(f) is clear and the Executive Order did not exceed any textual limits on the President's authority, the court found the suspension legally permissible.

Consequently, as part of its stay pending appeal, the court stayed the district court's preliminary injunctions to the extent they prohibited the suspension of USRAP admissions. The court determined that the broad discretion conferred on the President regarding domestic and foreign interests weighed heavily in favor of the United States.

# Statutory Mandates for Refugee Resettlement under 8 U.S.C. § 1522

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit addressed a critical distinction in immigration law: the difference between the President’s broad authority to control the entry of aliens into the United States and the executive branch's statutory obligations to those who have already been admitted. While the court deferred to the President's power to suspend the refugee admissions program under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), it held that this authority does not exempt the government from following specific congressional mandates regarding the domestic resettlement of refugees.

## The requirements of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (Reception and Placement services)

The core of the dispute involves "reception and placement" services, which are the domestic support systems provided to refugees after they arrive in the United States. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is tasked with ensuring that refugees are integrated into American society. Because the government does not typically provide these services directly, § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) allows the government to fulfill these duties through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies.

In this case, the government terminated nearly all cooperative agreements with these nonprofit organizations. The legal question for the court was whether the government had the discretion to terminate these services entirely or whether the statute created a mandatory obligation to ensure these services remained available to refugees already within the country.

## Mandatory obligations for employment training, English language training, and cash assistance

The court's analysis centered on the language of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A), which governs the conditions of resettlement assistance. The statute states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "shall"—to the extent of available appropriations—provide several specific resources:
1. Employment training and placement to achieve economic self-sufficiency.
2. English language training.
3. Cash assistance administered in a way that does not discourage self-sufficiency.

The court emphasized the use of the word "shall," interpreting it as a mandatory command rather than a permissive authorization. Although this mandate is qualified by the availability of appropriations, the court noted that Congress had indeed appropriated billions of dollars for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025. Consequently, the court found that the government was likely to lose its challenge under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) because it could not ignore a clear statutory mandate to provide these services simply because of a policy shift regarding new admissions.

## The legal status of unaccompanied minor refugees under § 1522(d)(2)(B)

The court further identified heightened protections for unaccompanied minor refugees. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(d)(2)(B), the Director is required to assume legal and financial responsibility for an unaccompanied child if such responsibility is "necessary" during the interim period before the child is placed in a permanent setting. 

The court viewed this provision as another example of a statutory obligation that persists regardless of the President's decision to suspend the broader refugee admissions program. By using the phrase "shall assume legal responsibility," the statute creates a specific duty of care for a vulnerable population that the executive branch cannot unilaterally waive.

## The government's failure to establish a new framework for providing mandated services

A pivotal moment in the court's reasoning occurred during oral argument. The government had previously represented to the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals" to find new agencies to provide the mandated resettlement services. However, when questioned by the panel during oral argument, the government admitted it was unaware of whether any actual progress had been made toward securing new providers.

This admission was critical to the court's decision. Because the government had terminated the existing cooperative agreements but had failed to implement a replacement framework, the court concluded that the statutory mandates of § 1522 were not being met. The government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide these services created a gap in legal compliance that the court felt compelled to close.

## The court's order to reinstate cooperative agreements to ensure statutory compliance

Because the government failed to demonstrate that it was fulfilling its duties under § 1522, the Ninth Circuit issued a specific directive: the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The court specified that these must include the "usual and customary services" provided under the prior agreements.

It is important to note the tension within the court on this point. While the majority viewed § 1522 as a mandate, Judge Lee wrote a partial dissent arguing that the statute is merely "authorizing." Judge Lee pointed to the section title ("Authorization for programs...") and the repeated use of the word "authorized" in subsections (b) through (e) to argue that Congress gave the President *permission* to fund these programs, but did not *require* the government to operate them. The majority, however, rejected this reading, holding that the "shall" in section (a) created a binding obligation that the government must fulfill.

# The Dissent: Authorization vs. Mandate

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the majority and the dissenting opinion of Judge Lee agree on one primary point: the President possesses broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of refugees into the United States. However, they diverge sharply on whether the government is legally required to provide resettlement services to refugees who have already been admitted. While the majority views these services as a statutory mandate, Judge Lee argues that the Refugee Act provides the executive branch with the permission, but not the obligation, to fund such programs.

## The interpretation of 'Authorization' as permissive rather than mandatory

The core of Judge Lee’s dissent rests on a linguistic and structural analysis of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. In statutory interpretation, the distinction between a "mandate" (a requirement that must be performed) and an "authorization" (a grant of permission to perform an act) is critical. Judge Lee points first to the title of Section 1522, which is explicitly labeled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.”

Drawing on *Black's Law Dictionary*, Judge Lee argues that "authorization" refers to the "permission to do something." He contends that by titling the section as an authorization, Congress signaled that the executive branch has the legal power to create and fund resettlement programs, but is not compelled to do so. This interpretation is further supported by the language in subsections (b) through (e), which state that the Director is “authorized” to provide various forms of assistance. To Judge Lee, these are "statutory signals" that funding and implementation remain within the discretion of the executive branch.

## The distinction between 'shall' as a condition of funding versus a mandate to fund

A primary point of contention between the majority and the dissent is the use of the word "shall" in § 1522(a). In legal writing, "shall" is often interpreted as creating a mandatory obligation. The majority relied on this word to conclude that the government must provide specific resources for employment training, English language training, and cash assistance.

Judge Lee counters this by analyzing the structural context of the "shall" language. He observes that the mandatory language in § 1522(a) is preceded by the phrase, “In providing assistance under this section...” He argues that this makes the "shall" requirements conditional. In his view, § 1522(a) does not command the government to provide assistance; rather, it mandates *how* that assistance must be administered *if* the government chooses to exercise its discretionary authority under subsections (b) through (e). Essentially, the "shall" creates a set of rules for the program's operation, not a requirement that the program must exist.

## The role of 'available appropriations' and discretionary phrases

Judge Lee further argues that the statute is peppered with qualifiers that undermine the idea of a rigid mandate. He highlights the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” in § 1522(a), suggesting that the government's obligations are inherently limited by the funding provided by Congress and the decision of the executive to allocate those funds.

Beyond the budget, Judge Lee identifies "discretionary phrases" within the statute that grant the executive branch leeway in determining the scope of services. He points to § 1522(d)(2)(B), which requires the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied children only “if necessary,” and § 1522(e)(7)(A), which requires the implementation of medical support projects “as needed.” By using terms like “if necessary” and “as needed,” Judge Lee argues that Congress left the “metes and bounds” of these programs to executive discretion, rather than establishing a firm, non-negotiable mandate.

## The argument for broader executive discretion over domestic resettlement programs

Ultimately, Judge Lee’s dissent is an argument for judicial restraint in the face of executive policy decisions. He posits that because Section 1522 is permissive rather than mandatory, the court has no legal basis to force the executive branch to reinstate cooperative agreements or provide resettlement services.

From Lee's perspective, the decision to limit or terminate the provision of services for refugees is a policy choice. Because he views the statute as granting the executive the power to craft these programs as it sees fit, he concludes that the court cannot mandate the operation of these programs. Consequently, he would have stayed the district court's injunctions in their entirety, allowing the executive branch to implement its policy of limiting both the admission and the domestic support of refugees.
