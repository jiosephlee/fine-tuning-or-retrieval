Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Standards for Reviewing Indictment Sufficiency

When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment, the court must determine whether the charging document is legally sufficient to support a conviction if all the allegations are proven. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit addressed this threshold issue after a district court dismissed an indictment for securities fraud, ruling that the government failed to allege the deprivation of a traditional property interest.

## De Novo Review: The Standard for Reviewing the Sufficiency of an Indictment

The Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of an indictment *de novo*. This means the appellate court does not defer to the district court's legal conclusions; instead, it examines the indictment independently to determine if it meets the legal requirements for a valid charge. Because the question of whether an indictment states an offense is a legal determination, the appellate court applies the same standard of review it would use for any question of law.

## The 'Presumption of Truth': Treating Indictment Allegations as True

A critical aspect of this review is the "presumption of truth." When determining whether an indictment is sufficient, the court is not weighing evidence or deciding if the government's claims are actually true. Rather, the court must take the allegations in the indictment as true. If the facts alleged in the document, assuming they are accurate, describe a crime, the indictment is sufficient to proceed to trial. The court's role at this stage is not to evaluate the strength of the government's case, but simply to determine whether an offense has been stated.

## Three Pillars of a Sufficient Indictment: Prima Facie Elements, Notice to the Defendant, and Double Jeopardy Protection

To be legally sufficient under Fifth Circuit precedent, an indictment must satisfy three specific functions. First, it must enumerate each *prima facie* element of the charged offense. This ensures that the government has alleged every legal component required to prove the crime. Second, the indictment must fairly inform the defendant of the charges against them. This "notice" requirement is essential for the defendant to prepare an adequate legal defense. Third, the indictment must provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. By specifying the acts and the timeframe of the alleged crime, the indictment prevents the government from charging the defendant again for the same conduct following an acquittal.

## The Material Element Requirement

Ultimately, these three pillars coalesce into a single requirement: an indictment must allege each material element of the offense. A material element is a necessary legal component of the crime that the government must prove to secure a conviction. 

In *Constantinescu*, the central dispute revolved around these material elements. The defendants argued that the indictment was insufficient because it failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a "traditional property interest," which they contended was a material element of fraud. The Fifth Circuit's analysis focused on whether the government's "fraudulent-inducement theory"—alleging that defendants tricked followers into spending money to buy securities—sufficiently alleged the material elements of a scheme and intent to defraud.
