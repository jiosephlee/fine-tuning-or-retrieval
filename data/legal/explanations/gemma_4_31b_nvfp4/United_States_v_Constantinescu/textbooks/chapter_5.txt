Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 5: # Intent and the Requirement of Economic Harm

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the defendants challenged the sufficiency of their indictment by arguing that the government failed to allege a proper "intent to defraud." Their argument rested on a specific interpretation of "harm": they contended that because the government alleged they intended to profit from their "pump and dump" scheme, rather than specifically intending to cause their followers to lose money, the legal requirement for intent was not met.

## The 'Net Pecuniary Loss' Argument

The defendants argued that the indictment did not sufficiently allege an intent to defraud because it did not claim they intended to leave their victims economically worse off. In their view, the objective of the scheme was their own enrichment, not the financial injury of their social media followers. This argument suggests that for a defendant to possess the requisite "intent to defraud," the government must prove that the defendant specifically sought to "hurt the victim’s bottom line" or cause a net pecuniary loss.

## Analysis of Kousisis v. United States

The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument by relying on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. In *Kousisis*, a contractor used a "pass-through" entity to falsely satisfy a requirement to subcontract with a disadvantaged business in order to obtain government contracts. The Supreme Court in that case clarified the distinction between "obtaining" property and "hurting the bottom line."

The Court held that the federal fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an actual economic loss, nor do they require the defendant to intend that the victim suffer such a loss. Instead, the statutes are violated when a defendant schemes to "obtain" the victim's money or property through deceit. The focus is on the act of obtaining the property through fraud, not the ultimate financial balance sheet of the victim.

## The Role of Consideration

A key point of contention in *Constantinescu* was the nature of the transactions. The defendants induced their followers to purchase securities. Because the followers received securities in exchange for their money, the defendants argued that there was no intent to harm, as the victims were receiving an asset of potentially equal market value.

The court, applying the reasoning from *Kousisis*, explained that providing something of value in return—whether it be money, property, or services—does not negate the fraud. A defendant may be liable for securities fraud even if they provide the victim with something of equal value. The crime lies in the use of material misrepresentations to induce the victim to part with their money; the "fairness" of the exchange or the lack of a net loss does not erase the fraudulent nature of the inducement.

## Conclusion on Intent

The court concluded that the "intent to defraud" is satisfied when a defendant's object is to obtain money or property through deception. In this case, the indictment alleged that the defendants posted false information specifically so that investors would be induced to purchase securities. 

Under the "fraudulent-inducement theory," a fraud is complete the moment a defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses. The court determined that the defendants' desire to profit from the "pump and dump" demonstrated that they "had money in mind," which is the essence of the intent required by the statute. Consequently, the lack of an intended net pecuniary loss to the victims did not render the indictment insufficient.
