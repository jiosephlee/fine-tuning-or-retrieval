Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 4: # Property Interests: Right-to-Control vs. Fraudulent Inducement

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit addresses a critical distinction in federal fraud law: what constitutes a "property interest" sufficient to support a criminal conviction. To understand the court's reasoning, a student must distinguish between the "right-to-control" theory of fraud and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. This distinction determines whether a defendant’s deceptive conduct is a federal crime or merely an unethical deprivation of information.

## The Right-to-Control Theory: Deprivation of Valuable Economic Information

The "right-to-control" theory is a legal framework previously used by some prosecutors to argue that a victim is defrauded if they are deprived of "potentially valuable economic information" necessary to make a discretionary decision. Under this theory, the "property" being stolen is not necessarily money or a physical asset, but rather the victim's *right to control* their own assets by having access to honest information. 

In the context of a "pump and dump" scheme, a defendant relying on this theory would argue that by posting misleading information about a stock, they deprived their followers of the truthful information needed to decide whether or not to invest. However, as the court explains, this theory focuses on the loss of information rather than the loss of a tangible asset.

## Analysis of Ciminelli v. United States: Why the Right-to-Control Theory is Insufficient

The Supreme Court fundamentally rejected the right-to-control theory in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In that case, the government attempted to convict a construction company owner for wire fraud because he used a lobbyist to help secure state-funded jobs, thereby depriving the state of the honest information necessary to make an informed decision about awarding contracts. 

The Supreme Court held that the federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests." Because the "right to valuable economic information" is not a traditional property interest, it cannot form the basis for a fraud conviction. The Court's reasoning is that the fraud statutes are intended to protect money and property, not to police every instance of dishonesty or the deprivation of information. Therefore, if a government's case rests *solely* on the idea that a victim was denied information, it fails as a matter of law.

## The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory: Tricking Victims into Parting with Money or Property

In contrast to the right-to-control theory, the "fraudulent-inducement theory" focuses on the actual transfer of assets. This theory applies when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into entering a contract or a transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. 

As the court notes via the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, fraudulent inducement is not a "repackaging" of the right-to-control theory. The key difference is the object of the fraud: while the right-to-control theory seeks to protect the *decision-making process* (information), the fraudulent-inducement theory protects the *assets themselves* (money and property). Crucially, under *Kousisis*, a defendant can be liable under this theory even if the victim receives something of equal value in return, or if the defendant did not intend to cause a net pecuniary loss, so long as the defendant's object was to obtain the victim's money or property through deceit.

## Application to the Case: Why Inducing the Purchase of Securities Constitutes a Deprivation of a Traditional Property Interest

In *Constantinescu*, the defendants argued that the indictment was insufficient because it merely alleged they deprived followers of economic information. The district court agreed, effectively treating the indictment as if it were based on the right-to-control theory. However, the Fifth Circuit reversed this conclusion by analyzing the specific allegations in the indictment.

The court found that the indictment did not claim the victims were merely deprived of information; rather, it alleged that the defendants used false information to *induce* their followers to purchase securities. By inducing followers to buy stocks, the defendants were tricking them into parting with their money—a traditional property interest. 

The court clarified that the "pump and dump" scheme's goal was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing purchases. Because the indictment alleged that the defendants' object was to obtain money via deception, it successfully alleged a fraudulent-inducement scheme. This distinguished the case from *Ciminelli* and confirmed that the indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme and an intent to defraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348.
