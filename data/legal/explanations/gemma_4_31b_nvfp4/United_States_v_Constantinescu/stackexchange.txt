Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How does de novo review apply to the sufficiency of an indictment?
Question:
I'm reading this opinion and the court says they review the sufficiency of an indictment 'de novo.' I'm a bit confused about what that means in this specific context. Since the district court already dismissed the indictment, is the Fifth Circuit ignoring the lower court's reasoning entirely and just looking at the indictment itself? Also, when they say they 'take the allegations as true,' does that mean the defendants can't argue that the facts are wrong at this stage?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the context of the sufficiency of an indictment, **de novo review** means that the appellate court reviews the legal question from scratch, giving no deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions.

To answer your specific questions based on the opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*:

### 1. Does the court ignore the lower court's reasoning?
Essentially, yes. When a court reviews a matter *de novo*, it is not asking "Did the district court make a mistake?" but rather "What is the correct legal answer?" 

In this case, the district court concluded that the indictment failed to state an offense because it allegedly only targeted the deprivation of "economic information" rather than a "traditional property interest." By reviewing the matter *de novo*, the Fifth Circuit did not have to defer to that conclusion. Instead, the court looked at the indictment itself and determined that the district court's interpretation was incorrect—finding that the indictment actually alleged a "fraudulent-inducement theory" that protects money and property.

### 2. Does "taking the allegations as true" mean defendants cannot argue the facts are wrong?
**Yes, at this specific stage.** There is a critical distinction between the *sufficiency* of an indictment and the *truth* of the evidence. 

A motion to dismiss the indictment is not a trial on the merits; it is a challenge to whether the charging document (the indictment) contains the necessary legal elements to support a conviction if the facts alleged are eventually proven. As the court notes, citing *United States v. Fontenot*, the court is "required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated."

Therefore, the defendants cannot argue that the government's claims are factually false at this stage. The court's only concern is whether the government has alleged:
1. Each prima facie element of the charged offense;
2. Enough detail to fairly inform the defendant of the charges; and
3. Enough to provide a double jeopardy defense.

As the court explains in Section III, the defendants' only viable argument at this stage was that the indictment—**even assuming every allegation in it was true**—did not describe a crime under the law (specifically, that it failed to allege a "scheme to defraud" or an "intent to defraud" as defined by the Supreme Court). Since the Fifth Circuit found that the indictment did indeed allege those elements, the "truth" of those allegations is a matter for a later stage of the proceedings, not for a motion to dismiss.

### What is the actual distinction between the 'right-to-control' theory and 'fraudulent inducement'?
Question:
I'm struggling with the court's analysis of Ciminelli. In Ciminelli, the Supreme Court rejected the 'right-to-control' theory because depriving someone of economic information isn't a traditional property interest. But in this case, the court says 'fraudulent inducement' is different. In both scenarios, the victim is being lied to so they make a bad economic decision. I don't understand why tricking someone into buying a stock is a deprivation of property, while tricking someone about a lobbyist is just a deprivation of information.
Answer:
**The distinction lies in what the defendant is alleged to have "taken" from the victim.** 

To understand the difference, you have to look at what the government identifies as the "property interest" being deprived. In federal fraud statutes, the government must allege the deprivation of a *traditional* property interest.

### 1. Right-to-Control Theory (The "Information" Approach)
Under the right-to-control theory, the government argues that the "property" being stolen is the victim's **right to make an informed economic decision**. 

As explained in *Ciminelli*, the government attempted to establish fraud by showing that the defendant deprived the victim of "potentially valuable economic information" necessary to make a discretionary decision. In this theory, the "property interest" is the **right to control one's assets**. The Supreme Court rejected this, holding that the right to information is not a traditional property interest. If the only thing the victim "lost" was the information they needed to avoid a bad deal, there is no fraud.

### 2. Fraudulent Inducement Theory (The "Money" Approach)
Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, the government is not claiming that "information" was stolen; it is claiming that **actual money or property** was stolen.

As noted in *Kousisis* and applied in *Constantinescu*, this theory occurs when a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." Here, the "property interest" is the money itself. The lie is simply the *tool* used to obtain the money.

### Summary of the Distinction

The confusion usually stems from the fact that both involve a lie and a bad decision. The distinction is found in the **object** of the fraud:

*   **Right-to-Control:** "I lied to you, and because of that lie, you didn't have the information you needed to manage your assets. I have therefore deprived you of your *right to control* those assets." $\rightarrow$ **Not a crime** (Information $\neq$ Property).
*   **Fraudulent Inducement:** "I lied to you, and because of that lie, you gave me $1,000 (or bought a stock). I have therefore deprived you of your *money*." $\rightarrow$ **A crime** (Money $=$ Property).

In *Constantinescu*, the court held that the indictment did not rely on the right-to-control theory. Instead, it alleged that the defendants induced their followers to **purchase securities**—meaning the followers parted with their actual money. Because the defendants' object was to "obtain the victim's money or property," the fraudulent-inducement theory applies, and the "traditional property interest" requirement is satisfied.

### Can a defendant be convicted of fraud if the victim suffers no actual net pecuniary loss?
Question:
The defendants in this case argued that they didn't intend to 'harm' the victims, only to profit. The court cites Kousisis to say that a 'net pecuniary loss' isn't required and that providing something of equal value (like market-value securities) doesn't stop it from being fraud. This seems counterintuitive—if I trade you a $10 bill for a $10 gift card, but I lied about why I'm doing it, have I actually 'defrauded' you of property if you still have $10 of value? I'm confused about how the 'intent to defraud' element is satisfied here.
Answer:
**Yes, a defendant can be convicted of fraud even if the victim suffers no actual net pecuniary loss.**

The confusion you're experiencing usually stems from conflating the *result* of the transaction (the bottom line) with the *nature* of the act (the deception used to obtain property). Under the "fraudulent-inducement theory" affirmed in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the focus is on the deceptive act used to acquire money or property, not whether the victim ended up "poorer" after the trade.

### The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory
As explained in *Constantinescu*, the court distinguishes between two theories of fraud:

1.  **The Right-to-Control Theory (Rejected):** This theory argues that fraud occurs when a victim is deprived of "valuable economic information" needed to make a decision. The Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* rejected this, ruling that information is not a "traditional property interest."
2.  **The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory (Upheld):** This occurs when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires the victim to hand over money or property. Unlike the right-to-control theory, this focuses on the actual deprivation of money or property.

### Why Net Pecuniary Loss is Irrelevant
The court cites *Kousisis v. United States* to clarify that the fraud statutes do not require a victim to be left "economically worse off." Specifically, a defendant violates the law when they scheme to "obtain" the victim's money or property, **"regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss."**

In the *Constantinescu* case, the defendants induced followers to buy securities through false and misleading posts. The defendants argued that because the followers received securities (which may have had market value), there was no "harm." The court rejected this, stating that providing something of equal value does not obviate the fraud charges. The fraud is complete the moment the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses.

### Addressing "Intent to Defraud"
You asked how "intent to defraud" is satisfied if the defendant only intended to profit, not to harm. 

According to the opinion, the "intent to defraud" element is satisfied by two things:
1.  An intent to **deceive**; and
2.  An intent to **cause some harm** to result from that deceit.

Crucially, the court clarifies that intending to profit from a deception is a "distinction without a difference" from intending to harm. If a defendant's "object" was to obtain the victim's money through a lie, the "intent to defraud" is satisfied. The legal "injury" here is not the loss of cents and dollars on a balance sheet, but the deprivation of money or property via deception.

**Summary:** In your $10 gift card example, if you lied to induce the trade, you have "obtained" the $10 bill through a material misrepresentation. The fact that the victim still possesses $10 of value (the gift card) does not erase the fact that you used fraud to obtain the cash.

### Why is the court using mail and wire fraud precedents to interpret 18 U.S.C. § 1348?
Question:
The opinion mentions that there is 'scant caselaw' regarding the specific securities fraud statute (§ 1348) in the Fifth Circuit, so they are 'borrowing' key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes. Is this a common interpretive tool in federal law, or is § 1348 legally linked to the mail/wire fraud statutes in a way that makes this mandatory? I'm trying to understand if the elements of § 1348 are identical to wire fraud or if the court is just using them as a helpful guide.
Answer:
### Answer

The court utilizes mail and wire fraud precedents to interpret 18 U.S.C. § 1348 because the securities fraud statute is designed to incorporate the same fundamental legal concepts as those broader fraud statutes.

According to the court in *United States v. Constantinescu*, there is "scant caselaw" specifically construing § 1348 within the Fifth Circuit. To resolve this gap, the court relies on the fact that § 1348 "**borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes**." Because the statutes share these conceptual roots, the court notes that "courts have given the terms similar treatment" and frequently rely on mail and wire fraud jurisprudence to analyze securities fraud charges.

Regarding your specific questions on the nature of this link:

**Is this a mandatory link or a helpful guide?**
The opinion treats the link as a matter of statutory construction rather than a mere "helpful guide." By stating that § 1348 *borrows* these concepts, the court is indicating that the meaning of terms like "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud" in the context of securities fraud is derived from the established meanings of those terms in the mail and wire fraud statutes.

**Are the elements identical?**
The court identifies two primary elements for securities fraud under § 1348, both of which are defined using mail and wire fraud precedents:

1.  **A "scheme to defraud":** Defined as a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value, such as money.
2.  **An "intent to defraud":** Defined as the intent to (1) deceive and (2) cause some harm to result from that deceit.

The court further applies this conceptual overlap by using Supreme Court precedents governing the general "fraud statutes" to determine what constitutes a protected interest. For example, the court cites *Ciminelli v. United States* (a wire fraud case) to clarify that § 1348 requires the deprivation of a "traditional property interest" (like money) rather than merely "valuable economic information." Similarly, it uses *Kousisis v. United States* to establish that a defendant can be liable under the fraud statutes even if they provide something of equal value in return, so long as the object of the fraud was to obtain the victim's money or property.

### How does an indictment provide a 'double jeopardy defense' against future prosecutions?
Question:
In Section II, the court lists three requirements for a sufficient indictment, one of which is that it must 'provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.' I don't understand how the wording of an indictment achieves this. Does the specificity of the charges prevent the government from charging the same conduct under a different statute later, or is this referring to something else?
Answer:
***

To understand how an indictment provides a double jeopardy defense, you have to look at the relationship between the three requirements for sufficiency listed in Section II of the opinion.

The court notes that a sufficient indictment must:
1. Enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense;
2. Fairly inform the defendant of the charges; and
3. Provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.

The court summarizes these requirements by stating that, "in sum, to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense."

**How this creates a "defense"**
The "double jeopardy defense" is not a separate set of magic words written into the indictment; rather, it is a result of the **specificity** of the charges. 

The Double Jeopardy Clause prevents a defendant from being prosecuted twice for the same offense. For a defendant to successfully assert this defense in the future, there must be a clear, written record of exactly what conduct and what legal elements were adjudicated in the first proceeding. 

If an indictment is too vague, the government might later argue that a subsequent prosecution is for a *different* offense because the first indictment didn't explicitly cover the specific elements of the conduct in question. However, by requiring the indictment to "enumerate each prima facie element" and "allege each material element," the court ensures that the scope of the prosecution is precisely defined. 

In short: by forcing the government to commit to specific material elements now, the indictment creates a "boundary" around the charges. If the government attempts to charge the defendant again for the same conduct, the defendant can point to the specific elements in the original indictment to prove that the new charges are identical to the ones already litigated, thereby triggering the double jeopardy protection.

### Does the 'pump and dump' mechanism inherently fit the fraudulent-inducement theory?
Question:
The court concludes that the indictment is sufficient because it alleges defendants induced followers to purchase securities. In a 'pump and dump,' the fraud is usually the false hype used to inflate the price. But at the moment the follower buys the stock, they are receiving an asset. Is the 'fraudulent inducement' the lie about the stock's future value, or is the 'property deprivation' specifically the money used to buy the stock? I'm trying to figure out if the crime is the lie itself or the actual transfer of money.
Answer:
***

**Does the 'pump and dump' mechanism inherently fit the fraudulent-inducement theory?**

**Yes.** According to the Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Constantinescu*, a "pump and dump" scheme fits the fraudulent-inducement theory because the fraud is not merely the deprivation of information, but the use of misrepresentations to trick victims into parting with their money.

To understand why, it is necessary to distinguish between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory:

### 1. Right-to-Control vs. Fraudulent Inducement
The "right-to-control" theory (rejected in *Ciminelli*) argues that fraud occurs when a victim is deprived of "potentially valuable economic information." The Supreme Court held that such information is not a "traditional property interest" and therefore cannot support a fraud conviction.

In contrast, the **fraudulent-inducement theory**—which the court applied in *Constantinescu*—occurs when a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property" (*Kousisis*). In a pump-and-dump scheme, the defendants do not simply deprive followers of information; they use false information to induce them to **purchase securities**, thereby causing the followers to part with their actual money.

### 2. The "Property Deprivation" and the Asset Problem
You asked whether the crime is the "lie itself" or the "transfer of money," and whether receiving an asset (the stock) negates the fraud. 

The court clarifies that the "fraudulent-inducement" is the **means** (the lie), and the "money or property" is the **object** of the fraud. The transfer of money used to buy the stock constitutes the deprivation of a protected property interest.

Crucially, the receipt of an asset does not shield a defendant from liability. Citing *Kousisis*, the court explains that:
*   The fraud statutes **do not require a victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss**.
*   A defendant can be liable even if they provide something of "equal value in return" (such as market-value securities).
*   The fraud is "complete" the moment the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses.

**Conclusion**
In the eyes of the court, the "pump" (the lie about the stock's value) is the mechanism used to achieve the "fraudulent inducement" (tricking the victim into paying money). The fact that the victim receives a security in exchange is irrelevant to whether a crime was committed; the crime is the act of obtaining that money through deception.
