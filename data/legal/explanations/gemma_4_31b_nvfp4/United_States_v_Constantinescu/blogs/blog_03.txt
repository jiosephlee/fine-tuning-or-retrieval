Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Finfluencers in the Crosshairs: Traditional Fraud Doctrines in the Social Media Age

The rise of the "finfluencer"—the social media personality who blends financial advice with lifestyle branding—has created a regulatory gray area. When a trader with a million followers tells their audience to buy a specific stock while secretly preparing to sell their own shares, is that merely "hyping" a trade or is it a federal crime? The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* clarifies that traditional securities fraud statutes are more than capable of capturing the modern "pump and dump" scheme, even when the defendants claim their only goal was personal profit rather than victim harm.

The case centers on several defendants who allegedly used their social media platforms to mislead followers about their trading positions and the potential value of certain securities. By inducing their followers to buy, they artificially inflated the prices, allowing them to "dump" their own holdings for a reported $114 million profit. The district court originally dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government was merely alleging a deprivation of "valuable economic information"—a theory the Supreme Court recently rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*.

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal hinges on a critical distinction between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court held that federal fraud statutes do not protect a victim's "right to control" their assets via the possession of honest information. However, as the Fifth Circuit correctly notes, *Constantinescu* is not about a right to information; it is about the inducement to part with money. Under the fraudulent-inducement theory—upheld in the recent *Kousisis* decision—the crime is committed when a defendant uses a material lie to trick a victim into a transaction involving money or property. By inducing followers to purchase securities under false pretenses, the defendants were not just withholding information; they were actively manipulating followers into handing over cash.

Perhaps more significant for future "finfluencer" prosecutions is the court's treatment of the "intent to defraud" element. The defendants argued that because they sought to enrich themselves rather than specifically intend to cause their followers a financial loss, they lacked the requisite criminal intent. The Fifth Circuit dismantled this argument using *Kousisis*, affirming that the fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" of loss. In other words, the government does not need to prove that the defendant wanted to "hurt" the victim’s bottom line, only that the defendant intended to "obtain" money or property through deception. Even if a follower received a security of equivalent market value in the exchange, the act of inducing that purchase through a lie satisfies the statutory requirement.

This ruling creates a dangerous precedent for social media traders who operate in the space between "opinion" and "misrepresentation." By grounding the decision in *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*, the Fifth Circuit has signaled that the government does not need to prove a net pecuniary loss to secure a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The mere act of using a material misrepresentation to move a follower's money into a security is sufficient.

The downstream effect of *Constantinescu* is a broadening of the pathway for prosecuting the "finfluencer" economy. Many social media traders rely on the premise that their followers are taking a known risk and that the "pump" is simply part of the game. However, this decision clarifies that once a trader misrepresents their own position or the facts of a security to induce others to buy, they have crossed from marketing into federal fraud. For those who treat their social media following as a tool for market manipulation, the "right-to-control" defense is now officially dead.
