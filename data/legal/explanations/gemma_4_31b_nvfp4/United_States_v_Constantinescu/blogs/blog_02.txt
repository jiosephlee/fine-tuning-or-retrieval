Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Profit Without Pecuniary Loss: Does 'Intent to Defraud' Require an Intent to Harm?

The Fifth Circuit recently weighed in on a critical question regarding the boundaries of federal fraud statutes: must a defendant intend to cause a victim a net economic loss to possess the "intent to defraud," or is the intent to simply obtain money through deceit sufficient? In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the court held the latter, reviving a securities fraud indictment against a group of social-media "finfluencers" accused of orchestrating a classic pump-and-dump scheme.

The case centers on a group of defendants who allegedly used their large social media followings to artificially inflate the price of securities they already owned. By posting false and misleading information to induce their followers to buy, the defendants were able to "dump" their shares at a profit, allegedly netting $114 million. The district court originally dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government failed to allege a deprivation of a traditional property interest—essentially arguing that the victims were deprived of *information* rather than *money*—and that the defendants intended to profit rather than cause harm.

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal hinges on a sharp distinction between two theories of fraud: the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. The district court’s analysis relied heavily on *Ciminelli v. United States*, where the Supreme Court rejected the right-to-control theory, holding that the government cannot sustain a fraud conviction based solely on the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information. In *Ciminelli*, the "property" allegedly stolen was the victim's right to make an informed discretionary decision.

However, Judge Engelhardt correctly noted that the *Constantinescu* indictment does not rely on the right-to-control theory. Instead, it invokes the fraudulent-inducement theory, which the Supreme Court recently validated in *Kousisis v. United States*. Under this theory, a defendant is liable if they use a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. The distinction is subtle but dispositive: while *Ciminelli* prevents the government from criminalizing the theft of *information*, *Kousisis* confirms that the government can still criminalize the theft of *money* obtained through lies.

The most provocative aspect of the court's reasoning is its treatment of "intent to defraud." The defendants argued that because they were selling securities—and the victims were receiving those securities in exchange for their money—there was no intent to cause the victims a net pecuniary loss. They essentially argued that if a victim buys a stock at market value, they haven't been "harmed" in the traditional sense, regardless of the lies told to induce the purchase.

The Fifth Circuit rejected this, leaning on *Kousisis* to clarify that the fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" of loss and gain. The "intent to defraud" is satisfied when the defendant intends to deceive the victim to obtain their money or property, regardless of whether the defendant provides something of equal value in return. In the context of a pump-and-dump, the "injury" is the fraudulent inducement to part with money. The fact that the victim received a security in exchange does not sanitize the deception.

This holding has significant implications for the prosecution of digital-age securities fraud. By decoupling "intent to defraud" from "intent to cause net economic loss," the court has closed a potential loophole for social media promoters who might argue that their victims were not "harmed" as long as they received an asset in exchange for their cash. For "finfluencers" and traders, this serves as a stark reminder that the legal focus is on the integrity of the transaction and the honesty of the inducement, not just the final balance sheet of the victim.
