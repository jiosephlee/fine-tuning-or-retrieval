Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # Statutory Interpretation of the MPWA

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the court was tasked with determining whether an employee has a private right to seek "average daily wage penalties" under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). This analysis provides a clear example of how courts apply rules of statutory interpretation to determine whether a specific remedy—such as a civil penalty—is available to a private plaintiff or reserved exclusively for a government entity.

## Analysis of Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101 and § 181.171

To understand the court's reasoning, one must first understand the interplay between the general enforcement provision of the MPWA and the specific substantive sections it protects. The court looked to § 181.171, which serves as the mechanism for bringing a civil action. This section allows an aggrieved person to seek redress for violations of other parts of the Act, including § 181.101. Under § 181.171, a prevailing employee is entitled to compensatory damages and "other appropriate relief." 

However, the statute contains a critical limitation regarding penalties: an employee can recover civil penalties only if those penalties are explicitly "provided for in the section" that was violated. Consequently, the availability of penalties does not depend on the general enforcement section (§ 181.171), but rather on the language of the specific section being invoked—in this case, § 181.101.

## The Role of the Commissioner in Triggering and Collecting Penalties

The court’s focus then shifted to the text of § 181.101(a) to see if it actually "provided for" penalties recoverable by an employee. Upon review, the court found that the penalty mechanism is exclusively tied to the Commissioner of Labor. According to the statute, penalties are triggered only after the Commissioner serves a demand for payment on behalf of an employee. If the employer fails to pay within ten days, the *commissioner*—not the employee—is empowered to charge and collect the wages and the associated penalty. 

The court noted that the statute specifies that any money collected by the commissioner "must be paid to the employee." This distinction is vital: while the employee may ultimately receive the funds, the legal authority to seek and collect the penalty rests solely with the government.

## The Distinction Between 'Prosecuting a Claim for Wages' and Collecting Civil Penalties

A central point of contention was how to interpret the phrase in § 181.101(a) stating that the section "does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages." Cohen argued that this language authorized him to seek penalties. The court rejected this interpretation, drawing a sharp line between a "claim for wages" and a "claim for penalties."

The court reasoned that while the MPWA provides a substantive right for employees to be paid their earned wages and commissions, it does not automatically extend that right to include the collection of statutory penalties. The ability to "prosecute a claim for wages" refers to the recovery of the underlying money owed, whereas the penalty is a separate punitive measure reserved for the Commissioner's enforcement actions.

## The 'Plain and Unambiguous' Rule of Statutory Interpretation

The court reached these conclusions by applying the "plain and unambiguous" rule of statutory interpretation. Under Minnesota law, when the language of a statute is clear and lacks ambiguity, the court must give effect to that plain meaning as the definitive manifestation of legislative intent. 

In this case, the court found that the text of § 181.101 was unambiguous because it explicitly named the Commissioner as the party responsible for penalties. Because the text was clear, the court declined to search for outside evidence or alternative interpretations that would have expanded the employee's rights beyond the literal text of the statute.

## Comparative Statutory Analysis: The Use of § 181.13

To reinforce its conclusion, the court employed a comparative analysis of other sections within the MPWA. This technique involves looking at how the legislature handled similar issues in different parts of the same statutory scheme to determine if an omission was intentional.

The court pointed to § 181.13(a) and § 181.14, which deal with discharged or resigning employees. In those specific sections, the legislature explicitly stated that the *employee* "may charge and collect a penalty." The court reasoned that because the legislature knew how to empower an employee to collect penalties—and did so in other sections—the decision *not* to include such language in § 181.101 was a deliberate choice. This "action in one statute but inaction in another" served as evidence that the legislature intended to limit § 181.101 penalties to the Commissioner.
