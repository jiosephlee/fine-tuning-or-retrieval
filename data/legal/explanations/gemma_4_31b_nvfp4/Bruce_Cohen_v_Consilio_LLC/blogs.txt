Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Who Gets the Penalty? The Eighth Circuit’s Strict Read of the MPWA

In the realm of wage-and-hour litigation, the difference between recovering unpaid wages and recovering statutory penalties is often the difference between a modest settlement and a windfall. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit recently addressed a critical question of statutory interpretation: can a private employee recover "average daily wage penalties" under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), or are those penalties the exclusive province of the state?

The court’s answer is a firm "no" regarding the MPWA, signaling a disciplined, textualist approach to state labor statutes that may discourage "penalty-seeking" litigation in the circuit.

## The Distinction Between Wages and Penalties

The dispute centered on Consilio’s decision to eliminate overtime premium pay for certain attorney document reviewers. After the employer paid the back wages and liquidated damages, the parties remained deadlocked over statutory penalties. Cohen argued that the MPWA authorized employees to seek average daily wage penalties directly.

The Eighth Circuit rejected this, drawing a sharp line between the right to *wages* and the right to *penalties*. Under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101, the court observed that the power to charge and collect a penalty is vested specifically in the Commissioner of Labor. While the statute allows an employee to "prosecute a claim for wages," the mechanism for triggering a penalty requires the Commissioner to first serve a demand for payment. If the employer fails to comply, the Commissioner—not the employee—collects the penalty.

Crucially, the court noted that while any money collected by the Commissioner is eventually paid to the employee, the *authority* to levy that penalty does not reside with the private plaintiff.

## The "Legislature Knows How" Canon

The most analytically significant part of the opinion is the court’s application of the "legislature knows how" canon of construction. Cohen had argued that the plain language of the MPWA implied a right to these penalties. The court countered by pointing to other sections of the same statutory scheme.

Specifically, the court highlighted Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, which explicitly allow discharged or resigning employees to "charge and collect a penalty." By contrasting these sections with § 181.101, the court applied a classic textualist logic: if the Minnesota legislature knew how to grant employees the power to collect penalties in some contexts, its failure to do so in § 181.101 must be interpreted as an intentional omission.

This is a potent tool for defense counsel. It suggests that where a statute is silent on a private right to a penalty—or where that penalty is framed as an administrative tool for a government agency—the court will not read in a private right of action just to achieve a "fairer" result for the employee.

## Mootness and the "Private Attorney General" Problem

While the MPWA claim was straightforwardly dismissed, the court’s treatment of the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim reveals a deeper jurisdictional tension. The parties agreed that any MFLSA penalties would be payable to the Commissioner, not Cohen. This raises a challenging question: if a plaintiff cannot personally pocket the penalty, do they still have standing to sue for it?

The court vacated and remanded the MFLSA claim to determine if it was moot. If Consilio had already scrapped the illegal overtime policy, the court questioned whether Cohen had any continuing "personal interest" in a penalty that would only benefit the state treasury. 

Judge Kobes’ special concurrence pushed this further, questioning whether Cohen has standing at all to seek penalties that he cannot personally receive. This touches on the "private attorney general" theory—the idea that private citizens can sue to enforce public laws even if the remedy goes to the government. The Eighth Circuit seems skeptical of this theory when the alleged harm (the illegal policy) has been cured.

## Doctrinal Implications

The implications of *Cohen v. Consilio* are twofold. First, it clarifies that the MPWA is not a blank check for private penalty recovery; the administrative process via the Commissioner is a prerequisite. Second, it warns plaintiffs that if their only remaining claim is a statutory penalty payable to the state, they may face a standing or mootness challenge the moment the employer changes its policy.

By strictly adhering to the text and the "legislature knows how" canon, the Eighth Circuit has limited the ability of plaintiffs to use statutory penalties as leverage in settlement negotiations, provided the employer is willing to pay the underlying wages. For practitioners, the case serves as a reminder that in the Eighth Circuit, the gap between a "substantive right to wages" and a "statutory right to penalties" is a wide one.



# Standing to Sue for the State: Civil Penalties and the Mootness Doctrine

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit recently grappled with a fundamental jurisdictional question: can a plaintiff maintain a lawsuit for the sole purpose of recovering civil penalties that go to the government, even after the plaintiff’s own financial losses have been fully compensated? 

The case arises from a dispute over unpaid overtime wages under Minnesota law. While the court spent considerable time on the statutory interpretation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA)—correctly concluding that certain "average daily wage penalties" are the exclusive province of the state Commissioner—the more doctrinally significant portion of the opinion concerns the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA).

The MFLSA claim presents a classic Article III standing and mootness puzzle. By the time the case reached the appellate stage, Consilio had paid Cohen all the overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. From a purely compensatory standpoint, Cohen was "made whole." However, the MFLSA also allows for statutory penalties. Crucially, these penalties are payable to the Commissioner of Labor, not to the plaintiff. 

The court’s analysis here hinges on whether a plaintiff can act as a "private attorney general." Under the Supreme Court’s reasoning in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, civil penalties can provide a form of redress to an injured plaintiff by deterring the defendant from committing future violations. In other words, the "injury" is not just the missing paycheck, but the existence of an unlawful corporate policy that threatens employees. Therefore, as long as that policy remains in place, the plaintiff may have a continuing interest in the case, even if their own pocketbook is full.

However, this leads directly into the "voluntary cessation" doctrine. Generally, if a defendant voluntarily stops the challenged practice, a case may become moot. But the law is wary of this; if a defendant could simply stop a practice to kill a lawsuit and then resume it the moment the case is dismissed, the courts would be powerless. The Eighth Circuit noted that if Consilio’s illegal overtime policy is still in effect, the claim remains viable. But if the policy has been rescinded and it is "absolutely clear" that the conduct will not recur, the case is moot.

The court’s decision to vacate and remand for a jurisdictional determination on the MFLSA claim is a cautious, correct application of these principles. It refuses to assume that payment of damages equals the end of the controversy.

The downstream implications of this reasoning are significant for corporate compliance and wage-and-hour litigation. It suggests that simply paying off the aggrieved employee is not a "get out of jail free" card if the underlying policy remains unchanged. To truly moot a claim for civil penalties, an employer may need to demonstrate a systemic change in policy that precludes future violations.

From an analytical perspective, this creates a precarious tension. We are seeing a potential gap where a plaintiff—who no longer seeks money for themselves—continues to litigate to ensure the government collects a penalty. While *Laidlaw* provides the theoretical bridge for standing, this approach risks turning private litigants into enforcement arms of the state, potentially incentivizing "nuisance" litigation where the primary goal is the imposition of state penalties rather than personal restitution. By remanding the issue, the Eighth Circuit has pushed the factual determination of "policy persistence" back to the district court, ensuring that the "voluntary cessation" exception is applied based on evidence rather than assumption.



# Briefs Are Not Complaints: The Perils of Shifting Relief at Summary Judgment

For litigants, the transition from the pleading stage to summary judgment is often a period of refinement. As discovery unfolds and the factual record crystallizes, it is tempting for a plaintiff to sharpen their requested relief to better align with the evidence. However, as the Eighth Circuit recently reminded practitioners in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, there is a fundamental procedural divide between an opposition brief and a complaint. Attempting to bridge that gap without a formal amendment is a recipe for dismissal.

The litigation in *Cohen* began as a wage-and-hour dispute. Cohen alleged that his employer, Consilio, violated several Minnesota statutes—including the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA)—by eliminating overtime premium pay for licensed attorney document reviewers. By the time the case reached the summary judgment stage, the core financial dispute had narrowed. Consilio had paid Cohen and his colleagues the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under the MFLSA, leaving only the question of statutory penalties and injunctive relief.

The court’s treatment of the penalty claims provides a useful study in statutory interpretation and jurisdiction. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the MPWA penalties, reasoning that the statute empowers only the Commissioner—not private employees—to collect those specific penalties. On the MFLSA claim, the court took a more cautious approach, vacating the summary judgment and remanding the case to determine whether the claim had become moot. If Consilio had already abandoned the challenged overtime policy, the court reasoned, Cohen might no longer have a "continuing interest" in the litigation, regardless of whether the policy was ever legal.

However, the most instructive part of the opinion for the cautious practitioner is the court’s treatment of Cohen’s request for injunctive relief. In his original complaint, Cohen had sought certain forms of injunctive relief. But by the time the parties reached summary judgment, Cohen "changed course," requesting a specific order that Consilio rescind its 2019 overtime policy. Because this specific request appeared for the first time in his summary judgment briefing rather than in an amended complaint, the Eighth Circuit refused to even consider the merits of the request.

The court’s reasoning is a blunt application of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15. The court held that the district court was correct to ignore the request because "new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs." This highlights a critical doctrinal boundary: the complaint serves as the notice pleading that defines the scope of the controversy. A party cannot use a motion for summary judgment—which is designed to determine if a trial is necessary on the *pleaded* claims—as a vehicle to introduce new theories of relief.

The downstream effect of this ruling is a stern warning against "briefing-by-surprise." In the heat of litigation, it is easy to mistake an opposition brief for a flexible document where one can pivot their strategy based on the strengths and weaknesses of the record. But the Eighth Circuit has reaffirmed that the pleading requirements of the Federal Rules are not mere formalities; they are jurisdictional guardrails. 

For plaintiffs, the lesson is clear: if the desired remedy shifts during discovery, the only safe path is a motion to amend. Relying on a summary judgment brief to expand the scope of requested relief is not a strategy; it is a procedural gamble that, in the eyes of the Eighth Circuit, is an invitation to be ignored.
