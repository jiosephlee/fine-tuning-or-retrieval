Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# The Consistency Trap: Federal Circuit Corrects TTAB's Bifurcated Similarity Analysis

In trademark opposition proceedings, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) often balances a series of factors—known as the *DuPont* factors—to determine if a likelihood of confusion exists between two marks. However, the Federal Circuit recently reminded the Board that this balancing act requires internal logical consistency. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the court addressed a common but flawed tactical approach: the "bifurcated similarity analysis," where the Board defines the scope of related services broadly to find a likelihood of confusion, but narrowly to dismiss evidence of a crowded field.

The dispute centered on CC Serve’s registration of the mark "ASPIRE" for credit card services and Apex Bank’s attempt to register "ASPIRE BANK" for banking and financing services. The TTAB originally refused Apex’s registration. Under the second *DuPont* factor, which assesses the similarity of goods or services, the Board found that "credit card services" and "banking and financing services" were highly similar—even "legally identical, in part." This finding served as a pillar for the Board's ultimate conclusion that consumers would likely be confused.

The legal error emerged when the Board pivoted to the sixth *DuPont* factor, which examines the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. This factor is critical because a "crowded field" of similar marks suggests that a mark is commercially weak, meaning consumers are trained to distinguish between subtle differences. Despite having already ruled that banking and credit card services were nearly identical, the Board suddenly narrowed its lens. It considered only those third-party marks specifically offering "credit card services," dismissing 42 other "Aspire"-formative marks used in the broader banking and financing industry as "essentially irrelevant."

The Federal Circuit found this shift unacceptable. Judge Hughes, writing for the court, noted that the Board cannot "have it both ways." If the Board determines as a matter of fact that banking and credit card services are highly similar for the purpose of establishing confusion, it must maintain that same standard of similarity when evaluating whether the mark is weak due to third-party use. The court vacated the Board’s findings on the sixth factor—and by extension the first factor, since commercial strength informs the overall impression of a mark—remanding the case for a consistent analysis.

From an analytical perspective, this ruling closes a loophole that allowed the TTAB to insulate a prior registrant's mark from "crowded field" defenses. By decoupling the definition of similarity between Factor 2 and Factor 6, the Board was effectively creating a one-way street: using broad definitions to expand the scope of protection, but using narrow definitions to ignore the existence of competitors. The Federal Circuit’s insistence on a unified standard of similarity prevents the Board from cherry-picking the evidence to reach a predetermined result.

The downstream implications for trademark practitioners are significant. When challenging a registration or defending against an opposition, the record must be built to lock the Board into its own logic. If a practitioner successfully argues that two categories of services are related under the second *DuPont* factor, they should immediately leverage that finding to broaden the universe of third-party marks considered under the sixth factor. 

Ultimately, *Apex Bank* reinforces a fundamental principle of administrative law: factual findings must be coherent across the entire analysis. The Board may disagree with the weight of certain evidence, but it cannot redefine the relevant market mid-stream to avoid a conclusion that a mark is commercially weak.



# Quantifying 'Weakness': The Evidentiary Threshold for Third-Party Use

The "crowded field" theory is a cornerstone of trademark defense. When a specific word or phrase is ubiquitous within an industry, the law presumes that consumers have become conditioned to distinguish between marks using that term. Consequently, the senior mark is deemed "weak" and entitled to a narrower scope of protection. However, as the Federal Circuit recently reminded us in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) cannot move the goalposts on what constitutes a "similar" service depending on which *DuPont* factor it is analyzing.

The dispute in *Apex Bank* centered on the word "ASPIRE." CC Serve, which provides credit card services, opposed Apex Bank’s attempt to register "ASPIRE BANK" for banking and financing services. The TTAB originally ruled against Apex, finding a likelihood of confusion. In doing so, the Board leaned heavily on the second *DuPont* factor—the similarity of the services. The Board concluded that "credit card services" and "banking and financing services" were, in part, "legally identical," as the dictionary definition of banking encompasses the issuance of credit cards.

The legal friction arose when the Board shifted to the sixth *DuPont* factor: the number and nature of similar marks in use. Here, the Board performed a sudden intellectual pivot. Despite having just declared the services "legally identical," the Board restricted its search for third-party "ASPIRE" marks solely to those providing "credit card services." By ignoring 42 other marks used in the broader banking and finance sector and focusing only on nine credit card-related marks, the Board concluded that the field was not "crowded" enough to render CC Serve’s mark conceptually weak.

The Federal Circuit found this inconsistency untenable. Judge Hughes's opinion highlights a critical logical failure: if the Board determines that two services are highly similar (or even identical) for the purpose of finding a likelihood of confusion, it must maintain that same scope of similarity when evaluating whether the mark is weak. The court effectively held that the TTAB cannot use a broad definition of "similarity" to establish a threat of confusion while simultaneously using a narrow definition to insulate a mark from being labeled "weak."

This decision exposes the precarious nature of quantifying "weakness" in trademark law. The Board had relied on precedents like *Juice Generation* and *Jack Wolfskin*—where "considerable" evidence of third-party use involved 26 marks or 14 "notable" examples—to suggest that nine marks were insufficient to prove weakness. This creates a frustrating evidentiary vacuum for applicants. If there is no predictable numerical threshold for what constitutes a "crowded field," the determination of a mark's strength becomes an exercise in judicial discretion rather than a predictable legal standard.

The downstream effect of *Apex Bank* is a demand for internal coherence in TTAB proceedings. Boards can no longer "cherry-pick" the relevant public to suit the specific factor they are analyzing. If a mark is analyzed as being in the same market as a competitor for the purpose of an opposition, it must be analyzed within that same market when the competitor argues the mark is generic or weak. 

By vacating the findings on both the sixth factor (crowded field) and the first factor (overall commercial impression), the Federal Circuit has signaled that commercial strength is not a standalone fact, but a foundational element that informs the entire likelihood-of-confusion analysis. For practitioners, the lesson is clear: when challenging a mark's strength, the evidentiary record must be broad enough to survive the Board's attempt to narrow the field, but the legal argument must insist on the consistency of the Board's own findings.



# The Domino Effect: How Factor Six Collapses the Analysis of Factor One

In trademark disputes, the "DuPont factors" are often treated as a checklist—a series of independent inquiries that a tribunal weighs to determine if a likelihood of confusion exists. However, the Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* serves as a potent reminder that these factors are not silos. They are an interconnected web. In a concise but impactful ruling, the court demonstrated how a legal error in the analysis of third-party marks (the sixth factor) can trigger a domino effect, necessitating the collapse and reconsideration of the analysis of the marks' overall similarity (the first factor).

The dispute centered on the word "ASPIRE." CC Serve Corp. owns a registration for ASPIRE in connection with credit card services. Apex Bank sought to register ASPIRE BANK for "banking and financing services." The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) initially blocked Apex, finding a likelihood of confusion. To get there, the Board determined under the second DuPont factor that credit card services and general banking services were "legally identical, in part." This finding of high similarity between the services was the cornerstone of the Board's conclusion that consumers would likely be confused.

The legal breakdown occurred when the Board pivoted to the sixth DuPont factor: the number and nature of similar marks in use. This factor is critical because it determines a mark's "commercial strength." If a field is crowded with similar marks, the mark is considered weak, and the owner is entitled to a narrower scope of protection. Paradoxically, while the Board had already decided that banking and credit card services were virtually the same for the purpose of finding confusion, it suddenly narrowed its lens when looking for third-party marks. The Board ignored 42 marks using "Aspire" in the broader banking and finance sector, focusing only on those specifically tied to credit card services.

The Federal Circuit found this logical inconsistency untenable. Judge Hughes, writing for the court, noted that there is no reason to impose a "more stringent legal standard for similarity" under the sixth factor than the Board did under the second. If the services are similar enough to cause confusion, they must be similar enough to prove that the mark is weak. By limiting the universe of third-party marks, the Board had artificially inflated the strength of CC Serve’s mark.

The most analytically significant part of the opinion, however, is the court's treatment of the first DuPont factor—the similarity of the marks in their entireties. The Board had already analyzed the appearance, sound, and connotation of "ASPIRE" versus "ASPIRE BANK." Yet, the Federal Circuit vacated this analysis as well, despite the fact that the marks' literal spellings hadn't changed.

The court's reasoning reveals a critical doctrinal link: commercial strength informs commercial impression. If the sixth factor is recalculated and "ASPIRE" is found to be a weak mark due to a crowded field of financial services, the "commercial impression" of the mark changes. A weak mark is perceived differently by the consuming public; users become more adept at distinguishing between similar marks when they see them everywhere. Consequently, a finding of weakness under factor six can diminish the perceived similarity under factor one.

For trademark practitioners, *Apex Bank* is a cautionary tale regarding internal consistency in TTAB proceedings. A party cannot argue that two services are "legally identical" to establish a likelihood of confusion while simultaneously arguing they are "distinct" to shield a mark from a weakness analysis. By vacating both the sixth and first factors, the Federal Circuit has signaled that the DuPont analysis must be a coherent whole. When the foundation of a mark's strength is undermined, the entire structure of the "commercial impression" analysis may need to be rebuilt.
