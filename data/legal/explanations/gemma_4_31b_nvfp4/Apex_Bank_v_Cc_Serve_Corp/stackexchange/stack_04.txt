Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why is the Board prohibited from using different standards of 'similarity' for different DuPont factors?
Question:
The court vacated the Board's finding on the sixth factor because the Board used a very broad definition of similarity for the second factor (banking $\approx$ credit cards) but a very narrow definition for the sixth factor (only counting marks specifically for credit card services). Is there a general rule that the definition of 'similar goods/services' must be consistent across all DuPont factors, or is this a specific error made by the Board in this case?
Answer:
***

### Short Answer
The Board is prohibited from using inconsistent definitions of "similarity" because doing so creates an illogical legal contradiction. If the Board finds that two sets of services are "highly similar" for the purpose of establishing a likelihood of confusion (the second *DuPont* factor), it cannot then claim those same services are "dissimilar" when determining which third-party marks are relevant to assess the mark's strength (the sixth *DuPont* factor).

### Legal Analysis

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit focused on the internal consistency of the Board's application of the *DuPont* factors. To understand why the Board's approach was legally flawed, it is necessary to look at how the second and sixth factors interact.

**The Second Factor: Similarity of Goods/Services**
The second factor examines whether the parties' goods or services are related such that consumers might believe they emanate from the same source. In this case, the Board determined that "banking and financing services" (Apex) and "credit card services" (CC Serve) were "highly similar" and even "legally identical, in part." This finding weighed heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion.

**The Sixth Factor: Third-Party Use (The "Crowded Field" Analysis)**
The sixth factor considers the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods. This is used to determine if a mark is "weak" because the field is crowded, meaning consumers are better trained to distinguish between similar marks. 

The legal error occurred when the Board shifted its definition of "similarity" between these two steps:
1. For the **second factor**, the Board used a **broad** definition: *Banking $\approx$ Credit Cards*.
2. For the **sixth factor**, the Board used a **narrow** definition: *Only Credit Cards $\approx$ Credit Cards*.

By restricting its analysis to marks specifically for credit card services and dismissing marks used for other banking services as "essentially irrelevant," the Board ignored the very similarity it had already established.

### The Court's Reasoning
The court held that the sixth *DuPont* factor does not require the goods to be identical—only **similar**. 

The court reasoned that once the Board makes a factual finding that services are highly similar (or partially legally identical) under the second factor, it must "retain the same scope" when evaluating similarity under other factors. The court explicitly stated that there is no justification for imposing a "different and more stringent legal standard for similarity" under the sixth factor than the one used under the second.

**Conclusion**
The Board cannot "have it both ways." It cannot define services broadly to find a likelihood of confusion, while simultaneously defining them narrowly to exclude third-party evidence that might prove the mark is weak. This inconsistency constitutes a legal error.
