Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Relationship between the Sixth Factor (Third-Party Use) and the First Factor (Commercial Impression)
Question:
I don't understand why the court vacated the analysis of the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks' appearance/sound/connotation) based on the error in the sixth factor (third-party use). The first factor seems to be about comparing the two marks at issue (ASPIRE vs. ASPIRE BANK), while the sixth is about other people's marks. Why does a change in the number of third-party marks change the 'commercial impression' of the marks in the specific dispute?
Answer:
***

### The link between "Commercial Strength" and "Commercial Impression"

The short answer is that the first *DuPont* factor—which analyzes the "commercial impression" of the marks—is not analyzed in a vacuum. It is informed by the "commercial strength" of the marks, which is determined by the analysis in the sixth factor.

Here is the detailed breakdown of the court's reasoning in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*

**1. The Sixth Factor determines "Commercial Strength"**
The sixth *DuPont* factor examines the number of third-party marks using similar terms on similar goods. The purpose of this analysis is to determine if a mark is "relatively weak" or "commercially/conceptually weak." If a field is "crowded" with similar marks, consumers become more adept at distinguishing between them, meaning the mark is entitled to only a "narrow scope of protection."

In this case, the Board erred by limiting its search for third-party marks to "credit card services" only, ignoring 42 other "Aspire"-formative marks in the broader banking and financing sector. Because the court already affirmed that these services were "highly similar," it held that the pool of third-party marks should have been equally broad.

**2. Commercial Strength informs "Commercial Impression" (The First Factor)**
You are correct that the first factor involves comparing the two marks at issue (*ASPIRE* vs. *ASPIRE BANK*). However, the court explicitly states that the "commercial impression" analyzed under the first factor is informed by the mark's commercial strength.

The logic works like this: 
*   **If a mark is strong** (few third-party users), it has a broad scope of protection, and a similar mark is more likely to create a confusing commercial impression.
*   **If a mark is weak** (many third-party users), it has a narrow scope of protection. Consumers are used to seeing the word "Aspire" in the financial sector, which changes the "overall commercial impression" and makes them more likely to perceive *ASPIRE* and *ASPIRE BANK* as coming from different sources.

**Conclusion**
Because the Board's analysis of the sixth factor was legally flawed, the court could not be sure if the mark *ASPIRE* was strong or weak. Since that determination of strength directly affects the "overall commercial impression" of the marks, the court was required to vacate the first factor analysis and remand it for reconsideration in light of the corrected third-party evidence.
