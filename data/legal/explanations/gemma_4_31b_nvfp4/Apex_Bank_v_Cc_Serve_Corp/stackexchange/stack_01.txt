Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Standard of review for DuPont factors: Question of law or question of fact?
Question:
I'm confused about the standard of review in this case. The court says that 'likelihood of confusion is a question of law,' but then says that 'each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact' which is reviewed for 'substantial evidence.' If the ultimate conclusion is a question of law (de novo), why are the individual factors treated as findings of fact? How does the court reconcile these two different standards in a single analysis?
Answer:
***

### The distinction between "inputs" (facts) and "outputs" (legal conclusions)

To understand the standard of review in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, you have to distinguish between the **factual building blocks** used in the analysis and the **ultimate legal determination** derived from those blocks.

#### 1. The DuPont Factors as Factual "Inputs"
The court explains that "each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact" (*Bose Corp.*). These factors are the evidence-gathering phase of the analysis. For example, determining whether two services are similar (Factor 2) or identifying how many third-party marks exist in the marketplace (Factor 6) requires the Board to look at evidence—dictionaries, third-party registrations, and industry usage.

Because these are findings of fact, the appellate court applies the **substantial evidence** standard. The court does not ask if it would have reached the same conclusion, but rather if a "reasonable mind might accept [the evidence] as adequate to support a conclusion." This is why the court affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor; it determined that substantial evidence supported the finding that the parties' services were highly similar.

#### 2. Likelihood of Confusion as the Legal "Output"
Once the factual findings for the relevant DuPont factors are established, the Board must perform a final step: deciding if those facts, taken together, result in a "likelihood of confusion." 

The court explicitly states that this ultimate conclusion is a **question of law** (*M2 Software*). While the *inputs* are factual, the *process* of weighing those factors to reach a final verdict is a legal exercise. Therefore, the appellate court reviews this final determination **de novo**, meaning it gives no deference to the Board's conclusion.

#### 3. How the Court Reconciles Them in Practice
The reconciliation is straightforward: the court reviews the factual findings (the factors) for substantial evidence, and then reviews the legal conclusion (likelihood of confusion) de novo, based on those facts.

However, as seen in the court's treatment of the sixth DuPont factor, a "factual" factor can still be "legally flawed." In this case, the Board found that only nine marks were relevant because it restricted the "relevant public" to credit card services. The court held that this was a legal error: because the Board had already found (factually) that banking and credit card services were highly similar under the second factor, it was legally inconsistent to apply a more "stringent legal standard for similarity" when analyzing the sixth factor.
