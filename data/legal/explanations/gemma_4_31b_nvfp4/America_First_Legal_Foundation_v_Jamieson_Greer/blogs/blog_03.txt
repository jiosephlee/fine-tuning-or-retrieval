Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The FOIA Enforcement Gap: Navigating the Tension Between § 1216 and § 552

For decades, FOIA requesters have faced a recurring frustration: agencies often provide records only after a court order, yet the individual officials who delayed the production rarely face personal consequences. While FOIA allows a court to find that an agency acted "arbitrarily or capriciously," the bridge between a judicial finding and actual employee discipline is narrow and difficult to cross. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit has effectively reinforced that bridge’s instability, not by deciding the statutory conflict on the merits, but by deploying a rigorous standing analysis to keep the judiciary out of the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) business.

The case centers on a tension between two provisions of the U.S. Code. On one hand, 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) appears to be a broad mandate, stating that the OSC “shall… conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information” prohibited by FOIA. On the other hand, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates a much higher bar, triggering an OSC proceeding only after a court has ordered production, awarded attorney’s fees, and specifically questioned the agency’s conduct.

The America First Legal Foundation (AFL) argued that § 1216 is a standalone duty. In their view, if a requester can allege an arbitrary withholding policy—such as the DOJ’s alleged refusal to search employee emails without consent—the OSC must investigate, regardless of whether the strict predicates of § 552 have been met. This interpretation would transform the OSC into a more accessible watchdog, allowing private parties to trigger disciplinary investigations into federal employees without first winning a full-blown FOIA lawsuit.

However, the D.C. Circuit avoided the merits of this statutory clash entirely, ruling that AFL lacked Article III standing. The court’s reasoning highlights a critical distinction in administrative law: the difference between a "procedural right" and a "concrete interest." AFL attempted to argue that the OSC’s failure to investigate constituted a procedural injury. But as Judge Katsas noted, a procedural right in a vacuum is not an injury in fact. To establish standing, AFL had to show that the lack of an investigation caused a concrete harm.

AFL’s attempt to link the OSC’s inaction to the non-production of records by the DOJ failed on the grounds of causation and redressability. The court identified a "speculative chain" of events: a court order to investigate would lead to an OSC finding, which would lead to a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) sanction, which *might* eventually persuade the DOJ to release the records. The court correctly noted that judicial remedies cannot rely on the "unfettered choices" of independent actors not before the court. Essentially, the court reminded us that the OSC is an enforcement agency, not a records-production agency. If you want documents, you sue the agency under FOIA; you cannot use the OSC as a back-door mechanism to compel production.

The doctrinal implications of this decision are significant. By denying standing, the court has preserved a wide berth of prosecutorial discretion for the OSC. If the court had reached the merits and held that § 1216 is a mandatory, non-discretionary duty triggered by a mere allegation, it would have opened the floodgates for "watchdog" litigation. Every disgruntled FOIA requester could potentially compel the OSC to launch investigations into the conduct of federal employees, effectively turning the judiciary into a manager of the OSC’s investigative docket.

Furthermore, the court’s rejection of AFL’s second theory—that they had an informational interest in the *results* of the investigation—closes a potential loophole. Because § 1216 does not explicitly entitle a complainant to receive the fruits of an investigation, and because the notice requirements of § 1214 are discretionary in this context, there is no "statutory entitlement" to information.

Ultimately, *Greer* reinforces the reality that holding federal employees personally accountable for FOIA violations remains an uphill battle. The "enforcement gap" remains wide: unless a requester can navigate the grueling path to a specific judicial finding under § 552, the OSC’s general duty under § 1216 is unlikely to be a viable tool for private litigants. For now, the court has signaled that it will not use its equitable powers to force the OSC to act, ensuring that the decision of whom to investigate remains firmly within the executive branch's discretion.
