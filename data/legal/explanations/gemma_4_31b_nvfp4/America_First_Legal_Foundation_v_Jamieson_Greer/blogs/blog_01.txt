Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights Are Not Concrete Injuries: The DC Circuit Reaffirms Standing Rigor

In the realm of administrative law, there is often a tempting temptation for litigants to conflate a government agency's statutory duty with a private citizen's right to sue. The D.C. Circuit’s recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a sharp reminder that a statutory mandate—even one phrased in the imperative "shall"—does not automatically grant a plaintiff a ticket into federal court. By dismissing the case for lack of Article III standing, the court reaffirmed that a "procedural right in vacuo" is insufficient to trigger judicial power.

The dispute centered on the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and its role in policing the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). America First Legal (AFL) sought to compel the OSC to investigate the Department of Justice’s (DOJ) alleged policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which states that the OSC "shall... conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. On its face, the statute seems to command action. However, the D.C. Circuit focused not on whether the OSC had a legal obligation to investigate, but on whether AFL had suffered a concrete, particularized injury that a court could actually redress.

The court's analysis of AFL's two standing theories provides a masterclass in the rigor of the *Lujan* framework. First, AFL argued that the OSC’s failure to investigate prevented AFL from obtaining the very records it sought from the DOJ. The court rejected this as a "speculative chain" of causation. For AFL to get its records, the OSC would have to investigate, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would have to sanction DOJ employees, and the DOJ would then have to decide to produce the records. To the court, this was too many "unfettered choices" by independent actors. It is a critical distinction: the injury (not getting records) was traceable to the DOJ, not the OSC.

Second, AFL attempted to claim an "informational injury," arguing that it was entitled to the fruits of the investigation itself. The court dismantled this theory by distinguishing between a mandate for an agency to *act* and a mandate for an agency to *provide information*. While § 1216 may require the OSC to investigate, it does not create a statutory entitlement for the complainant to receive the results. Because AFL could not point to a specific legal right to receive information, the "procedural right" to an investigation remained a right without a concrete interest—precisely the "right in vacuo" the Supreme Court rejected in *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*

From an analytical perspective, the court is signaling a refusal to allow statutory "watchdog" provisions to bypass the Constitution's case-or-controversy requirement. Litigants often attempt to use "procedural standing" to lower the bar for imminence and redressability. Here, the court clarified that while procedural standing relaxes certain requirements, it does not eliminate the need for a baseline injury-in-fact. If the outcome of a court order (forcing an investigation) does not logically and predictably result in a concrete benefit to the plaintiff, the court has no business intervening.

The doctrinal implications of *Greer* are significant for practitioners challenging agency inaction. It reinforces the high hurdle for "informational standing," which now requires a clear statutory entitlement to the data in question, rather than a general interest in the agency's compliance with the law. Downstream, this decision limits the ability of advocacy groups to use the judiciary to force internal government oversight. In short, the D.C. Circuit has made it clear: a statutory violation is not an injury, and a government duty is not a private right to relief.
