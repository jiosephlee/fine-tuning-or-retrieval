Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Propensity or Possession? Navigating the Line Between Rule 404(b) and Intrinsic Evidence

In felon-in-possession cases, the government often faces a recurring evidentiary hurdle: how to link a defendant to a weapon when the recovery of the firearm does not occur in a "smoking gun" scenario—such as a direct arrest with the weapon in hand. The recent decision by the Eighth Circuit in *United States v. Justin Cutbank* clarifies a critical distinction in the Federal Rules of Evidence, ruling that testimony regarding a defendant's prior possession of a specific firearm is intrinsic evidence of ownership and control, rather than prohibited propensity evidence under Rule 404(b).

The case arose from a violent incident involving a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. While the government had DNA evidence and witnesses who identified the weapon, the defendant sought to exclude testimony from two witnesses who had seen him with that specific rifle on several occasions prior to the crime. Cutbank argued that this testimony was a backdoor attempt to introduce propensity evidence—essentially suggesting that because he possessed a firearm in the past, he was the type of person to possess one during the incident in question.

The Eighth Circuit rejected this characterization, relying on the principle that prior possession of a specific item is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same item. By citing *United States v. Battle*, the court reasoned that such evidence does not serve to prove a defendant's general "bad character" or a predisposition toward crime. Instead, it establishes a factual link of ownership or control. The court viewed this as "intrinsic evidence," meaning it goes to the heart of the element of possession itself, rather than serving as circumstantial evidence of a behavioral pattern.

From an analytical perspective, the court’s reasoning highlights a narrow but vital corridor in the application of Rule 404(b). The distinction rests on whether the evidence targets the *person* or the *object*. If the government had introduced evidence that Cutbank had possessed *various* firearms in the past to suggest he is "a gun-toting felon," the court likely would have found a Rule 404(b) violation. However, by tethering the prior possession to the specific weapon used in the offense, the evidence transforms from a character attack into a provenance trail.

The doctrinal implications of this ruling are significant for the government's burden of proof. By affirming that prior possession of a specific weapon is intrinsic to the charge, the Eighth Circuit provides prosecutors with a powerful tool to establish "constructive possession" or ownership even when the physical recovery of the weapon is circumstantial. This reduces the risk that a defendant can evade conviction simply because they were not caught in the immediate act of holding the weapon, provided the government can show a history of control over that specific firearm.

Downstream, this decision may invite more aggressive use of "prior possession" testimony in firearms cases. Defense counsel will likely continue to challenge these admissions as propensity evidence, but *Cutbank* suggests that as long as the evidence is specific to the weapon at issue, the Eighth Circuit views the link as a matter of fact rather than a matter of character. Ultimately, the court prioritized the practical reality of proving possession over a formalist reading of Rule 404(b), ensuring that a defendant's history with a specific weapon remains a fair game for the jury.



# Timing the Warning: Limiting Instructions and the Confrontation Clause

In the heat of a criminal trial, the sequence of evidence is often as critical as the evidence itself. When a prosecutor introduces hearsay—such as a witness's statement to police—before that witness has taken the stand, a tension arises between the trial court’s desire for a fluid narrative and the defendant's right to prevent the jury from prematurely adopting unreliable testimony. The Eighth Circuit recently addressed this tension in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, reaffirming a deferential approach to the timing of limiting instructions and the satisfaction of the Confrontation Clause.

The dispute in *Cutbank* centered on a request for a "stop-gap" instruction. The defendant asked the district court to instruct the jury that they could not consider the statements made by a key witness, D.F., to police officers as substantive evidence of the facts until D.F. herself testified. Essentially, Cutbank argued that allowing the jury to hear the "what" of the statements before the "who" (the witness) was subjected to cross-examination created an impermissible risk of prejudice and a potential Confrontation Clause violation.

The district court declined to give the immediate instruction, suggesting instead that a limiting instruction at the close of the trial, if necessary, would suffice. The Eighth Circuit affirmed this decision, leaning heavily on the "abuse of discretion" standard. The court’s reasoning is rooted in a pragmatic view of trial management: a judge does not need to pause proceedings to provide a limiting instruction for every individual piece of evidence as it is admitted. Instead, the court may aggregate these warnings into a final set of instructions to the jury.

From a doctrinal standpoint, the court’s analysis of the Confrontation Clause in this case is straightforward but pivotal. The court noted that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant actually appears in court and testifies in person. Because D.F. did eventually testify, the court viewed the earlier introduction of her statements to police not as a constitutional breach, but as a procedural lead-in that was ultimately "cured" by her presence on the stand.

However, this reasoning underscores a precarious gamble for defendants. The court’s reliance on the eventual testimony of the witness to satisfy the Confrontation Clause suggests that the "timing" of the hearsay is largely irrelevant so long as the witness eventually appears. But what happens if the witness disappears, asserts the Fifth Amendment, or is deemed incompetent after the jury has already been exposed to their statements via police testimony? While *Cutbank* does not address that counterfactual, it reinforces a trial environment where the government can establish the "context" of an investigation through hearsay, leaving the defendant to hope that the eventual testimony will correct any initial impressions.

The downstream effect of this ruling is a continued expansion of judicial discretion in the Eighth Circuit. By treating the timing of limiting instructions as a discretionary matter rather than a strict requirement, the court lowers the bar for government prosecutors to introduce "contextual" hearsay early in a case. For practitioners, *Cutbank* serves as a reminder that unless a defendant can demonstrate actual prejudice—a high hurdle given the "harmless error" standard applied to evidentiary rulings—the appellate courts are unlikely to second-guess a trial judge's decision to delay a jury warning.

Ultimately, *Cutbank* reaffirms that the Confrontation Clause is viewed by the court as a requirement of *opportunity*—the opportunity to cross-examine—rather than a requirement of *sequencing*. As long as the witness eventually speaks, the court is content to let the trial judge manage the clock.



# The 'Greater Offense Level' Tug-of-War: USSG Enhancements and § 3553(a) Reasonableness

In the complex machinery of federal sentencing, there is a persistent tension between the mathematical precision of the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) and the individualized mandate of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The Eighth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank* serves as a stark reminder of how the "greater offense level" rule can effectively swallow a defendant's argument for a more lenient, "sufficient but not greater than necessary" sentence.

The case involves a violent encounter where Justin Cutbank, a felon, held his girlfriend at gunpoint with a sawed-off rifle before barricading himself in a garage and assaulting a police officer. While much of the appeal focused on evidentiary rulings—which the court dismissed as either correct or harmless—the core legal conflict resides in the sentencing calculations. Cutbank challenged the application of a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies when a firearm is possessed in connection with another felony.

The doctrinal knot here is the interaction between this specific enhancement and the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). Under USSG § 4B1.4(b), when a defendant’s history triggers the ACCA, the court must apply the greater of the offense level calculated under the specific guidelines or the level calculated under the Armed Career Criminal Guideline. In Cutbank’s case, the specific guidelines—bolstered by the felony-connection enhancement and other factors—produced a higher offense level than the ACCA guideline. This resulted in an advisory range of 292 to 365 months.

Cutbank’s argument was not that the guidelines were calculated incorrectly, but that the resulting sentence was substantively unreasonable. He contended that the four-level enhancement pushed his sentence beyond what was "sufficient" to achieve the goals of sentencing under § 3553(a). Essentially, Cutbank was arguing that the rigid application of a guideline enhancement can occasionally clash with the statutory requirement that a sentence not be "greater than necessary."

The Eighth Circuit’s reasoning reinforces a high bar for defendants seeking to challenge within-range sentences. The court emphasized a two-step process: first, the district court must correctly calculate the guidelines (a procedural requirement), and second, it must consider the § 3553(a) factors to determine if a variance is warranted (a substantive requirement). Because the district court arrived at the correct guidelines range and noted its review of the presentence report and sentencing memoranda, the Eighth Circuit applied a strong presumption of reasonableness.

From an analytical perspective, the court’s refusal to require a "mechanical recitation" of the § 3553(a) factors is significant. By holding that a general acknowledgment of the defendant's arguments is sufficient to prove the court "considered" them, the Eighth Circuit grants district judges substantial leeway. This approach effectively shields guidelines-based sentences from substantive challenge unless the defendant can demonstrate an extraordinary imbalance between the crime and the punishment.

The downstream implication of *Cutbank* is that the "greater offense level" rule continues to operate as a ceiling that is very difficult to break. When the USSG and ACCA intersect, the more severe guideline almost always prevails, and the "presumption of reasonableness" attached to a within-range sentence makes it nearly impossible for a defendant to argue that the guidelines themselves have produced a result that is "greater than necessary." In the eyes of the Eighth Circuit, if the math is right and the judge says they thought about it, the sentence stands.
