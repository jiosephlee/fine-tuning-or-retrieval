Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Does a failure to report persecution to the government always defeat the 'unable or unwilling' prong of an asylum claim?
Question:
The court emphasizes that the petitioners didn't report the threats to the government, which undercut their claim that Colombia is unable to protect them. But the petitioners argued that reporting would be futile because the persecutors are aligned with the president. Is there a legal mechanism to prove 'futility' without first reporting the crime, or does the law effectively require a petitioner to risk reporting to the police to prove the police are corrupt?
Answer:
***

### Short Answer: No.

A failure to report persecution to the government is not automatically fatal to an asylum claim, although it creates a significant evidentiary hurdle. While reporting is a "key fact" in determining whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect a citizen, the law provides a mechanism to prove that reporting would have been **futile or dangerous**.

### Legal Analysis

Under the framework adopted in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the "unable-or-unwilling" analysis focuses on the government's response to persecution. Because the government cannot respond to a crime it does not know about, the court emphasizes that "without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped."

However, the court explicitly states that a failure to report is **"not necessarily fatal"** to the claim. To overcome the lack of a police report, an applicant must demonstrate that doing so would have been:
1. **Futile**, or
2. **Dangerous**.

### Why the "Futility" Argument Failed in *Jimenez*

In your specific example, the petitioners argued that reporting was futile because their persecutors (Don Rafa and his associates) were aligned with the president. The court rejected this argument for three primary reasons, which provide a roadmap for what constitutes "sufficient" evidence of futility:

**1. Individual vs. Systemic Failure**
The petitioners testified that two individual police officers told them not to "seek trouble." The court held that a "one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer" does not prove the government as a whole is unable or unwilling. Citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the court noted that evidence of **systemic**, rather than individual, failures is generally required.

**2. Contradictory Evidence in the Record**
The court found that the petitioners' claim of futility was contradicted by the facts of the case. The record showed that the Colombian government was already aware of the criminal activity at Don Rafa’s property and had "attempted several searches and seizures." This evidence suggested that the government was, in fact, willing and able to act against the persecutors, undermining the argument that reporting would have been useless.

**3. Subjective Belief vs. Objective Evidence**
The court relied on *Chhetri v. Rosen* to illustrate that a petitioner's subjective belief—such as believing the persecutors are "part of the government"—is insufficient without objective evidence to support that the government would not have provided protection. In *Jimenez*, the fact that the lead petitioner and his father both worked for the government/criminal justice system yet failed to report corruption further weakened their claim that the system was entirely compromised.

### Summary for the Law Student
To prove futility without reporting, you cannot rely solely on the petitioner's subjective fear or a few anecdotal encounters with low-level officers. You must provide objective evidence that the government is systemically unable or unwilling to act, or evidence that the government's existing relationship with the persecutors would make reporting objectively dangerous or useless.
