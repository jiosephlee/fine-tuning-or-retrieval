Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# The Reporting Paradox: Proving Government Inaction in Jimenez v. Bondi

The Tenth Circuit’s recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a stark reminder of the evidentiary hurdles asylum seekers face when claiming that a foreign government is "unable or unwilling" to protect them from private actors. While the court's ruling touches on several key asylum pillars—including the definition of persecution and the "nexus" to a protected ground—the most striking aspect of the opinion is its treatment of the reporting requirement. The decision highlights a persistent paradox in immigration law: applicants must often prove a government’s failure by seeking help from that very government, even when the alleged persecutors are deeply entwined with the state.

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners—Colombian nationals—claimed they were targeted by a narco-trafficking leader known as "Don Rafa," who allegedly had political ties to the current Colombian presidency. After a series of death threats and an attempted abduction of their son, the family fled to the United States. The Tenth Circuit affirmed the denial of their asylum claims, reasoning that the petitioners failed to demonstrate that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. Central to this finding was the court's observation that the petitioners had not formally reported the threats to the authorities, relying instead on a few informal conversations with police officers who advised them to "not seek trouble" and to leave the country.

The court’s reasoning rests on a rigorous interpretation of the "unable or unwilling" standard. Judge Tymkovich, writing for the court, emphasized that reporting is a "key fact" in the analysis. Without a formal record of a request for help, the court argued, there is no objective way to measure the government's response. The court dismissed the petitioners' accounts of police apathy as "one-off conversations" with individual officers, asserting that such encounters do not prove a systemic failure of the Colombian state. To the court, the fact that the Colombian government had previously attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property suggested that the state was, in fact, capable of challenging the criminal organization.

This logic creates a challenging evidentiary loop for asylum seekers. The court acknowledges that a failure to report is not always fatal if the applicant can show that reporting would have been "futile or dangerous." However, the bar for proving such futility is set incredibly high. In *Jimenez*, the court essentially required the petitioners to provide evidence of systemic corruption or impotence that superseded the government's occasional law enforcement successes. By pointing to the government's raids on the property as evidence of its capability, the court ignored the possibility that a government can be capable of general law enforcement while remaining complicit in—or unable to stop—the targeted persecution of specific individuals.

The doctrinal implication here is a narrowing of the path for those fleeing "private" persecution by powerful non-state actors. When a persecutor is a "narco-trafficking guerrilla" with ties to the presidency, the risk of reporting to local police is not merely a hypothetical hardship but a central component of the fear. By characterizing the police's warning to the petitioners to "leave" as an anecdotal encounter rather than a symptom of state failure, the Tenth Circuit reinforces a standard where the "reasonable" asylum seeker is expected to risk their safety by filing formal reports to prove the government won't help them.

Downstream, *Jimenez* signals that the Tenth Circuit will continue to demand concrete, systemic evidence of government failure rather than relying on the credibility of an applicant's fear of reporting. For practitioners, the case underscores the necessity of documenting not just the threats themselves, but the specific reasons why reporting those threats to the state would be perilous. Without a paper trail of government inaction—which is, by definition, difficult to produce when the government is the problem—petitioners may find their claims defeated by the very paradox the court has here reinforced.
