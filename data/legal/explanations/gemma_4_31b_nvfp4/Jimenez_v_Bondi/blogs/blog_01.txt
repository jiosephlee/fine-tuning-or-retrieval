Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# The High Bar of Harm: The Tenth Circuit’s Strict Interpretation of 'Persecution'

In the realm of asylum law, there is often a profound disconnect between the colloquial understanding of "persecution" and its rigorous legal definition. The Tenth Circuit’s recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a stark reminder that, in the eyes of the court, severe psychological trauma and credible death threats may not be enough to qualify a claimant for protection if they are not accompanied by actual physical violence. By denying the petition of a Colombian family targeted by a narco-trafficking leader, the court has reaffirmed a high evidentiary bar that prioritizes tangible harm over the anticipatory terror of violence.

The case centers on Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, a provisional lawyer and political activist in Colombia. Jimenez and his family were targeted by "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking guerrilla leader. The harassment was severe: Jimenez received death threats via phone and brochure, two men held him at gunpoint and ordered him to leave his neighborhood, and in a chilling escalation, a man attempted to abduct Jimenez’s young son outside his school. The psychological fallout was significant, manifested in the child’s panic attacks and the parents’ insomnia. However, the Tenth Circuit held that these events did not constitute "past persecution."

The court's reasoning rests on a strict reading of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). While the court acknowledged that the events were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," it adhered to a bright-line rule that threats alone—even death threats—are insufficient to establish persecution. Because the petitioners conceded they suffered no lasting physical harm, the court found the harm did not rise to the level of "offensive" suffering required by law. This interpretation effectively creates a "violence threshold," where the legal clock of persecution only begins to tick once a threat is materialized into a physical blow or a prolonged detention.

Beyond the definition of harm, the court’s analysis of "nexus"—the causal link between the harm and a protected ground—further narrows the path to relief. The petitioners argued they were targeted for their political affiliation with the Liberal Party. Yet, the court found that the primary motive was not political, but personal and professional. Because Jimenez had investigated Don Rafa’s illegal property usage, the court viewed the political rhetoric used by the persecutors as "tangential and subordinate" to the motive of silencing a "snitch." This distinction is critical; it suggests that even when a persecutor explicitly mentions a protected ground, the court may look past the rhetoric to the underlying economic or criminal conflict to find the "central reason" for the harm.

The doctrinal implications of *Jimenez* are significant for practitioners and claimants in the Tenth Circuit. First, the decision underscores the precariousness of "psychological harm" claims. By dismissing panic attacks and insomnia as insufficient, the court signals that emotional trauma is viewed as a byproduct of threat rather than a form of persecution in itself. This puts claimants in a difficult position: they must wait for physical violence to occur to meet the legal definition of persecution, but doing so may leave them too incapacitated—or dead—to seek asylum.

Furthermore, the court’s treatment of government protection reinforces the necessity of an exhaustive administrative paper trail. The court noted that Jimenez’s failure to formally report the threats to the Colombian government, despite his professional ties to the legal system, undercut his claim that the state was "unable or unwilling" to protect him. By characterizing a conversation with an unhelpful police officer as a "one-off" occurrence rather than systemic failure, the court maintains a high standard for proving state complicity or impotence.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates a judicial philosophy that views asylum as a narrow remedy for the most extreme forms of physical victimization. While the court’s reasoning is grounded in existing precedent, the result highlights a gap in the law where the lived reality of coercion—the constant, suffocating fear of a guerrilla leader’s reach—is legally invisible until it results in a physical wound.
