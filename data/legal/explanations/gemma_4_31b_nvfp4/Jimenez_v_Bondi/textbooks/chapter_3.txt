Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # The Threshold of Past Persecution

To qualify for asylum or withholding of removal, an applicant must prove they are a "refugee." One of the primary ways to establish this status is by demonstrating "past persecution," which creates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well-founded fear of future persecution. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court focuses heavily on the first element of this analysis: whether the harm the petitioners suffered actually rises to the legal level of "persecution."

## Defining Persecution: 'Suffering or Harm' vs. 'Restrictions or Threats'

A central challenge in asylum cases is that the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not explicitly define the term "persecution." Consequently, the court relies on judicial precedent to establish the threshold. In this opinion, the Tenth Circuit clarifies that persecution requires the "infliction of suffering or harm" upon individuals who differ from their persecutors in a protected way (such as political opinion) that is regarded as offensive. 

Crucially, the court distinguishes between "persecution" and "harassment" or "mistreatment." To meet the legal standard for persecution, the harm must be more than mere "restrictions or threats to life and liberty." In the context of *Jimenez*, the petitioners experienced threatening phone calls, a "death to snitches" brochure, and an encounter where they were held at gunpoint and told to leave the neighborhood. While these events are undoubtedly frightening, the court analyzed whether they constituted the "infliction of suffering" required by law or if they remained in the category of "threats."

## The Cumulative Effect of Multiple Incidents

When evaluating whether a series of events constitutes persecution, the court does not view each incident in isolation. Instead, it employs a cumulative analysis. This means the court considers the total impact of multiple incidents, acknowledging that while a single event might not rise to the level of persecution, the aggregate effect of several incidents might.

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners urged the court to look at the totality of their experience: the threats against the family, the murder of a colleague, the gunpoint confrontations, and the intimidating encounter involving their son at school. However, the court concluded that even when viewed collectively, the events did not cross the threshold. The reasoning here is that if the individual components of the experience are merely threats—and not actual physical harm or severe restrictions on liberty—summing them together does not qualitatively transform "threats" into "persecution."

## The Legal Weight of Threats in the Absence of Physical Harm

The court emphasizes a strict distinction between the *threat* of harm and the *actual infliction* of harm. Under Tenth Circuit precedent, "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." This is a critical point for law students: while physical harm is not the only way to prove persecution, its absence makes the burden significantly harder to meet.

In this case, the petitioners conceded during their merits hearing that they were not physically harmed. This admission was pivotal. The court noted that because there was no physical injury, the harm suffered had to be "offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats." By citing cases where even more extreme patterns—such as being robbed six times or receiving machete threats—were found insufficient because the threats were contingent on the victim's compliance, the court signaled that the petitioners' experiences did not reach the necessary severity.

## Evaluating Psychological and Emotional Harm as a Basis for Asylum

Finally, the court addresses whether psychological and emotional distress can substitute for physical harm to establish persecution. The petitioners argued that the trauma of their experiences led to insomnia for the mother and panic attacks for the son. 

The court acknowledges that such experiences are "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," but it maintains a high bar for legal relief. The court explains that it has rarely, if ever, granted asylum based solely on threats and the resulting emotional or psychological harm. By referencing the *Brandy* case, the court clarifies that the emotional fallout of a threat is a consequence of the threat itself, not a separate "infliction of harm" that elevates a case to the level of persecution. Consequently, the psychological distress experienced by the Jimenez family was deemed insufficient to establish past persecution under the INA.
