Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 5: # Government Protection and Internal Relocation

In an application for asylum, an applicant must demonstrate that their persecution was committed by the government or by forces that the government is either unable or unwilling to control. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit focused on whether the Petitioners established that the Colombian government failed to protect them from the threats posed by "Don Rafa" and his associates. This analysis centers on the relationship between the victim, the persecutor, and the state's capacity to intervene.

## The 'Unable or Unwilling' Analysis: Government Response and Notice

To determine if a government is "unable or unwilling" to provide protection, courts examine how the state responded to the applicant's persecution. Specifically, they look at two primary factors: whether the government attempted to stop the persecutors and whether it offered the applicant protective measures, such as protective custody. 

Central to this analysis is the concept of "notice." For a court to evaluate a government's response, the government must first have been made aware of the harm. Without notice, there is no benchmark to determine whether the police would have reacted appropriately or if the state would have provided aid. Consequently, the court in *Jimenez* emphasized that a key fact in this analysis is whether the applicant reported the persecution to the authorities.

## The Significance of Reporting Persecution to Authorities

Generally, a failure to report persecution to the government undercuts a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. However, reporting is not an absolute requirement. An applicant can satisfy this requirement without a formal police report if they can demonstrate that filing such a report would have been futile or dangerous.

In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners argued that reporting was futile because the persecutors were closely aligned with the Colombian government. The court rejected this argument based on the factual record. The evidence showed that the Colombian government was already aware of Don Rafa’s criminal activities and had attempted several searches and seizures of his property. This indicated that the government was not merely passive or aligned with the criminal group, but was actively opposing them, thereby undermining the claim that seeking official help would have been futile.

## Systemic Government Failure vs. Individual Officer Apathy

When evaluating government protection, a distinction must be made between the conduct of a few individual officers and the systemic failure of the state. The court noted that while police apathy can be an indicator of a government's unwillingness to protect, a "one-off conversation" with an unhelpful officer does not prove a systemic failure.

The Petitioners testified that they spoke to two individual police officers who advised them not to "seek trouble." The court found this insufficient to establish that the Colombian government as a whole was unable or unwilling to protect them. Because Jimenez and his father both had connections to the Colombian criminal justice system, the court found it significant that they did not seek help through those professional channels or beyond their immediate neighborhood.

## The Feasibility and Reasonableness of Internal Relocation

Even if an applicant demonstrates a well-founded fear of persecution, the government can rebut this claim by showing that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their home country. The inquiry is whether such relocation is both feasible and reasonable.

In this case, the court found that the Petitioners could have safely relocated within Colombia. This conclusion was supported by several facts: first, the Petitioners’ relatives continued to live unharmed in Colombia, including Jimenez’s father, who remained employed by the criminal justice system. Second, the Petitioners had already successfully lived in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. Finally, the court noted that the threats from Don Rafa’s associates specifically told the Petitioners to leave their *neighborhood*, not the country. Therefore, relocating to a different region of Colombia was a viable alternative to seeking asylum in the United States.

## The Final Holding and the Relationship Between Asylum and Withholding of Removal

The Tenth Circuit ultimately denied the petition for review, finding substantial evidence to support the BIA's conclusion that the Petitioners did not qualify for asylum. They failed to prove past persecution, failed to establish a nexus to a protected ground, and failed to show that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

This outcome also resolved the claim for withholding of removal. The court explained that there is a hierarchical relationship between the standards for asylum and withholding of removal. Withholding of removal is a more stringent standard; it requires a higher level of proof regarding the likelihood of future persecution. Because the Petitioners failed to meet the lower evidentiary threshold required for asylum, they "necessarily" failed to meet the higher standard required for withholding of removal.
