Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the 'substantial evidence' standard of review affect an asylum appeal in the Tenth Circuit?
Question:
I'm confused about the standard of review used in Jimenez v. Bondi. The court mentions that findings of fact are reviewed under a 'substantial evidence' standard and are conclusive unless any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude otherwise. Does this mean the appellate court cannot reconsider the facts even if they find the IJ's logic flawed, as long as there is some evidence in the record to support the conclusion?
Answer:
***

**How does the 'substantial evidence' standard of review affect an asylum appeal in the Tenth Circuit?**

In the Tenth Circuit, the "substantial evidence" standard is highly deferential and prevents the appellate court from substituting its own judgment for that of the Immigration Judge (IJ) or the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA).

### The Legal Standard
As stated in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, findings of fact are reviewed under a substantial-evidence standard, meaning they are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." 

This is a critical distinction from a *de novo* review. The court is not asking if the BIA's conclusion was the "best" or "most correct" interpretation of the facts, but rather whether the conclusion was reasonable based on the available record.

### Application to Your Question
To answer your specific question: **Yes, the appellate court will generally not reconsider the facts even if you believe the IJ's logic is flawed, provided there is evidence in the record that could support the conclusion.**

The court in *Jimenez* explicitly clarifies that it is "not [its] prerogative to reweigh the evidence." Instead, the court's only role is to decide if substantial evidence supports the decision. 

You can see this applied in the "Nexus" section of the opinion. The Petitioners argued that they were targeted because of their political opinions. While the record did contain evidence of political affiliations and some political threats, the IJ and BIA found that the *predominant* motive was actually a property dispute and Jimenez's professional involvement with "Don Rafa." 

The Tenth Circuit refused to "reweigh" the political evidence to see if it outweighed the property dispute evidence. Because the record contained facts that a reasonable adjudicator could use to conclude that the property dispute was the primary driver—specifically that the threats escalated only after the property dispute began—the court found "substantial evidence" to support the BIA's finding.

### Summary for the Law Student
If you are arguing an appeal under this standard, you cannot simply argue that the IJ "got it wrong" or that a different set of facts is more persuasive. To succeed, you must demonstrate that the record is so devoid of supporting evidence that **any** reasonable adjudicator would be **compelled** to reach a different result.

### At what point do threats and psychological harm cross the threshold from 'harassment' to 'persecution'?
Question:
In this case, the court acknowledged that the petitioners suffered death threats and a terrifying encounter with their child, but still ruled that this didn't amount to 'past persecution' because there was no lasting physical harm. If physical violence isn't strictly required, what specific markers is the court looking for to distinguish between 'psychologically unsettling' events and actual persecution?
Answer:
***

To understand the threshold between "harassment" (or mere psychological distress) and "persecution" under the Tenth Circuit's analysis in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, it is necessary to look at how the court defines the "infliction of suffering."

### The Legal Standard for Persecution
Because the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not define "persecution," the Tenth Circuit relies on case law to establish the boundary. The court holds that persecution requires the infliction of harm that is **"regarded as offensive"** and must **"entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty."** (*Wiransane v. Ashcroft*).

While the court does not look at incidents in isolation—instead considering the **cumulative effects** of multiple incidents—there are specific markers the court uses to determine if that cumulative effect reaches the level of persecution.

### The Role of Threats and Psychological Harm
The court distinguishes between events that are "psychologically unsettling" and those that constitute legal persecution using the following markers:

**1. Threats alone are insufficient.**
The court explicitly states that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." (*Karki v. Holder*). Even repeated threats—such as the four telephone threats and one vague in-person threat in *Chhetri v. Rosen*—do not cross the threshold.

**2. Psychological harm is rarely a standalone basis for asylum.**
The court acknowledges that certain experiences can be "physically stressful or psychologically unsettling," but concludes that fears and emotional distress stemming from threats are generally insufficient. In *Jimenez*, the court noted it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone."

**3. The "Offensiveness" and Severity Threshold.**
While physical harm is not strictly required to satisfy the persecution prong, the harm must be more than "mere restrictions or sporadic threats." (*Brandy v. Holder*). To illustrate this high bar, the court cited examples of harm that **did not** rise to the level of persecution:
*   A 10-hour arrest and detention combined with threats (*Brandy*).
*   Six robberies and threats made with machetes, where the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand (*Carias-Mejia v. Garland*).

### Application to the Case
In *Jimenez*, the petitioners suffered death threats and a "terrifying encounter" where a man grabbed their son and covered his mouth. Despite the psychological trauma of these events, the court found they did not amount to past persecution because:
*   The petitioners conceded there was **no lasting physical harm**.
*   The events, even when viewed cumulatively, remained in the category of being "psychologically unsettling" rather than "offensive" suffering that transcends mere threats.

**Summary:** For a law student analyzing this, the "marker" is not simply the *intensity* of the fear or the *terrifying nature* of the encounter, but whether the harm transcends "threats to life and liberty" to become an actual infliction of offensive suffering—typically evidenced by lasting physical harm or severe restrictions on liberty.

### How do courts distinguish between a 'central reason' and a 'tangential reason' in mixed-motive nexus cases?
Question:
The court found that the petitioners' political opinion was 'tangential and subordinate' to the actual reason for the harm (the professional dispute with Don Rafa). However, the persecutors specifically called them 'snitches from Liberal Party.' I don't understand how a threat that explicitly mentions a protected ground (political party) can be dismissed as tangential if the persecutor also has a personal vendetta. How is the 'central reason' test applied when both motives are present?
Answer:
**The "Central Reason" test focuses on the primary motivator for the harm, even when a protected ground is explicitly mentioned. In mixed-motive cases, the court distinguishes a "central reason" from a "tangential" one by analyzing the causal link between the protected characteristic and the persecutor's actions.**

### The Legal Standard
Under the INA, to establish a nexus for asylum, an applicant must prove that a protected ground (such as political opinion) was **"at least one central reason"** for the persecution. As the court explains in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, this means the motive cannot be "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm," nor can it play a "minor role" in the treatment.

In "mixed-motive" cases—where a persecutor has multiple reasons for targeting a victim—the protected ground must still be a central driver of the harm to satisfy the nexus requirement.

### Application in *Jimenez v. Bondi*
You noted that the persecutors explicitly used political language ("snitches from Liberal Party"). However, the court dismissed this as tangential by looking at the **timing** and **context** of the threats:

1.  **Timing of Escalation:** The court observed that the Petitioners had been active in the Liberal Party since 2012 and had campaigned for a rival candidate. While they received threatening phone calls and a colleague was killed, the Petitioners remained "undeterred" and continued their political activities. The threats only escalated into targeted violence *after* Jimenez became involved in a professional dispute with Don Rafa.
2.  **The Primary Catalyst:** The court found that the trigger for the actual persecution was not the Petitioners' political membership, but Jimenez's professional conduct as a provisional lawyer. Specifically, Jimenez investigated Don Rafa’s property and discovered it was being used for narco-trafficking and prostitution. 
3.  **The Nature of the "Snitch" Label:** While the persecutors called them "snitches from Liberal Party," the court grounded its analysis in the fact that the men also accused Jimenez of being a "snitch for the prosecutor’s office" and held him responsible for "searches and seizures" at Don Rafa's property.

### Conclusion
The court concluded that the "predominant motive" was Don Rafa's desire to protect his "revenue and security" from Jimenez’s professional interference. Because the political label was used in the context of a personal and professional vendetta, and because the political activity itself had not previously triggered such escalation, the political opinion was deemed **subordinate** to the actual reason for the harm. In this view, the political rhetoric was a descriptive label for the target, but the professional dispute was the actual engine driving the persecution.

### Does a failure to report persecution to the government always defeat the 'unable or unwilling' prong of an asylum claim?
Question:
The court emphasizes that the petitioners didn't report the threats to the government, which undercut their claim that Colombia is unable to protect them. But the petitioners argued that reporting would be futile because the persecutors are aligned with the president. Is there a legal mechanism to prove 'futility' without first reporting the crime, or does the law effectively require a petitioner to risk reporting to the police to prove the police are corrupt?
Answer:
***

### Short Answer: No.

A failure to report persecution to the government is not automatically fatal to an asylum claim, although it creates a significant evidentiary hurdle. While reporting is a "key fact" in determining whether a government is unable or unwilling to protect a citizen, the law provides a mechanism to prove that reporting would have been **futile or dangerous**.

### Legal Analysis

Under the framework adopted in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the "unable-or-unwilling" analysis focuses on the government's response to persecution. Because the government cannot respond to a crime it does not know about, the court emphasizes that "without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped."

However, the court explicitly states that a failure to report is **"not necessarily fatal"** to the claim. To overcome the lack of a police report, an applicant must demonstrate that doing so would have been:
1. **Futile**, or
2. **Dangerous**.

### Why the "Futility" Argument Failed in *Jimenez*

In your specific example, the petitioners argued that reporting was futile because their persecutors (Don Rafa and his associates) were aligned with the president. The court rejected this argument for three primary reasons, which provide a roadmap for what constitutes "sufficient" evidence of futility:

**1. Individual vs. Systemic Failure**
The petitioners testified that two individual police officers told them not to "seek trouble." The court held that a "one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer" does not prove the government as a whole is unable or unwilling. Citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the court noted that evidence of **systemic**, rather than individual, failures is generally required.

**2. Contradictory Evidence in the Record**
The court found that the petitioners' claim of futility was contradicted by the facts of the case. The record showed that the Colombian government was already aware of the criminal activity at Don Rafa’s property and had "attempted several searches and seizures." This evidence suggested that the government was, in fact, willing and able to act against the persecutors, undermining the argument that reporting would have been useless.

**3. Subjective Belief vs. Objective Evidence**
The court relied on *Chhetri v. Rosen* to illustrate that a petitioner's subjective belief—such as believing the persecutors are "part of the government"—is insufficient without objective evidence to support that the government would not have provided protection. In *Jimenez*, the fact that the lead petitioner and his father both worked for the government/criminal justice system yet failed to report corruption further weakened their claim that the system was entirely compromised.

### Summary for the Law Student
To prove futility without reporting, you cannot rely solely on the petitioner's subjective fear or a few anecdotal encounters with low-level officers. You must provide objective evidence that the government is systemically unable or unwilling to act, or evidence that the government's existing relationship with the persecutors would make reporting objectively dangerous or useless.

### What constitutes 'reasonable' internal relocation if the persecutor has national-level political ties?
Question:
The court suggests that because the petitioners spent three weeks in the Bogota countryside and their father still works for the government, relocation within Colombia is feasible. But if the persecutor (Don Rafa) is aligned with the sitting President of the country, doesn't that imply a level of reach that makes relocation anywhere in the country risky? I'm struggling to see how the court balances the 'reasonableness' of relocation against the political reach of the persecutor.
Answer:
***

### Short Answer
In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit balances the alleged political reach of a persecutor against the "reasonableness" of internal relocation by focusing on the **scope of the threats** and **empirical evidence of safety** rather than theoretical risks. The court found relocation feasible because the threats were localized to a specific neighborhood and the petitioners had already successfully resided in another area of the country without incident.

### Analysis
Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), the government can rebut a claim of well-founded fear if it demonstrates that an applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their home country. In this case, the court determined that internal relocation was reasonable based on several key factors:

**1. The Scope of the Threat**
The court placed significant weight on the specific nature of the persecutors' demands. The court noted that the men who threatened the petitioners told them to "move out of the neighborhood," not to "move out of the country." This indicated that the threat was geographically limited rather than a national mandate for the petitioners' removal or death regardless of their location within Colombia.

**2. Empirical Evidence of Safety**
The court looked to the petitioners' own history to determine feasibility. The record showed that the family had already lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before departing for the United States. Because they were able to reside there safely, the court viewed this as evidence that relocating within the country was a viable option.

**3. Government Action vs. Political Alignment**
While the petitioners argued that Don Rafa's alignment with the President made relocation risky, the court found this argument unsupported by the facts. Specifically, the court pointed out that the Colombian government was already aware of Don Rafa’s criminal activities and had "attempted several searches and seizures" of his property. This evidence countered the claim that Don Rafa possessed such absolute political protection that the government would be unable or unwilling to protect the petitioners in other parts of the country.

**4. Family Ties and Institutional Stability**
The court also noted that the petitioners' family members continued to live in Colombia unharmed and that Jimenez’s father remained employed within the criminal justice system. This suggested that the "political reach" of the persecutor did not extend to a level that rendered the entire national infrastructure—or the petitioners' immediate family—unsafe.

### Conclusion
For a law student analyzing this, the takeaway is that the court treats "reasonable relocation" as a fact-intensive inquiry. The court essentially ruled that a general allegation of a persecutor's national political ties is insufficient to overcome specific evidence that the threat is localized and that the government is actively engaging in law enforcement actions against that same persecutor.

### Why does a failure to qualify for asylum automatically result in a denial of Withholding of Removal?
Question:
The conclusion of the opinion states that because the petitioners failed to meet the asylum standards, they 'necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.' I thought withholding of removal had a different burden of proof (clear probability vs. well-founded fear). Why does the failure of the asylum claim automatically preclude the withholding claim in this instance?
Answer:
***

While you are correct that asylum and withholding of removal have different burdens of proof regarding the *probability* of future harm (a "well-founded fear" versus a "clear probability"), both forms of relief share the same fundamental eligibility requirements. Specifically, an applicant must demonstrate that they were persecuted (or fear persecution) **on account of** a protected ground and that the government is **unable or unwilling** to protect them.

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court did not deny the claims based on the *degree* of the petitioners' fear, but rather on the *absence* of the underlying legal elements required for either claim. 

The court found that the petitioners failed to establish three core requirements:

1.  **Persecution:** The court determined that the threats and emotional distress suffered by the petitioners did not rise to the level of "persecution" under the INA.
2.  **Nexus:** The court found that the harm was not "on account of" a protected ground. Instead, it concluded that the predominant motive was Jimenez’s professional involvement in a property dispute and his perceived role as a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office, rendering the political opinion "tangential and subordinate."
3.  **Government Protection:** The court found no evidence that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the petitioners, noting that the petitioners had minimal contact with authorities and that the government had actually attempted searches and seizures at the property in question.

Because the court found that there was no persecution and no nexus to a protected ground, there is no "fear of persecution" to measure. If a petitioner cannot establish the basic elements of persecution and nexus, they cannot meet *any* standard of probability. Consequently, if the evidence is insufficient to establish a "well-founded fear" (the lower asylum standard), it is logically impossible for it to establish a "clear probability" (the higher withholding standard).
