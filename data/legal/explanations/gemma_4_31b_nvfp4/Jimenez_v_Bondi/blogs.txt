Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# The High Bar of Harm: The Tenth Circuit’s Strict Interpretation of 'Persecution'

In the realm of asylum law, there is often a profound disconnect between the colloquial understanding of "persecution" and its rigorous legal definition. The Tenth Circuit’s recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a stark reminder that, in the eyes of the court, severe psychological trauma and credible death threats may not be enough to qualify a claimant for protection if they are not accompanied by actual physical violence. By denying the petition of a Colombian family targeted by a narco-trafficking leader, the court has reaffirmed a high evidentiary bar that prioritizes tangible harm over the anticipatory terror of violence.

The case centers on Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, a provisional lawyer and political activist in Colombia. Jimenez and his family were targeted by "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking guerrilla leader. The harassment was severe: Jimenez received death threats via phone and brochure, two men held him at gunpoint and ordered him to leave his neighborhood, and in a chilling escalation, a man attempted to abduct Jimenez’s young son outside his school. The psychological fallout was significant, manifested in the child’s panic attacks and the parents’ insomnia. However, the Tenth Circuit held that these events did not constitute "past persecution."

The court's reasoning rests on a strict reading of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). While the court acknowledged that the events were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," it adhered to a bright-line rule that threats alone—even death threats—are insufficient to establish persecution. Because the petitioners conceded they suffered no lasting physical harm, the court found the harm did not rise to the level of "offensive" suffering required by law. This interpretation effectively creates a "violence threshold," where the legal clock of persecution only begins to tick once a threat is materialized into a physical blow or a prolonged detention.

Beyond the definition of harm, the court’s analysis of "nexus"—the causal link between the harm and a protected ground—further narrows the path to relief. The petitioners argued they were targeted for their political affiliation with the Liberal Party. Yet, the court found that the primary motive was not political, but personal and professional. Because Jimenez had investigated Don Rafa’s illegal property usage, the court viewed the political rhetoric used by the persecutors as "tangential and subordinate" to the motive of silencing a "snitch." This distinction is critical; it suggests that even when a persecutor explicitly mentions a protected ground, the court may look past the rhetoric to the underlying economic or criminal conflict to find the "central reason" for the harm.

The doctrinal implications of *Jimenez* are significant for practitioners and claimants in the Tenth Circuit. First, the decision underscores the precariousness of "psychological harm" claims. By dismissing panic attacks and insomnia as insufficient, the court signals that emotional trauma is viewed as a byproduct of threat rather than a form of persecution in itself. This puts claimants in a difficult position: they must wait for physical violence to occur to meet the legal definition of persecution, but doing so may leave them too incapacitated—or dead—to seek asylum.

Furthermore, the court’s treatment of government protection reinforces the necessity of an exhaustive administrative paper trail. The court noted that Jimenez’s failure to formally report the threats to the Colombian government, despite his professional ties to the legal system, undercut his claim that the state was "unable or unwilling" to protect him. By characterizing a conversation with an unhelpful police officer as a "one-off" occurrence rather than systemic failure, the court maintains a high standard for proving state complicity or impotence.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates a judicial philosophy that views asylum as a narrow remedy for the most extreme forms of physical victimization. While the court’s reasoning is grounded in existing precedent, the result highlights a gap in the law where the lived reality of coercion—the constant, suffocating fear of a guerrilla leader’s reach—is legally invisible until it results in a physical wound.



# Political Opinion or Personal Vendetta? Deciphering the 'Central Reason' Nexus

In the complex arena of asylum law, the "nexus" requirement—the demand that a claimant prove they were targeted *because of* a protected characteristic—is often the most contentious battleground. The Tenth Circuit’s recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* provides a stark illustration of how courts distinguish between a targeted political dissident and an individual caught in a personal or professional vendetta. By parsing the motivations of a narco-trafficking leader, the court reaffirmed a rigorous standard for what constitutes a "central reason" for persecution.

The case centered on Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, Colombian citizens who alleged they were targeted due to their membership in the Colombian Liberal Party. While the petitioners did engage in political campaigning, the timeline of their mistreatment revealed a more complicated narrative. The escalation of threats and violence occurred not during their door-to-door campaigning, but after Jimenez, acting as a provisional lawyer, attempted to resolve a property dispute involving a tenant known as “Don Rafa,” a narco-trafficking leader. When Jimenez discovered the property was being used for criminal activity, Don Rafa’s associates targeted him, explicitly labeling him a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office.

The Tenth Circuit’s analysis of the nexus requirement turns on the distinction between a "central reason" and a "tangential" one. Under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), a protected ground—such as political opinion—cannot be incidental or subordinate to another motive. In mixed-motive cases, where a persecutor may have several reasons for their actions, the claimant must still demonstrate that the protected ground was at least one central reason for the harm.

The court found that the petitioners failed this test. The reasoning was primarily chronological and evidentiary: the petitioners had continued their political activities despite initial threats, but the violence spiked only after Jimenez interfered with Don Rafa’s business interests. The court noted that the persecutors specifically cited Jimenez’s role as a "snitch" and his professional activities rather than his party affiliation. Consequently, the court concluded that the political opinion was merely subordinate to the primary motivator: a narco-trafficker’s desire to silence a legal professional who had uncovered his criminal operations.

From an analytical perspective, *Jimenez* underscores the precarious position of claimants who possess "overlapping" identities. Jimenez was both a political activist and a member of the legal community. When those identities collide, the court is likely to attribute the persecution to the "most efficient" or "most immediate" cause of the persecutor's anger. In this case, the immediate threat to Don Rafa’s revenue and security outweighed the ideological threat of a Liberal Party campaigner. The court essentially held that while the petitioners' political identity existed, it was not the *engine* of the persecution.

The doctrinal implications of this decision are significant for future asylum seekers, particularly those from regions where criminal organizations and political factions are intertwined. The ruling suggests that when a claimant’s professional duties (such as law enforcement or legal work) overlap with a protected ground (such as political opposition to a regime), the court will look for a clear, independent link between the harm and the protected ground. If the harm can be explained by a non-protected professional or personal conflict, the "central reason" threshold becomes incredibly difficult to meet.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a reminder that credibility—even when the court finds the petitioner truthful, as it did here—is not the same as eligibility. The case highlights the evidentiary burden on claimants to not only prove they were harmed, but to isolate the specific, protected motivation behind that harm in a way that precludes alternative, non-protected explanations.



# The Reporting Paradox: Proving Government Inaction in Jimenez v. Bondi

The Tenth Circuit’s recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a stark reminder of the evidentiary hurdles asylum seekers face when claiming that a foreign government is "unable or unwilling" to protect them from private actors. While the court's ruling touches on several key asylum pillars—including the definition of persecution and the "nexus" to a protected ground—the most striking aspect of the opinion is its treatment of the reporting requirement. The decision highlights a persistent paradox in immigration law: applicants must often prove a government’s failure by seeking help from that very government, even when the alleged persecutors are deeply entwined with the state.

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners—Colombian nationals—claimed they were targeted by a narco-trafficking leader known as "Don Rafa," who allegedly had political ties to the current Colombian presidency. After a series of death threats and an attempted abduction of their son, the family fled to the United States. The Tenth Circuit affirmed the denial of their asylum claims, reasoning that the petitioners failed to demonstrate that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. Central to this finding was the court's observation that the petitioners had not formally reported the threats to the authorities, relying instead on a few informal conversations with police officers who advised them to "not seek trouble" and to leave the country.

The court’s reasoning rests on a rigorous interpretation of the "unable or unwilling" standard. Judge Tymkovich, writing for the court, emphasized that reporting is a "key fact" in the analysis. Without a formal record of a request for help, the court argued, there is no objective way to measure the government's response. The court dismissed the petitioners' accounts of police apathy as "one-off conversations" with individual officers, asserting that such encounters do not prove a systemic failure of the Colombian state. To the court, the fact that the Colombian government had previously attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property suggested that the state was, in fact, capable of challenging the criminal organization.

This logic creates a challenging evidentiary loop for asylum seekers. The court acknowledges that a failure to report is not always fatal if the applicant can show that reporting would have been "futile or dangerous." However, the bar for proving such futility is set incredibly high. In *Jimenez*, the court essentially required the petitioners to provide evidence of systemic corruption or impotence that superseded the government's occasional law enforcement successes. By pointing to the government's raids on the property as evidence of its capability, the court ignored the possibility that a government can be capable of general law enforcement while remaining complicit in—or unable to stop—the targeted persecution of specific individuals.

The doctrinal implication here is a narrowing of the path for those fleeing "private" persecution by powerful non-state actors. When a persecutor is a "narco-trafficking guerrilla" with ties to the presidency, the risk of reporting to local police is not merely a hypothetical hardship but a central component of the fear. By characterizing the police's warning to the petitioners to "leave" as an anecdotal encounter rather than a symptom of state failure, the Tenth Circuit reinforces a standard where the "reasonable" asylum seeker is expected to risk their safety by filing formal reports to prove the government won't help them.

Downstream, *Jimenez* signals that the Tenth Circuit will continue to demand concrete, systemic evidence of government failure rather than relying on the credibility of an applicant's fear of reporting. For practitioners, the case underscores the necessity of documenting not just the threats themselves, but the specific reasons why reporting those threats to the state would be perilous. Without a paper trail of government inaction—which is, by definition, difficult to produce when the government is the problem—petitioners may find their claims defeated by the very paradox the court has here reinforced.



# Safe Havens or Danger Zones? The Feasibility of Internal Relocation

In the complex architecture of asylum law, the "internal relocation" analysis often serves as the final hurdle for petitioners who have otherwise established a fear of persecution. The question is deceptively simple: if a petitioner is unsafe in their hometown, is there another part of their home country where they could live safely? In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit recently reaffirmed a stringent approach to this inquiry, highlighting the difficult tension between localized threats and the perceived reach of powerful criminal organizations.

The case of the Jimenez family illustrates the steep climb asylum seekers face when their trauma is primarily psychological or based on threats rather than physical violence. The petitioners, Colombian nationals, alleged they were targeted by "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking leader with ties to the current Colombian presidency. The conflict stemmed from a volatile mix of politics—the family supported the Liberal Party—and a legal dispute over a property used for trafficking and prostitution, which the lead petitioner, a provisional lawyer, had investigated.

The Tenth Circuit’s denial of the petition for review rested on three primary pillars: the lack of physical persecution, a missing "nexus" to a protected ground, and the feasibility of internal relocation. On the first two points, the court adhered to a strict reading of the law. It held that threats alone, even when repeated and frightening, do not constitute persecution without accompanying physical harm or extreme restrictions on liberty. More importantly, the court found that the "nexus" for the harm was not the petitioners' political opinion, but rather their interference with Don Rafa’s business interests. In the eyes of the court, the political affiliation was tangential; the real driver was a professional dispute.

However, the court's analysis of internal relocation is where the decision becomes most provocative. To deny asylum, the government must show that a petitioner could avoid future persecution by relocating within their own country. Here, the court pointed to two key facts: first, the family had lived safely in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before fleeing to the U.S.; second, the persecutors had specifically told the petitioners to leave their *neighborhood*, not the country.

From a doctrinal standpoint, the court’s reasoning is logically consistent with the administrative record. By focusing on the specific geography of the threats, the court treated the danger as a localized phenomenon. If the threat is "get out of this neighborhood," then moving to a different city or region is a reasonable solution.

Yet, this "localized" lens arguably clashes with the systemic reality of narco-trafficking organizations. The petitioners argued that Don Rafa’s alignment with the Colombian presidency meant that no part of the country was truly a safe haven. In many asylum contexts, the reach of a "cartel" or a politically connected gang is national, not municipal. By dismissing the petitioners' concerns about the president’s ties to the persecutors as "hypothetical," the Tenth Circuit reinforced a high evidentiary bar: petitioners must prove not just that a persecutor is powerful, but that their power is omnipresent and inescapable across every square inch of the national territory.

The downstream effects of *Jimenez* are significant. It signals that the Tenth Circuit is unlikely to infer a nationwide danger from the mere existence of political ties between a criminal actor and a head of state. Instead, the court requires a tighter, more evidence-based link between the persecutor's reach and the specific areas where the petitioner might relocate. 

Furthermore, the court's treatment of "government protection" serves as a warning to practitioners. The court noted that the petitioners failed to report the threats to the broader Colombian government, relying instead on a few conversations with unhelpful local officers. By emphasizing that a "one-off conversation" does not prove a systemic failure of the state, the court has reiterated that the failure to exhaust official police channels in the home country can be fatal to an asylum claim.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* underscores a rigorous application of the INA that prioritizes tangible physical harm and localized geography over systemic fears of political corruption. For those seeking refuge from powerful non-state actors, the "safe haven" required for internal relocation is becoming an increasingly narrow target to hit.
