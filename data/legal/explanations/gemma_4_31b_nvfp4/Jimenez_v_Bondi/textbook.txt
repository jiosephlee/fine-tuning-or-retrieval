Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Case Background and Factual Narrative

To understand the court's analysis in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, it is first necessary to trace the sequence of events in Colombia that led the petitioners to seek asylum in the United States. The facts of this case are critical because the court must determine whether the harm the petitioners suffered rose to the level of "persecution" and whether that harm was motivated by a protected ground, such as political opinion.

## Petitioners' political affiliation with the Colombian Liberal Party

The petitioners—Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons—were residents of Bogota, Colombia, where they owned and operated a furniture factory. Since 2012, the couple had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party. In 2022, they actively campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, a candidate in the Colombian presidential election. 

Their political activity involved door-to-door campaigning in local neighborhoods. This activity coincided with the first wave of threats: beginning in May 2022, Jimenez received threatening phone calls once or twice a week. These threats were not isolated to Jimenez; other Liberal Party colleagues also received threats, and one colleague was kidnapped and murdered. Despite these events, the petitioners continued their political campaigning.

## The property dispute involving 'Don Rafa' and the narco-trafficking connection

Parallel to his political activities, Jimenez was a law student and a provisional lawyer who interned for the National Prosecutor’s office. In June 2022, Jimenez was asked to assist a client in a property dispute. The client’s ex-husband was occupying her property and refusing to leave. 

Upon investigating, Jimenez discovered that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as “Don Rafa.” Don Rafa was a leader of narco-trafficking guerrillas who were politically aligned with the current president of Colombia, Gustavo Petro. Jimenez further discovered that the property was being used as a base for narco-trafficking, prostitution, and day-rentals for migrants. Although law enforcement had attempted several searches and seizures of the premises, a local police officer warned Jimenez to stay away from the property and avoid "seeking problems."

## The escalation of threats: phone calls, brochures, and armed confrontations

Following Jimenez's intervention in the property dispute, the threats against the family escalated and changed in nature. In July 2022, Jimenez received a brochure at his factory stating, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” 

In August 2022, the situation turned violent. Two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at his furniture factory and ordered him to leave the neighborhood. During this confrontation, the men accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for the law enforcement searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. A few weeks later, these same two men returned to the factory while the petitioners were away. They assaulted a factory employee and demanded the petitioners' location, explicitly stating that the order to target them came from Don Rafa because they were "snitches for the police" who were involved in politics.

## The encounter involving the petitioners' minor son

The climax of the harassment occurred shortly after the second attack on the factory. Jimenez witnessed a man approach his younger son as the boy exited his school. The man grabbed the child, placed his hand over the boy’s mouth to prevent him from screaming, and told the boy to “run to your dad.” Jimenez intervened, pushing the man away and quickly getting his son into the car.

## The family's flight to the United States

Following the incident at the school, the family sought temporary refuge at Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. During this time, Jimenez reported the events to a police patrol officer. The officer informed him that nothing could be done because police officers themselves were contributing to the problem, and he advised the family to leave the country. 

In December 2022, the petitioners relocated to the United States. Three months after their arrival, they were served with Notices to Appear by the Department of Homeland Security, initiating the removal proceedings that eventually led to the asylum and withholding of removal applications reviewed in this case.

# Procedural History and Standards of Review

## Initial proceedings before the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the BIA

The legal journey for the petitioners in *Jimenez v. Bondi* began after they entered the United States without admission or parole. Following their arrival, the Department of Homeland Security served them with Notices to Appear, charging them as removable. In response, the petitioners applied for asylum and withholding of removal, while also seeking protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT).

The initial adjudication took place before an Immigration Judge (IJ). Although the IJ found the petitioners to be credible witnesses, the application was denied on the merits. Specifically, the IJ concluded that the petitioners failed to establish four critical elements: that they suffered past persecution, that there was a nexus between that harm and a protected ground, that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, and that internal relocation within Colombia was not a reasonable option. The petitioners appealed this decision to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), which affirmed the IJ’s findings and dismissed the appeal. This sequence—from the trial-level IJ to the administrative appellate-level BIA—establishes the administrative record that the federal courts must later review.

## Jurisdiction of the Court of Appeals under 8 U.S.C. § 1252

Once the BIA issued its final order, the petitioners sought judicial review in the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit. The court’s authority to hear this case is derived from 8 U.S.C. § 1252, which provides the statutory framework for reviewing final orders of removal. 

In this case, the court exercised jurisdiction under § 1252 to determine whether the BIA erred in its denial of asylum and withholding of removal. It is important to note that while the petitioners challenged multiple aspects of their case, the court's review is limited to the specific issues raised on appeal. Here, the petitioners focused their challenge on the findings regarding past persecution, the nexus to a protected ground, and the government's inability to protect them, while choosing not to appeal the denial of protection under the CAT.

## The 'substantial evidence' standard for reviewing factual findings

A critical component of understanding this opinion is the "substantial evidence" standard, which the court uses to review the BIA's findings of fact. This is a highly deferential standard of review. Under this framework, the BIA’s factual conclusions are considered conclusive unless the administrative record contains such an abundance of evidence that "any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary."

This means the Court of Appeals does not re-weigh the evidence or decide if the BIA's conclusion was the *best* or *most likely* result. Instead, the court asks only whether the BIA's conclusion was reasonable based on the evidence. If there is substantial evidence to support the BIA's finding—even if the court might have decided differently if it were the first instance—the court must uphold the decision.

## The limitation of review to the administrative record

When reviewing an asylum claim, the Court of Appeals is not conducting a new trial. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A), the court's review is strictly limited to the "administrative record on which the order of removal is based." This record includes the testimony given at the IJ hearing, the documents submitted by the petitioners, and the legal briefs considered by the BIA. The court cannot consider new evidence or facts that were not presented during the administrative proceedings.

Finally, the court clarified a procedural nuance regarding the relationship between the IJ and the BIA. Because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ's decision without a detailed independent analysis, the court reviewed the BIA's decision while simultaneously consulting the IJ’s more comprehensive explanation. This ensures that the court considers the full context of the factual findings while remaining tethered to the official administrative record.

# The Threshold of Past Persecution

To qualify for asylum or withholding of removal, an applicant must prove they are a "refugee." One of the primary ways to establish this status is by demonstrating "past persecution," which creates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well-founded fear of future persecution. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court focuses heavily on the first element of this analysis: whether the harm the petitioners suffered actually rises to the legal level of "persecution."

## Defining Persecution: 'Suffering or Harm' vs. 'Restrictions or Threats'

A central challenge in asylum cases is that the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not explicitly define the term "persecution." Consequently, the court relies on judicial precedent to establish the threshold. In this opinion, the Tenth Circuit clarifies that persecution requires the "infliction of suffering or harm" upon individuals who differ from their persecutors in a protected way (such as political opinion) that is regarded as offensive. 

Crucially, the court distinguishes between "persecution" and "harassment" or "mistreatment." To meet the legal standard for persecution, the harm must be more than mere "restrictions or threats to life and liberty." In the context of *Jimenez*, the petitioners experienced threatening phone calls, a "death to snitches" brochure, and an encounter where they were held at gunpoint and told to leave the neighborhood. While these events are undoubtedly frightening, the court analyzed whether they constituted the "infliction of suffering" required by law or if they remained in the category of "threats."

## The Cumulative Effect of Multiple Incidents

When evaluating whether a series of events constitutes persecution, the court does not view each incident in isolation. Instead, it employs a cumulative analysis. This means the court considers the total impact of multiple incidents, acknowledging that while a single event might not rise to the level of persecution, the aggregate effect of several incidents might.

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners urged the court to look at the totality of their experience: the threats against the family, the murder of a colleague, the gunpoint confrontations, and the intimidating encounter involving their son at school. However, the court concluded that even when viewed collectively, the events did not cross the threshold. The reasoning here is that if the individual components of the experience are merely threats—and not actual physical harm or severe restrictions on liberty—summing them together does not qualitatively transform "threats" into "persecution."

## The Legal Weight of Threats in the Absence of Physical Harm

The court emphasizes a strict distinction between the *threat* of harm and the *actual infliction* of harm. Under Tenth Circuit precedent, "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." This is a critical point for law students: while physical harm is not the only way to prove persecution, its absence makes the burden significantly harder to meet.

In this case, the petitioners conceded during their merits hearing that they were not physically harmed. This admission was pivotal. The court noted that because there was no physical injury, the harm suffered had to be "offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats." By citing cases where even more extreme patterns—such as being robbed six times or receiving machete threats—were found insufficient because the threats were contingent on the victim's compliance, the court signaled that the petitioners' experiences did not reach the necessary severity.

## Evaluating Psychological and Emotional Harm as a Basis for Asylum

Finally, the court addresses whether psychological and emotional distress can substitute for physical harm to establish persecution. The petitioners argued that the trauma of their experiences led to insomnia for the mother and panic attacks for the son. 

The court acknowledges that such experiences are "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," but it maintains a high bar for legal relief. The court explains that it has rarely, if ever, granted asylum based solely on threats and the resulting emotional or psychological harm. By referencing the *Brandy* case, the court clarifies that the emotional fallout of a threat is a consequence of the threat itself, not a separate "infliction of harm" that elevates a case to the level of persecution. Consequently, the psychological distress experienced by the Jimenez family was deemed insufficient to establish past persecution under the INA.

# The Nexus Requirement: Central Reason vs. Tangential Motive

To qualify for asylum, a petitioner must prove not only that they suffered persecution, but that the persecution occurred "on account of" one of five statutorily protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. This causal link is known as the "nexus" requirement. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court's analysis focuses on whether the petitioners' political opinions provided the necessary nexus to their mistreatment, or whether the harm was driven by a non-protected, personal dispute.

## The 'At Least One Central Reason' Standard for Protected Grounds

The legal standard for establishing a nexus is rigorous. To show persecution on account of a protected ground, an applicant must demonstrate that the protected ground was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. This means that the motive behind the harm cannot be merely incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason. 

In the context of a law student reading this opinion, it is important to distinguish between a motive that is *present* and a motive that is *central*. The court does not ask if the persecutor was aware of the petitioner's political opinion, but rather if that opinion was a driving force behind the decision to inflict harm. If the protected ground plays only a minor role in the persecutor's motivation, the nexus requirement is not satisfied.

## Analyzing Mixed-Motive Persecution

The court acknowledges that persecutors often have "mixed motives," meaning they may target a victim for several different reasons simultaneously. For example, a persecutor might dislike a victim’s political party while also harboring a personal grudge over a business deal. 

Under the "at least one central reason" framework, the presence of multiple motives does not automatically disqualify a petitioner. However, even in mixed-motive cases, the applicant must still prove that at least one of those motives is a protected ground and that this specific motive was central to the harm. If the protected ground is eclipsed by a non-protected motive—such as a criminal dispute or a professional rivalry—the claim for asylum will fail.

## Distinguishing Between Political Targets and Targets of Professional/Legal Interference

A critical part of the *Jimenez* analysis is the distinction between being targeted for one's political beliefs and being targeted for one's professional actions. The petitioners in this case were members of the Colombian Liberal Party and had campaigned for a presidential candidate. While this constitutes a protected political opinion, the court looked at the timing and nature of the threats to determine if this was the actual driver of the persecution.

The court distinguishes between "political activity" (campaigning) and "professional interference" (legal work). In *Jimenez*, the petitioners continued their political activities despite initial threats, suggesting that their political affiliation alone had not yet triggered a level of harm that forced them to flee. The situation changed only when Jimenez, acting in his capacity as a provisional lawyer and intern for the National Prosecutor’s office, became involved in a property dispute. By attempting to recover property from a narco-trafficker known as "Don Rafa," Jimenez shifted from being a political opponent to a professional adversary who threatened Don Rafa’s revenue and security.

## Evaluating the Evidence Regarding Don Rafa's Primary Motivation

In determining the primary motivation of the persecutor, the court relied heavily on the specific language used by the attackers and the chronology of the events. The record showed that the threats escalated significantly only *after* Jimenez investigated Don Rafa's property and discovered its use for narco-trafficking and prostitution.

The "smoking gun" for the court was the specific accusation made by the attackers. During the threats, the men explicitly called Jimenez a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for the searches and seizures conducted at Don Rafa's property. By labeling Jimenez a "snitch" rather than a "political dissident," the persecutors revealed that the central reason for the targeting was Jimenez's perceived role in law enforcement and legal interference.

Consequently, the court held that while the petitioners' political opinions may have played a role, they were "merely tangential and subordinate" to the central motive: Don Rafa's desire to punish Jimenez for his professional actions and his perceived cooperation with the police. Because the central reason for the harm was not a protected ground, the nexus requirement was not met.

# Government Protection and Internal Relocation

In an application for asylum, an applicant must demonstrate that their persecution was committed by the government or by forces that the government is either unable or unwilling to control. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit focused on whether the Petitioners established that the Colombian government failed to protect them from the threats posed by "Don Rafa" and his associates. This analysis centers on the relationship between the victim, the persecutor, and the state's capacity to intervene.

## The 'Unable or Unwilling' Analysis: Government Response and Notice

To determine if a government is "unable or unwilling" to provide protection, courts examine how the state responded to the applicant's persecution. Specifically, they look at two primary factors: whether the government attempted to stop the persecutors and whether it offered the applicant protective measures, such as protective custody. 

Central to this analysis is the concept of "notice." For a court to evaluate a government's response, the government must first have been made aware of the harm. Without notice, there is no benchmark to determine whether the police would have reacted appropriately or if the state would have provided aid. Consequently, the court in *Jimenez* emphasized that a key fact in this analysis is whether the applicant reported the persecution to the authorities.

## The Significance of Reporting Persecution to Authorities

Generally, a failure to report persecution to the government undercuts a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. However, reporting is not an absolute requirement. An applicant can satisfy this requirement without a formal police report if they can demonstrate that filing such a report would have been futile or dangerous.

In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners argued that reporting was futile because the persecutors were closely aligned with the Colombian government. The court rejected this argument based on the factual record. The evidence showed that the Colombian government was already aware of Don Rafa’s criminal activities and had attempted several searches and seizures of his property. This indicated that the government was not merely passive or aligned with the criminal group, but was actively opposing them, thereby undermining the claim that seeking official help would have been futile.

## Systemic Government Failure vs. Individual Officer Apathy

When evaluating government protection, a distinction must be made between the conduct of a few individual officers and the systemic failure of the state. The court noted that while police apathy can be an indicator of a government's unwillingness to protect, a "one-off conversation" with an unhelpful officer does not prove a systemic failure.

The Petitioners testified that they spoke to two individual police officers who advised them not to "seek trouble." The court found this insufficient to establish that the Colombian government as a whole was unable or unwilling to protect them. Because Jimenez and his father both had connections to the Colombian criminal justice system, the court found it significant that they did not seek help through those professional channels or beyond their immediate neighborhood.

## The Feasibility and Reasonableness of Internal Relocation

Even if an applicant demonstrates a well-founded fear of persecution, the government can rebut this claim by showing that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their home country. The inquiry is whether such relocation is both feasible and reasonable.

In this case, the court found that the Petitioners could have safely relocated within Colombia. This conclusion was supported by several facts: first, the Petitioners’ relatives continued to live unharmed in Colombia, including Jimenez’s father, who remained employed by the criminal justice system. Second, the Petitioners had already successfully lived in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. Finally, the court noted that the threats from Don Rafa’s associates specifically told the Petitioners to leave their *neighborhood*, not the country. Therefore, relocating to a different region of Colombia was a viable alternative to seeking asylum in the United States.

## The Final Holding and the Relationship Between Asylum and Withholding of Removal

The Tenth Circuit ultimately denied the petition for review, finding substantial evidence to support the BIA's conclusion that the Petitioners did not qualify for asylum. They failed to prove past persecution, failed to establish a nexus to a protected ground, and failed to show that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

This outcome also resolved the claim for withholding of removal. The court explained that there is a hierarchical relationship between the standards for asylum and withholding of removal. Withholding of removal is a more stringent standard; it requires a higher level of proof regarding the likelihood of future persecution. Because the Petitioners failed to meet the lower evidentiary threshold required for asylum, they "necessarily" failed to meet the higher standard required for withholding of removal.
