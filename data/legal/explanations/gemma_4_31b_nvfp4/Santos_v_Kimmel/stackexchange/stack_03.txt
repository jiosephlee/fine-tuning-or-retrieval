Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Does 'bad faith' or fraudulent acquisition of a work negate a fair use defense?
Question:
I'm struggling with the court's treatment of the Harper & Row precedent. Santos argued that because Kimmel used fictitious names to trick him into making the videos, this 'bad faith' should nullify the fair use defense. The court mentioned that the Supreme Court has 'distanced itself' from the requirement of good faith. Does this mean a person can essentially commit fraud to obtain a copyrighted work and still successfully plead fair use if the final output is transformative?
Answer:
***

### Short Answer: No. 

Based on the court's reasoning in *Santos v. Kimmel*, "bad faith" or fraudulent acquisition of a work does not automatically nullify a fair use defense. While the Supreme Court previously suggested that fair use "presupposes good faith and fair dealing" in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.*, the Second Circuit notes that the Supreme Court has since "distanced itself" from that requirement.

### Legal Analysis

In this case, Santos argued that Jimmy Kimmel’s use of fictitious names to trick him into creating Cameo videos constituted bad faith that should disqualify the defendants from claiming fair use. The court rejected this argument on two primary grounds:

**1. The Shift in Precedent**
The court acknowledged the language in *Harper & Row* regarding good faith but explicitly stated that the Supreme Court has moved away from that standard. This indicates that a user's subjective "bad faith" in how they obtained the material is not a dispositive barrier to a fair use defense.

**2. The Distinction Between Acquisition and Market Supplantation**
The court distinguished between the fraudulent means used to *acquire* the work and the *purpose* of the subsequent use. For bad faith to be legally significant in a fair use analysis, it typically must involve an intent to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work (citing *Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*). 

Here, the court found that Kimmel's conduct did not seek to replace the market for the original videos. Instead, the defendants were motivated by "sarcastic criticism and commentary," which falls squarely within the goals of fair use.

### Conclusion

If the resulting use of the work is transformative—meaning it adds something new with a further purpose or different character—it can still qualify as fair use even if the work was obtained through deception. In *Santos*, the court found the use was transformative because it shifted the purpose of the videos from "hope, strength, [and] perseverance" to a commentary on the plaintiff's "willingness to say absurd things for money." Because the use was transformative and did not serve as a competing substitute for the original work, the "bad faith" of the acquisition did not negate the defense.
