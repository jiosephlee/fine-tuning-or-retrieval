Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a court dismiss a copyright claim based on fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) pleading stage?
Question:
I am confused about the procedural timing here. Usually, fair use is treated as an affirmative defense, which I thought meant it was a factual determination made after discovery. In Santos v. Kimmel, the court affirmed a dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), stating fair use was "so clearly established by a complaint." How is it possible to determine fair use without a full evidentiary record?
Answer:
**Yes, a court may dismiss a copyright claim based on fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage if the fair use is "so clearly established by a complaint" that further evidentiary discovery is unnecessary.**

While you are correct that fair use is typically treated as an affirmative defense—which often requires a factual determination after discovery—it is not an absolute rule that it must be decided at summary judgment or trial. As the Second Circuit explains in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the fair use defense can be determined at the pleading stage when the facts alleged in the complaint, the documents attached as exhibits, and those incorporated by reference provide a sufficient basis for the court to apply the four-factor test under 17 U.S.C. § 107.

In *Santos*, the court relied on the following reasoning to justify dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6):

**1. The Transformative Nature of the Use**
The court focused on the "purpose and character of the use" (the first factor). A use is transformative if it adds something new or has a different purpose rather than merely superseding the original. The court determined that a reasonable observer would see a clear distinction between the purpose of the original videos (which conveyed "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity") and the purpose of the defendants' use (commenting on Santos's "willingness to say absurd things for money"). The court noted that the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the user does not determine this factor; rather, it is the perspective of a reasonable observer.

**2. Lack of Market Harm**
Regarding the fourth factor (the effect upon the potential market), the court found it was "clear on the face of Santos’s complaint" that the use did not usurp the market for the original work by offering a competing substitute. Instead, it functioned as a "lampoon" or critical review, which generally does not constitute market harm under the Copyright Act.

**3. Sufficiency of the Pleading Record**
Crucially, the court held that because the determination of fair use in this instance only required a comparison between "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue," the court had enough information to rule without a full evidentiary record.

In short, while fair use is flexible and context-dependent, if the pleadings establish that the use is transformative and does not harm the market for the original work, the court may conclude that the claim fails as a matter of law and dismiss it under Rule 12(b)(6).
