Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the 'reasonable observer' test resolve conflicts between a creator's intent and a user's purpose in transformative use?
Question:
In this case, Santos argued that the use wasn't transformative because Kimmel actually paid for and 'instigated' the videos, meaning the purpose of the creation was aligned with Kimmel's eventual use. However, the court held that transformativeness depends on what a 'reasonable observer' thinks, not the subjective intent of the parties. If the defendant essentially commissioned the work to be mocked, why does the 'reasonable observer' standard override the actual intent of the creation?
Answer:
**The "reasonable observer" standard prioritizes the objective meaning of the work over the subjective intent of the parties to determine if a use is "transformative."**

In copyright law, the first factor of fair use focuses on the "purpose and character of the use," specifically whether the new work is transformative. As the court explains in *Santos v. Kimmel*, a work is transformative if it does not merely supersede the objects of the original creation but instead "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character."

The conflict you identified—where a user (Kimmel) instigates the creation of a work specifically to use it for a certain purpose—is resolved by shifting the analysis from the **subjective intent** of the parties to the **objective perception** of the work.

### Why Subjective Intent is Irrelevant
The court explicitly rejects the argument that the "designer" of a work's original purpose can dictate whether a subsequent use is transformative. Relying on the Supreme Court's holding in *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, the court notes that the "subjective intent of the user" does not determine the purpose of the use. 

If the law relied on subjective intent, a defendant could potentially defeat a fair use defense simply by claiming they never intended to be transformative, or a plaintiff could defeat it by claiming the defendant "planned" the work all along. To avoid this, the court applies the "reasonable observer" test.

### Application in *Santos v. Kimmel*
To determine if Kimmel's use was transformative, the court compared the perceived purpose of the original videos with the perceived purpose of the broadcast segments:

1.  **The Original Work:** According to the court, a reasonable observer would perceive Santos’s original videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity."
2.  **The Secondary Use:** The purpose of the *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* segments was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money."

Because the reasonable observer sees two distinct purposes—one of positivity/encouragement and one of sarcastic criticism/commentary—the use is transformative. 

**The bottom line:** It does not matter that Kimmel "instigated" the videos or that he had a specific intent when he commissioned them. The legal analysis focuses on the contrast between what the original work *appears* to be to the public and what the new work *does* with it. Because those two objective purposes differ, the use is transformative regardless of the "bad faith" or "instigation" involved in the creation.
