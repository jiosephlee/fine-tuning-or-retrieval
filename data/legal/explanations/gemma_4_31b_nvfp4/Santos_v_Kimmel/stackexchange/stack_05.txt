Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### When is a non-signatory considered a third-party beneficiary of a platform's Terms of Service?
Question:
Santos tried to sue for a breach of the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS), which Kimmel agreed to. The court dismissed this because Santos wasn't a party to the contract and there was no 'express declaration' of third-party beneficiary status. If a platform's TOS is specifically designed to regulate the behavior of users to protect the sellers on that platform, why isn't that an implicit third-party beneficiary right?
Answer:
***

### When is a non-signatory considered a third-party beneficiary of a platform's Terms of Service?

The short answer is: **Almost never, unless the contract contains an express declaration conferring those rights.**

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit addressed this exact issue regarding the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS). The court rejected the notion that a non-signatory (like a seller on the platform) can claim third-party beneficiary status based on the "implicit" design or purpose of the TOS.

#### The Legal Standard: The "Strong Presumption"
Under the governing law in this case (Illinois law), there is a **"strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties."** 

To overcome this presumption, the claimant cannot simply point to the general intent or a beneficial outcome of the contract. Instead, the implication that the contract applies to a third party must be **"so strong as to be practically an express declaration."**

#### Application to the TOS
The court’s reasoning for dismissing Santos’s claim highlights two key points that answer your question about "implicit" rights:

1.  **Express vs. Implicit Intent:** The court did not find that the TOS contained any "express declaration" that the relevant provisions applied to Santos or other non-contracting parties. The fact that a TOS is designed to regulate user behavior to protect sellers does not rise to the level of an express declaration of a third-party right.
2.  **The Presence of Other Third-Party Clauses:** Crucially, the court noted that other provisions within the Cameo TOS *did* contain specific third-party beneficiary language. This serves as strong evidence that the drafting parties knew how to confer rights on third parties and chose **not** to do so for the specific provisions Santos attempted to enforce.

#### Summary
You cannot rely on the "implicit" purpose of a platform's TOS to establish third-party beneficiary status. Because of the strong legal presumption against such benefits, a non-signatory must identify language in the contract that is essentially an express grant of rights. If the contract contains explicit third-party language in some sections but not in others, courts will generally interpret that as a deliberate choice to exclude non-signatories from the rest of the agreement.
