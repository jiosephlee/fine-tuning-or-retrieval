Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a court dismiss a copyright claim based on fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) pleading stage?
Question:
I am confused about the procedural timing here. Usually, fair use is treated as an affirmative defense, which I thought meant it was a factual determination made after discovery. In Santos v. Kimmel, the court affirmed a dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), stating fair use was "so clearly established by a complaint." How is it possible to determine fair use without a full evidentiary record?
Answer:
**Yes, a court may dismiss a copyright claim based on fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage if the fair use is "so clearly established by a complaint" that further evidentiary discovery is unnecessary.**

While you are correct that fair use is typically treated as an affirmative defense—which often requires a factual determination after discovery—it is not an absolute rule that it must be decided at summary judgment or trial. As the Second Circuit explains in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the fair use defense can be determined at the pleading stage when the facts alleged in the complaint, the documents attached as exhibits, and those incorporated by reference provide a sufficient basis for the court to apply the four-factor test under 17 U.S.C. § 107.

In *Santos*, the court relied on the following reasoning to justify dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6):

**1. The Transformative Nature of the Use**
The court focused on the "purpose and character of the use" (the first factor). A use is transformative if it adds something new or has a different purpose rather than merely superseding the original. The court determined that a reasonable observer would see a clear distinction between the purpose of the original videos (which conveyed "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity") and the purpose of the defendants' use (commenting on Santos's "willingness to say absurd things for money"). The court noted that the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the user does not determine this factor; rather, it is the perspective of a reasonable observer.

**2. Lack of Market Harm**
Regarding the fourth factor (the effect upon the potential market), the court found it was "clear on the face of Santos’s complaint" that the use did not usurp the market for the original work by offering a competing substitute. Instead, it functioned as a "lampoon" or critical review, which generally does not constitute market harm under the Copyright Act.

**3. Sufficiency of the Pleading Record**
Crucially, the court held that because the determination of fair use in this instance only required a comparison between "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue," the court had enough information to rule without a full evidentiary record.

In short, while fair use is flexible and context-dependent, if the pleadings establish that the use is transformative and does not harm the market for the original work, the court may conclude that the claim fails as a matter of law and dismiss it under Rule 12(b)(6).

### How does the 'reasonable observer' test resolve conflicts between a creator's intent and a user's purpose in transformative use?
Question:
In this case, Santos argued that the use wasn't transformative because Kimmel actually paid for and 'instigated' the videos, meaning the purpose of the creation was aligned with Kimmel's eventual use. However, the court held that transformativeness depends on what a 'reasonable observer' thinks, not the subjective intent of the parties. If the defendant essentially commissioned the work to be mocked, why does the 'reasonable observer' standard override the actual intent of the creation?
Answer:
**The "reasonable observer" standard prioritizes the objective meaning of the work over the subjective intent of the parties to determine if a use is "transformative."**

In copyright law, the first factor of fair use focuses on the "purpose and character of the use," specifically whether the new work is transformative. As the court explains in *Santos v. Kimmel*, a work is transformative if it does not merely supersede the objects of the original creation but instead "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character."

The conflict you identified—where a user (Kimmel) instigates the creation of a work specifically to use it for a certain purpose—is resolved by shifting the analysis from the **subjective intent** of the parties to the **objective perception** of the work.

### Why Subjective Intent is Irrelevant
The court explicitly rejects the argument that the "designer" of a work's original purpose can dictate whether a subsequent use is transformative. Relying on the Supreme Court's holding in *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, the court notes that the "subjective intent of the user" does not determine the purpose of the use. 

If the law relied on subjective intent, a defendant could potentially defeat a fair use defense simply by claiming they never intended to be transformative, or a plaintiff could defeat it by claiming the defendant "planned" the work all along. To avoid this, the court applies the "reasonable observer" test.

### Application in *Santos v. Kimmel*
To determine if Kimmel's use was transformative, the court compared the perceived purpose of the original videos with the perceived purpose of the broadcast segments:

1.  **The Original Work:** According to the court, a reasonable observer would perceive Santos’s original videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity."
2.  **The Secondary Use:** The purpose of the *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* segments was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money."

Because the reasonable observer sees two distinct purposes—one of positivity/encouragement and one of sarcastic criticism/commentary—the use is transformative. 

**The bottom line:** It does not matter that Kimmel "instigated" the videos or that he had a specific intent when he commissioned them. The legal analysis focuses on the contrast between what the original work *appears* to be to the public and what the new work *does* with it. Because those two objective purposes differ, the use is transformative regardless of the "bad faith" or "instigation" involved in the creation.

### Does 'bad faith' or fraudulent acquisition of a work negate a fair use defense?
Question:
I'm struggling with the court's treatment of the Harper & Row precedent. Santos argued that because Kimmel used fictitious names to trick him into making the videos, this 'bad faith' should nullify the fair use defense. The court mentioned that the Supreme Court has 'distanced itself' from the requirement of good faith. Does this mean a person can essentially commit fraud to obtain a copyrighted work and still successfully plead fair use if the final output is transformative?
Answer:
***

### Short Answer: No. 

Based on the court's reasoning in *Santos v. Kimmel*, "bad faith" or fraudulent acquisition of a work does not automatically nullify a fair use defense. While the Supreme Court previously suggested that fair use "presupposes good faith and fair dealing" in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.*, the Second Circuit notes that the Supreme Court has since "distanced itself" from that requirement.

### Legal Analysis

In this case, Santos argued that Jimmy Kimmel’s use of fictitious names to trick him into creating Cameo videos constituted bad faith that should disqualify the defendants from claiming fair use. The court rejected this argument on two primary grounds:

**1. The Shift in Precedent**
The court acknowledged the language in *Harper & Row* regarding good faith but explicitly stated that the Supreme Court has moved away from that standard. This indicates that a user's subjective "bad faith" in how they obtained the material is not a dispositive barrier to a fair use defense.

**2. The Distinction Between Acquisition and Market Supplantation**
The court distinguished between the fraudulent means used to *acquire* the work and the *purpose* of the subsequent use. For bad faith to be legally significant in a fair use analysis, it typically must involve an intent to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work (citing *Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*). 

Here, the court found that Kimmel's conduct did not seek to replace the market for the original videos. Instead, the defendants were motivated by "sarcastic criticism and commentary," which falls squarely within the goals of fair use.

### Conclusion

If the resulting use of the work is transformative—meaning it adds something new with a further purpose or different character—it can still qualify as fair use even if the work was obtained through deception. In *Santos*, the court found the use was transformative because it shifted the purpose of the videos from "hope, strength, [and] perseverance" to a commentary on the plaintiff's "willingness to say absurd things for money." Because the use was transformative and did not serve as a competing substitute for the original work, the "bad faith" of the acquisition did not negate the defense.

### Under what circumstances does the Copyright Act 'preempt' state law breach of contract claims?
Question:
The court mentions that the District Court dismissed Santos's contract claims because they were 'preempted by the Copyright Act.' I don't understand how a contract claim (which is about an agreement between parties) can be preempted by copyright law (which is about the right to control a work). What does it mean for a contract claim to be 'qualitatively' similar to a copyright claim in a way that triggers preemption?
Answer:
***

### Under what circumstances does the Copyright Act 'preempt' state law breach of contract claims?

Based on the provided opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the court **does not** establish the specific circumstances or the legal standard under which the Copyright Act preempts state law breach of contract claims.

While the District Court originally dismissed George Santos’s contract claims on the grounds that they were "preempted by the Copyright Act," the Second Circuit Court of Appeals explicitly declined to rule on the preemption issue.

The relevant portion of the opinion states:

> "Santos responds that those claims are ‘qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim’ and thus not preempted. *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.*, 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). **We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law.**"

In short, while the opinion acknowledges the argument that a claim must be "qualitatively different" from a copyright claim to avoid preemption, the court bypassed the legal analysis of preemption entirely. The court instead affirmed the dismissal of the contract claims based on the merits of state law:
1. **Direct Breach of Contract:** Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service he alleged were breached.
2. **Implied Contract:** The complaint failed to allege the essential terms of a contract or a "meeting of the minds."

Because the claims were legally insufficient under state law, the court found it unnecessary to determine whether they were preempted by federal copyright law.

### When is a non-signatory considered a third-party beneficiary of a platform's Terms of Service?
Question:
Santos tried to sue for a breach of the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS), which Kimmel agreed to. The court dismissed this because Santos wasn't a party to the contract and there was no 'express declaration' of third-party beneficiary status. If a platform's TOS is specifically designed to regulate the behavior of users to protect the sellers on that platform, why isn't that an implicit third-party beneficiary right?
Answer:
***

### When is a non-signatory considered a third-party beneficiary of a platform's Terms of Service?

The short answer is: **Almost never, unless the contract contains an express declaration conferring those rights.**

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit addressed this exact issue regarding the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS). The court rejected the notion that a non-signatory (like a seller on the platform) can claim third-party beneficiary status based on the "implicit" design or purpose of the TOS.

#### The Legal Standard: The "Strong Presumption"
Under the governing law in this case (Illinois law), there is a **"strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties."** 

To overcome this presumption, the claimant cannot simply point to the general intent or a beneficial outcome of the contract. Instead, the implication that the contract applies to a third party must be **"so strong as to be practically an express declaration."**

#### Application to the TOS
The court’s reasoning for dismissing Santos’s claim highlights two key points that answer your question about "implicit" rights:

1.  **Express vs. Implicit Intent:** The court did not find that the TOS contained any "express declaration" that the relevant provisions applied to Santos or other non-contracting parties. The fact that a TOS is designed to regulate user behavior to protect sellers does not rise to the level of an express declaration of a third-party right.
2.  **The Presence of Other Third-Party Clauses:** Crucially, the court noted that other provisions within the Cameo TOS *did* contain specific third-party beneficiary language. This serves as strong evidence that the drafting parties knew how to confer rights on third parties and chose **not** to do so for the specific provisions Santos attempted to enforce.

#### Summary
You cannot rely on the "implicit" purpose of a platform's TOS to establish third-party beneficiary status. Because of the strong legal presumption against such benefits, a non-signatory must identify language in the contract that is essentially an express grant of rights. If the contract contains explicit third-party language in some sections but not in others, courts will generally interpret that as a deliberate choice to exclude non-signatories from the rest of the agreement.

### Is an actual 'out-of-pocket' monetary loss required for a fraudulent inducement claim to survive a motion to dismiss?
Question:
The court affirmed the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim because Santos failed to allege any 'out-of-pocket loss.' I thought that fraudulent inducement was about the deception and the fact that the plaintiff relied on that deception to their detriment. If someone is tricked into providing a service, is that not 'detriment,' or does New York law specifically require a quantifiable cash loss for the claim to be valid?
Answer:
***

**Yes, under New York law, a plaintiff must allege an actual "out-of-pocket" monetary loss for a fraudulent inducement claim to survive a motion to dismiss.**

While you are correct that the general concept of fraudulent inducement involves reliance on a deception to one's detriment, New York law applies a more specific and stringent requirement regarding the nature of that detriment. As the Second Circuit clarified in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the "detriment" must be an actual out-of-pocket loss.

In *Santos*, the plaintiff alleged that he was tricked into providing personalized videos through the Cameo platform by a user (Jimmy Kimmel) who used fictitious names. Although Santos fulfilled these requests—effectively providing a service based on a deception—the court held that this was insufficient to sustain the claim. The court explicitly affirmed the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim because Santos "failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud."

To support this requirement, the court relied on New York state precedents, specifically citing *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* 

In short, for the purposes of pleading a fraudulent inducement claim in New York, the mere provision of a service or the loss of time/effort does not satisfy the requirement of "detriment"; the plaintiff must allege a quantifiable monetary loss.
