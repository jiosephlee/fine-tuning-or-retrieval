Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Doctrine

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the court addressed whether the unauthorized broadcasting of personalized videos on a late-night talk show constituted copyright infringement or was protected by the fair use doctrine. Because the defendants moved to dismiss the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6), the court had to determine if the fair use defense was so clearly established by the allegations in the complaint that the copyright claims could be barred even at the pleading stage.

## The four-factor test under 17 U.S.C. § 107

Fair use is an affirmative defense that allows for the use of copyrighted material without the owner's permission to avoid the "rigid application" of the Copyright Act, which might otherwise stifle creativity. To determine whether a use is "fair," courts apply a non-exclusive four-factor test codified in 17 U.S.C. § 107: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

## Factor 1: Purpose and character of the use (Transformative use and objective observer standard)

The first factor focuses primarily on whether the secondary use is "transformative"—meaning it adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, rather than merely superseding the original work. In this case, the court found the use highly transformative. While George Santos argued that the videos were not transformative because Kimmel "instigated" their creation, the court rejected this, noting that the purpose of the *use* on the show *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (JKL) was to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money.

Crucially, the court clarified that transformativeness is determined by an objective observer standard. Santos argued that his own purpose in making the videos was the same as Kimmel's (to show a willingness to say absurd things for money). However, the court held that the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the user is irrelevant. Instead, the court looked at what a reasonable observer would perceive. Because the original videos themselves conveyed "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity," the use of those videos to mock Santos was fundamentally different from the original purpose and thus transformative.

## The role of 'good faith' and 'fair dealing' in fair use analysis

Santos attempted to defeat the fair use defense by arguing that Kimmel acted in bad faith by using fictitious names to deceive him into filming the videos. This argument relied on a statement from the Supreme Court in *Harper & Row* suggesting that fair use presupposes "good faith and fair dealing."

The Second Circuit rejected this argument, noting that the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that requirement. More importantly, the court found that the "bad faith" in this instance did not result in the defendants attempting to "supplant" Santos’s commercially valuable right to the videos. Because the defendants were motivated by sarcastic criticism and commentary—core purposes of fair use—the deceptive nature of the solicitation did not nullify the defense.

## Factors 2 and 3: Nature of the copyrighted work and amount of the portion used

The court spent less time on the second and third factors, as they did not weigh heavily against a finding of fair use. Regarding the nature of the work, the court noted that the copyright registrations indicated the videos were published on the day they were created. Regarding the amount used, the court found that the use of the videos in the "Will Santos Say It?" segments was justified by the transformative purpose of the commentary.

## Factor 4: Market effect (Distinguishing between market substitution and critical devaluation)

The fourth factor examines whether the secondary use harms the potential market for the original work. The court highlighted a critical distinction here: fair use analysis does not ask if the secondary work *damages* the market for the original (for example, by making the original look ridiculous through parody), but whether it *usurps* the market by acting as a "competing substitute."

In this case, the court found no market harm. Kimmel’s segments were not substitutes for personalized Cameo videos; no one would watch a mocking late-night comedy segment instead of purchasing a personalized greeting. Because the devaluation of the videos resulted from criticism and lampooning rather than market substitution, the fourth factor supported a finding of fair use.

## Applying fair use at the pleading stage

Typically, affirmative defenses are decided after discovery. However, the court affirmed that if the facts alleged in a complaint (and documents incorporated by reference) make the fair use defense "so clearly established," a court may dismiss a copyright infringement claim under Rule 12(b)(6). Because the nature of the JKL segments and the original videos were clear from the pleadings, the court concluded that fair use barred the claims as a matter of law without the need for further litigation.
