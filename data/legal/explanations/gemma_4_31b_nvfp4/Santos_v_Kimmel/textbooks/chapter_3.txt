Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # State Law Contractual Claims

After addressing the copyright infringement claims, the court turned to the state law claims brought by George Santos, specifically those involving breach of contract and breach of implied contract. These claims were dismissed at the district court level and affirmed on appeal, providing a study in the requirements of contractual privity, the standards for pleading implied contracts, and the necessity of mutual assent.

## Copyright Act Preemption of State Law Claims

The District Court initially dismissed Santos’s breach of contract and implied contract claims on the grounds that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Preemption occurs when a federal statute, such as the Copyright Act, overrides a state law claim because the state claim effectively seeks the same remedy for the same right that the federal law governs. Santos argued that his claims were "qualitatively different" from a copyright claim and therefore should not be preempted. However, the Second Circuit declined to resolve this technical legal question. The court determined that the state law claims were insufficient as a matter of law regardless of whether they were preempted, meaning they failed to meet basic contractual requirements under state law.

## Direct Breach of Contract: Third-Party Beneficiary Status under Illinois Law

Santos alleged that the defendants breached the Terms of Service (ToS) of the Cameo platform. Because the ToS are governed by Illinois law, the court applied Illinois legal standards to determine if Santos had the standing to sue for their breach. A primary issue here is privity—the legal relationship between the parties to a contract. Santos was not a party to the agreement between the user (Kimmel) and the platform (Cameo).

Under Illinois law, there is a strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties. For a third party to enforce a contract, the implication that the contract applies to them must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration. The court found that the Cameo ToS contained no such declaration for Santos. In fact, the court noted that other sections of the ToS *did* contain explicit third-party beneficiary language, which demonstrated that when the parties intended to allow third parties to enforce a provision, they did so expressly. Because there was no express intent to benefit Santos in the relevant provisions, his direct breach of contract claim failed.

## Implied-in-Fact Contracts under New York Law

Unable to rely on the written Terms of Service, Santos also argued that an "implied-in-fact" contract existed between him and the defendants. Unlike an express contract, an implied-in-fact contract is formed by the conduct of the parties rather than written or spoken words. This specific claim was governed by New York law.

To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), a plaintiff must allege more than mere conclusions; they must plead the essential terms of the agreement in nonconclusory language. The court found that Santos’s complaint failed this pleading standard. He did not specify the essential terms of the putative contract or the specific provisions that the defendants allegedly breached. Because the complaint lacked these factual specifics, it failed to state a plausible claim for an implied-in-fact contract.

## The 'Meeting of the Minds' Requirement for Contractual Agreements

Beyond the pleading of terms, the court addressed the fundamental requirement of any contract: the "meeting of the minds." This refers to mutual assent, where both parties agree to the same essential terms at the same time. 

The court found no evidence in the complaint that there was a meeting of the minds between Santos and Kimmel regarding the Terms of Service or any other binding agreement. Specifically, there was no indication that Kimmel had agreed to adhere to the platform's Terms of Service in a manner that created a binding contractual obligation toward Santos. Without a demonstrated agreement or mutual assent, the court concluded that no enforceable contract existed, leading to the affirmation of the dismissal.
