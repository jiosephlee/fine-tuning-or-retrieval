Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Shared Spaces, Divided Rights: Third-Party Consent in Domestic Violence Calls

The sanctity of the home is the cornerstone of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence, yet the "third-party consent" doctrine creates a significant loophole when that home is shared. In *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit recently addressed a high-tension scenario where this doctrine intersects with the volatility of domestic violence calls. The case centers on a fundamental question: when a spouse consents to a police entry during a domestic dispute, is that consent truly "voluntary," or is it the product of a coercive environment?

The facts of *Coleman* are captured on body camera footage, which the court emphasized as a tool to "evaporate" factual disputes. After responding to a call about threats of violence, Officer Kizer encountered the defendant’s wife, Lisa. Initially, Lisa was hesitant; she explicitly told the officer, “I’d prefer you not,” when asked to let him inside. However, after a brief hiatus and a second request where the officer stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay],” Lisa agreed. This entry eventually led to the discovery of firearms and Coleman’s subsequent conviction as a felon in possession.

The court’s reasoning rests on the established framework of *United States v. Matlock*, which allows a third party with joint authority over a premises to consent to a search. The legal battle here was not over whether Lisa *could* consent, but whether she *did* so voluntarily. Applying the "totality of the circumstances" test, the Seventh Circuit found that Officer Kizer’s tone was concerned rather than coercive. Crucially, the court pointed to Lisa’s initial refusal as evidence of her agency; because she had already said "no" once, her subsequent "okay" was viewed as a conscious choice rather than a reflexive submission to authority.

From an analytical perspective, the court's treatment of the word "need" is the most contentious part of the ruling. Coleman argued that by stating the police "need" to enter, the officer shifted the interaction from a request to a demand. The court rejected this, viewing the phrase as an expression of urgency regarding child safety rather than a legal command. This distinction is subtle but critical. By characterizing the officer's language as "concern" rather than "coercion," the court maintained a high degree of deference to police discretion in the context of welfare checks.

The doctrinal implications of *Coleman* suggest that in the Seventh Circuit, the "voluntariness" of consent in domestic violence situations will not be heightened simply because the environment is stressful. There is a strong argument that victims of domestic abuse are inherently more susceptible to perceived pressure from authority figures, potentially rendering the "voluntary" standard a fiction in these contexts. However, the court here refused to create a special category of scrutiny for DV calls, instead sticking to the traditional *Bumper* and *Matlock* factors.

Furthermore, the court’s ruling on the "scope" of consent—finding that a request to check on children justifies a general scan of the home to locate all occupants—broadens the operational latitude for officers once they cross the threshold. If the initial entry is deemed legal via third-party consent, the subsequent "protective sweep" is likely to be upheld as objectively reasonable. This creates a potential downstream effect where the threshold for entry is lowered by the "concern for safety" narrative, effectively granting police broad access to a home provided they can secure the consent of a single, potentially distressed, co-occupant.
