Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# From Filing Cabinets to API Calls: Redefining 'Receipt' for the Digital Age

In the world of insurance law, "strict adherence" to statutory notice requirements is often the difference between a valid policy cancellation and a multimillion-dollar bad faith claim. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit was asked to decide whether a digital-first cancellation process—driven by automated web services rather than signed papers—satisfied a Louisiana statute that seems to envision a world of physical documents and filing cabinets.

The case centers on the interaction between an insurance company (GoAuto) and a premium finance company (APAC). Under Louisiana law, when a policyholder defaults on their financing, the finance company can trigger a cancellation by sending the insurer a notice "certifying" specific facts. The insurer then cancels the policy "upon receipt" of that notice. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s process was a legal fiction: the "certification" was unsigned at the moment of cancellation, and the "receipt" was merely an automated API call between two computers, not a physical delivery of a document.

The court’s reasoning on the "certification" requirement is a textbook example of judicial restraint in statutory interpretation. The plaintiffs argued that the word "certifying" implies a signature. However, the court noted that the statute itself never mentions a signature. While one lower court had previously mentioned a lack of signature as a flaw, that case involved a document that didn't even purport to certify the necessary facts. Here, the court held that "strict adherence" to a statute is not a license for courts to read additional requirements into the text. If the legislature wanted a signature, it should have required one.

More significant, however, is the court's treatment of "receipt." The plaintiffs leaned heavily on the traditional definition of "receipt" as taking "physical possession" of an object. This is the kind of argument that often succeeds in rigid jurisdictions: if the document isn't in a human's hand or a physical mailbox, it hasn't been "received."

The Fifth Circuit rejected this formalist approach in favor of a pragmatic, modern reading. By acknowledging that a "web service is like a listener" that performs an activity when called, the court effectively updated the definition of "possession" for the digital age. The court reasoned that in a modern statutory framework, an e-mail or an automated data transfer satisfies the requirement of receipt. This is a crucial distinction; the court refused to treat the law as a time capsule, insisting instead that "receipt" must be interpreted in the context of how current business is actually conducted.

Interestingly, the Fifth Circuit had to walk back some of the district court's more creative reasoning. The lower court had attempted to bridge the gap by labeling the APAC employee who delivered the documents as a "private carrier." Judge Southwick correctly dismissed this as "imaginative and unconventional." By abandoning the "private carrier" theory and focusing on the electronic transmission, the Fifth Circuit provided a much cleaner, more scalable precedent for the digital economy.

The doctrinal implications here are broad. As more business-to-business transactions migrate to APIs and automated triggers, courts will increasingly encounter "receipt" and "delivery" requirements written for the era of certified mail. *Williams* signals that the Fifth Circuit will not allow antiquated definitions of "physical possession" to invalidate modern automated processes, provided the substantive requirements of the statute are met. For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: while "strict adherence" remains the standard, that adherence is measured by the functional reality of the technology used, not by a nostalgic requirement for ink and paper.
