Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 4: # The 'Receipt' Requirement in the Digital Age

## The Definition of 'Receipt' and 'Receive'

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the central legal dispute turned on whether GoAuto had complied with the strict requirements of Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3), which governs the cancellation of insurance policies financed by a third party. Under the statute, an insurer may proceed to cancel a policy only "upon receipt" of a notice of cancellation and a certifying statement from the premium finance company. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s automated process did not constitute "receipt" because the company did not take physical possession of a signed document before the policy was cancelled.

Because the Louisiana statute and the Louisiana Supreme Court provided no specific definition for the term "receipt," the Fifth Circuit turned to Black's Law Dictionary for guidance. The court adopted the dictionary's definitions: "receipt" is the act of receiving something, specifically by taking physical possession, and "receive" means to take something offered, given, or sent, and to come into possession of it from an outside source. By establishing this baseline, the court focused the legal analysis on whether GoAuto had taken "possession, delivery, or custody" of the cancellation request before the cancellation was effectuated.

## Electronic Communication and Web Services

The court examined the specific technical interaction between the finance company (APAC) and the insurer (GoAuto) to determine if it met the definition of receipt. The evidence showed that APAC’s computer system was programmed to automatically send an e-mail to GoAuto containing the notice of cancellation and the required certifications once a payment default became uncured. 

This e-mail was not merely a message sitting in an inbox; it triggered a "web service" within GoAuto’s computer system. The court described this web service as a "listener"—a program that constantly waits to be called by an external source. When the APAC system "calls" the GoAuto web service, the service understands the request and automatically performs the cancellation of the policy. This entire electronic exchange occurred in the early morning hours, well before any human employee became involved in the process.

## Legal Validity of Automated 'Listeners' as Receipt

The plaintiffs contended that the statutory requirement of "receipt" required a human being to take possession of a physical notice. They pointed to the fact that an APAC employee did not affix a signature stamp to printed documents, nor did a GoAuto employee stamp them as "received," until later in the morning after the policy had already been cancelled.

The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, reasoning that the statute is a "modern statute set in current times." The court held that the functions previously performed by individuals are now frequently handled by computer systems. By concluding that the web service’s "listening" and subsequent execution of the cancellation constituted the insurer taking possession of the notice, the court affirmed that electronic triggers and automated interactions satisfy the statutory requirement of receipt. The court thus held that the automated e-mail interaction between APAC and GoAuto was legally sufficient to trigger the insurer's right to cancel the policies.

## Critique of the District Court's 'Private Carrier' Theory

The Fifth Circuit also addressed a specific conclusion reached by the district court. The district court had held that GoAuto complied with the statute by claiming that the APAC employee who physically delivered the notices of cancellation fit the definition of a "private carrier"—one of the approved methods of delivery under the statute. To reach this conclusion, the district court relied on a different panel's interpretation of a "private carrier" in the context of a railroad.

The Fifth Circuit explicitly distanced itself from this reasoning, characterizing the district court’s definition of a "private carrier" as both "imaginative and unconventional." While the Fifth Circuit did not feel the need to fully dismantle the theory because the e-mail delivery already satisfied the statute, the court used this critique to emphasize that statutory interpretation should be grounded in established law and the plain text of the statute rather than unconventional analogies.
