# Redressability and Independent Actors: AHM’s Shadow Over Administrative Challenges

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is a compact but telling application of modern Article III standing doctrine to a type of administrativechallenge that has become increasingly common: litigants who seek to force one federal actor to prod or punish another into producing records or taking enforcement steps that the plaintiff wants. At stake is the scope of judicially cognizable injury when the asserted wrong is an agency’s refusal to investigate or discipline, and the alleged benefit flows only if several other, independent actors (including other agencies and adjudicative bodies) make a chain of discretionary choices. The court’s reliance on the Supreme Court’s recent pronouncements about speculation concerning “unfettered choices” by non‑parties — most prominently Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine (AHM) and Haaland — marks another waypoint in the narrowing of redressability for procedural‑rights claims grounded in statutory enforcement mechanisms.

The outcome: AFL lacked Article III standing because any relief compelling the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate would not “very likely” lead to production of the FOIA records AFL sought. The causal chain from an OSC investigation to potential MSPB sanctions to DOJ disclosure was too attenuated and speculative to satisfy redressability and causation.

## The court’s core reasoning

The panel accepted (without resolving) that AFL’s asserted right to an OSC investigation might be a statutory procedural entitlement. But that concession did not suffice. The court proceeded to ask the familiar three‑part standing question and focused on two distinct informational injuries AFL advanced: (1) that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused DOJ to withhold FOIA records; and (2) that AFL was deprived of information and notices that would flow from an OSC investigation itself.

The court rejected the first theory on straightforward causation and redressability grounds. Any informational injury from DOJ’s nondisclosure is directly traceable to DOJ, not OSC. To connect OSC’s alleged refusal to investigate with DOJ’s decision to withhold records required an intricate, multi‑step causal story: OSC might investigate; OSC might seek corrective or disciplinary relief under 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214–1215; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) might impose sanctions; DOJ might then change course and release documents. The court, relying on AHM and Haaland, characterized that chain as impermissibly dependent on the “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the court.” As a result, a judicial mandate compelling OSC to investigate would not be “likely” to spur the next steps necessary to redress AFL’s alleged injury.

The second theory — that AFL had a standalone informational stake in whatever notices or investigatory reports OSC would produce if it investigated — also faltered. Even assuming § 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, the provision does not, by its terms, create a right to receive the informational outputs AFL claims. Any entitlement to the particular notices AFL sought would depend on whether OSC elected to invoke § 1214’s procedural notice regime in a § 1216 investigation, a choice prior case law indicates OSC may make in its discretion. Without a statutory entitlement to the information, AFL lacked the kind of concrete informational injury that the court has recognized as sufficient for standing.

With both theories collapsed, the panel concluded that AFL failed even the relaxed standing rules applicable to procedural injuries and ordered dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

## Analytical perspective: AHM’s gravitational pull

Two doctrinal strands animate this decision. First is the court’s literal engagement with the “causation and redressability” prong of standing. It underscores that plaintiffs cannot bootstrap generalized statutory or procedural grievances into Article III cases unless a favorable court order would, with reasonable probability, produce the concrete relief they seek. Second is the heightened insistence that the chain linking judicial relief to the plaintiff’s injury avoid reliance on discretionary acts by third parties (including other agencies and administrative adjudicators). That insistence is straight out of AHM and Haaland, and the D.C. Circuit applies it here to a statutory enforcement structure intentionally designed to distribute decisionmaking across several actors.

The court’s approach is defensible as a fidelity to separation‑of‑powers limits on federal courts. A remedy that simply authorizes the court to “fix” an alleged procedural lapse but leaves the substantive remedy contingent on the uninvolved discretionary choices of other offices arguably offers no meaningful redress and transforms district courts into forums for issuing advisory proclamations about how other actors might behave.

At the same time, this strictness risks a chill on accountability suits aimed at enforcement offices that sit in the middle of regulatory or records ecosystems. OSC’s investigatory choices are often consequential primarily insofar as they trigger downstream enforcement or remedial actions elsewhere. If plaintiffs cannot sue to compel such investigations because the causal chain to actual relief is attenuated, there will be fewer judicial avenues for probing institutional non‑enforcement or systemic policies that frustrate statutory obligations (like FOIA production). In other words, AHM’s anti‑speculation principle protects against overbroad judicial intervention, but it may also make it harder to vindicate statutory rights where the statutory enforcement architecture intentionally relies on multi‑actor sequences.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several implications deserve attention.

First, plaintiffs bringing cases against enforcement intermediaries (like OSC, OIGs, or independent counsels) will face an uphill battle proving redressability when the injury claimed requires action by other agencies or adjudicators to materialize. Litigants will need to identify concrete, agency‑specific harms that are directly traceable to the defendant and likely to be remedied by the relief sought.

Second, the decision signals that statutory procedural entitlements remain fragile when disentangled from substantive rights. Courts will demand either an express statutory entitlement to the informational relief sought, or a clear causal pathway from the defendant’s statutory duty to the plaintiff’s concrete injury that does not depend on the unpredictable behavior of non‑parties.

Third, government defendants will have increased leverage to resist suits that aim to weaponize one agency’s obligations to influence another agency’s behavior. Parties seeking to force interagency domino effects will need to proceed either against the agency whose conduct is directly responsible for the complained‑of injury (e.g., DOJ for FOIA refusals) or provide a tighter evidentiary and legal account showing how the defendant’s action is likely to produce the desired outcome.

Finally, the decision may steer more litigants toward seeking declaratory findings or mandamus against the primary withholding agency (DOJ in this case) rather than indirect suits against an intermediary like OSC. But mandamus and FOIA litigation bring their own obstacles, and not all enforcement patterns map neatly onto those procedural routes.

## Conclusion

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is a concise illustration of a broader doctrinal trend: courts are increasingly reluctant to entertain suits whose redress depends on a chain of discretionary acts by actors not before the court. That posture preserves judicial modesty and avoids speculative remedies, but it also constrains suits that attempt to leverage one agency’s investigatory obligations to influence another’s substantive compliance. For practitioners and public‑interest litigants, the decision underscores the importance of drawing a direct line from the defendant’s duty to the plaintiff’s concrete injury — or else accepting that the courthouse may not be the place to reorder interagency dynamics.
