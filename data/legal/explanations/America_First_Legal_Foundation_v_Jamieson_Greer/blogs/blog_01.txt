Title: Blog about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

# When “Shall Investigate” Isn’t a Right to Know: Informational Standing After Akins

## Why this decision matters

The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is a succinct, consequential reminder of the limits of so‑called “informational standing” after Akins and its progeny. At first blush, a statutory command that the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) “shall … conduct an investigation” into alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings looks like a promising avenue for watchdog groups seeking federal court review when an agency declines to probe misconduct. But the court rejected that shortcut: a statutory duty to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) does not, by itself, create a cognizable private entitlement to the investigatory fruits — notices, reports, or other information — that would supply Article III standing under Akins. That framing narrows one route that litigants have used to transform procedural mandates into concrete informational injuries.

The immediate consequence is procedural: the D.C. Circuit vacated the district court’s judgment ordering further proceedings and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction. More broadly, this decision clarifies what kinds of statutory language will support an informational injury claim and what will not, with practical and doctrinal implications for public‑interest litigants and for how courts treat “shall investigate” clauses across administrative statutes.

## The court’s core reasoning

The opinion confronts two distinct standing theories advanced by AFL. First, AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused it to be deprived of the DOJ records it sought under FOIA. Second, AFL claimed an independent informational injury: that OSC’s failure to investigate denied it the notices and other information OSC would provide if it were conducting the investigation.

The court disposed of both theories under settled Article III rules.

On the first theory, the court emphasized causation and redressability. Any injury from DOJ’s non‑production is directly traceable to DOJ, not OSC. And even if an OSC investigation might set in motion disciplinary or corrective proceedings that could lead DOJ to disclose records, that causal chain is too speculative and attenuated to support standing. It would require courts to assume discretionary enforcement choices by OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and DOJ — independent actors not before the court — and thus would rely on contingencies courts cannot treat as likely redress.

On the second theory, the court applied the Akins line of authority: informational standing requires that the plaintiff be denied information to which a statute entitles it. The panel concluded § 1216(a)(3) commands OSC to investigate alleged FOIA violations, but it does not create any entitlement to receive information from OSC. AFL’s suggestion that OSC would have to provide section 1214’s notice and information if it conducted a § 1216 investigation was unavailing: AFL itself acknowledged that OSC’s decision whether to apply § 1214’s procedural protections in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, and the court relied on Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel to that effect. Because AFL could not point to a statutory entitlement to information, it could not show the concrete informational injury necessary under Akins.

The court therefore reached the familiar Summers rule — a procedural right without some concrete interest affected by the deprivation cannot confer standing — and concluded AFL’s asserted procedural injury was insufficient to meet Article III.

## Putting the opinion in doctrinal context

Akins and its successors stand for the proposition that a plaintiff may have standing when a statute entitles it to certain information and that entitlement is frustrated. That doctrine has been important where disclosure regimes create specific entitlements — classic examples include FACA’s access provisions or FOIA’s right to agency records. Akins, Public Citizen, and Federal Election Commission v. Akins all treated statutory disclosure entitlements as cognizable injuries when denied.

But not all statutory duties are disclosure entitlements. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion here reiterates a key boundary: a mandate that an agency investigate wrongdoing does not automatically generate a private right to the investigative fruits. The distinction the court draws is conceptually straightforward, if sometimes messy in application: “shall investigate” is an enforcement/prosecutorial obligation, not a clearestatement of an entitlement to information for private parties. Put differently, the statute places a duty on OSC; it does not create a private cause of action for members of the public to receive investigatory reports or notices from OSC.

The Carson decision — which the panel cites — is particularly relevant. Carson recognized that OSC’s choice whether to apply section 1214’s procedural provisions in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. That doctrinal point undercuts attempts to bootstrap § 1214 notice protections onto § 1216 actions whenever a private plaintiff wants the information those procedures might require.

The court also sensibly treated redressability as linked to the real-world mechanics of administrative enforcement. A judicial order requiring OSC to investigate does not bind third parties (like DOJ or the MSPB) or eliminate the discretionary choices those agencies retain. Courts have repeatedly warned against standing theories that rest on speculative chains of discretionary decisions by multiple actors; the opinion adheres to that tradition.

## Implications for litigants and doctrine

For public‑interest groups and litigators, the lesson is practical and strategic. If your standing theory depends on being deprived of information, you should identify a statutory provision that unmistakably confers a right to receive that information. A “shall investigate” clause is helpful for policy and oversight arguments, but without accompanying entitlements to notice or reports, it is a risky foundation for Article III jurisdiction. Courts will distinguish between: (1) statutes that confer a private legal entitlement to information (good candidates for Akins‑style standing), and (2) statutes that impose an investigatory duty on an agency without creating a cognizable informational entitlement (poor candidates).

Because § 1216 itself nowhere specifies that the public — or the complainant — is entitled to particular notices, reports, or access as a matter of right, OSC’s refusal to investigate does not, standing alone, deprive a litigant of a statutory entitlement. Nor can litigants rely reliably on the discretionary spillover of § 1214 protections into § 1216 investigations; Carson and the D.C. Circuit’s opinion signal that such spillover cannot be presumed. That will push watchdogs toward two alternative tactics:

- Focusing challenges directly at agencies that withhold records under FOIA, where FOIA’s disclosure obligations are concrete and have clearer standing consequences. If the injury is non‑production by the agency holding the records, Plaintiffs can establish a more direct causation and redressability link.

- Seeking statutes or regulatory rules that explicitly create entitlements to notices, reports, or access in conjunction with investigatory duties. That may involve legislative advocacy to turn “shall investigate” into “shall investigate and shall provide X to complainants or the public.”

There are also doctrinal ripple effects to consider. This opinion tightens the synergy between procedural‑rights jurisprudence and the redressability inquiry. Plaintiffs can sometimes survive on relaxed imminence and redressability when a procedural right protects a threatened concrete interest; but as the court emphasizes, that relaxation does not eliminate the requirement that the procedural deprivation cause a concrete harm that is likely to be redressed by the court. The opinion thus keeps in place the two critical gatekeepers: a statutory entitlement to information and a non‑speculative causal path to redress.

Finally, the decision reinforces judicial reluctance to micromanage agency enforcement discretion. Courts are wary of ordering agencies to pursue particular enforcement pathways in the hope that those pathways will produce desired outcomes from other, independent actors. That concern animates both Heckler v. Chaney’s rule against review of certain agency non‑enforcement decisions and the redressability analysis the D.C. Circuit applied here.

## Bottom line

A statutory “shall investigate” can be an important accountability tool, but it is not, by itself, a ticket to federal court under Akins. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer draws a clear line: informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to information — a right to receive specific disclosures — and a plausible causal pathway by which a judicial remedy will redress the deprivation. Where a statute commands an agency to investigate but does not guarantee the public any particular informational returns from that investigation, courts will treat the failure to investigate as an incomplete, often non‑justiciable grievance rather than a cognizable informational injury. Watchdogs who want to litigate over investigatory refusals should therefore anchor their claims to statutory disclosure entitlements or pursue legislative and rulemaking avenues to convert investigatory duties into clear private informational rights.
