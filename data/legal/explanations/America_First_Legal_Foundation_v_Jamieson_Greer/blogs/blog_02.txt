# The FOIA–OSC Interface Left Unresolved: Does § 1216 Impose a Non‑Discretionary Duty?

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (Oct. 3, 2025) leaves unresolved a consequential question at the intersection of FOIA enforcement and civil‑service policing: when Congress directed the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to “shall … conduct an investigation” of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings in 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), did it create a judicially enforceable, non‑discretionary duty that OSC must follow independent of FOIA’s separate enforcement trigger at 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)? Or did Congress instead lodge the decision whether to investigate such allegations within OSC’s prosecutorial and discretionary authority?

The district court in AFL’s challenge answered the question affirmatively: FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not exhaust OSC’s authority and § 1216 independently requires OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary withholdings. The D.C. Circuit, however, avoided that merits determination entirely, concluding AFL lacked Article III standing to sue and vacating the district court judgment for lack of jurisdiction. That outcome leaves the doctrinal and practical problems the district court tried to address unresolved and sets the stage for renewed litigation or congressional clarification.

## The statutory architecture

Understanding the issue requires attention to several overlapping provisions.

- Section 1216(a)(3) of Title 5 (immediately after a provision empowering OSC to investigate certain forms of misconduct) states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—FOIA. Subsection (c) then authorizes OSC, for such alleged FOIA violations, to seek corrective action under § 1214 (the corrective action machinery) and disciplinary action under § 1215 “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”

- Section 1214 establishes procedures for investigations of “prohibited personnel practices,” including notice and procedural safeguards for complaining parties and describes remedies OSC may seek before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB).

- Section 1215 deals with disciplinary proceedings.

- FOIA itself contains a discrete provision—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—that requires OSC to initiate a proceeding only when (1) a court orders production of improperly withheld records, (2) attorneys’ fees are awarded against the agency, and (3) the court specifically questions whether the withholding was arbitrary or capricious.

One reading of the text sees § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” as an independent statutory command: Congress meant OSC to be able to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings without being tethered to the three judicial predicates in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). That reading emphasizes the plain‑language obligation and § 1216(c)’s explicit cross‑reference to § 1214 and § 1215 remedies, suggesting Congress intended OSC to have proactive investigatory authority into FOIA compliance.

An opposing reading treats § 1216(a)(3) as best read in conjunction with FOIA’s enforcement scheme: the “shall” may be directional, tied to the remedial architecture that Congress adopted in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), or otherwise constrained by the traditional prosecutorial discretion that courts are reluctant to displace. On this view, § 1216 creates authority and an enforcement mechanism but does not strip OSC of its discretion to decide whether to pursue investigations in particular cases absent the judicial trigger FOIA itself provides.

## What the courts did — and did not — decide

The district court concluded OSC had misinterpreted FOIA: § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s § 1216 authority, and OSC erred as a matter of law in declining to investigate AFL’s allegations that DOJ had a policy of not searching employee emails without consent. But the district court stopped short of ordering specific investigatory steps, reasoning that § 1216(c) leaves certain procedural choices to OSC.

The D.C. Circuit, by contrast, disposed of the case on standing grounds. The panel did not reach the statutory question. It held AFL lacked Article III standing under both of the informational‑injury theories it advanced. First, any FOIA production injury was traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and redress depended on a speculative chain of enforcement steps by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ—the kind of “highly attenuated” causal chain courts treat as insufficient for redressability. Second, AFL could not show an entitlement to the information that a judicial order would directly secure: even if § 1216 commanded OSC to investigate, AFL pointed to no statutory right to receive investigatory materials or notices outside the discrete § 1214 notice obligations, which OSC may permissibly decline to apply in § 1216 proceedings. Because AFL failed to allege a concrete informational injury tied to a judicially enforceable statutory entitlement, the court vacated and remanded for dismissal.

## Analytical perspective: the merits question remains live and important

The panel’s standing disposition is faithful to established Article III limits, but it leaves unresolved the central statutory question that prompted the suit. The D.C. Circuit’s analysis exposes a genuine doctrinal tension.

First, statutory text points in both directions. “Shall … conduct an investigation” reads mandatory, and Congress’s willingness elsewhere to create FOIA‑specific enforcement triggers (i.e., § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)) does not necessarily negate a separate investigatory obligation. Section 1216(c)’s explicit treatment of FOIA allegations “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” buttresses the argument that Congress intended OSC to treat arbitrary FOIA withholdings seriously and with the full panoply of remedies.

But the canon against judicial interference in prosecutorial discretion complicates matters. Courts routinely treat enforcement decisions—particularly whether to open or pursue investigations—as committed to agency discretion unless Congress has unambiguously removed that discretion. If § 1216’s “shall” is interpreted as an unqualified command to investigate every allegation of arbitrary withholding, courts would be asked to police what OSC must investigate and perhaps how it must do so—an intrusion the Supreme Court and this circuit have been skeptical of absent clearer statutory indicators.

Second, the remedy dynamics matter. For claimants like AFL, the attraction of a mandatory § 1216 investigatory duty is that it potentially bypasses FOIA’s narrow judicial trigger—enabling oversight even where no court has ordered production or awarded fees. That could strengthen administrative accountability over agency FOIA compliance. Yet, absent a statutory right to receive investigatory materials or process, victims who cannot meet FOIA’s judicial prerequisites may still struggle to obtain meaningful redress even if OSC’s investigatory duty is enforced.

## Doctrinal and practical downstream effects

If a future panel were to hold § 1216 imposes a judicially enforceable duty, the practical consequences would be immediate. Advocacy groups and FOIA requesters could compel OSC to investigate agency FOIA policies and practices more frequently, potentially leading to MSPB corrective orders or disciplinary actions where OSC pursues remedies. That would enhance an administrative check on agency FOIA behavior but could also push courts into closer review of OSC’s investigative choices.

Conversely, if the prevailing view treats § 1216 as empowering but discretionary, OSC retains gatekeeping authority: it can open investigations when resources and priorities justify them and decline otherwise. That preserves executive‑branch control over enforcement but leaves a gap between statutory text and private enforcement prospects—precisely the gap AFL sought to fill.

Finally, the court’s standing ruling signals a practical hurdle for litigants seeking to force OSC’s hand: plaintiffs must show a concrete, particularized informational injury or a statutory entitlement to information, not merely the deprivation of a procedural investigatory right. That will make it harder to litigate the question in the abstract and pushes aggrieved requesters toward hybrid strategies—either securing the FOIA judicial predicates that trigger § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) or identifying a more tangible informational harm traceable to OSC’s refusal to act.

## What’s next

AFL could seek en banc rehearing or petition the Supreme Court, but the pathway is uncertain: circuits are not yet clearly fractured on the point, and the D.C. Circuit’s standing rationale is robust. The unsettled statutory interpretation, however, guarantees that litigants and Congress will keep this issue alive. Lower courts, OSC’s own policy choices, and any legislative action clarifying OSC’s role vis‑à‑vis FOIA will determine whether the “shall” in § 1216 becomes an enforceable command or remains, in practice, an aspirational statutory directive subject to prosecutorial judgment.

For now, the case leaves a door ajar: the D.C. Circuit has not spoken to the merits, and the substantive FOIA–OSC question remains one of the more consequential unresolved intersections in administrative law.
