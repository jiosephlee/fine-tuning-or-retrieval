Chapter 9: # Suing Oversight Agencies: Reviewability, Discretion, and Justiciability

The decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (D.C. Cir. 2025) provides a useful vantage point for studying the recurring set of problems that arises when private parties attempt to compel executive-branch oversight or enforcement actions. The case sits at the intersection of four doctrines that together usually foreclose such litigation: (1) the presumption of unreviewability for nonenforcement decisions embodied in Heckler v. Chaney; (2) the limits of statutes’ “shall” language to convert discretionary enforcement authority into a judicially enforceable duty; (3) the practical and doctrinal constraints on mandamus and Administrative Procedure Act relief that would force investigations; and (4) Article III standing’s requirements—particularly for suits alleging procedural harms or informational injuries. Overlaying all of these is a deep separation-of-powers concern: courts must avoid micromanaging executive enforcement, discipline, or prosecutorial choices.

This chapter examines those doctrines with frequent reference to the facts and reasoning of AFL v. Greer. The D.C. Circuit resolved the appeal on standing grounds and did not finally decide whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) must investigate alleged FOIA-withholdings under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. But the opinion illustrates how courts treat claims that an agency has refused to investigate or prosecute alleged misconduct, and it exposes the limits of judicial power in this field.

## Heckler v. Chaney and the Presumption of Unreviewability of Enforcement Discretion

Heckler v. Chaney (1985) is the lodestar for courts’ treatment of agency nonenforcement. There the Supreme Court held that an agency’s decision not to pursue an enforcement action ordinarily is “committed to agency discretion by law” and presumptively unreviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act. The reasons are familiar and weighty: enforcement involves resource allocation, prosecutorial judgment, and assessments of litigation risk and policy priorities that courts are ill-equipped to make. When an agency declines to act, courts risk entangling themselves in sensitive functions reserved to the political branches.

Heckler’s presumption is not absolute. The Court recognized two categories in which a court might review nonenforcement: where Congress has supplied “meaningful standard[s]” to constrain the agency’s choice, or where the decision is alleged to be based on unconstitutional considerations (for example, race). More generally, the Court has signaled that reviewability depends on whether there are judicially manageable standards to apply to the agency’s conduct. If Congress has written concrete, specific, nondiscretionary duties, a refusal to act may be reviewable; if the statute confers broad discretion or lacks standards, the decision is for the agency.

In the OSC context, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion in AFL v. Greer discussed Heckler only implicitly, because the court disposed of the case on standing. But the parties and the district court litigated precisely the question Heckler raises: whether OSC’s statutory text created a nondiscretionary duty to investigate alleged FOIA violations. The district court thought the text did; an OSC refusal to investigate was thus reviewable as a legal error. The D.C. Circuit did not reach that statutory question because it found AFL lacked Article III standing. Nevertheless, the case demonstrates the typical two-step pattern: plaintiffs first try to overcome Heckler by pointing to mandatory statutory language or detailed standards; the agency responds that the language is discretionary, and courts then evaluate whether the statute displaces Heckler’s presumption.

A practical lesson from Heckler is that courts will not lightly substitute judicial judgment for executive assessment. Even when statutory text appears to be mandatory, courts scrutinize whether the statute really furnishes a “meaningful standard” or instead merely sets out goals and general duties that leave core enforcement priorities to the agency.

## When “Shall” Language Overcomes Discretion—and When It Does Not

Plaintiffs commonly hinge their claims on a statute’s “shall”: “shall investigate,” “shall commence,” “shall take” are phrases that seemingly impose command obligations. But courts have long emphasized that “shall” is not dispositive. The Supreme Court’s decisions—most notably in Norton v. Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance (SUWA) and in post-Heckler cases—indicate that a court must look beyond the word to the statutory context to decide whether Congress imposed a judicially enforceable duty.

Norton/SUWA is particularly instructive for APA claims that an agency “failed to act.” SUWA held that § 706(1) of the APA authorizes courts to compel an agency to perform a specific, nondiscretionary duty, but the court cannot create a duty that Congress did not impose. The analysis therefore focuses on whether the statute creates a “discrete, mandatory” obligation that is “not so infused with a high level of discretion” that courts cannot enforce it. A pleading that asserts only a generalized failure to carry out broad responsibilities will not suffice.

Applying that framework, courts ask several questions when confronted with “shall” language: Does the statutory provision contain specific, judicially manageable standards? Does the surrounding statutory scheme contemplate agency discretion in timing, manner, or methods? Is the agency’s task essentially prosecutorial or bound up with judgments that courts cannot review? Where a statutory duty is operationally discrete and administrable—e.g., “within 60 days, the agency must do X”—courts are likelier to treat “shall” as enforceable. Where “shall” exists in a broad remedial or policy context, courts are reluctant.

In AFL v. Greer, the relevant texts illustrate the difficulty. Section 1216(a)(3) of Title 5 provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” On its face that language reads binding. But FOIA itself contains a separate provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), that triggers OSC duties only where (1) a court orders production, (2) attorneys’ fees are awarded, and (3) the court questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. The district court read § 1216 as independent of the FOIA provision, concluding that an OSC obligation under § 1216 exists notwithstanding FOIA’s three-part trigger. OSC and DOJ pressed the contrary view—either that Congress confined OSC to the FOIA-triggered enforcement route or that §1216 left significant discretion in OSC about procedures and use of §1214’s notification provisions.

Even where “shall” seems clear, courts will examine statutory purpose, structure, and implications. A “shall” that would require an agency to pursue investigations in a manner that forces prosecutorial decisions to be reviewed or micromanaged by courts may be treated as aspirational rather than mandatory. The practical upshot is that plaintiffs seeking to convert “shall” into judicially enforceable obligations must show more than a literal command: they must demonstrate that Congress supplied precise standards that courts can apply without intruding into core executive judgment.

## Carson v. OSC and the OSC’s Procedural Flexibility

Carson v. United States Office of Special Counsel (6th Cir. 2011) supplies a circuit-level precedent directly addressing OSC’s discretion within § 1216 investigations. The Sixth Circuit held that OSC retains discretion whether to apply the procedural requirements of § 1214 (which governs “prohibited personnel practices”) when it conducts an investigation under § 1216. That decision recognizes OSC’s twin roles—sometimes playing a corrective or prosecutorial role akin to a personnel-enforcement agency, and sometimes serving as the repository of duties related to FOIA violations—and allows OSC to choose procedures appropriate to the type of claim it is pursuing.

AFL v. Greer cited Carson. AFL had conceded that OSC need not mechanically apply § 1214’s notice and the like to every § 1216 probe; it instead argued that § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” nonetheless imposes an obligation to investigate in some circumstances. The district court disagreed with OSC’s reading and found legal error in OSC’s declination; the circuit court did not reach the statutory question. But Carson illustrates a common doctrinal accommodation: courts often allow agencies procedural flexibility even when statutes impose some substantive investigatory duties.

For students, Carson teaches two lessons. First, even a statute that appears to impose a duty to investigate may have built-in flexibility about what an “investigation” entails. Second, the presence of a discretionary choice about procedures—whether to give notice, whether to apply certain internal rules—makes judicial review more difficult. A statute that imposes a duty but leaves the agency broad control over the form and scope of investigation tends to--for separation-of-powers reasons—conceal the core enforcement judgment within the executive branch.

## Limits of Mandamus and APA Review in Compelling Investigations

When plaintiffs ask courts to compel the executive to investigate, two principal judicial tools are invoked: the writ of mandamus and the APA’s provision allowing review of agency failures to act (5 U.S.C. § 706(1)). Both are available in theory, but in practice they are constrained.

Mandamus (28 U.S.C. § 1361 and traditional equitable writs) requires (1) a clear and indisputable legal right in the plaintiff to the relief sought; (2) a clear and non-discretionary duty in the defendant to perform the act; and (3) no other adequate means to obtain relief. Mandamus is powerful but narrow: it will not issue to compel the exercise of discretion. Courts treat enforcement and investigative choices as quintessentially discretionary; mandamus will not ordinarily be used to direct prosecutorial or disciplinary judgments. Thus, if the duty a plaintiff cites is anything less than an unambiguous statutory command with manageable standards, mandamus relief is unlikely.

APA § 706(1) permits courts to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.” After SUWA, however, courts interpret § 706(1) to require that Congress has imposed a specific, non-discretionary duty. A general statutory responsibility to “enforce” or to “protect” does not create a judicially enforceable mandate. Moreover, even where a discrete duty exists, courts must be able to craft a remedy that does not intrude impermissibly into executive decision-making. The SUWA Court rejected a broad reading of § 706(1) because it would have entitled courts to direct agencies to act in ways that require substantive policy judgments. So when the duty would require courts to dictate how an agency allocates resources, prioritizes cases, or exercises prosecutorial judgments, § 706(1) is an inadequate vehicle.

AFL v. Greer illustrates these limits. The district court attempted a middle course: it found OSC’s declination legally erroneous and remanded for OSC to reconsider, rather than ordering OSC to investigate in a particular way. Remand is a typical judicial response where courts detect legal error in the agency’s denial yet prefer not to micromanage subsequent prosecutorial choices. The D.C. Circuit, by contrast, did not reach the APA/motion-to-compel issues because it concluded AFL lacked Article III standing. But the case shows the doctrinal reluctance to convert Congress’s investigatory aims into automatic, court-enforceable commands.

Practically speaking, a plaintiff seeking mandamus or § 706(1) relief should (1) identify a narrow statutory duty that is concrete in its requirements; (2) demonstrate that the duty is not infused with discretionary considerations; and (3) propose a judicially manageable remedy that does not require day-to-day supervision of executive law-enforcement choices.

## Separation-of-Powers Concerns in Judicial Supervision of Executive Enforcement Choices

Separation-of-powers principles are the constitutional backdrop to judicial restraint in these contexts. The political branches share responsibility for enforcement and oversight—Congress designs mechanisms for enforcement; the executive executes law enforcement and discipline; courts adjudicate legal disputes arising from those processes. When plaintiffs ask courts to review or direct investigations, courts must be mindful that enforcement entails constitutional prerogatives of the executive: prosecutorial discretion, selection of targets, timing, and policy priorities.

Heckler recognized this point explicitly: a judicial command that an agency “prosecute” or “investigate” in particular ways would intrude upon the executive’s sworn duties and undermine the President’s constitutional responsibility to “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.” Courts therefore are cautious about granting relief that would require continuous oversight of executive compliance or that would reduce complex policy choices to judicial orders.

AFL v. Greer underscores a complementary constitutional consideration: standing doctrine itself limits judicial intervention. By insisting on concrete, particularized, and redressable injuries, Article III acts as a gatekeeper that prevents courts from transforming diffuse grievances about executive priorities into federal-court controversies. In the AFL case the D.C. Circuit held that even if OSC committed legal error in declining to investigate, AFL lacked the kind of injury cognizable in federal court—thus avoiding the constitutional entanglement that would otherwise follow.

In short, separation-of-powers concerns operate both directly (through doctrines of nonreviewability and limits on remedies) and indirectly (through standing principles that limit the cases the courts may hear).

## Comparisons to FEC Nonenforcement and Akins-Type Informational Injuries

Two richer contexts for comparison are (1) suits challenging the Federal Election Commission’s (FEC) refusal to enforce campaign-finance laws, and (2) claims that rest on informational injuries—situations in which a plaintiff alleges a statutory entitlement to certain information.

Akins v. FEC (1998) is the seminal “informational standing” case. There the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff had standing to sue the FEC because the statute promised that certain information about contributors would be publicly available; the plaintiff alleged he had been deprived of that information and alleged injury was concrete. Akins teaches two propositions: first, the deprivation of a statutorily bestowed information right can be a cognizable injury; second, that entitlement must be specific and created by statute—an abstract interest in information or “a general interest in government affairs” is insufficient.

Yet informational standing has limits. Post-Akins cases require that the statute create a specific entitlement to information and that the claimed deprivation be traceable to the defendant’s conduct and redressable by the court. In AFL v. Greer the D.C. Circuit applied these principles and rejected AFL’s informational-injury theories. AFL argued that OSC had denied it two informational benefits: production of DOJ records that OSC’s investigation would have spurred, and the procedural notices OSC would have given if it had investigated and followed § 1214. The court found both rationales defective.

First, the causal chain between OSC’s declination and DOJ’s nonproduction was too speculative. Courts will not accept a chain of contingent events involving independent actors—OSC might investigate, then might seek corrective action, MSPB might sanction, DOJ might produce—particularly where the actions of these other actors are discretionary and not before the court. The D.C. Circuit relied on recent standing precedents (including FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine) to stress that redressability cannot rest on speculation about independent decisions.

Second, the court held AFL had no statutory entitlement to the procedural notices it sought. Even if § 1216 requires OSC to investigate, OSC need not apply § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 inquiries; Carson allowed OSC procedural discretion. Because AFL did not allege that § 1216 itself created an entitlement to the specific information it sought, it lacked an Akins-type informational injury.

Similar dynamics recur in FEC cases. Plaintiffs often sue the FEC for alleged nonenforcement and attempt to frame their injury as the absence of required disclosures. Akins-style standing is available if the statute unambiguously mandates disclosure and the defendant’s noncompliance causes the deprivation. But if the alleged injury depends on a chain of discretionary choices—an enforcement action that the FEC might have brought, followed by agency or private compliance—standing falters.

Thus, informational standing can be a route into court, but it demands a clear statutory entitlement and a plausible causal and redressability link to the defendant’s action or inaction.

## Practical Takeaways and Strategic Considerations

AFL v. Greer illustrates the practical difficulties of litigating to compel an oversight agency to investigate or discipline another executive branch component or employee. Some practical—and doctrinal—rules to keep in mind:

- Start with standing. Courts frequently dispose of these suits on Article III grounds. To overcome standing hurdles, plaintiffs should identify a concrete, particularized injury caused by the defendant and likely redressed by a favorable ruling. Procedural-right theories can relax imminence and redressability somewhat, but plaintiffs still must tie the procedural right to a concrete interest that is affected.

- If relying on a statutory “shall,” analyze the provision’s context: Is the duty discrete and judicially manageable? Or is it embedded in a broader enforcement scheme that leaves weighty discretionary judgments to the agency? Absent clear, specific standards, “shall” language frequently will not yield judicially enforceable duties.

- Mandamus and APA § 706(1) are narrow tools for compelling agency action. Mandamus demands a clear, non-discretionary duty; § 706(1) requires a discrete statutory mandate. Both doctrines are ineffective where compliance would require courts to manage or second-guess prosecutorial priorities.

- Consider informational standing where Congress has created a specific entitlement to information. Akins remains a viable route when the deprivation is concrete and the statutory right to information is clear. But do not rely on long causal chains that involve independent actors and discretionary choices, because redressability collapses under such contingency.

- Expect courts to favor remand and reconsideration over intrusive remedies. When a court perceives legal error, a typical remedy is remand for the agency’s reconsideration rather than an order that compels particular investigatory steps.

- Keep separation-of-powers concerns in mind. Courts will avoid remedies and doctrines that unmoor the judiciary from its proper role by commanding the executive’s policy and enforcement judgments.

Conclusion

AFL v. Greer is not surprising in its outcome. Even when a statute appears to mandate an agency investigation, litigants face a trenchant combination of doctrines—Heckler’s presumption of unreviewability, the careful reading of “shall,” the limits of mandamus and § 706(1) relief, separation-of-powers constraints, and strict Article III standing requirements—that jointly limit courts’ ability to compel investigations. The most promising route for plaintiffs is to identify a narrow statutory entitlement to information or to show a specifically mandated, non-discretionary duty with manageable standards. Absent that, courts will usually leave the enforcement or disciplinary judgment within the executive branch.
