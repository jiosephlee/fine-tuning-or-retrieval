Chapter 6: # The Road to the D.C. Circuit: Facts and Procedural History of AFL v. Greer

This chapter traces the factual and procedural path that brought America First Legal Foundation’s (AFL) challenge to the D.C. Circuit. The dispute arises at the intersection of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and the investigatory authority of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC). The issues are fact-bound and statutory, but the litigation trajectory reflects recurring themes in administrative and constitutional law: the scope of agency investigatory powers, the relationship between parallel administrative and judicial remedies, and the limits that Article III standing imposes on judicial review of procedural complaints. We first set out AFL’s FOIA request and the policy it alleged; then describe the parallel FOIA litigation and ongoing productions; recount AFL’s petition to OSC and OSC’s statutory response; recount the district court’s hybrid ruling and remand; and finally narrate OSC’s renewed refusal, the entry of final judgment, and how the court of appeals came to resolve standing on its own motion.

## AFL’s FOIA request to DOJ and the alleged ‘consent to search emails’ policy

AFL’s underlying public-records request to the Department of Justice (DOJ) focused on emails bearing on judicial recusals. Specifically, AFL requested communications between an outside attorney and the Civil Division of DOJ that AFL believed would illuminate decisionmaking relevant to recusal issues. DOJ’s initial response to that FOIA request is the fulcrum of AFL’s complaint: according to AFL, the Civil Division refused to conduct searches of employees’ emails unless the individual employee consented to that search. AFL characterized that response not as a one-off refusal but as evidence of a broader DOJ practice or policy—namely, a policy of not searching employee email accounts without the employees’ consent.

That allegation implicates a discrete doctrinal point under FOIA. FOIA obligates agencies to conduct reasonable searches for records responsive to a request; when records are held in employee email accounts, the agency’s obligation typically extends to reasonable efforts to locate them. The question whether an agency may condition searches on individual employee consent is thus legally significant: if an agency legitimately refuses to search such accounts, requesters may be deprived of information that FOIA is designed to make public. AFL’s framing of DOJ’s reply as a systemic policy rather than a mere ad hoc response is why AFL sought both judicial relief under FOIA and administrative enforcement through OSC.

## Parallel FOIA litigation and ongoing record production

Almost at once, AFL pursued the alternative judicial path FOIA ordinarily provides: it filed a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ to compel production of the requested records. That parallel litigation matters because FOIA contains its own remedial mechanism and makes OSC’s involvement contingent in certain situations. Under FOIA’s fee-and-judgment provision, see 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(E)–(F), certain judicial remedies (including a court’s finding of arbitrary or capricious withholding, plus an award of attorneys’ fees) can trigger a mandatory OSC inquiry into the conduct of the individual government employees who were “primarily responsible” for the withholding. The involvement of the courts, awards of fees, and an explicit judicial finding about arbitrary withholding thus operate as a kind of statutory trigger for OSC activity under FOIA’s separate enforcement provision.

Factually, DOJ did begin to produce records responsive to AFL’s FOIA request; production was ongoing at the time of the OSC complaint and the district-court proceedings. Importantly, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion notes that the sufficiency of DOJ’s production was not the issue presented on appeal—AFL’s challenge instead concerned whether OSC had an independent duty to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy under the Civil Service Reform Act provisions that create OSC’s investigatory powers. But the existence of parallel FOIA litigation and continuing agency production influenced the parties’ positions: OSC relied on FOIA’s own enforcement provision to justify declining to investigate, and AFL emphasized that judicial remedies had not yet been obtained and that OSC’s investigatory authority under § 1216(a)(3) should not depend on FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates.

## AFL’s petition to OSC invoking § 1216(a)(3) and OSC’s reliance on § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates

AFL filed a petition with OSC alleging that DOJ’s asserted practice of declining to search employee emails without consent constituted an “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552” of Title 5—i.e., a FOIA violation. AFL invoked 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), a provision enacted as part of the Civil Service Reform Act that instructs OSC to “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” Section 1216(c) further authorizes OSC to investigate alleged FOIA violations and “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Read in isolation, these provisions appear to impose a mandatory investigatory duty on OSC when it receives allegations that an agency has committed an arbitrary FOIA withholding.

OSC declined to open an investigation. Its stated reason is the heart of the statutory dispute in this case: OSC read FOIA’s separate enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), as imposing three prerequisites before OSC must (or may) initiate disciplinary or corrective proceedings under FOIA: (1) a court must order production of unlawfully withheld records; (2) the court must award attorneys’ fees against the agency; and (3) the court must specifically question whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.” OSC concluded that these three judicial predicates were not satisfied in AFL’s case and therefore that it lacked authority under FOIA to open the kind of enforcement action FOIA contemplates.

AFL rejected OSC’s reading. It argued that § 1216 imposes an independent, mandatory duty to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings even when the FOIA-specific prerequisites in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) have not been met. The statutory text is susceptible to both readings: the “shall … conduct an investigation” language of § 1216(a)(3) suggests a nondiscretionary duty, while § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) looks like an explicit FOIA trigger for OSC’s disciplinary powers in cases where the courts have already acted. The statutory tension between the Civil Service Reform Act’s grant of investigatory power and FOIA’s own enforcement clause thus produced a straight statutory-interpretation dispute that OSC resolved in favor of a narrower, FOIA-contingent role.

## District court’s conclusions: legal error on § 1216 scope; discretion under § 1216(c); remand without ordering investigation

When AFL sued OSC in the District of Columbia, the district court confronted a mixed question of statutory interpretation and judicial review of agency action. The court concluded—contrary to OSC’s position—that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC’s authority under § 1216. In other words, the district court held that OSC was legally mistaken to read FOIA’s three-circumstance trigger as exclusive. The district court viewed § 1216’s “shall … conduct an investigation” language as establishing at least some independent duty to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholding, regardless of whether a FOIA lawsuit had produced the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates.

Yet the district court did not simply order OSC to open an investigation. It recognized another dimension of OSC’s statutory regime: § 1216(c) authorizes OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The court read that cross-reference as preserving OSC’s discretion over whether and how to apply § 1214’s procedural and remedial apparatus when the initial statutory hook derives from § 1216. The court saw OSC’s internal choice about whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary actions—and how to structure any investigation—as entrusted to agency competence and discretion. Because of that discretion, the district court declined to compel a particular remedial course. Instead it remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration in light of the court’s legal interpretation of § 1216.

Two points about the district court’s approach deserve emphasis. First, the court performed the familiar judicial task of correcting a legal error: it set aside OSC’s statutory interpretation that would have tied § 1216 to FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers. Second, the court exercised remedial restraint: it declined to intrude into the agency’s prosecutorial and enforcement judgment by directing OSC to take a particular enforcement step, instead remanding for the agency to reconsider consistent with the court’s statutory ruling. That remedial posture is common in administrative-law litigation where courts find statutory misinterpretations but recognize the agency’s prosecutorial discretion and technical competence in deciding how to proceed.

## OSC’s renewed refusal; entry of final judgment; appeal

On remand, OSC again declined to investigate. The agency apparently considered the district court’s statutory ruling but adhered to a decision not to initiate an OSC investigation of DOJ’s FOIA practices. AFL then sought a final judgment in the district court. The district court entered final judgment dismissing AFL’s remaining claims for failure to state a claim. The dispositive dismissal at the district-court level concluded the case there, and AFL appealed to the D.C. Circuit.

The appellate record reflects that the district court’s disposition left intact two unresolved statutory tensions: whether § 1216 imposes a truly mandatory duty to investigate FOIA complaints in every instance, and whether OSC must—when it does investigate under § 1216—apply the procedural notice and other provisions of § 1214. The district court had answered the first question against OSC but declined to second-guess the agency’s exercise of enforcement discretion. When OSC again declined to act, AFL renewed its attack in the D.C. Circuit.

## De novo standard of review and the court’s sua sponte standing inquiry and supplemental briefing

On appeal, the D.C. Circuit announced that its review of the district court’s legal determinations would be de novo. That is the standard for pure questions of statutory interpretation and for many legal determinations about agency action: appellate courts give no deference to a district court’s statutory interpretations and instead resolve the governing legal questions anew. But before reaching the merits, the court raised a threshold jurisdictional issue sua sponte—whether AFL had Article III standing. Standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite that federal courts must address even if the parties omit it. As the D.C. Circuit explained, Article III confines courts to “Cases” and “Controversies,” and any litigant must show a concrete injury that is fairly traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by the relief sought. See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992).

The D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing on standing because neither party had initially pressed the point. That procedural step is significant: courts can raise standing independently because it bears on subject-matter jurisdiction; and appellate courts are especially exacting in distinguishing a procedural statutory right asserted in a vacuum from a cognizable Article III injury. The court recited the governing framework for cases involving asserted procedural injuries: where a plaintiff alleges that a statutory procedure afforded it was denied, courts may relax the strict requirements of imminence and redressability, but not the core elements of injury-in-fact and causation. See Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488 (2009); Center for Law & Educ. v. Department of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005). Under that line of authorities, AFL could proceed only if it showed (1) that a procedural right was violated, and (2) that the violation harmed a concrete, particularized interest that the statute at issue was designed to protect.

Two distinct standing theories framed AFL’s supplemental briefing and the D.C. Circuit’s analysis. First, AFL claimed that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused it a concrete informational injury because DOJ refused to produce the records AFL had requested under FOIA. Second, AFL argued that OSC’s failure to investigate deprived it of the information and notices that OSC would provide during a § 1216 investigation—i.e., that AFL had an informational entitlement to the fruits or at least the procedural outputs of an OSC inquiry.

The court treated each theory in turn and found both insufficient. For the first theory, the court stressed the causation and redressability problems. Any present withholding-of-information injury is traceable directly to DOJ’s decisions about whether and how to comply with FOIA, not to OSC’s decision not to investigate. More importantly, to show that OSC’s action was the but-for (or at least proximate) cause of the alleged deprivation would require a speculative chain: OSC might investigate; OSC might decide to seek corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) might impose sanctions; DOJ might, as a result, decide to produce the records. That chain depends on the independent choices of multiple actors (OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ), and courts generally cannot base standing on speculation about the uncabined choices of third parties. The court invoked the principle that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts,” citing recent Supreme Court authority for that proposition (among earlier cases, Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), underscores the non-reviewability of certain exercises of prosecutorial discretion). Because a favorable judgment directing OSC to reconsider would not “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s informational injury, the D.C. Circuit concluded AFL’s first theory failed.

The second theory—informational injury in the form of a right to receive OSC’s investigative notices and results—met a different defect: absence of entitlement. The Supreme Court and this circuit have recognized that a plaintiff can suffer an Article III injury when a statute entitles it to receive certain information and the government withholds that information. See FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998); Public Citizen v. U.S. Department of Justice, 491 U.S. 440 (1989). But § 1216, the D.C. Circuit explained, does not create a statutory entitlement to OSC’s investigative outputs. Even if § 1216 mandates an OSC investigation when allegations of FOIA withholding are presented, it does not compel OSC to provide to the complainant the same notices and periodic reports that § 1214 requires when investigating “prohibited personnel practices.” AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to apply § 1214’s procedural requirements in the § 1216 context is treated as discretionary in existing precedent—Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), being a leading example. Because AFL could point to no statute that unequivocally entitles it to particular investigatory information from OSC, the court found no basis for an informational injury-in-fact.

Synthesizing these holdings, the D.C. Circuit concluded that AFL failed to demonstrate a concrete injury traceable to OSC that would be redressed by a favorable ruling. Because standing is jurisdictional, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. In short, the court never reached the underlying merits of the statutory dispute about the full scope of § 1216 or the remedial consequences of a finding that DOJ had a policy of conditioning email searches on employee consent.

## Teaching points and doctrinal afterthoughts

Several doctrinal lessons flow from the factual and procedural story of AFL v. Greer. First, litigants who pursue parallel administrative complaints and judicial FOIA suits must care for sequencing and causation. OSC’s enforcement authority walks a statutory tightrope: § 1216 appears to require OSC attention to alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides an explicit, court-centered enforcement trigger in some circumstances. How those provisions interact is a pure statutory-interpretation question, but for Article III purposes it matters whether a denial of an administrative investigatory procedure is the proximate cause of an informational deprivation.

Second, standing doctrine imposes substantial barriers for plaintiffs who assert procedural or informational injuries. The Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit have clarified that violations of procedural statutory rights do not automatically confer Article III standing unless the plaintiff shows that the procedural deprivation has harmed a concrete, particularized interest. Summers v. Earth Island Institute and the D.C. Circuit’s own cases place the burden on plaintiffs to show both causation and redressability—in practice, often a hard task when the chain of causation depends on decisions by other agencies or on the exercise of agency prosecutorial discretion. Speculation about how an agency might exercise enforcement tools is typically insufficient.

Third, when statutes create investigatory powers rather than explicit individual entitlements to information, claimants who seek to convert those powers into judicially enforceable rights confront an additional hurdle: informational standing often depends on a statutory entitlement to the particular information sought. Absent such an entitlement, courts are reluctant to hold that a denial of investigatory process itself produces a concrete injury.

Finally, the procedural posture of the district court—correcting a legal error but remanding rather than ordering a specific remedy—illustrates judicial prudence in agency oversight. Courts will sometimes recognize statutory misinterpretation but nevertheless leave agencies room to decide how to proceed, especially where prosecutorial discretion is implicated. But that prudence does not immunize the issue from appellate scrutiny when jurisdictional defects are present; appellate courts must ensure litigants have Article III standing before resolving statutory questions.

For students preparing to apply these principles to new fact patterns, two practical rules of thumb are useful. If a plaintiff’s claimed injury depends on the independent actions of other agencies or tribunals, ask whether the chain from the defendant’s conduct to the asserted harm is speculative. If a plaintiff claims an informational injury rooted in an administrative procedure, search the statute (and regulations) carefully for any express entitlement to particular notices, reports, or information; absent a clear statutory entitlement, courts are likely to find no informational injury sufficient to invoke Article III jurisdiction.
