Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

Chapter 3: # Procedural Injury: Relaxed, Not Eliminated Requirements

When a plaintiff alleges that an agency violated a statutory procedural requirement, courts apply a distinctive standing analysis. They may relax some elements of the usual Article III standing rules—most notably imminence and redressability—but they do not eliminate the need for a concrete, particularized injury and a causal connection between the defendant’s conduct and that injury. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework that governs claims of procedural injury, and shows how that framework applies to statutory investigatory or notice requirements by using recent caselaw as illustrations. The discussion uses the D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (2025) as a focal example of how courts apply the relaxed-but-real test, and draws on leading Supreme Court and circuit precedents—Summers v. Earth Island Institute, Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, Massachusetts v. EPA, Akins, Public Citizen, Center for Law & Education, Florida Audubon, and others—to set the doctrinal boundaries.

## What counts as a procedural right and why it matters

A “procedural right” in the standing context is a statutory or constitutional entitlement to a procedure that is designed, at least in part, to protect some concrete interest of the claimant. The paradigmatic examples are statutory provisions that require notice, an opportunity to comment, a discrete investigatory step, or disclosure of information. Whether a right is called “procedural” rather than “substantive” matters because courts treating an alleged procedural violation often “relax—while not wholly eliminating—issues of imminence and redressability.” But this relaxation does not mean that any statutory procedure gives rise to standing in the absence of a real, particularized harm. The analytical task is therefore twofold: identify the procedural entitlement, and identify the concrete interest that the procedure was enacted to protect.

A given statute can sometimes supply both kinds of claims. A procedural provision can be “designed to protect” an interest by creating a guaranteed opportunity to obtain information, to participate, or to trigger enforcement action that would protect a concrete interest. For example, FOIA and other disclosure statutes often create statutory entitlements to information; when a statute creates such an entitlement, denial of that information can itself be a concrete injury (informational standing). Other procedural provisions—notice-and-comment, investigatory duties, internal notice requirements—may be designed to protect future substantive interests (e.g., the right to rely on an agency’s fully informed decisionmaking or the chance to obtain relief from misconduct). The critical point is that the plaintiff must link the procedural requirement to a threatened or actual concrete interest that the procedure is meant to protect.

The statutory text and Congress’s evident purpose are central to this inquiry. A provision that merely authorizes discretionary agency action—“may” rather than “shall,” or that explicitly commits enforcement to agency judgment—less commonly creates an individual entitlement sufficient for standing. Conversely, text that mandates disclosure or a specific investigatory duty, or that ties procedural steps to enforcement mechanisms, is more likely to create an entitlement on which informational standing can rest. But statutory language is only the starting point; courts also examine whether the plaintiff is within the class of persons the provision was meant to benefit, and whether the denial of the procedure has in fact affected a concrete interest.

## Summers v. Earth Island Institute: no procedural right in vacuo

Summers v. Earth Island Institute (2009) is the touchstone for the proposition that a procedural right, standing alone, is not enough to establish Article III standing. There the plaintiffs challenged a Bureau of Land Management rule that exempted certain small fires from required environmental review. The Supreme Court held that the plaintiffs’ alleged procedural right to notice and comment could not confer standing where the plaintiffs could not show a concrete, imminent injury that the required procedure was designed to prevent or mitigate. The Court explained that a deprivation of a procedural right “without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation” is insufficient to constitute an injury in fact.

Summers teaches that courts must look beyond the bare procedural entitlement to the substantive interest the procedure was meant to protect and to the nexus between the procedural violation and the plaintiff’s own injury. If a procedural rule simply provides abstract bureaucratic protection that does not meaningfully affect the plaintiff’s concrete interests, then the plaintiff cannot bootstrap standing from the procedural right alone. Summers thus places a substantive floor under any relaxed procedural-injury doctrine: the plaintiff must show a real, particularized harm that the procedure was designed to prevent.

The Summers rule also has practical consequences for plaintiffs seeking to challenge agency behavior by attacking only procedural steps. Plaintiffs must plead and ultimately prove that the procedural violation had some tangible effect on the plaintiff’s interests or that the denial of the procedure made a concrete future harm substantially more likely. Where the connection between the procedural violation and the plaintiff’s injury is speculative, Summers will bar Article III jurisdiction.

## Center for Law & Education; Florida Audubon (en banc): relaxed but real requirements

While Summers imposes a limit, the D.C. Circuit has long recognized a more forgiving posture toward procedural injuries when the statutory procedure was enacted to protect a plaintiff’s threatened concrete interest. In Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education (2005), the court explained that when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a statutory procedural right “designed to protect” a threatened concrete interest, the courts should relax, though not eliminate, the requirements of imminence and redressability. Similarly, the en banc D.C. Circuit in Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen emphasized that procedural claims may proceed where the plaintiff can connect the procedural violation to a concrete interest that is itself threatened, but that plaintiffs must still demonstrate injury in fact and causation.

These decisions set out a calibrated test. First, identify whether the plaintiff alleges the violation of a procedural right created by statute. Second, ask whether that procedural right was designed to protect a concrete interest of the plaintiff (not merely a collective or ideological concern). Third, require that the violation result in a harm to that concrete interest. If the right was designed to protect a plaintiff’s concrete interest and the procedural violation resulted in injury to that interest, courts will not demand the same degree of immediacy or certainty of redress that they require for ordinary substantive injuries. But courts will still scrutinize whether the alleged procedural failure caused the concrete harm and whether a favorable ruling would likely redress that harm.

The relaxed approach recognizes the democratic function of many procedural rights: they ensure informed decisionmaking, transparency, and accountability. Where the procedure is expressly tied to an interest—say, a statute requiring notice to affected property owners or a disclosure statute aimed at ensuring informed voting—the courts will be more accepting that the denial of the procedure is injurious. But the relaxed standard is not a free pass; plaintiffs must still anchor their claim to a particularized, concrete interest and show a plausible causal and redressable link.

## Massachusetts v. EPA’s special solicitude (limitations and context)

Massachusetts v. EPA (2007) introduced the idea of “special solicitude” for certain plaintiffs—particularly States—when assessing standing in environmental cases. The Supreme Court treated the State as having a quasi-sovereign interest in protecting its territory and residents from harm, and concluded that Massachusetts had standing to challenge EPA’s refusal to regulate greenhouse gases. That case is often cited for the proposition that certain plaintiffs, by virtue of their status and the types of interests they protect, may be afforded greater leeway in demonstrating a concrete and particularized injury.

But Massachusetts’s special solicitude is context-limited. It applies most naturally to sovereign or quasi-sovereign plaintiffs asserting injuries to state lands, resources, or sovereign interests. The decision does not suggest that private litigants can receive a similar, broad relaxation of Article III requirements merely by asserting generalized public interests or procedural claims. Subsequent cases have therefore read Massachusetts narrowly: it permits less stringent standing proof for states acting in a quasi-sovereign capacity, but it does not authorize wholesale abandonment of the injury-in-fact or causation requirements in non-state cases.

Consequently, when evaluating procedural-injury claims by private parties or organizations, courts will not extend Massachusetts’s “special solicitude.” Private plaintiffs must still show that the procedural violation harmed a particularized concrete interest. For states, by contrast, a combination of quasi-sovereign harms and procedural deprivations may enable standing in situations where private parties would fail. In short, Massachusetts matters, but its domain is limited; it informs but does not nullify the Summers and Center for Law & Education principles.

## Identifying the threatened concrete interest the procedure is designed to protect

The central analytical move in any procedural-injury case is to identify the specific substantive interest that the procedural requirement is meant to protect. This is necessary both to satisfy Summers’s bar on “procedural rights in vacuo” and to apply the D.C. Circuit’s relaxed-but-real test. Courts ask not merely whether the plaintiff cares about the procedure, but whether the statute’s procedural protection is plausibly tied to a concrete interest of the plaintiff in the real world.

Identifying the protected interest begins with statutory text and legislative purpose. Where the statute explicitly connects procedural protections to enforcement mechanisms or to the rights of particular individuals (for example, a requirement to notify a permit applicant of a pending adverse action), the link is straightforward. Where the statute's language is general or discretionary, the court must inquire whether Congress intended the procedure to protect a specific class of beneficiaries and whether the plaintiff is a member of that class.

Beyond text, courts examine the functional effect of the procedure. For example, notice-and-comment rules are designed to give affected parties an opportunity to influence agency decisions; a plaintiff who can show that the loss of a comment opportunity was likely to affect the agency decision in a way that harms the plaintiff has a plausible concrete injury. Disclosure statutes are designed to give the public and regulated parties information material to their interests; denial of guaranteed information can therefore create an informational injury if the statute confers an entitlement. Conversely, where a procedure is intended mainly as an internal check on agency competence or as a discretionary investigatory option to be exercised in the agency’s judgment, its denial is less likely to yield a cognizable injury to an outside party.

The case law offers recurrent examples. In Public Citizen v. Department of Justice and in Akins, plaintiffs had statutory or quasi-statutory rights to information, and the denial of that information constituted a recognizable injury. In other cases, such as Summers itself, the nexus between the procedure and any concrete interest was too attenuated to support standing. The identifying task is fact-sensitive: plaintiffs should plead—and courts should require—specific facts explaining how the procedural lapse affects the plaintiff’s concrete interest, not only general assertions of public right or abstract oversight interest.

## Applying procedural-injury doctrine to investigative and notice provisions

Investigative and notice provisions present recurring standing questions because they often provide procedures aimed at identifying misconduct or keeping affected persons informed—functions that can plausibly protect concrete interests. But whether a denied investigatory or notice procedure gives rise to Article III standing depends on (1) whether the statute creates a private entitlement to certain information or process, (2) whether the plaintiff belongs to the class the procedure protects, and (3) whether the denial has a non-speculative causal connection to a concrete injury that a judicial remedy can redress.

The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (2025) illustrates the mechanics. AFL alleged that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate an alleged FOIA policy deprived AFL both of the records it sought from the Department of Justice and of the notices and information that would attend an OSC investigation under a different statutory provision. The court examined both theories in light of Summers and related precedents and concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing.

First, the court rejected the contention that OSC’s refusal to investigate was the but-for cause of DOJ’s failure to produce the FOIA records. That causal theory required a speculative chain: if OSC had investigated, it might have pursued corrective or disciplinary proceedings; the Merit Systems Protection Board might have sanctioned DOJ employees; DOJ then might have produced the records. The court emphasized that causation cannot rest on speculation about the independent choices of nonparty actors (the MSPB and DOJ) or about how an enforcement agency might exercise discretion. This reasoning draws directly on the principle that standing must avoid attenuated causal chains that depend on future discretionary acts by independent actors, as reflected in cases such as Lujan and more recent decisions emphasizing the limits on reliance on third parties’ unfettered choices.

Second, the court rejected AFL’s claim of an informational injury in the OSC-investigation process itself because the statute governing OSC’s FOIA investigations (5 U.S.C. § 1216) did not, by its terms, create an entitlement to receive the notice requirements located elsewhere in the statute (section 1214). AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to apply section 1214’s procedural rules in section 1216 investigations is discretionary; that concession was fatal to its informational-standing claim because a plaintiff’s informational injury must generally be a denial of information the statute entitled it to receive. The court thus applied the Akins/Public Citizen line of law: informational standing requires either a statutory entitlement to information or a sufficient nexus between the procedure denied and the plaintiff’s own concrete informational interest.

The America First Legal decision also illustrates the interaction of investigatory discretion and standing. When a statute does not create a clear entitlement to a particular investigatory result or informational product, a court’s order compelling an investigation may be unlikely to produce the particular relief the plaintiff seeks. Where relief depends on how an agency exercises subsequent enforcement discretion—or on decisions by distinct agencies—the court will treat the redressability question with skepticism and may find standing lacking.

Two further doctrinal limits are important. First, where the statute expressly commits the enforcement decision to agency discretion, courts are reluctant to treat a plaintiff as having a private right to a particular enforcement outcome (Heckler v. Chaney). Even when the underlying statute requires an investigation, courts will not assume that an aggrieved plaintiff can trade the court’s imprimatur for a guaranteed chain of causation leading to production of documents or imposition of sanctions. Second, even where an investigation would generate useful information, courts require that the plaintiff show an entitlement to that information or a plausible theory that receiving the information would alter the plaintiff’s substantive position in a way the court can remedy.

## Practical application: pleading and proving procedural injury

For practitioners and students, the doctrinal rules yield concrete pleading and proof strategies. At the pleading stage, a plaintiff alleging a procedural deprivation should do three things: first, plead the precise statutory procedure at issue and explain how that procedure is designed to protect a specific class of persons or a particular interest; second, allege facts showing that the plaintiff is within the intended class and that the denial of the procedure concretely injured the plaintiff’s interest; and third, show that a favorable judicial decision would likely redress the injury without relying on multiple speculative future acts by nonparties.

A successful informational-standing claim typically requires either an allegation that the statute creates an entitlement to a particular disclosure or a fact-specific showing that denial of the information frustrated the plaintiff’s ability to protect a particular, concrete interest. Where the statutory language is permissive or where internal agency procedures are discretionary, plaintiffs should plead particularized injury—lost opportunity to influence agency action that directly governs plaintiffs’ legal rights, missed deadlines, economic harm that flowed from lack of notice, or specific reputational or regulatory consequences that the procedure was meant to prevent.

When the alleged injury hinges on subsequent enforcement actions by other agencies or bodies, plaintiffs face an uphill causation and redressability burden. Courts will require more than hope that an investigation would trigger remedial steps. The America First Legal decision shows that speculative causal chains—investigation leads to enforcement leads to another agency’s sanction leads to production of documents—are insufficient. Plaintiffs should instead demonstrate either (a) a direct linkage between the procedural right and an enforceable entitlement to relief (for example, a statutory mechanism that compels production or imposes automatic consequences), or (b) a plausible and relatively direct causal route by which a judicially compelled procedure would cause a remedial action likely to redress the plaintiff.

Finally, mindful of Summers and its progeny, litigants should not treat procedural rights as stand-alone claims divorced from substantive stakes. Courts will expect plaintiffs to say concretely what they lost, how the lost procedure caused that loss, and why a judicial remedy ordering the procedure would likely redress the harm. Absent that connected showing, courts will dismiss for lack of Article III jurisdiction.

## Conclusion

Procedural-injury standing occupies a middle ground. Courts recognize that procedures—notice, investigation, disclosure—serve important democratic and individual-protective functions, and they will relax some standing requirements where a plaintiff can show that a procedural violation threatens or injures a concrete interest that the statute was designed to protect. But Summers and subsequent decisions insist that the relaxation is limited: plaintiffs still must identify a concrete, particularized injury and demonstrate a causal link between the procedural failure and that injury and a realistic prospect that a favorable ruling would redress the harm. The America First Legal Foundation decision demonstrates how these principles operate in practice: an alleged statutory duty to investigate or disclose does not confer standing unless the plaintiff can tie the denial to a non-speculative injury and show that judicial relief would likely redress it. For students and practitioners, the lesson is emphatic: procedural rights matter, but they cannot be invoked in the abstract; the courts require a concrete, fact-specific demonstration that the denied procedure actually harmed the plaintiff in a way a judicial order can remedy.
