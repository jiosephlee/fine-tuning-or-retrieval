Chapter 5: # Independent Actors, Speculative Chains, and Redressability Limits

This chapter examines a familiar but fact-sensitive set of standing problems that arise when a plaintiff’s claimed injury depends on the decisions of independent actors or on a sequence of discretionary choices by multiple public bodies. The controlling question is whether the plaintiff has alleged an injury that is (1) fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct and (2) likely to be redressed by the relief sought. When the causal route from the defendant’s act to the plaintiff’s injury runs through the hands of parties not before the court—or through agency choices that are committed to enforcement discretion—the traceability and redressability inquiries often collapse under the doctrine the Supreme Court and this Circuit have developed. America First Legal Foundation v. Greer, decided by the D.C. Circuit in 2025, furnishes a compact, contemporary illustration: AFL sued the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) for declining to investigate an alleged FOIA policy at the Department of Justice. The court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing because its claimed informational injuries rested on a speculative chain of events—an OSC investigation would (maybe) lead to MSPB proceedings, which (maybe) would lead DOJ to disclose records—rather than on an injury fairly traceable to OSC or likely to be redressed by a judicial order against OSC.

The doctrinal material in this chapter is organized by topic: the independent-actor problem and the general prohibition on standing based on highly speculative causal chains; recent applications of that principle in FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Hecate Energy; the related limit that judgments bind the parties before the court rather than nonparties who might be persuaded by a court’s reasoning (Haaland v. Brackeen); the precedential roots in Clapper v. Amnesty International and Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights; the distinct problem presented by agency enforcement discretion (Heckler v. Chaney); and practical rules for litigators confronting multi-agency remedial paths like OSC → MSPB → agency compliance.

## Independent-actor doctrine and speculation concerns

The core of the independent-actor doctrine is straightforward: an injury cannot be fairly traced to a defendant when the chain of causation depends on the voluntary actions of one or more independent actors not before the court. That is so because standing requires a causal connection between the assertedly unlawful conduct of the defendant and the plaintiff’s injury; speculative or attenuated links supplied by the discretionary choices of third parties break that causal link.

The Supreme Court summarized this prudential and constitutional limit in multiple venues. In situations where the complained-of action is not the proximate cause of the asserted harm, the Court requires plaintiffs to show more than a mere possibility that a favorable ruling will lead third parties to act in ways that redress the injury. The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly applied this principle: when the causal route from the defendant’s conduct to the plaintiff’s injury requires “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts,” the plaintiff lacks the traceability necessary to establish Article III standing.

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer exemplifies these points. AFL alleged that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ’s FOIA practices injured AFL by (1) leaving DOJ’s FOIA production incomplete and (2) depriving AFL of notices and other information an OSC investigation might yield. The court recognized that DOJ’s decision not to produce records is the immediate cause of any FOIA-related informational injury. To link that injury to OSC, AFL needed to show a realistic, non-speculative sequence: an OSC investigation would lead OSC to seek corrective or disciplinary action (under 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214–1215); the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would impose sanctions or order corrective relief; and DOJ would then comply and disclose the records. The court found this chain too speculative: it required successive discretionary choices by two independent actors (OSC and MSPB), followed by unilateral voluntary action by DOJ. Because standing “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts,” the causal link failed.

When applying the independent-actor rule, focus on two questions: (1) Would the third party’s action be compelled by law or purely discretionary? (2) Would the court’s judgment bind the third party or only the defendant? If the third party’s action is mandatory under law or directly ordered by the court’s judgment, the chain is less speculative and traceability may be plausible. By contrast, if the third party has unfettered discretion, courts will require a much more direct nexus between the defendant’s conduct and the injury.

## FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine: redressability and independent choices

The Supreme Court’s decision in FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine (2024) is an important contemporary application of the independent-actor/redressability concern. Although the case concerned FDA regulation of mifepristone, the Court articulated a general rule about causal speculation: when redressability depends on “the unfettered choices” of nonparty actors, that dependence undermines standing. The opinion emphasized that plaintiffs must do more than articulate a chain of contingencies; they must show that a favorable ruling would make the relief “substantially likely” or otherwise “very likely” to occur, not merely possible.

The D.C. Circuit invoked the Alliance principle in America First Legal Foundation, relying on the Court’s admonition that causation “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” That passage was central to the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that AFL’s claim of informational injury was not redressable: even if OSC had been ordered to investigate, the ensuing remedial steps that would obligate DOJ to produce records remained the product of contingent choices by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ.

Alliance underscores that courts will examine the nature of the intervening choices. If a favorable judgment would change only the legal landscape in ways that might persuade an independent agency or tribunal to act, that possibility is usually insufficient. By contrast, if the judgment would alter legal obligations in a way that directly compels third-party action—either because the third party is legally bound by the decision or because the decision would bind an entity that can command the third party’s compliance—redressability is more likely to be found.

For practitioners, Alliance is a reminder that pleading and proof on redressability must attend to the quality of the intervening links. Allegations that a judicial ruling “would persuade” another institution to act are weak; allegations that the ruling would lawfully require that institution to act—or would directly subject it to judicial oversight—are stronger.

## Haaland v. Brackeen: judgments bind parties, not nonparties persuaded by reasoning

Haaland v. Brackeen (2023) contributes a distinct but related principle: when determining redressability, courts consider whether a favorable judgment would bind the relevant actors, not whether it might “persuade” nonparties to act. The Court made clear that the measure of redressability is the court’s ability to alter the legal relations of the parties before it, not its ability to emit a persuasive opinion that induces third parties to change behavior.

The D.C. Circuit relied on that language in AFL: the opinion quoted the Haaland passage to explain why AFL’s attempt to rely on the possibility that OSC’s mandated investigation might “persuade” MSPB or DOJ to act could not satisfy redressability. An order against OSC would bind OSC, but it would not bind DOJ or the MSPB; whether those nonparties would respond by producing documents or imposing sanctions would remain a matter of sovereign discretion beyond the court’s control.

The Haaland principle is particularly important where plaintiffs attempt to vindicate rights by compelling courts to issue purely declaratory or persuasive rulings intended to induce action by nonparties. Such litigants must show that the relief would have some binding effect on the actor that actually controls the injury. If not, the claim faces dismissal for lack of redressability.

## Clapper v. Amnesty International and Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights: causal attenuation

The modern jurisprudence of speculative injury and attenuated causation finds deep roots in Clapper v. Amnesty International USA (2013) and Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization (1976). Both decisions teach that the Article III injury-in-fact requirement cannot be satisfied by conjectural fears or by claims that rest on chains of speculative events.

In Clapper, the Supreme Court rejected standing for plaintiffs who feared surveillance targetting because their claimed future injury relied on a series of independent, discretionary decisions by government actors and foreign entities. The Court underscored that a chain of possibilities, each contingent on future events that may never occur, cannot substitute for a concrete and imminent injury. Similarly, in Simon the Court made clear that a plaintiff cannot manufacture standing through speculative future benefits that depend on governmental actions not yet taken.

America First Legal Foundation’s standing problems echo these precedents: AFL’s alleged informational injury arising from DOJ’s refusal to search employee emails would have required a chain of events—OSC investigation → OSC enforcement action → MSPB action → DOJ compliance—each link contingent on independent judgments. The D.C. Circuit applied the Clapper/Simon logic to conclude that the causal and redressability elements were not met.

These cases teach a practical rule: if a plaintiff seeks to vindicate a present injury by pointing at future, uncertain events, she must show that those future events are not mere possibilities but are substantially likely or legally compelled. Absent that showing, courts will treat the causal story as an unlawful fiction crafted to create standing where none exists.

## Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC: ‘very likely spur’ standard in multi-step causal chains

The D.C. Circuit’s recent decision in Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC (2025) offers a formulation that bridges the traceability and redressability inquiries for multi-step chains. In Hecate the court explained that when redress depends on a sequence of steps, the plaintiff must show that a favorable decision would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury. That standard demands a degree of likelihood higher than mere possibility but lower than mathematical certainty.

In America First Legal Foundation, the D.C. Circuit applied the “very likely spur” formulation and found it unmet. AFL’s narrative required OSC to initiate and pursue enforcement, the MSPB to impose a corrective or disciplinary remedy, and DOJ subsequently to produce documents. Each step involved discretionary judgments; AFL did not (and could not) plausibly allege that those steps were “very likely” to occur in the event of a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate. Thus, under Hecate’s articulation, the redressability prong failed.

The practical import of Hecate is twofold. First, Hecate recognizes that courts should not be hyper-technical in rejecting multi-step causal stories where the sequence is predictable and likely—for example, when a statutory procedure requires certain downstream consequences. Second, it preserves constitutional limits by insisting that plaintiffs show a meaningful probability of redress, not a speculative hope. Litigants seeking to rely on multi-stage causal narratives should therefore plead facts demonstrating how a court’s order would realistically trigger the subsequent steps (e.g., because a statute creates a mandatory enforcement path or because the downstream actor’s discretion is meaningfully constrained).

## Heckler v. Chaney: enforcement discretion as a barrier to judicial compulsion

Heckler v. Chaney (1985) addresses a different but related barrier to standing and judicial relief: the presumption that agency decisions not to enforce are committed to agency discretion and thus generally not subject to judicial review. The doctrine is not framed as a standing rule per se, but the practical consequence is the same: when relief would require a court to command an agency to prosecute, enforce, or otherwise exercise its statutory powers, Heckler warns that courts are ordinarily ill-suited and often lack statutory authority to intrude.

Heckler established that an agency’s decision to decline enforcement is presumptively unreviewable because it typically involves the exercise of broad discretion grounded in agency expertise and policy judgments. Courts will entertain review only where a statute provides meaningful standards to constrain agency action or where an agency’s refusal amounts to an abdication of a ministerial duty. Thus, when a plaintiff’s theory of redress depends on compelling an enforcement agency to act, the combined effect of Heckler and standing doctrine can be decisive: even if the causal link to the plaintiff’s injury were traceable, a plaintiff may still be unable to obtain relief because courts lack the power to force the agency’s hand.

The AFL case illustrates the Heckler problem indirectly. AFL sought to compel OSC to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). But even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, subsequent redress would depend on OSC’s choice to pursue corrective or disciplinary actions (and on MSPB’s further action). To the extent that OSC’s enforcement choices are discretionary, Heckler supports the idea that courts should hesitate to compel specific prosecutorial or enforcement responses. Moreover, even affirmative judicial orders against OSC do not bind the MSPB or DOJ. Heckler thus reinforces the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that AFL’s injury was not likely to be redressed by judicial relief aimed at OSC.

Two clarifications are worth emphasizing. First, Heckler does not render all agency inaction immune from review; where statutory language supplies clear standards for enforcement or where an agency refuses to exercise a nondiscretionary duty, courts may intervene. Second, Heckler concerns reviewability and relief, distinct from Article III standing; nonetheless, the doctrines interact because a plaintiff who cannot obtain meaningful relief against the actor whose conduct causes the injury will often fail redressability as well.

## Applying these principles to multi-agency remedial paths (OSC → MSPB → agency compliance)

The OSC → MSPB → agency compliance sequence presented in America First Legal Foundation is a useful case-study for applying the doctrines described above. The plaintiff sought to compel OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholding and argued that such an investigation would produce the information it sought. The court rejected that argument for traceability and redressability reasons: DOJ’s FOIA decisions, not OSC’s investigatory posture, were the immediate cause of any informational deprivation; the causal chain to connect OSC’s inaction to AFL’s injury required a series of discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ; and the court’s prospective judgment would bind only OSC, not the other actors.

From a doctrinal standpoint, litigators should analyze similar multi-agency scenarios on three axes.

- Which actor actually controls the conduct that causes the injury? If the defendant is not the actor whose behavior produces the injury, the plaintiff must explain why relief against the defendant would nevertheless eliminate the injury. If that explanation depends on the discretionary acts of nonparty agencies, standing will be difficult to establish.

- Are the intervening actors’ responses legally compelled or discretionary? If downstream action is mandated by statute or the judicial order would bind the downstream actor, the chain is less speculative. If the intervening choices are discretionary (as when an enforcement agency may decline to prosecute or an adjudicative body like the MSPB retains remedial discretion), courts will require strong evidence that the favorable judgment would make downstream relief “very likely.”

- Would a judicial order actually change the legal relations of the parties who cause the injury? Haaland reminds us that persuasive authority is not the same as binding authority. A court’s ruling against one agency may alter the legal environment, but unless it imposes a duty on the agency that controls the injury, the judgment may not redress the plaintiff.

Applying these points to OSC→MSPB→agency compliance demonstrates why AFL’s theory fell short. Even assuming OSC had a legal duty under § 1216 to investigate, AFL did not plausibly show that an OSC investigation would predictably produce MSPB sanctions or compel DOJ disclosure. MSPB’s remedial decisions and DOJ’s FOIA choices involve independent exercise of discretion. The court emphasized that redressability turns on how judgments bind parties, not on how persuasive the court’s reasoning might be; that distinction proved fatal to AFL.

There are permutations in which a multi-step path will suffice. For example, suppose statute X requires Agency A to investigate and to refer proven violations to Agency B for enforcement, and Agency B must impose a specific remedial consequence upon Agency A’s referral. If a plaintiff sues Agency A for failing to investigate, a court order compelling Agency A to follow statute X may directly set in motion a series of non-discretionary steps that make redress virtually certain. Under those circumstances, the chain is not speculative, and traceability/redressability problems are less severe. The difference is that the statutory structure removes or constrains the independent discretion that otherwise produces attenuation.

Practical litigation strategies therefore include: (1) suing the actor who actually controls the contested behavior (e.g., suing DOJ directly for FOIA delays or denials rather than an agency that may investigate DOJ); (2) pleading facts that show downstream actions are mandatory or very likely (e.g., statutory referral or non-discretionary remedy rules); (3) seeking relief that directly binds the actor who controls the injury (court orders directed at the entity whose inaction is claimed to cause the harm); or (4) pressing statutory entitlements to information where the statute confers a direct informational right that an award of relief would vindicate (a route sometimes available in “informational standing” cases).

Conclusion

The interplay of independent-actor, causation, and redressability doctrines places significant limits on standing when a plaintiff’s injury depends on a speculative sequence of discretionary acts by nonparties or by multiple agencies. Clapper and Simon teach that courts will not entertain claims premised on conjectural future events; Haaland emphasizes that judicial relief binds the parties before the court, not nonparties whom the court might persuade; Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Hecate reinforce the need for a realistic showing that a favorable judgment will “very likely spur” the remedial steps that will redress the injury; and Heckler signals the judiciary’s limited power to compel enforcement actions. America First Legal Foundation v. Greer synthesizes these principles in a practical setting: to survive a standing challenge when the alleged injury flows through multiple public actors, a plaintiff must do more than hypothesize a chain of events—she must plausibly allege a direct, non-speculative causal pathway from the defendant’s conduct to the injury and a realistic prospect that judicial relief will remedy that injury.
