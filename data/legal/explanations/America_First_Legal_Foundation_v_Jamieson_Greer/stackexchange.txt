Law Stack Exchange Q&A: America_First_Legal_Foundation_v_Jamieson_Greer

### Why did the D.C. Circuit vacate and remand for dismissal for lack of jurisdiction instead of affirming the 12(b)(6) merits dismissal?
Question:
The district court reached the merits (holding OSC misread § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) but still dismissing for failure to state a claim), yet the court of appeals vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Is this because Steel Co. bars assuming jurisdiction to reach the merits? If so, why couldn’t the panel simply affirm on any ground supported by the record? What compels vacatur of the merits ruling when Article III standing is missing?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes—Steel Co. (and basic Article III limits) required the court to resolve standing first, and because AFL lacked Article III standing the district court never had subject‑matter jurisdiction. A merits disposition by a court that lacked jurisdiction is a nullity, so the panel could not leave the district court’s merits ruling intact; it therefore vacated the lower court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

Explanation, tied to the opinion
1) Steel Co. governs the sequencing question. The opinion explicitly cites Steel Co. at the start of Part II: “Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject‑matter jurisdiction. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998).” Under Steel Co., courts must resolve jurisdictional questions before deciding the merits because jurisdiction is a prerequisite to the power to decide substantive claims.

2) Why the panel did not simply affirm the district court’s merits ruling. Two related points explain that:

- A merits ruling entered without Article III jurisdiction is void: the district court “did not have subject‑matter jurisdiction,” so its merits decision cannot stand as a valid exercise of judicial power. The panel states the consequence directly in Part III: “Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.” Leaving the merits ruling in place would permit a decision issued by a court that had no authority to decide the case to remain effective—contrary to Steel Co. and Article III limits.

- The appellate court cannot use an alternative ground to sustain a judgment if the underlying case and controversy is absent. The opinion explains that standing is an element of subject‑matter jurisdiction that must be satisfied before relief can be ordered; AFL “lacks Article III standing” and thus there is no judicial power to reach or affirm the merits. The panel therefore did not treat the district court’s merits analysis as a proper fallback ground to affirm.

3) Practical and doctrinal constraints. The panel’s approach addresses both separation‑of‑powers and redressability concerns the court analyzes at length in Part II: because AFL’s alleged injuries were not fairly traceable to OSC and not likely redressed by a judicial order, the requisite case or controversy was missing. When that foundational jurisdictional predicate is absent, appellate courts ordinarily vacate and instruct dismissal so that no federal court judgment survives that was entered without jurisdiction.

Bottom line
Steel Co. required the court to decide standing first. Having concluded Article III standing was absent, the panel treated the district court’s merits disposition as entered without jurisdiction and therefore vacated the judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss. The panel could not permissibly leave a merits ruling in place that was issued by a court lacking the constitutional power to decide the dispute.

### How did the court decide that AFL lacked a concrete injury despite the 'shall ... conduct an investigation' command in 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)?
Question:
AFL argued it had a procedural right to an OSC investigation under § 1216(a)(3). The panel cites Summers to say a procedural right in vacuo is insufficient. But when a statute uses 'shall,' why doesn’t the failure to perform that nondiscretionary duty itself constitute a cognizable injury? What doctrinal step is the court using to require a further 'concrete and particularized' interest beyond the statutory command?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel applied the familiar Summers rule: a statutory command to do something (here, OSC “shall … conduct an investigation”) creates only a procedural right; that procedural right does not by itself satisfy Article III unless the plaintiff shows a concrete, particularized interest that the deprivation of the procedure harms. The court therefore required AFL to identify a non‑speculative, concrete injury caused by OSC’s failure to investigate (and likely to be redressed by ordering an investigation). Because AFL’s alleged injuries depended on a speculative chain of third‑party decisions (MSPB sanctions or DOJ production) and because §1216 does not itself entitle AFL to the informational benefits it sought, the panel concluded AFL had no Article III standing.

Explanation of the doctrinal steps, tied to the opinion
1) Summers principle (procedural right in vacuo). The court began from Summers: “the ‘deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient’ to confer Article III standing.” Op. §II (quoting Summers). That is the threshold: a statutory “shall” may create a procedural entitlement, but standing still requires a concrete injury-in-fact.

2) Relaxed—but not eliminated—requirements for procedural injuries. The opinion acknowledged that procedural‑rights cases get a relaxed treatment of imminence/redressability, but emphasized the relaxation does not erase the injury‑in‑fact or causation requirements: plaintiffs must show the procedural violation “resulted in injury” to a “concrete, particularized interest.” Op. §II (citing Center for Law & Educ. and Fla. Audubon).

3) First alleged concrete interest (records from DOJ) fails causation/redressability. The court analyzed AFL’s first theory and rejected it because the injury (DOJ’s withholding) is directly traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and any redress depends on a long, speculative chain: OSC might investigate → OSC might seek corrective/disciplinary action → MSPB might sanction → DOJ might produce records. The opinion treats those intervening decisions and independent actors as too attenuated: “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” Op. §II (citing All. for Hippocratic Med.; Haaland v. Brackeen). Thus, even if §1216 creates a duty, AFL failed to show a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its informational injury. Op. §II.

4) Second alleged concrete interest (information from OSC investigation) fails because §1216 creates no entitlement to receive investigation‑information. The court explained that informational standing requires a statute that “entitled [the plaintiff] to receive” the information (citing Zivotofsky/ASPCAs/ Akin). Here, even if §1216 mandates an investigation, AFL conceded OSC’s application of §1214’s notice rules to §1216 investigations is discretionary (and the opinion cites Carson for that point). AFL did not claim a statutory right to receive the investigation notices or reports it wanted. Therefore, AFL could not show it was denied information to which it was entitled, and a judicial command to investigate would not be likely to redress that asserted informational injury. Op. §II.

Bottom line in doctrinal terms
The court did not deny that §1216 contains a nondiscretionary “shall.” Rather, it applied standing doctrine in three steps: (1) treat the statute as creating a procedural right (Summers); (2) require a concrete, particularized interest that is harmed by denial of that procedure; and (3) test causation/redressability—rejecting injuries that depend on speculative chains or on information that the statute does not actually entitle the plaintiff to receive. Because AFL could not meet those requirements, the panel found no Article III injury despite the “shall … conduct an investigation” language.

### Why isn’t AFL’s asserted informational injury satisfied by § 1216 investigations given Akins and Public Citizen?
Question:
The panel says informational standing requires denial of information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive. AFL pointed to the notices and updates available in § 1214 processes and argued § 1216(c) allows similar treatment. The court dismisses this because OSC need not import § 1214’s notice regime into § 1216 matters. Can someone unpack why § 1216(a)(3)’s mandatory investigation language is not itself an entitlement to specific information (as in Akins/Public Citizen)? Is the key that § 1216 lacks a disclosure mandate to the complainant, so no 'entitlement' exists?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the panel’s holding turns on the difference between a statutory duty to investigate and a statutory entitlement to receive information. Under Akins and Public Citizen, a plaintiff suffers an “informational” injury only when a statute creates a right to receive specific information; § 1216(a)(3) commands OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings but does not entitle a complainant to notices or investigation reports. Because § 1216 does not impose a disclosure mandate to the complainant (and OSC need not import § 1214’s notice rules into § 1216 matters), AFL has no concrete informational injury under Akins/Public Citizen.

Why the opinion says so (pointing to the opinion’s reasoning)
- Akins/Public Citizen standard: The court repeats the settled rule that an informational injury exists only where the plaintiff is “denied information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive” (citing Akins, Pub. Citizen, and Feld Ent.). The panel therefore asks whether § 1216 creates any such entitlement. (Op. § II, para 2–3.)
- What § 1216 actually does: § 1216(a)(3) provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of alleged FOIA withholdings; subsection (c) says OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The panel treats those provisions as authorizing OSC to act, not as creating a separate duty to disclose investigation-related information to the complainant. (Op. I.A.)
- No statutory entitlement to information: The court concludes explicitly that “even if section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information.” The opinion emphasizes that AFL did not (and could not) argue that § 1216 itself contains notice or disclosure obligations to complainants equivalent to § 1214’s. (Op. § II, para 3.)
- Discretion whether to apply § 1214’s notice regime: The panel notes AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, and it cites Carson for the proposition that importing § 1214 procedures into § 1216 is not mandatory. Because OSC need not “engraft section 1214 notice requirements” into § 1216 matters, AFL cannot point to a statutory right to the notices it seeks. (Op. § II, para 3; citing Carson language discussed in the opinion.)
- Consequence for standing: Without a statutory entitlement to information, AFL’s claimed informational injuries fail the Akins/Public Citizen test and thus lack the concrete injury required for Article III standing. The court therefore rejects AFL’s second standing theory. (Op. § II, para 3–4.)

Putting it succinctly for doctrinal memory
- Akins/Public Citizen: informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the information.
- § 1216: imposes an investigatory duty on OSC, but does not require OSC to provide investigation-related notices/reports to the complainant.
- Because there is no disclosure mandate to AFL under § 1216 (and OSC may, but need not, apply § 1214’s notice scheme under § 1216(c)), AFL cannot say it was denied information that the statute entitles it to receive — so no informational injury under Akins/Public Citizen.

That is precisely the point the opinion makes when it says, “it creates no entitlement to receive information,” and rejects AFL’s attempt to rely on speculative possibility that OSC “may choose to give AFL notices and information” by importing § 1214. (Op. § II, para 3.)

### How did the panel use the 'independent actors' doctrine to find causation and redressability too speculative?
Question:
For AFL’s first theory, the court emphasizes that even if OSC investigated, only a chain of further discretionary decisions (OSC’s enforcement choices, MSPB sanctions, and DOJ’s production) could lead to AFL getting the FOIA records, citing FDA v. AHM and Haaland v. Brackeen. Why is that chain too speculative here? Are there cases where agency investigation or enforcement pressure sufficed for causation/redressability, and what distinguishes those from this context?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel applied the “independent actors” principle to hold that the causal chain from an OSC investigation to AFL obtaining DOJ FOIA records rested on too many discretionary, independent decisions to support Article III causation and redressability. The court relied on three related points in the opinion: (1) OSC’s enforcement choices are discretionary and courts generally cannot compel particular enforcement actions; (2) any relief would depend on the independent, speculative choices of two other agencies (the MSPB and DOJ); and (3) redressability focuses on what a judicial order would bind the defendant to do, not on whether a ruling might persuade non‑parties to act. Because of those contingencies, the panel found AFL’s theory too speculative to satisfy standing.

How the opinion reasons it through (with citations)
- The risky chain the panel isolates. The court explicitly lays out the chain it says is speculative: if OSC had investigated, it “might have decided to pursue a corrective action under section 1214, or a disciplinary action under section 1215”; the MSPB “might have decided to sanction those employees”; and then DOJ “might have decided to produce the requested records.” J.A. (Op.) § II (third paragraph) (pp. 11–12 of the opinion excerpt). The court emphasizes that each step requires discretionary choices by actors not before the court.
- Independent actors / speculation rule. The court invokes FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024): “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” Op. § II (same paragraph). That principle is applied here because the MSPB and DOJ are independent actors whose willingness to act cannot be presumed.
- Limits on judicially compelling enforcement. The panel also notes Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), as authority that courts generally may not require a particular exercise of enforcement discretion by an agency. Op. § II (same paragraph). That cuts against assuming OSC would be forced to pursue the specific actions that would lead to production.
- Redressability focuses on what a judgment binds. The court stresses that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court.’” (quoting Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023)). Here a favorable ruling against OSC would not itself bind MSPB or DOJ to produce the records, so the relief is not likely to redress AFL’s informational injury. Op. § II (same paragraph).
- Procedural/informational standing context. The court contrasts this with situations where a statute creates an entitlement to information (Akins, Pub. Citizen, Zivotofsky), noting that AFL cannot point to a statutory entitlement to the investigative notices or to DOJ’s FOIA records that would make the denial a concrete, redressable injury. Op. § II (second half).

Why some cases differ: when investigation or enforcement pressure suffices
- Cases where investigation/enforcement sufficed typically rest on (a) a statutory entitlement to information or (b) a close, non‑speculative causal relationship between the defendant’s conduct and the plaintiff’s injury.
  - Entitlement to information: In Akins, Pub. Citizen, and Zivotofsky, plaintiffs had statutory rights to particular information and a defendant’s withholding of that statutorily conferred information was the direct cause of the injury. The court here contrasted those authorities because AFL has no statutory entitlement to the investigative notices or to compel OSC to apply section 1214’s notice rules in a section 1216 investigation. Op. § II (second half).
  - Less speculative enforcement link: Some precedents sustain standing when a judgment would directly require the defendant to take steps that remedy the plaintiff’s injury (for example, an agency order that itself produces the records or an order compelling the agency to turn over documents). The panel rejected AFL’s attempt to characterize an OSC investigation as producing that kind of direct, compelled effect because any productive effect would depend on others’ independent choices. Op. § II (middle paragraphs).
- The distinguishing features here: (1) multiple independent decisionmakers (OSC’s enforcement discretion; MSPB’s independent adjudication; DOJ’s discretionary production choices); (2) no statutory entitlement to the investigative information AFL seeks under section 1216; and (3) the impossibility of binding non‑parties (MSPB/DOJ) by a judgment against OSC. All three make the chain too attenuated compared to authorities that permit standing.

Bottom line
The panel found causation and redressability speculative because AFL’s theory required a sequence of discretionary acts by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ—none of which a court could be said to bind—and there is no statutory entitlement that would make the denial of information a concrete, redressable injury. Thus, under the independent‑actors/“speculation” principle (FDA v. AHM), Heckler’s limits on compelling enforcement, and the Haaland rule about binding parties, AFL’s claim fails for lack of Article III standing.

### Why doesn’t a court order compelling an OSC investigation 'very likely' lead to DOJ compliance for redressability purposes?
Question:
The court says redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not on whether its rationale might persuade non-parties (Haaland). But if OSC is ordered to investigate and potentially seek corrective or disciplinary action, wouldn’t that predictably induce DOJ to change its practices? What threshold of likelihood is the D.C. Circuit applying when it rejects this as too speculative under Hecate Energy?
Answer:
Short answer: Because redressability requires a high likelihood that a plaintiff’s injury would be cured by the court’s order — not merely a plausible chain of events in which independent, discretionary actors might change course. The D.C. Circuit applied the “very likely” standard from Hecate Energy to reject AFL’s theory that an order forcing OSC to investigate would predictably produce DOJ disclosure.

Why — doctrinally — that fails here
- Redressability asks whether a favorable judgment would likely (not merely possibly) remedy the plaintiff’s injury. Lujan sets the general test; Hecate Energy (cited by the court) framed the applicable standard here as whether the judgment “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury.
- The focus is on how the judgment actually binds the parties before the court, not whether the reasoning might persuade third parties. The opinion quotes Haaland for the principle that you cannot base redressability on speculation that nonparties will be persuaded to act differently.
- Courts do not assume away the independent choices of other agencies or officials. The opinion relies on FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for the familiar point that causation cannot rest on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.”
- And where an enforcement agency has prosecutorial or enforcement discretion (here, OSC’s discretion to pursue corrective or disciplinary action and MSPB/DOJ discretion in responding), courts generally cannot compel particular enforcement outcomes. The opinion relies on Heckler v. Chaney for the proposition that courts cannot second-guess agency enforcement choices.

How those rules apply to AFL’s theory
- AFL’s causal story requires multiple contingent steps: (1) a court order forces OSC to investigate under §1216; (2) OSC decides to pursue corrective or disciplinary action under §1214/§1215; (3) MSPB (or some process) sanctions DOJ employees; and (4) DOJ then changes its FOIA practice and produces AFL’s requested records.
- The court held that chain is too attenuated and speculative. It pointed to three independent layers of discretion/status not before the court (OSC’s enforcement discretion, MSPB choices, DOJ’s choices) and concluded AFL has not shown a high enough likelihood that a judicial order would produce the requested records.
- The opinion expressly frames the defect as both causation and redressability: the informational injury from DOJ’s nonproduction is traceable to DOJ (not OSC), and even if OSC were ordered to investigate, it is speculative that the investigation would lead to corrective proceedings and then to DOJ disclosure. Hence it fails the Hecate “very likely” test.

What threshold the D.C. Circuit is applying
- The court is applying a demanding likelihood standard: redressability requires something more than a merely plausible or possible chain of events; the remedy must be likely to “spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury. Speculation about how nonparties or agencies exercising prosecutorial discretion will respond does not meet that threshold.
- Concretely here, the court rejected an inference that an OSC investigation would “very likely” cause either (a) OSC to bring enforcement that would lead MSPB to sanction DOJ personnel, or (b) DOJ to change its FOIA practices — because each link depends on discretionary decisions by independent actors.

Key citations from the opinion supporting this answer
- The court’s summary of the speculative causal chain and why it fails: “If OSC had conducted … it might have decided to pursue … Then, the MSPB might have decided to sanction … Next, DOJ might have decided to produce … causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” (Opinion § II, paragraph beginning “This theory suffers from fatal causation…”)
- Hecate Energy standard quoted: a favorable judgment must “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury. (Same paragraph.)
- Haaland principle that redressability “turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court.’” (Same paragraph.)
- Heckler and the court’s point that courts generally may not compel an agency’s enforcement choices. (Same paragraph.)

Bottom line: ordering OSC to investigate would bind OSC, but AFL’s claimed harm (DOJ’s nonproduction) depends on a speculative sequence of discretionary acts by multiple other actors. Under the D.C. Circuit’s reading of redressability (Hecate Energy plus Haaland/Allied/Heckler), that speculative chain is insufficiently likely to remedy AFL’s injury, so the court held AFL lacks Article III standing.

### What standard of review was applied, and why is it de novo in this procedural posture?
Question:
The opinion states 'our review is de novo,' citing N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf. Is this because the case was dismissed at the pleading stage and standing is a legal question reviewed de novo? Does the same de novo standard apply to both the district court’s interpretation of the statutes and to the Article III standing determination based on the pleadings?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. The court applied de novo review because the appeal principally raises legal questions — (1) whether OSC’s decision was legally correct under the statutes and (2) whether AFL has Article III standing (a jurisdictional/legal question) based on the pleaded facts. The opinion explicitly states “our review is de novo” (citing N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf), explains that standing is a question of subject‑matter jurisdiction, and treats the standing issues as legal (and pleadings‑based) issues appropriate for de novo review. See Opinion at pp. 1–2 (statement of standard), II (standing as jurisdictional and pleading‑stage standard).

Why de novo applies in this posture
- De novo review of legal questions: The court invoked de novo review for the appeal of the district court’s legal rulings. The opinion begins by saying “our review is de novo” and then proceeds to decide statutory‑interpretation and redressability/causation questions as matters of law rather than deferring to the district court’s judgment (Op. at 1–2).

- Standing is jurisdictional and reviewed de novo: The opinion treats Article III standing as a threshold question of subject‑matter jurisdiction — “a question bearing on whether the district court had subject‑matter jurisdiction” (Op. § II). Jurisdictional questions are legal in nature and routinely reviewed de novo on appeal. The court therefore reviews whether the plaintiff’s pleaded facts plausibly support each element of standing at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage (citing Food & Water Watch) and applies settled Supreme Court standards (Lujan, Summers, etc.) to the pleaded allegations (Op. § II).

- Pleadings posture dictates the type of review: Because the case was resolved on the pleadings (dismissal and later dismissal after remand), the factual record is the complaint and related filings. At that stage the relevant standard is whether the plaintiff has plausibly alleged the elements of standing; that is a legal determination about the sufficiency of pleaded facts and is therefore reviewed de novo. The opinion states that “At the motion‑to‑dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of its standing” and then applies legal standards (causation, redressability, and whether any procedural statutory right confers a concrete informational injury) to those allegations (Op. § II).

When de novo would not apply
- If the appeal turned on the district court’s findings of disputed historical facts or credibility determinations, those factfindings would generally receive clear‑error or deferential review. But this opinion contains no such contested factfinding; it resolves standing and statutory questions by applying settled legal standards to the complaint and the statutory text (Op. §§ I–III).

Conclusion
The court’s “de novo” language is appropriate here because the appeal raises purely legal questions (statutory interpretation and whether the pleaded facts suffice for Article III standing). The same de novo standard therefore governs both the district court’s statutory interpretation and its standing determination based on the pleadings, as reflected throughout the opinion (see the “our review is de novo” citation and the standing analysis in Section II).

### Why did the court avoid deciding whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits OSC’s authority under § 1216?
Question:
The district court decided that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin § 1216 investigations, but the D.C. Circuit expressly did not reach that merits question. Is this purely Steel Co. sequencing (no jurisdiction, no merits)? Or did the unresolved statutory-interpretation dispute bear on standing (e.g., entitlement to information) in a way the court thought unnecessary to decide?
Answer:
Short answer: It was a jurisdictional (Steel Co.) decision. The D.C. Circuit declined to reach the statutory question because it concluded AFL lacked Article III standing, and that defect made the merits unnecessary to decide — in particular, even assuming §1216 required OSC to investigate, AFL could not show the requisite injury, causation, or redressability (and had no statutory entitlement to the information it sought).

Why the court said so (with pointers to the opinion)

- The court opened the standing analysis by framing it as jurisdictional: “Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject‑matter jurisdiction. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998).” (Op. § II.) That signals the controlling sequence: resolve jurisdiction first.

- After briefing standing, the court applied the settled standing tests and the relaxed standard for alleged procedural injuries, but concluded AFL failed even that relaxed showing: “Because AFL cannot satisfy even the more relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries, we need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive.” (Op. § II.)

- The court explained two independent standing failures that made any ruling on the §552(a)(4)(F)(i) / §1216 relationship unnecessary:
  - Causation/redressability problem as to DOJ production of records: any informational injury from DOJ’s withholding “is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC,” and any chain tying an OSC investigation to DOJ’s production would be “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” (Op. § II, paras on first standing theory.)
  - No entitlement to the investigatory information: even if §1216 required OSC to investigate, AFL had not shown a statutory entitlement to receive the investigative notices or information (it conceded OSC’s decision to apply §1214 procedures in §1216 matters is discretionary). Thus AFL lacked the informational right that would give it concrete injury/redressability. (Op. § II, paras on second standing theory; cites Carson and related discussion.)

- Because those jurisdictional defects would persist even if the court accepted AFL’s contention about §1216 and §552(a)(4)(F)(i), the court properly declined to reach the merits: “we need not decide” whether the injuries were procedural or whether §552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits §1216. (Op. § II.)

Bottom line: This is not an avoidance by choice on a close statutory issue; it is classic Steel Co. sequencing. The panel resolved that it lacked Article III jurisdiction (standing) and therefore had no power to decide the statutory merits; moreover, the standing defects were independent of (and would not be cured by) any particular resolution of the §552/§1216 question.

### How does Heckler v. Chaney factor into a standing analysis rather than just APA reviewability?
Question:
The court cites Chaney to emphasize courts generally may not compel agency enforcement discretion and uses that to undermine causation/redressability. Is the idea that when downstream relief depends on discretionary enforcement choices, a plaintiff cannot show a likely remedy? How is this use of Chaney different from, or overlapping with, the 'committed to agency discretion' bar under the APA?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. The court is invoking the principle from Heckler v. Chaney not to say “this claim is nonreviewable under the APA,” but to show that the causal chain and redressability elements of Article III standing fail here: because OSC (and then MSPB and DOJ) would have to exercise prosecutorial/enforcement discretion, a favorable judicial ruling would not very likely produce the informational relief AFL seeks. That use of Chaney overlaps with, but is logically distinct from, the APA “committed to agency discretion” rule.

Why Chaney helps defeat standing in this case
- Chaney’s core holding: courts ordinarily may not compel an agency to take enforcement or prosecutorial action because such action is presumptively discretionary. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). The opinion quotes Chaney to support the point that “courts generally may not compel agency enforcement discretion” (Op. § II, para on causal chain).
- Standing requires a plaintiff to show an injury that is “likely to be redressed by a favorable decision.” Lujan; Op. § II. If the only plausible way a court’s order could lead to the plaintiff’s requested relief is by triggering a sequence of discretionary decisions by agencies not before the court, that chain is too speculative to supply redressability/causation.
- The opinion applies that reasoning to AFL’s theory: even if OSC were ordered to investigate under §1216, the remaining steps — OSC choosing corrective or disciplinary action, MSPB imposing sanctions, and DOJ producing the FOIA records — all involve discretionary choices by actors not bound by the court’s order. The court cites Chaney in saying it would be speculative to assume those discretionary steps would occur, so AFL has not shown a remedy would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its injury (Op. § II, paras 3–4).

How this differs from (but overlaps) the APA “committed to agency discretion” bar
- The APA “committed to agency discretion” doctrine is a merits/jurisdictional rule about reviewability: when a statute leaves agency action to agency discretion with no meaningful standard, courts lack statutory authority to review that decision under the APA. That is a limit on judicial review of the agency’s action or inaction.
- By contrast, the court here uses Chaney’s prosecutorial-discretion rationale as part of the Article III standing inquiry — specifically to show that a favorable judicial ruling would not likely produce the plaintiff’s desired result because it would still depend on one or more discretionary decisions. So the focus is on causation and redressability, not on whether the court has statutory authority to review the agency’s choice.
- They overlap because both doctrines rest on the same underlying rule about enforcement discretion: agencies have broad discretion not to enforce, and courts generally cannot force particular enforcement outcomes. But the functional role of the rule differs:
  - APA committed-to-agency-discretion → blocks judicial review on the merits when the statute gives no judicially manageable standards.
  - Chaney-as-used-in-standing → explains why an injunction or declaratory relief would not likely redress the plaintiff’s harm, defeating Article III jurisdiction.

How the opinion demonstrates the distinction
- The district court originally found a legal error but declined to compel OSC to investigate because it thought §1216(c) committed the decision to OSC’s discretion (Op. I.B). That is a classic commitment-to-discretion argument on the merits.
- The appellate court, however, does not rest on that nonreviewability ruling alone. It goes further and holds AFL lacks Article III standing because the causal chain from a court order to the FOIA disclosures is speculative and dependent on discretionary decisions by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ — and cites Chaney in support of that point (Op. § II). The court expressly frames this as a redressability/causation problem, not merely an APA nonreviewability holding.

Takeaway for future standing analyses
- When a plaintiff’s claimed injury would be remedied only if an agency (or other executive actor) exercises enforcement or prosecutorial discretion in a particular way, Chaney’s emphasis on noncompellability can be used to show the necessary causal link is too speculative — and thus Article III standing may fail.
- But that use of Chaney is analytically distinct from invoking the APA’s “committed to agency discretion” bar to defeat review on the merits; a plaintiff can fail for lack of redressability even where review on the merits might theoretically be available.

This is exactly the reasoning the court applied: Chaney supplies the rule that discretionary enforcement choices are generally not compellable, and that rule undermines AFL’s ability to show its FOIA injuries would likely be redressed by a favorable judgment (Op. § II, paras addressing causation/redressability).

### What does the court mean by relaxing imminence/redressability for procedural injuries but 'not injury in fact or causation'?
Question:
The opinion cites Center for Law & Education and Florida Audubon to say procedural-injury cases relax some elements but not injury in fact or causation. Yet it then finds both causation and redressability lacking and also no concrete informational entitlement. How, exactly, are the 'relaxed' requirements supposed to operate here, and why could AFL not satisfy them even under that more forgiving framework?
Answer:
Short answer
“Relaxing imminence and redressability” means the court will be more forgiving about how imminent the threatened harm must be and how directly a favorable ruling must redress that harm when the plaintiff alleges denial of a statute-created procedural right that is designed to protect a concrete interest. What the court will not relax are the basic Article III requirements that there be a concrete injury-in-fact and that the injury be caused by the defendant (traceability). In this case AFL failed on those non‑relaxed requirements (and on redressability for one theory), so even the more forgiving procedural‑injury framework did not save its standing.

Explanation and how the opinion applies that framework
1) The doctrinal framework (as the opinion states)
- The opinion quotes the familiar formulation: where plaintiffs allege a violation of a procedural right “designed to protect” a threatened concrete interest, courts “relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157; Fla. Audubon, 94 F.3d at 664–65 (opinion, ¶ beginning “Through this framing…”). Put differently:
  - Imminence and redressability: may be less demanding because the statute’s procedures exist to protect future or informational harms.
  - Injury-in-fact and causation: remain strict—plaintiffs still must show a concrete, particularized injury and a causal connection to the defendant’s actions.

2) How that plays out factually here (two theories AFL advanced)
- Theory A: OSC’s refusal to investigate caused DOJ not to produce FOIA records.
  - The opinion rejects causation and redressability as too speculative. It explains the required causal chain: OSC might investigate → OSC might pursue corrective/disciplinary action → MSPB might sanction DOJ employees → DOJ might produce records. That chain depends on independent actors (MSPB, DOJ) and discretionary choices by those entities and by OSC. The court says courts cannot ground standing on such speculation about the “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” and about how an agency would exercise enforcement discretion. See FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., Heckler v. Chaney, and the opinion’s discussion of the speculative chain (¶ beginning “This theory suffers from fatal causation and redressability problems.”). Because causation and redressability fail here, AFL’s informational injury vis‑à‑vis DOJ production cannot support standing even under the relaxed imminence/redressability approach.
- Theory B: AFL was denied information that OSC would have had to provide as part of an investigation (an “informational injury” from OSC’s own process).
  - The opinion explains that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive the information (citations: Feld Ent., Akins, Pub. Citizen). Even assuming §1216(a)(3) requires OSC to conduct an investigation, the statute does not plainly create an entitlement to the notices/information that OSC provides under §1214. The opinion stresses that OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214’s procedural requirements in a §1216 investigation is discretionary (citing Carson), and AFL does not allege a statutory right to the §1214 notices. Thus there is no concrete informational entitlement and no injury-in-fact on this theory (¶ beginning “AFL’s second standing theory… fares no better.”). Because the alleged entitlement is absent, a court order forcing OSC to investigate would not be likely to produce the informational relief AFL seeks—so redressability fails as well.

3) Why the “relaxed” approach did not help AFL
- The “relaxed” part of the test only eases imminence and how directly relief must remedy the claimed harm; it does not eliminate the need for a concrete injury or for traceable causation. The opinion applies that limitation directly: it says AFL “satisfies neither those requirements nor even a relaxed standard of redressability” (opinion ¶ beginning “In such cases…”). For Theory A, the chain from OSC to DOJ production is too attenuated—causation and redressability fail. For Theory B, there is no statutory informational entitlement—no concrete injury-in-fact—and so redressability fails as well. Because these non‑relaxed elements are not met, the fact that the court would be more lenient on imminence/redressability does not rescue AFL’s standing.

Bottom line
The court embraced the “procedural‑injury” framework only to the point of acknowledging the limited relaxation of imminence/redressability. It then held that AFL could not clear the still‑required hurdles: it had no concrete entitlement to information from OSC (no injury‑in‑fact on that theory), and its claim that OSC’s inaction caused DOJ’s continued nondisclosure was too speculative (causation and redressability fail). Those defects independently doom standing.

### Could AFL have established standing via organizational 'resource-diversion' injury, and why might that fail under D.C. Circuit precedent?
Question:
The opinion doesn’t discuss organizational standing. If AFL had alleged it diverted resources to counteract DOJ’s FOIA practices or OSC’s refusal to investigate, would that suffice? My understanding is that mere litigation expenses don’t count, and the organization must show a perceptible impairment to its non-litigation activities—would that theory be foreclosed here because the injury still depends on independent actors and discretionary enforcement?
Answer:
Short answer: Probably not. Had AFL pleaded an organizational “resource diversion” injury, that theory would face two independent and dispositive obstacles under the reasoning in the opinion: (1) D.C. Circuit precedent requires a showing of a cognizable, non‑litigation impairment of the organization’s activities (not mere legal fees or “procedural rights in vacuo”); and (2) even a cognizable diversion injury would still founder on the same causation/redressability problems the court identifies—because any remedy depends on discretionary enforcement and independent actors (OSC’s enforcement choices, MSPB sanctions, and DOJ’s responses), making redress speculative.

Why — with references to the opinion — in a bit more detail

1) The doctrinal baseline for organizational diversion injuries
- The Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit treat organizational standing for diverted resources as different from a plaintiff’s personal informational injury: an organization can show Article III injury if it has to divert resources from its regular (non‑litigation) programs to counteract a defendant’s conduct (see Havens Realty & D.C. Circuit cases). But the diversion must produce a “perceptible impairment” of the organization’s ability to carry out its mission; litigation expenses alone are generally not enough.
- The opinion presses that AFL’s claims principally rest on alleged procedural rights (the asserted duty of OSC to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216). It emphasizes that “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” (slip op. § II, quoting Summers). That same analytic rule governs organizational diversion claims: the organization must identify a concrete, non‑procedural programmatic harm or diversion (not merely that it sued or spent attorney time).

2) Why a diversion theory would likely fail here under the opinion’s reasoning
- The opinion already rejects AFL’s two informational harms for lack of causation/redressability: (a) DOJ’s nondisclosure of records is traceable to DOJ, not OSC; and (b) any chain from an OSC investigation to MSPB sanction to DOJ disclosure is “too speculative and attenuated” because it depends on “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” and on OSC’s discretionary enforcement (slip op. § II).
- A diversion claim would for the most part run into precisely the same defects. If AFL alleged it diverted staff/time to try to obtain the records or to educate the public because OSC declined to investigate, the causal link between that diversion and a favorable judicial remedy would still rest on the speculative chain the court describes: that an OSC investigation would trigger MSPB proceedings and sanctions that in turn would induce DOJ to change its FOIA practices. The opinion holds that a favorable judgment compelling OSC to investigate would not “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational injury (slip op. § II (quoting Hecate Energy)).
- Further, the opinion stresses the barrier of enforcement discretion (citing Heckler v. Chaney): courts generally may not compel the particular enforcement decisions of agencies. A diversion injury premised on the idea that a court order forcing OSC to investigate would cause downstream remedial action is therefore weak: redressability “generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court’ ” (slip op. § II (quoting Haaland v. Brackeen)). If AFL’s diversion allegation depends on persuading MSPB or DOJ to act, that is the kind of speculative chain the court rejects.

3) Practical upshot for pleading strategy
- To survive, AFL would need to allege (and eventually prove) more than legal fees or programmatic activity undertaken in response to OSC’s declination that itself seeks to induce action by other agencies. It would need to plead a concrete, particularized impairment to its regular, non‑litigation programs that is traceable to OSC’s refusal and likely to be redressed by a judicial order against OSC — not by persuading third parties to act. Absent such a tauter causal and redressable link, the D.C. Circuit’s analysis in this opinion indicates a diversion theory would fail for the same reasons the court rejected AFL’s informational theories.

Bottom line: Under the court’s standing analysis, an organizational resource‑diversion theory would be unlikely to save AFL unless the complaint alleged a concrete, non‑litigation impairment directly traceable to OSC and redressable by a judgment against OSC — which the opinion shows is a hard burden here given the intervening discretionary choices of OSC, MSPB, and DOJ.

### Would meeting FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates have changed the standing analysis to compel OSC action?
Question:
FOIA says that when a court orders production, awards fees, and questions arbitrary/capricious withholding, OSC 'shall promptly initiate' a disciplinary proceeding. If AFL had those predicates from its separate FOIA suit, would it then have a concrete entitlement sufficient for informational standing, and would redressability be satisfied because the statute makes initiation mandatory? Or would Chaney-like discretion or other hurdles still defeat standing?
Answer:
Short answer: Probably yes as to causation/redressability, but not necessarily as to the “informational” injury itself. The opinion makes two points that split the issues.

1) Causation/redressability
- The court rejected AFL’s first theory because the causal chain from OSC’s refusal to investigate to DOJ’s production was too speculative: any relief would depend on choices by non‑parties (MSPB, DOJ) and on how OSC might exercise enforcement discretion. See slip op. § II (pp. 7–10) (explaining the speculative chain and citing FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Heckler v. Chaney).
- But the opinion describes a distinct statutory rule in FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i): if a court (1) orders production, (2) awards fees, and (3) questions arbitrary or capricious withholding, then OSC “shall promptly initiate” disciplinary proceedings. See slip op. I.A (pp. 2–3) (statement of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)). A mandatory statutory duty of that sort would break the very speculative chain the court relied on: a judgment satisfying those predicates would directly require OSC to act, so a plaintiff could more plausibly show that a favorable court decision would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” an informational injury. See slip op. § II (pp. 8–9) (redressability requires a judgment that would likely spur the next steps, not merely persuade independent actors).
- In short, if AFL actually had the three § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates in its FOIA case, the opinion suggests (though does not decide) that the causation and redressability problems that doomed AFL’s first standing theory would be substantially diminished, because OSC’s duty would be statutorily mandatory rather than dependent on discretionary enforcement choices.

2) Informational entitlement (the “right to receive information”)
- The court separately rejected AFL’s informational‑standing theory because § 1216, even if it mandates investigation, “creates no entitlement to receive information.” Slip op. § II (pp. 10–11). AFL’s fallback — that an investigation under § 1216 would necessarily carry with it the notice/receipt rights of § 1214 — fails because OSC’s choice to follow § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 investigations is treated as discretionary in the case law (Carson). See slip op. § II (p. 11) (citing Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Counsel).
- That reasoning does not rest on whether OSC’s investigation was initiated mandatorily under FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). Even if OSC were required to initiate a proceeding by operation of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), the opinion indicates a separate question remains: does initiation create a statutory entitlement to receive investigative notices or information? The court treated that as absent here and relied on Carson to say OSC’s provision of § 1214‑style notices in § 1216 investigations is discretionary. See slip op. § II (pp. 10–11).

Net of both points
- Meeting the FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates would likely cure the causation/redressability defect the court relied on: a court’s order that triggers a mandatory “shall promptly initiate” duty would make it far less speculative that judicial relief would lead to OSC action. The opinion explicitly ties its redressability holding to the speculative chain and Chaney‑style concerns (slip op. § II, pp. 8–9).
- But the opinion also makes clear that even a mandatory initiation may not, by itself, create the kind of statutory entitlement to investigatory information that produces informational standing. Whether initiation under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) supplies an enforceable right to particular notices or materials was not adjudicated in this opinion; the court relied on the absence of any such entitlement (and on Carson) to reject AFL’s informational‑standing claim. See slip op. § II (pp. 10–11).

Conclusion (practical take-away for a law student)
- If AFL had obtained the three FOIA predicates in its separate FOIA suit, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion suggests AFL would have a much stronger (possibly sufficient) Article III showing as to causation and redressability because OSC would have a statutory duty it could be ordered to perform. But the opinion also warns that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive information; absent a clear statutory right to notices/materials produced in an OSC proceeding, plaintiffs may still fail to show the concrete informational injury the court demanded. The opinion stops short of holding that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates such an entitlement.

### Is there a narrower form of relief that would have satisfied redressability, such as remanding for OSC to reconsider without the legal error?
Question:
The district court remanded to OSC after finding a legal error but declined to order an investigation. On appeal, the panel says AFL lacks standing altogether. Why wouldn’t a limited APA-style remedy (set aside the legal error and require reconsideration) be enough to show redressability, even if the ultimate decision remains discretionary? Does the lack of any statutory entitlement to notices/information under § 1216 defeat redressability for that narrower relief too?
Answer:
Short answer: No. The panel explained that even a narrowed APA-style remedy requiring OSC to reconsider would not cure redressability here, because (1) any path from an OSC investigation to AFL’s receiving DOJ records is too speculative and depends on independent actors, and (2) AFL has no statutory entitlement to the investigatory notices or information it claims to want—so ordering OSC to investigate would not necessarily redress an informational injury.

Why, in the panel’s terms

- Redressability requires that a favorable judgment make it “likely” (not merely speculative) that the plaintiff’s injury will be remedied. Lujan; Hecate Energy, slip op. at ___ (cited in opinion). The court rejected AFL’s first theory (that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused DOJ’s failure to produce FOIA records) because the causal chain is attenuated: an OSC investigation might lead to corrective or disciplinary proceedings (under §§1214/1215), the MSPB might then sanction employees, and DOJ might then produce records. The panel held that this sequence would require speculation about the choices of two independent agencies (MSPB and DOJ) and about OSC’s own enforcement discretion—precisely the kind of “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” that courts cannot rely on to establish causation or redressability. (Opinion II; citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., Heckler v. Chaney, and Haaland v. Brackeen.)

- The court separately rejected AFL’s informational-standing theory. Informational standing is available only when a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive information. The panel pointed out that §1216(a)(3) (if read to require an investigation) does not itself create any entitlement to receive OSC’s investigatory materials or to the notices that §1214 provides; and AFL conceded that OSC’s application of §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations is discretionary. Because AFL has no statutory right to the investigatory notices or information it seeks, a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would not necessarily confer the informational relief AFL claims. (Opinion II, discussion of informational injury; citing APSCA v. Feld Ent., Akins, Pub. Citizen, and Carson.)

Why a remand for reconsideration without other relief fails

- Even if the district court had simply set aside OSC’s legal error and remanded for OSC to reconsider, that remedy would not bind OSC to any substantive outcome (the decision to investigate or to pursue enforcement remains discretionary) nor would it bind the MSPB or DOJ. The panel’s redressability analysis focuses on whether a favorable ruling “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury; here it would not. The court therefore treated the speculative sequence of events as insufficient to satisfy Article III. (Opinion II.)

Takeaway for future plaintiffs

- If your theory of injury depends on downstream decisions by other agencies or on discretionary enforcement that the court cannot compel, a narrow remand under the APA is unlikely to establish redressability. And if you rely on an informational injury, you need a statute that actually entitles you to the information you claim was withheld; absent that statutory entitlement, courts will find no concrete, redressable informational injury. (Opinion II, paragraphs rejecting both theories.)

In short: the panel explicitly addressed the exact narrower remedy you describe and explained why it would not satisfy Article III redressability in this case.

### Does separation of powers influence the court’s skepticism about compelling OSC investigations?
Question:
The opinion leans on Chaney and emphasizes limits on compelling agency enforcement choices. Is there an underlying separation-of-powers rationale for treating redressability and causation as weak when judicial relief would steer prosecutorial/investigative discretion? How does that constitutional concern intersect with Article III’s injury and redressability requirements in this case?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The opinion grounds its standing skepticism in the same separation-of-powers concerns that underlie Heckler v. Chaney and related precedents, and it applies those concerns at the Article III causation/redressability step. Because compelling an OSC investigation would — directly or indirectly — attempt to steer prosecutorial/investigative choices by OSC (and downstream actors like the MSPB and DOJ), the court treats the causal chain to any FOIA-related relief as too speculative to satisfy redressability and causation.

Why the Court says this (doctrine and how it’s applied)
- Governing standing law. The opinion applies the familiar Article III test (Lujan): plaintiff must show injury in fact, causation, and redressability. It also recognizes the “relaxed” standing rules for statutory procedural rights but emphasizes that even those rules do not eliminate causation/redressability requirements. (Opinion §II, citing Lujan; Summers; Ctr. for Law & Educ.)
- Chaney and prosecutorial discretion. The opinion repeatedly invokes the general rule that courts “generally may not compel” agencies to exercise enforcement or prosecutorial discretion in any particular way, citing Heckler v. Chaney. It treats OSC’s investigative/enforcement choices as the sort of discretionary, prosecutorial-type decisions that courts should not command. (See §I.A describing OSC’s role; §II discussing that “[i]t requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985).”)
- Speculation about independent actors. The opinion frames the causal chain needed to connect OSC’s refusal to investigate to AFL’s alleged informational injury as dependent on multiple independent decisionmakers: OSC might investigate, then might seek corrective/disciplinary action, the MSPB might sanction employees, and DOJ (separately) might then produce records. The Court views that chain as “speculative and attenuated,” and therefore insufficient for causation/redressability. It cites FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for the principle that causation “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” (Opinion §II: “any causal connection…is too speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing” and later “requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit.”)
- How separation-of-powers concerns feed Article III. The opinion does not separately announce a new constitutional separation-of-powers holding. Rather, separation-of-powers concerns operate through established standing doctrine: because a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate or prescribing the manner of investigation would intrude on prosecutorial/enforcement discretion (a core executive function), the Court requires a more direct and non-speculative causal link before finding redressability. In short, constitutional reluctance to usurp executive enforcement judgment makes the court demand a firm chain of causation and realistic prospects of redress — and when that chain depends on discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, or DOJ, Article III is not satisfied. (See §II paragraphs rejecting both of AFL’s standing theories for failure of causation/redressability.)

Key passages the opinion relies on
- On the speculative causal chain and independent actors: “Then, the MSPB might have decided to sanction those employees… Next, DOJ might have decided to produce the requested records. This requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit… causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” (Opinion §II.)
- On prosecutorial discretion: “And it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985).”
- On redressability focus: “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court.’ Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023).”

Bottom line for your question
- The Court’s skepticism is not merely prudential or evidentiary: it is driven by the same separation-of-powers rationale that underlies Chaney — courts should not issue remedies that effectively direct or guarantee particular prosecutorial/enforcement outcomes. That constitutional concern is folded into the Article III inquiry by making causation and redressability harder to establish whenever relief would require or promise that kind of supervisory control over executive enforcement decisions. The opinion therefore denies standing because the required causal path from a judicial order to the plaintiff’s informational relief is too contingent on discretionary actions by executive actors.

### Does the availability of a direct FOIA remedy under § 552 undermine standing to demand OSC enforcement as an alternative pathway?
Question:
AFL was already litigating FOIA against DOJ and receiving rolling productions. Does the existence of that direct, adequate remedy cut against traceability/redressability for its claim to force OSC to investigate DOJ? Is the court implicitly saying that the proper avenue for the informational injury is the FOIA suit, not collateral enforcement through OSC?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The court treated AFL’s existing FOIA suit and the FOIA statutory scheme as dispositive on traceability and redressability. Because the informational injury (lack of records) is traceable to DOJ and would not be reliably redressed by forcing OSC to investigate, the availability of a direct FOIA remedy undermined AFL’s standing to pursue OSC enforcement as an alternative pathway.

Why (doctrinal and opinion-based explanation)

- Standing fundamentals. The court applied the familiar Article III tests: injury in fact, causation (traceability), and redressability. It reiterated that a procedural-right claim still must show a concrete, particularized interest and causation/redressability notwithstanding relaxed rules for procedural harms. (Opinion § II, citing Lujan; Summers; Ctr. for Law & Educ.)

- Traceability/redressability problem centered on the FOIA suit. The opinion explains that any injury from DOJ’s nondisclosure “is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.” It then analyzed the causal chain required to get relief via OSC and found it too speculative: OSC might investigate, might bring corrective or disciplinary actions before MSPB, MSPB might sanction, and DOJ might then produce records. The court rejected reliance on such speculative, multi-step outcomes and independent actors’ choices. (Opinion § II, paras 3–4; citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., Heckler v. Chaney, Haaland v. Brackeen.)

- The ongoing FOIA litigation matters. The opinion notes that AFL was already suing DOJ under FOIA and DOJ had been producing responsive records (and continuing to produce them). That fact undercuts any claim that an OSC investigation was necessary to obtain the records and reinforces that the documental injury is traceable to DOJ and is properly addressed in the FOIA suit. (Opinion § I.B; § II discussion of the first standing theory.)

- OSC investigation does not create a judicially enforceable informational entitlement. The court separately rejected AFL’s argument that it had an informational right to the fruits of an OSC investigation. Even if section 1216 requires OSC to investigate, that statutory provision does not itself entitle AFL to receive investigation notices or materials; OSC’s use of section 1214 procedures in a section 1216 probe is discretionary. The court relied on precedent (Carson) and concluded that AFL had no statutory entitlement to the investigation’s information, so ordering an investigation would not likely redress AFL’s asserted informational injury. (Opinion § II, paras 5–6; Carson cited.)

What the court is effectively saying
- The court is saying that where a plaintiff’s claimed informational injury arises from an agency’s withholding and the plaintiff already has (and is using) FOIA as a direct statutory avenue to obtain those records, standing to force a different agency (OSC) to act is weak unless the plaintiff can show a direct, non-speculative causal chain and a likelihood of redress. In other words, for the FOIA-related informational injury here, the FOIA suit is the proper—and constitutionally sufficient—avenue; collateral enforcement through OSC would be too speculative to supply Article III standing. (Opinion § II, especially the causation/redressability analysis.)

Practical takeaway for future litigation
- If you have an ongoing FOIA suit producing records, trying to bootstrap standing to compel a third-party enforcement agency to investigate that same FOIA dispute will face serious obstacles unless you can show a far more direct and non-speculative link between the agency’s inaction and your concrete injury, or a statutory entitlement to the investigatory information that a court can enforce.

### Why could the D.C. Circuit raise standing sua sponte and require supplemental briefing, and what factual record governs at this stage?
Question:
Neither party initially addressed standing; the panel ordered supplemental briefing and then dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. Is this because federal courts must ensure Article III standing at all stages? At the motion-to-dismiss posture, are the court’s standing findings constrained to the complaint’s well-pleaded allegations and judicially noticeable materials, or could it consider later developments like DOJ’s rolling FOIA production?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Because Article III standing is jurisdictional, the D.C. Circuit was entitled to raise it on its own and to order supplemental briefing. At the motion-to-dismiss stage the court’s standing inquiry is governed by the facts pleaded in the complaint (and materials properly subject to judicial notice or integral to the complaint); if resolution of standing requires disputed factual development the court must (and sometimes will) convert the case to a later procedural posture (summary judgment or an evidentiary hearing) before making fact-bound jurisdictional findings. The court here applied the motion-to-dismiss standard (requiring plausible allegations of each element of standing), rejected AFL’s causal and redressability arguments as speculative, and did not treat DOJ’s ongoing, rolling FOIA production as a substitute for the pleading required to establish Article III standing.

Why the court could raise standing sua sponte
Standing is a threshold question of subject-matter jurisdiction. The opinion cites Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment for the principle that courts must consider jurisdiction before reaching the merits (Op. § II, opening ¶). Because neither party had briefed standing, the panel properly ordered supplemental briefing rather than proceeding to the merits. A court’s obligation to ensure it has jurisdiction is not waived by the parties’ silence; the court therefore may raise standing on its own initiative and require the parties to address it.

What legal standard governed the court’s standing analysis
The court applied the familiar Article III test from Lujan (injury in fact, causation, redressability) and the pleading-stage rule that “at the motion-to-dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of its standing” (citing Food & Water Watch, Op. § II). It also recognized the “relaxed” burden sometimes applied when plaintiffs assert statutory procedural injuries, but emphasized that even under that relaxed approach plaintiffs still must show a concrete injury and causation (Op. § II, ¶s 2–4).

What factual record governs at this stage
- Pleadings and judicially noticeable materials: At the motion-to-dismiss stage courts look to the complaint’s well-pleaded factual allegations (and materials subject to judicial notice or otherwise integral to the complaint). The opinion explicitly frames the inquiry as whether AFL “allege[d] facts plausibly supporting each element of its standing” (Op. § II).
- Later developments are not automatically controlling: The court rejected AFL’s attempt to base standing on an attenuated chain of events that depended on actions by other agencies (OSC, MSPB, and DOJ) and on how OSC might exercise enforcement discretion. The court treated that chain as speculative and insufficient to show causation or redressability (Op. § II, ¶s 3–5). In short, the court did not treat DOJ’s ongoing FOIA production as supplying the necessary injury or curing AFL’s pleading failures; AFL’s alleged informational injuries remained too indirect and speculative to establish Article III standing.
- When factual development is necessary: The opinion does not undertake factfinding or convert the case to summary judgment. The court could have ordered factual development if it found disputed, material jurisdictional facts; instead it concluded the pleaded facts (and the reasonable inferences from them) were insufficient as a matter of law. If the presence or absence of standing had depended on concrete, provable facts (for example, definitive evidence that an OSC investigation would have compelled production), the court would have had to permit discovery or convert the motion before resolving jurisdictional fact disputes. The court’s approach here follows the usual sequence: resolve standing on the complaint where possible; require further proceedings only if resolution requires factfinding.

How the opinion applied these rules
- Causation/redressability failure: The opinion explains that any informational injury from DOJ’s withholding is directly traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the proposed causal chain (OSC investigation → MSPB enforcement → DOJ production) was too speculative because it depended on independent actors and discretionary choices (citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med. and Heckler v. Chaney) (Op. § II, ¶s 3–4).
- Informational-injury rule: The court also explained that an informational injury requires a statutory entitlement to information; AFL could not identify an entitlement under § 1216 (and conceded OSC’s discretion whether to apply § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations) (Op. § II, ¶s 5–6). Thus a judgment ordering OSC to investigate would not be likely to redress AFL’s asserted informational injuries.

Bottom line
The panel acted correctly in raising standing sua sponte and ordering briefing because jurisdictional defects can be considered at any time. At the motion-to-dismiss stage the court’s standing analysis is limited to the complaint’s allegations (and materials properly noticed); if standing cannot be decided from that record because factual disputes exist, the court must allow factual development before resolving jurisdiction. Here the court concluded the pleaded facts were legally insufficient — AFL’s alleged injuries were speculative and not redressable — and so dismissed for lack of Article III jurisdiction.
