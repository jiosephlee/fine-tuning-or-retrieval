A Casebook Chapter on: America_First_Legal_Foundation_v_Jamieson_Greer

# Setting the Stage: FOIA, the Civil Service Reform Act, and the Office of Special Counsel

This chapter explains the statutory architecture that framed the dispute in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer and that governs how disclosure claims under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) interact with the investigative and enforcement tools Congress created for the federal civil service. The litigation highlights three distinct but overlapping regimes: FOIA’s private right of action and a FOIA-specific disciplinary trigger; the Civil Service Reform Act’s (CSRA) remedial and enforcement structure; and the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC’s) investigatory and prosecutorial role, with the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) serving as the adjudicatory forum for corrective and disciplinary remedies. Understanding how these pieces fit (and sometimes rub against one another) is essential both to resolving the question presented in the case and to applying these statutes to future fact patterns.

## FOIA’s core disclosure right and private right of action

The Freedom of Information Act, 5 U.S.C. § 552, establishes a general right of public access to agency records. FOIA imposes on federal agencies an obligation to make agency records available to any person who requests them, subject to nine enumerated exemptions and certain procedural limits. The statute therefore creates a legal entitlement—an enforceable right—to information in the hands of the executive branch, with the corollary that an agency that withholds records must justify the withholding under one of the statutory exemptions.

FOIA also supplies a judicial enforcement mechanism. A requester denied the information sought may file suit in federal district court to compel disclosure under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). The courts review the agency’s withholding and the adequacy of its search and may order production of records found to have been improperly withheld. FOIA also contemplates fee-shifting and other discretionary awards; for example, courts may award attorneys’ fees and litigation costs in certain circumstances, which has both remedial and deterrent effects. These litigation tools make FOIA a private cause of action that is, in the ordinary case, the principal means by which a requester obtains redress for an alleged improper withholding.

Two features of FOIA litigation are especially important for what follows. First, FOIA disputes ordinarily center on the district court’s assessment of whether the agency’s withholding was justified under the statute; a court that finds that records were withheld improperly can order disclosure and, in some cases, impose costs and fees. Second, FOIA also contemplates a specific channel for disciplinary consequences outside the litigator–requester relationship: when a court orders production, awards fees, and explicitly questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding, Congress directed that OSC must “promptly initiate” an investigation to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the responsible employee. That FOIA-specific enforcement mechanism is discussed next.

## FOIA’s disciplinary trigger: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

Embedded within FOIA’s enforcement provisions is a narrow, court-triggered disciplinary mechanism. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides that if a district court (1) orders production of records improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding, then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” This provision does three things at once: it creates a mandatory, post-litigation obligation for OSC; it is triggered only upon a particular constellation of judicial findings and awards; and it focuses on individual accountability by directing OSC to determine whether disciplinary measures against responsible personnel are warranted.

Each predicate matters. A simple finding that an agency failed to justify a withholding does not by itself trigger OSC’s mandatory inquiry; the statute requires a court order for production and an award of fees, plus an explicit judicial questioning of arbitrariness. The “arbitrary or capricious” language echoes administrative law standards of review for agency decisionmaking, and when a court applies such a descriptor to a withholding decision it is not merely granting relief to the requester but signaling potential misconduct in how the agency handled the request. Congress’s design thus channels the most formal, career-consequential inquiry—discipline through OSC—through court-initiated findings that cross a defined evidentiary and legal threshold.

That narrow, court-anchored trigger plays a central role in disputes over OSC’s obligations. One line of argument treats § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as the exclusive means by which Congress intended OSC to be made to act on FOIA-related misconduct: only a court’s findings can compel OSC to pursue disciplinary or corrective action under FOIA. A contrary view, grounded in the CSRA’s own provisions, reads OSC’s independent investigatory duties as broader, allowing OSC to launch investigations into alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings without a court having first exercised the FOIA-specific triggers. The tension between these constructions is a central theme of the America First case.

## The Civil Service Reform Act: purposes and structure

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 rewrote substantial portions of the federal personnel system to promote the merit system, protect employees from prohibited practices, and create institutions to investigate and remedy misconduct. Its overarching purpose was to vindicate a lawful, efficient civil service by establishing entities—chief among them the Office of Special Counsel and the Merit Systems Protection Board—tasked respectively with investigating allegations of prohibited personnel practices and adjudicating disputes about corrective and disciplinary measures.

The CSRA separates roles across institutions. Congress vested OSC with investigative and prosecutorial responsibilities: to receive complaints, to investigate alleged prohibited personnel practices, and to seek corrective action or discipline when wrongdoing is uncovered. It vested the MSPB with adjudicatory authority: a neutral forum where the legal merits of corrective actions and disciplinary proposals are resolved, and where remedies such as reinstatement, back pay, removals, or other corrective measures may be ordered. This statutory structure seeks to ensure both investigative independence and adjudicative fairness: OSC brings enforcement efforts (and must operate within constraints of prosecutorial discretion), while the MSPB evaluates claims and imposes structured remedies through adversarial procedures.

The CSRA’s architecture also embeds a mix of mandatory duties and discretionary judgments. Congress instructed OSC to pursue prohibited personnel practices when brought to its attention, and it equipped the MSPB with a full array of remedies; but the statutes permit OSC to exercise prosecutorial judgment in deciding whether to pursue a matter at the MSPB or to take other enforcement steps. This separation of functions and balancing of mandatory and discretionary elements creates room for interpretive disputes about when an investigation is required and what procedural entitlements—if any—attach to complainants and other interested parties.

## OSC’s mandates: 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214, 1215, 1216

The CSRA’s principal enforcement provisions for OSC are §§ 1214, 1215, and 1216, each serving a distinct enforcement purpose.

Section 1214 governs OSC’s investigation of “prohibited personnel practices” and authorizes OSC to seek corrective relief before the Merit Systems Protection Board. It sets out procedural steps governing how investigations and corrective-action referrals are to be handled, and it includes provisions for notifying an aggrieved complaining party about the initiation, status, and outcome of an investigation. Section 1214’s corrective-action toolkit is targeted at restoring victims of prohibited personnel practices to the positions and benefits they would have had absent the wrongdoing. The statute thus combines investigative authority with specified procedural protections for complainants and a formal path to adjudicatory relief.

Section 1215 authorizes OSC to seek disciplinary action when warranted, again through procedures resembling those for prohibited personnel practices, and to seek penalties for employee misconduct up to and including removal. It recognizes that some misconduct implicates fitness for duty or breaches so serious that corrective rather than remedial measures are appropriate.

Section 1216 is the CSRA’s conduit for OSC authority over “other forms of misconduct” not captured by the strictly defined prohibited personnel practices. Crucially for FOIA disputes, § 1216(a)(3) requires that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” That language expresses a mandatory investigatory duty targeted at alleged FOIA misconduct. Section 1216(c) then supplies a cross-reference: for such FOIA-related matters OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Read together, subsections (a)(3) and (c) squarely place OSC at the intersection of FOIA enforcement and the CSRA’s broader enforcement machinery.

When read holistically, these provisions suggest that OSC has both a statutory duty to investigate certain FOIA allegations and the statutory tools to seek corrective or disciplinary relief, deployed through the procedures set out in §§ 1214 and 1215. But as with any statutory network of cross-references, the devil is in the details; § 1216 raises questions about which procedural protections and entitlements follow into FOIA-triggered investigations and about whether OSC’s investigatory duty under § 1216 is constrained by FOIA’s own court-triggered disciplinary mechanism in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). These questions are where interpretive disputes—and litigation like America First—arise.

## MSPB’s adjudicatory role and available remedies

The Merit Systems Protection Board is the CSRA’s adjudicative forum. OSC, after conducting an investigation and determining that corrective or disciplinary action is warranted, may seek relief before the MSPB. The MSPB adjudicates disputes between federal agencies and employees or complainants about personnel decisions, corrective actions, and disciplinary sanctions. Its authority includes ordering corrective relief—such as reinstatement, back pay, or reversal of agency actions—and it also presides over adverse actions and disciplinary consequences in appropriate cases.

The MSPB’s remedial palette differs somewhat depending on whether the claim is framed as a “prohibited personnel practice” case or as a disciplinary proceeding. For corrective actions under § 1214, remedies aim to restore victims and nullify the effects of unlawful personnel practices. For disciplinary actions under § 1215, the MSPB can impose penalties to hold employees accountable and to deter future misconduct. In all events, the MSPB provides procedural protections—notice, opportunity to respond, and formal adjudication—reflecting Congress’s intent that enforcement occur through a neutral forum rather than by unilateral executive fiat.

The role of the MSPB is central to the redress calculus in disputes that seek to compel OSC to act. An order compelling OSC to investigate under § 1216 does not itself change MSPB outcomes; what ultimately affects an aggrieved requester’s ability to obtain withheld records is whether OSC brings an enforcement action, what form that action takes before the MSPB, and whether the MSPB imposes a remedy that meaningfully alters the agency’s withholding decision. Thus, any causal theory tying a court-ordered OSC investigation to a requester’s receipt of records necessarily must pass through the MSPB’s adjudicatory processes and any agency actions in response to MSPB decisions—an often attenuated and speculative causal chain.

## How § 1216(c) cross-references §§ 1214 and 1215

Section 1216(c) operates by reference: it allows OSC investigating “other forms of misconduct,” including asserted arbitrary FOIA withholdings, to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That cross-reference is significant because it imports a body of procedural and remedial law developed for prohibited personnel practices into the FOIA-investigation context.

But the import is not mechanically absolute. Courts and commentators have debated whether § 1216(c) automatically carries into FOIA-related investigations all of the substantive entitlements and procedural protections that § 1214 supplies to complainants—such as the timing and scope of notices and any attendant rights to information—because § 1216(c) grants OSC authority to seek corrective and disciplinary relief “in the same way as if” the case involved a prohibited personnel practice. Some decisions have treated the adoption of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 matters as discretionary in certain respects. For example, Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel recognized that OSC has discretion about whether to apply § 1214’s procedural requirements when it investigates under § 1216. The consequence of that interpretive stance is to leave complainants and FOIA requesters uncertain about what process they will receive when OSC investigates alleged FOIA misconduct: OSC may, but is not always deemed to be required to, replicate § 1214’s detailed procedural steps.

This interpretive uncertainty is at the heart of the America First litigation. The plaintiff there advanced a reading of § 1216 that would produce an independent, OSC-enforceable investigatory entitlement—one that does not depend on FOIA’s court-triggered mechanism. OSC, by contrast, relied on § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) and other interpretive tools to argue that the FOIA-specific trigger governs OSC’s duties in this particular sphere. The district court accepted the plaintiff’s view in part, concluding that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit § 1216, but it found that § 1216(c) leaves OSC discretion about whether to adopt § 1214 procedures and therefore declined to order a particular set of investigative steps. The Court of Appeals did not reach these statutory questions, deciding instead that the plaintiff lacked Article III standing to compel OSC to act.

## Overlap and tensions between FOIA’s disciplinary mechanism and CSRA’s investigatory framework

The statutes’ cross-references create natural points of overlap and tension. FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is an apparently narrow, court-anchored disciplinary trigger, while the CSRA’s § 1216(a)(3) imposes a broader mandatory investigatory obligation on OSC for “arbitrary or capricious” FOIA withholdings. Two interpretive approaches compete: one treats FOIA’s court-dependent trigger as the exclusive means of triggering OSC’s FOIA-related discipline; the other treats § 1216 as an independent command that permits OSC to investigate alleged FOIA misconduct without awaiting a court’s specific findings. The district court in America First adopted the latter view as to OSC’s investigatory duty, while holding that § 1216(c) left remedial and procedural determinations to OSC’s discretion. OSC and other judges have resisted readings that would subject OSC to judicial compulsion to pursue particular investigative paths—a resistance rooted in two familiar principles of administrative law.

First, prosecutorial and enforcement discretion: OSC exercises prosecutorial judgment when deciding whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary remedies. The Supreme Court’s decision in Heckler v. Chaney established a general rule that if an agency declines to take enforcement action, that decision is presumptively committed to the agency’s discretion and is therefore not reviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act. Although Heckler dealt with an agency’s refusal to take enforcement action against regulated parties, its reasoning about agency enforcement discretion informs how courts treat efforts to compel internal investigatory action or prosecution—especially where compelling OSC’s exercise of discretion would require the courts to make sensitive operational judgments better left to the executive branch.

Second, standing and redressability constraints: even when a statute appears to create procedural rights, courts will not permit litigants to sue to vindicate those rights unless the litigant can show a concrete, particularized injury traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. The Supreme Court has recognized a limited relaxation of usual standing constraints in cases alleging deprivation of statutorily conferred procedural rights, but the plaintiff nonetheless must show that the procedural violation harmed some concrete interest that the statutory procedure was designed to protect. In America First, the D.C. Circuit took precisely this tack: it concluded that the plaintiff could not show a sufficiently immediate causal connection between OSC’s alleged failure to investigate and the plaintiff’s asserted informational injuries—both because any injury from an agency’s failure to disclose is directly traceable to that agency, and because a chain of contingencies (OSC initiates an investigation → OSC seeks corrective or disciplinary action → MSPB imposes a remedy → DOJ changes its disclosure position) was too speculative to qualify as a redressable injury.

Together, these principles create a high bar for litigants who seek to obligate OSC to act. Even if one reads § 1216 as imposing an independent investigatory duty that is not confined to cases producing a FOIA court order and fee award, courts remain wary of converting statutorily conferred investigatory commands into judicially enforceable rights to particular procedures or results—especially when doing so would impinge on prosecutorial discretion or require the courts to predict and compel a sequence of independent agency choices. The America First decision emphasizes this dynamic: although the district court found legal error in OSC’s initial interpretation of the statutes, the D.C. Circuit reversed on jurisdictional grounds, holding that the plaintiff lacked Article III standing to pursue the claim. Thus, the contest over the proper scope of OSC’s FOIA investigatory duties remains alive in principle, but litigants seeking to force OSC into action face both interpretive and constitutional hurdles.

Conclusion

The statutory framework that connects FOIA, the CSRA, OSC, and the MSPB is complex and carefully balanced. FOIA grants a private right to information and provides a narrow, court-activated channel for OSC discipline. The CSRA furnishes independent investigative and remedial authorities for alleged personnel and other misconduct, and § 1216 brings FOIA-related allegations into that enforcement realm. The MSPB adjudicates corrective and disciplinary claims and can generate remedies affecting agency operations. Tension arises where FOIA’s narrow judicial trigger collides with Congress’s broader command in § 1216: is OSC’s duty to investigate FOIA misconduct contingent on judicial findings, or may OSC be required to act on its own initiative? Courts addressing that question must navigate statutory text, the allocation of prosecutorial discretion, and Article III constraints on who may invoke judicial power. The America First litigation encapsulates these issues and illustrates how standing doctrine, prosecutorial discretion, and statutory interpretation together shape the enforceability of the federal government’s disclosure and accountability provisions.

# Article III Standing Fundamentals

Article III standing is the constitutional gateway to federal court. It derives from the case-or-controversy limitation in Article III, § 2, and requires that the plaintiff present a real, personal stake in the outcome of the litigation. Courts treat standing as a threshold, jurisdictional requirement: if a plaintiff cannot show standing, the court has no power to decide the merits. The United States Court of Appeals’ decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (No. 24‑5168, D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025) exemplifies modern standing doctrine and illustrates how the three “irreducible constitutional minimum” requirements—injury in fact, causation (traceability), and redressability—operate in practice. This chapter explains those requirements, clarifies burdens of proof at different stages of litigation, and explores related doctrines (procedural and informational injuries, organizational and representational standing), anchoring the exposition in the Greer opinion and leading Supreme Court and Circuit authorities.

## Case‑or‑controversy and the role of standing

The constitutional requirement that federal courts adjudicate only “Cases” or “Controversies” is the foundation of standing doctrine. As the Supreme Court explained in Allen v. Wright, standing is the doctrine by which courts ensure that plaintiffs present disputes appropriate for judicial resolution rather than generalized grievances or policy complaints more properly addressed to the political branches. Standing therefore serves a separation-of-powers and justiciability function: it confines federal adjudication to disputes in which judicial intervention is appropriate and effective.

Because standing is jurisdictional, courts ordinarily must resolve standing before reaching the merits. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment emphasizes this point: a court cannot assume jurisdiction in order to decide the merits; jurisdictional questions get priority. In practice, courts often address standing at an early stage—frequently on motions to dismiss or in supplemental briefing—but may sometimes order additional factual development where standing is contested.

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer exemplifies these principles. The plaintiff sought to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings by the Department of Justice (DOJ). The district court addressed legal questions about OSC’s statutory authority and remanded; on appeal the D.C. Circuit concluded that the plaintiff lacked Article III standing and therefore that the district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction to decide the merits. The decision underscores that standing is the threshold issue and that a court must be satisfied of jurisdiction before proceeding.

## Injury in fact: concrete, particularized, actual or imminent

The first constitutional requirement for standing is an injury in fact: “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both concrete and particularized, and that is actual or imminent (not conjectural or hypothetical). Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife frames every modern standing inquiry: concreteness and particularization are distinct requirements. Concreteness asks whether the harm is real and not merely procedural; particularization requires that the harm affect the plaintiff in a personal, individualized way rather than in some undifferentiated capacity as a member of the public.

Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins and TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez refine the “concrete” inquiry. Spokeo reminded courts that a statutory violation does not automatically yield Article III injury; plaintiffs must show that the statutory harm either has a close historical or common-law analogue or otherwise manifests an injury that judges recognize as concrete. TransUnion further clarified that merely exposing a class of people to a broad risk of future harm does not suffice for Article III injury unless the plaintiff has a distinct, present concrete injury—e.g., reputational or financial harms that are particularized and real.

Procedural-right claims are a special, but constrained, category. The Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit have recognized that denial of a statutory procedural protection can sometimes constitute an injury in fact—provided that the procedural right is designed to protect some concrete interest of the plaintiff and the violation of the procedural right actually harms that interest. Summers v. Earth Island Institute, for example, held that a procedural right in vacuo—i.e., a mere procedural violation without any concrete interest affected—does not create Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit has described a “relaxed” standard for procedural-right cases: courts may relax imminence and redressability requirements where the procedural right is intended to protect a concrete interest, but they do not eliminate the need to show a concrete and particularized injury and causal connection to the defendant’s conduct. See Center for Law & Educ. v. Department of Educ., and Fla. Audubon Soc’y v. Bentsen.

America First Legal Foundation illustrates how these rules operate. AFL pressed two injury theories. First, it claimed a classic informational injury: DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy caused it not to receive requested records. Second, AFL claimed that OSC’s refusal to investigate denied it information that it might receive as a participant in an OSC investigation—i.e., procedural/informational injury. The D.C. Circuit rejected both. The court found that the direct withholding of records was traceable to DOJ, not OSC (an issue discussed below), and that the alleged procedural entitlement to notices from OSC lacked a statutory basis: section 1216(a)(3)’s investigation mandate does not, by itself, confer an entitlement to OSC’s investigatory materials or notices. Because AFL could not show a concrete, particularized informational interest that the legislature had entitled it to receive, AFL’s claims failed the injury-in-fact requirement.

Practical lesson: when alleging an injury in fact, plaintiffs should identify a concrete interest affected and explain how the asserted procedural right is meant to protect that interest. Merely invoking statutory text without showing how a violation translates into a tangible harm—or asserting a procedural right “in vacuo”—is unlikely to survive threshold scrutiny.

## Causation / traceability: linking the injury to the defendant’s conduct

The second standing element requires that the injury be fairly traceable to the challenged action of the defendant, not to the independent action of some third party. Traceability is not a demanding but is a meaningful constraint: plaintiffs cannot rely on chains of speculative, attenuated events or on the discretionary decisions of multiple independent actors to establish a causal link.

Heckler v. Chaney illustrates limits on traceability where an agency exercises enforcement discretion. FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine (2024) and related decisions emphasize that standing cannot rest on speculation about how independent decisionmakers—especially those not parties to the suit—might respond to a court judgment or to other events. Haaland v. Brackeen reinforces that redressability and traceability focus on how a judicial remedy would bind the defendant, not on whether a court opinion might persuade third parties to act.

In the Greer case the D.C. Circuit applied these principles. AFL’s core claim was that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ caused AFL not to receive FOIA records. The court described the causal chain that would have to hold if AFL’s theory were valid: OSC would have to investigate under §1216(a)(3), then possibly bring corrective or disciplinary proceedings under §§1214–1215; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would then have to impose sanctions or otherwise order relief; and DOJ would then, in response to those sanctions or orders, produce the records AFL originally requested. The court found this chain too speculative. It would depend on discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ—none of which was bound by the requested judicial remedy—and thus could not sustain traceability. The injurious act (the withholding of records) was primarily traceable to DOJ; OSC’s failure to investigate was at too many removes to be the cause of AFL’s alleged deprivation.

Practical lesson: plaintiffs should tie their injury as directly as possible to the defendant’s conduct. If the asserted harm depends on multiple, independent decisions by non‑party actors or on a long chain of speculative events, the court may find the causal link lacking.

## Redressability: will a favorable decision cure the injury?

Redressability requires that a favorable judicial decision will likely (not merely hypothetically) remedy the plaintiff’s injury. The standard is one of reasonable likelihood, not certainty. But this is not an invitation to rely on attenuated or speculative remedies: courts ask whether the remedy will bind the defendant in a way that addresses the core injury.

Two distinct points are important. First, redressability generally depends on how the requested relief binds the defendant—not whether the court’s reasoning might persuade third parties. A decision that declares a statute unconstitutional or orders a defendant to act can be redressive if it constrains the defendant’s choices and leads to relief addressing the plaintiff’s injury. Second, redressability is evaluated in light of the remedy sought. An injunction compelling a defendant to act is more likely to redress an injury than an advisory opinion that merely expresses legal views.

In AFL’s case the D.C. Circuit concluded that redressability failed on both of AFL’s theories. An order requiring OSC to investigate would not bind DOJ or the MSPB; whether DOJ would produce any withheld records would still depend on those agencies’ subsequent decisions. Nor would an order compelling OSC to investigate necessarily entitle AFL to any notices or investigatory materials; OSC has discretion whether to follow the §1214 notice procedures when it conducts §1216 investigations. Because the relief AFL sought (an OSC investigation) would not be likely to produce the specific informational relief AFL wanted, redressability failed.

Practical lesson: when advising a client or preparing a complaint, think carefully about the remedy that will follow from a favorable decision and whether that remedy will realistically remedy the plaintiff’s asserted injury.

## Pleading‑stage standards and the plaintiff’s burden

Standing is a burden the plaintiff bears to invoke federal jurisdiction. The level of proof required varies with the procedural posture:

- At the pleading stage (motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(1) or 12(b)(6)), plaintiffs must allege facts that, if true, plausibly show each element of standing. The D.C. Circuit follows the familiar principle that bare legal conclusions are insufficient; the complaint must plead facts supporting an inference that the injury is concrete and particularized, that it is caused by the defendant’s conduct, and that it is redressable. See Food & Water Watch v. Vilsack for the D.C. Circuit’s articulation that standing allegations must be plausible at the motion-to-dismiss stage.

- If standing is contested on summary judgment, the plaintiff must adduce competent evidence from which a reasonable factfinder could conclude that standing elements are satisfied.

- At trial, the plaintiff must prove standing by a preponderance of the evidence.

Because standing is jurisdictional, courts sometimes request supplemental briefing or limited factual development when the record does not clearly establish standing. In Greer, neither party initially addressed standing sufficiently, so the court ordered supplemental briefing. That practice highlights that standing may be developed, but the plaintiff cannot rely on conclusory allegations alone where the defendant presents contrary evidence or where the court identifies weaknesses in the causal or redress arguments.

Practical lesson: plead standing with specificity—identify the injury, identify how the defendant caused it, and state with specificity how a favorable order will redress it. If your theory of standing depends on other agencies’ actions, be prepared to explain why the court order would be likely to produce the desired outcomes.

## No hypothetical jurisdiction: Steel Co. and jurisdictional primacy

Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment teaches a cardinal rule: a court must have jurisdiction before deciding the merits. If jurisdiction is doubtful, the court must resolve that doubt before reaching substantive claims. The lesson is both procedural and structural: standing and other jurisdictional questions cannot be bypassed simply because the merits seem straightforward.

Greer demonstrates the rule in practice. The D.C. Circuit vacated the district court’s judgment on jurisdictional grounds despite the district court’s earlier substantive rulings. The Circuit stressed that the absence of Article III standing defeats the court’s power to enforce any substantive judgment. This result underscores the importance of addressing jurisdictional issues at the outset and of plaintiffs’ obligation to bear the burden of establishing standing.

Practical lesson: attorneys should litigate standing early. Facing a jurisdictional challenge late in the case risks wasted effort on discovery or merits briefing that a court cannot consummate if standing is lacking.

## Informational standing and procedural rights

Two doctrines warrant special attention because they recur in FOIA and similar cases: informational standing and procedural-right standing.

Informational standing recognizes that a loss of access to information that a statute entitles a plaintiff to receive can be a concrete injury. Cases such as Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee and FEC v. Akins recognize that statutory entitlements to information can give rise to a concrete and particularized injury when denied. But not every statutory obligation yields an entitlement-to-information for Article III purposes. The plaintiff must show that the statute actually confers an entitlement and that denial of that entitlement injured the plaintiff.

Procedural-right standing (or “procedural” injury) arises when a plaintiff is denied a procedural protection that is designed to protect some concrete interest of the plaintiff. As discussed earlier, Summers instructs that a procedural right without any connection to a concrete interest is insufficient. The D.C. Circuit’s cases hold that courts may relax some aspects of imminence and redressability when a statutory procedure protects a concrete interest, but they do not eliminate the need for a concrete injury and causation.

The Greer opinion applied both doctrines to AFL’s two theories. AFL’s first theory—loss of FOIA records—is a prototypical information claim, but the court concluded the injury was traceable to DOJ rather than to OSC, so informational standing against OSC failed. AFL’s second theory—an entitlement to notices or participation in an OSC investigation—failed because AFL could not show a statutory entitlement to the investigatory materials it sought. The court cited Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel for the proposition that OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214’s procedural requirements in §1216 investigations is discretionary. Because AFL could not identify a statutory right to receive OSC investigatory information, informational standing was lacking.

Practical lesson: when asserting informational or procedural-standing claims, identify the statutory language that creates a specific entitlement to information and explain how denial of that entitlement concretely injured the plaintiff. If the statutory scheme leaves compliance or disclosure to agency discretion, informational standing is likely to be problematic.

## Organizational standing versus representational standing (overview)

Organizations may sue in federal court in two principal ways: (1) as plaintiffs in their own right, asserting organizational injuries, or (2) derivatively, representing their members’ interests (associational or representational standing).

For organizational standing in the organization’s own right, the organization must show an injury to itself—commonly the diversion of resources to counteract the defendant’s action, lost programmatic opportunities, or other concrete harms to the organization’s operations. Friends of the Earth and similar cases illustrate that an organization forced to divert staff time, funds, or programmatic focus to address a defendant’s conduct can establish injury in fact.

For associational standing, the association may sue on behalf of its members if: (a) at least one member would have standing to sue in his or her own right; (b) the interests the association seeks to protect are germane to the association’s purpose; and (c) neither the claim asserted nor the relief requested requires the participation of individual members. Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Comm’n is the canonical statement of these requirements. Where members’ standing is at issue, courts may require evidence that at least some members have suffered the requisite injury and that the association is an appropriate vehicle to vindicate their interests.

Greer is principally an individual/informational‑standing decision rather than an organizational‑standing decision; AFL is an organization, but its theories were informational and procedural. The court treated AFL’s claimed harms as personal informational injuries rather than as organizational diversions of resources or representational assertions on behalf of particular members.

Practical lesson: an organization seeking standing should frame its injury carefully. If the organization actually diverted resources to litigate, investigate, or otherwise respond to a defendant’s conduct, plead those expenditures and explain how they constitute a concrete organizational injury. If proceeding on behalf of members, identify members who would have standing and show that adjudication does not require individualized proof from all members.

## Synthesis and practical guidance

Modern standing doctrine is doctrinally layered and fact‑sensitive. The Supreme Court’s decisions build a structure that prevents courts from adjudicating generalized grievances or speculative disputes, while still allowing plaintiffs to vindicate concrete legal rights—especially where statutory entitlements to information or procedural protections are at stake.

A few practice pointers emerge from the doctrinal rules and from America First Legal Foundation v. Greer:

- Identify and plead a concrete, particularized injury. Avoid characterizations that make the injury merely “procedural” in the abstract. Show how the procedural violation harms a concrete interest.

- Tie the injury directly to the defendant. If the alleged harm principally stems from a third party, explain why the defendant’s conduct is the proximate cause.

- Explain how the relief will likely redress the injury. If the requested remedy requires other actors’ subsequent discretionary choices, explain why such choices are likely rather than speculative.

- At the pleading stage, provide factual allegations that make standing plausible. If standing depends on statutes or agency practices, allege the relevant statutory language and typical agency responses.

- Consider organizational theories when representing institutions: plead resource diversion or identify members with concrete injuries if pursuing associational standing.

- Remember the jurisdictional priority of standing: litigate standing early and be prepared to develop evidence if the defendant challenges factual predicates.

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is a paradigmatic example of how modern courts apply these principles. The opinion demonstrates the courts’ care in parsing statutory entitlements, in refusing to rest standing on attenuated chains of causation, and in recognizing that not all statutory procedures confer a standalone, enforceable informational right. For students and practitioners, the case is a useful template for understanding what must be alleged and proved to clear the Article III threshold.

# Procedural Injury: Relaxed, Not Eliminated Requirements

When a plaintiff alleges that an agency violated a statutory procedural requirement, courts apply a distinctive standing analysis. They may relax some elements of the usual Article III standing rules—most notably imminence and redressability—but they do not eliminate the need for a concrete, particularized injury and a causal connection between the defendant’s conduct and that injury. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework that governs claims of procedural injury, and shows how that framework applies to statutory investigatory or notice requirements by using recent caselaw as illustrations. The discussion uses the D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (2025) as a focal example of how courts apply the relaxed-but-real test, and draws on leading Supreme Court and circuit precedents—Summers v. Earth Island Institute, Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, Massachusetts v. EPA, Akins, Public Citizen, Center for Law & Education, Florida Audubon, and others—to set the doctrinal boundaries.

## What counts as a procedural right and why it matters

A “procedural right” in the standing context is a statutory or constitutional entitlement to a procedure that is designed, at least in part, to protect some concrete interest of the claimant. The paradigmatic examples are statutory provisions that require notice, an opportunity to comment, a discrete investigatory step, or disclosure of information. Whether a right is called “procedural” rather than “substantive” matters because courts treating an alleged procedural violation often “relax—while not wholly eliminating—issues of imminence and redressability.” But this relaxation does not mean that any statutory procedure gives rise to standing in the absence of a real, particularized harm. The analytical task is therefore twofold: identify the procedural entitlement, and identify the concrete interest that the procedure was enacted to protect.

A given statute can sometimes supply both kinds of claims. A procedural provision can be “designed to protect” an interest by creating a guaranteed opportunity to obtain information, to participate, or to trigger enforcement action that would protect a concrete interest. For example, FOIA and other disclosure statutes often create statutory entitlements to information; when a statute creates such an entitlement, denial of that information can itself be a concrete injury (informational standing). Other procedural provisions—notice-and-comment, investigatory duties, internal notice requirements—may be designed to protect future substantive interests (e.g., the right to rely on an agency’s fully informed decisionmaking or the chance to obtain relief from misconduct). The critical point is that the plaintiff must link the procedural requirement to a threatened or actual concrete interest that the procedure is meant to protect.

The statutory text and Congress’s evident purpose are central to this inquiry. A provision that merely authorizes discretionary agency action—“may” rather than “shall,” or that explicitly commits enforcement to agency judgment—less commonly creates an individual entitlement sufficient for standing. Conversely, text that mandates disclosure or a specific investigatory duty, or that ties procedural steps to enforcement mechanisms, is more likely to create an entitlement on which informational standing can rest. But statutory language is only the starting point; courts also examine whether the plaintiff is within the class of persons the provision was meant to benefit, and whether the denial of the procedure has in fact affected a concrete interest.

## Summers v. Earth Island Institute: no procedural right in vacuo

Summers v. Earth Island Institute (2009) is the touchstone for the proposition that a procedural right, standing alone, is not enough to establish Article III standing. There the plaintiffs challenged a Bureau of Land Management rule that exempted certain small fires from required environmental review. The Supreme Court held that the plaintiffs’ alleged procedural right to notice and comment could not confer standing where the plaintiffs could not show a concrete, imminent injury that the required procedure was designed to prevent or mitigate. The Court explained that a deprivation of a procedural right “without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation” is insufficient to constitute an injury in fact.

Summers teaches that courts must look beyond the bare procedural entitlement to the substantive interest the procedure was meant to protect and to the nexus between the procedural violation and the plaintiff’s own injury. If a procedural rule simply provides abstract bureaucratic protection that does not meaningfully affect the plaintiff’s concrete interests, then the plaintiff cannot bootstrap standing from the procedural right alone. Summers thus places a substantive floor under any relaxed procedural-injury doctrine: the plaintiff must show a real, particularized harm that the procedure was designed to prevent.

The Summers rule also has practical consequences for plaintiffs seeking to challenge agency behavior by attacking only procedural steps. Plaintiffs must plead and ultimately prove that the procedural violation had some tangible effect on the plaintiff’s interests or that the denial of the procedure made a concrete future harm substantially more likely. Where the connection between the procedural violation and the plaintiff’s injury is speculative, Summers will bar Article III jurisdiction.

## Center for Law & Education; Florida Audubon (en banc): relaxed but real requirements

While Summers imposes a limit, the D.C. Circuit has long recognized a more forgiving posture toward procedural injuries when the statutory procedure was enacted to protect a plaintiff’s threatened concrete interest. In Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education (2005), the court explained that when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a statutory procedural right “designed to protect” a threatened concrete interest, the courts should relax, though not eliminate, the requirements of imminence and redressability. Similarly, the en banc D.C. Circuit in Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen emphasized that procedural claims may proceed where the plaintiff can connect the procedural violation to a concrete interest that is itself threatened, but that plaintiffs must still demonstrate injury in fact and causation.

These decisions set out a calibrated test. First, identify whether the plaintiff alleges the violation of a procedural right created by statute. Second, ask whether that procedural right was designed to protect a concrete interest of the plaintiff (not merely a collective or ideological concern). Third, require that the violation result in a harm to that concrete interest. If the right was designed to protect a plaintiff’s concrete interest and the procedural violation resulted in injury to that interest, courts will not demand the same degree of immediacy or certainty of redress that they require for ordinary substantive injuries. But courts will still scrutinize whether the alleged procedural failure caused the concrete harm and whether a favorable ruling would likely redress that harm.

The relaxed approach recognizes the democratic function of many procedural rights: they ensure informed decisionmaking, transparency, and accountability. Where the procedure is expressly tied to an interest—say, a statute requiring notice to affected property owners or a disclosure statute aimed at ensuring informed voting—the courts will be more accepting that the denial of the procedure is injurious. But the relaxed standard is not a free pass; plaintiffs must still anchor their claim to a particularized, concrete interest and show a plausible causal and redressable link.

## Massachusetts v. EPA’s special solicitude (limitations and context)

Massachusetts v. EPA (2007) introduced the idea of “special solicitude” for certain plaintiffs—particularly States—when assessing standing in environmental cases. The Supreme Court treated the State as having a quasi-sovereign interest in protecting its territory and residents from harm, and concluded that Massachusetts had standing to challenge EPA’s refusal to regulate greenhouse gases. That case is often cited for the proposition that certain plaintiffs, by virtue of their status and the types of interests they protect, may be afforded greater leeway in demonstrating a concrete and particularized injury.

But Massachusetts’s special solicitude is context-limited. It applies most naturally to sovereign or quasi-sovereign plaintiffs asserting injuries to state lands, resources, or sovereign interests. The decision does not suggest that private litigants can receive a similar, broad relaxation of Article III requirements merely by asserting generalized public interests or procedural claims. Subsequent cases have therefore read Massachusetts narrowly: it permits less stringent standing proof for states acting in a quasi-sovereign capacity, but it does not authorize wholesale abandonment of the injury-in-fact or causation requirements in non-state cases.

Consequently, when evaluating procedural-injury claims by private parties or organizations, courts will not extend Massachusetts’s “special solicitude.” Private plaintiffs must still show that the procedural violation harmed a particularized concrete interest. For states, by contrast, a combination of quasi-sovereign harms and procedural deprivations may enable standing in situations where private parties would fail. In short, Massachusetts matters, but its domain is limited; it informs but does not nullify the Summers and Center for Law & Education principles.

## Identifying the threatened concrete interest the procedure is designed to protect

The central analytical move in any procedural-injury case is to identify the specific substantive interest that the procedural requirement is meant to protect. This is necessary both to satisfy Summers’s bar on “procedural rights in vacuo” and to apply the D.C. Circuit’s relaxed-but-real test. Courts ask not merely whether the plaintiff cares about the procedure, but whether the statute’s procedural protection is plausibly tied to a concrete interest of the plaintiff in the real world.

Identifying the protected interest begins with statutory text and legislative purpose. Where the statute explicitly connects procedural protections to enforcement mechanisms or to the rights of particular individuals (for example, a requirement to notify a permit applicant of a pending adverse action), the link is straightforward. Where the statute's language is general or discretionary, the court must inquire whether Congress intended the procedure to protect a specific class of beneficiaries and whether the plaintiff is a member of that class.

Beyond text, courts examine the functional effect of the procedure. For example, notice-and-comment rules are designed to give affected parties an opportunity to influence agency decisions; a plaintiff who can show that the loss of a comment opportunity was likely to affect the agency decision in a way that harms the plaintiff has a plausible concrete injury. Disclosure statutes are designed to give the public and regulated parties information material to their interests; denial of guaranteed information can therefore create an informational injury if the statute confers an entitlement. Conversely, where a procedure is intended mainly as an internal check on agency competence or as a discretionary investigatory option to be exercised in the agency’s judgment, its denial is less likely to yield a cognizable injury to an outside party.

The case law offers recurrent examples. In Public Citizen v. Department of Justice and in Akins, plaintiffs had statutory or quasi-statutory rights to information, and the denial of that information constituted a recognizable injury. In other cases, such as Summers itself, the nexus between the procedure and any concrete interest was too attenuated to support standing. The identifying task is fact-sensitive: plaintiffs should plead—and courts should require—specific facts explaining how the procedural lapse affects the plaintiff’s concrete interest, not only general assertions of public right or abstract oversight interest.

## Applying procedural-injury doctrine to investigative and notice provisions

Investigative and notice provisions present recurring standing questions because they often provide procedures aimed at identifying misconduct or keeping affected persons informed—functions that can plausibly protect concrete interests. But whether a denied investigatory or notice procedure gives rise to Article III standing depends on (1) whether the statute creates a private entitlement to certain information or process, (2) whether the plaintiff belongs to the class the procedure protects, and (3) whether the denial has a non-speculative causal connection to a concrete injury that a judicial remedy can redress.

The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (2025) illustrates the mechanics. AFL alleged that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate an alleged FOIA policy deprived AFL both of the records it sought from the Department of Justice and of the notices and information that would attend an OSC investigation under a different statutory provision. The court examined both theories in light of Summers and related precedents and concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing.

First, the court rejected the contention that OSC’s refusal to investigate was the but-for cause of DOJ’s failure to produce the FOIA records. That causal theory required a speculative chain: if OSC had investigated, it might have pursued corrective or disciplinary proceedings; the Merit Systems Protection Board might have sanctioned DOJ employees; DOJ then might have produced the records. The court emphasized that causation cannot rest on speculation about the independent choices of nonparty actors (the MSPB and DOJ) or about how an enforcement agency might exercise discretion. This reasoning draws directly on the principle that standing must avoid attenuated causal chains that depend on future discretionary acts by independent actors, as reflected in cases such as Lujan and more recent decisions emphasizing the limits on reliance on third parties’ unfettered choices.

Second, the court rejected AFL’s claim of an informational injury in the OSC-investigation process itself because the statute governing OSC’s FOIA investigations (5 U.S.C. § 1216) did not, by its terms, create an entitlement to receive the notice requirements located elsewhere in the statute (section 1214). AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to apply section 1214’s procedural rules in section 1216 investigations is discretionary; that concession was fatal to its informational-standing claim because a plaintiff’s informational injury must generally be a denial of information the statute entitled it to receive. The court thus applied the Akins/Public Citizen line of law: informational standing requires either a statutory entitlement to information or a sufficient nexus between the procedure denied and the plaintiff’s own concrete informational interest.

The America First Legal decision also illustrates the interaction of investigatory discretion and standing. When a statute does not create a clear entitlement to a particular investigatory result or informational product, a court’s order compelling an investigation may be unlikely to produce the particular relief the plaintiff seeks. Where relief depends on how an agency exercises subsequent enforcement discretion—or on decisions by distinct agencies—the court will treat the redressability question with skepticism and may find standing lacking.

Two further doctrinal limits are important. First, where the statute expressly commits the enforcement decision to agency discretion, courts are reluctant to treat a plaintiff as having a private right to a particular enforcement outcome (Heckler v. Chaney). Even when the underlying statute requires an investigation, courts will not assume that an aggrieved plaintiff can trade the court’s imprimatur for a guaranteed chain of causation leading to production of documents or imposition of sanctions. Second, even where an investigation would generate useful information, courts require that the plaintiff show an entitlement to that information or a plausible theory that receiving the information would alter the plaintiff’s substantive position in a way the court can remedy.

## Practical application: pleading and proving procedural injury

For practitioners and students, the doctrinal rules yield concrete pleading and proof strategies. At the pleading stage, a plaintiff alleging a procedural deprivation should do three things: first, plead the precise statutory procedure at issue and explain how that procedure is designed to protect a specific class of persons or a particular interest; second, allege facts showing that the plaintiff is within the intended class and that the denial of the procedure concretely injured the plaintiff’s interest; and third, show that a favorable judicial decision would likely redress the injury without relying on multiple speculative future acts by nonparties.

A successful informational-standing claim typically requires either an allegation that the statute creates an entitlement to a particular disclosure or a fact-specific showing that denial of the information frustrated the plaintiff’s ability to protect a particular, concrete interest. Where the statutory language is permissive or where internal agency procedures are discretionary, plaintiffs should plead particularized injury—lost opportunity to influence agency action that directly governs plaintiffs’ legal rights, missed deadlines, economic harm that flowed from lack of notice, or specific reputational or regulatory consequences that the procedure was meant to prevent.

When the alleged injury hinges on subsequent enforcement actions by other agencies or bodies, plaintiffs face an uphill causation and redressability burden. Courts will require more than hope that an investigation would trigger remedial steps. The America First Legal decision shows that speculative causal chains—investigation leads to enforcement leads to another agency’s sanction leads to production of documents—are insufficient. Plaintiffs should instead demonstrate either (a) a direct linkage between the procedural right and an enforceable entitlement to relief (for example, a statutory mechanism that compels production or imposes automatic consequences), or (b) a plausible and relatively direct causal route by which a judicially compelled procedure would cause a remedial action likely to redress the plaintiff.

Finally, mindful of Summers and its progeny, litigants should not treat procedural rights as stand-alone claims divorced from substantive stakes. Courts will expect plaintiffs to say concretely what they lost, how the lost procedure caused that loss, and why a judicial remedy ordering the procedure would likely redress the harm. Absent that connected showing, courts will dismiss for lack of Article III jurisdiction.

## Conclusion

Procedural-injury standing occupies a middle ground. Courts recognize that procedures—notice, investigation, disclosure—serve important democratic and individual-protective functions, and they will relax some standing requirements where a plaintiff can show that a procedural violation threatens or injures a concrete interest that the statute was designed to protect. But Summers and subsequent decisions insist that the relaxation is limited: plaintiffs still must identify a concrete, particularized injury and demonstrate a causal link between the procedural failure and that injury and a realistic prospect that a favorable ruling would redress the harm. The America First Legal Foundation decision demonstrates how these principles operate in practice: an alleged statutory duty to investigate or disclose does not confer standing unless the plaintiff can tie the denial to a non-speculative injury and show that judicial relief would likely redress it. For students and practitioners, the lesson is emphatic: procedural rights matter, but they cannot be invoked in the abstract; the courts require a concrete, fact-specific demonstration that the denied procedure actually harmed the plaintiff in a way a judicial order can remedy.

# Informational Standing: When Denial of Information Is a Concrete Injury

The law of Article III standing recognizes a special strand of injury—informational injury—where a plaintiff is denied information to which a statute entitles him or her. That strand is important in public-interest litigation, freedom-of-information suits, and suits challenging agency inaction that frustrates a statutory disclosure regime. But not every statutory violation that touches on information produces a judicially cognizable injury. This chapter explains the doctrinal contours of informational standing, shows how courts analyze claims that a denial of information is a “concrete” injury, and applies those principles to the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) context illustrated by America First Legal Foundation v. Greer, 2025 D.C. Cir. (No. 24-5168).

We begin with the foundational cases that established the proposition that a plaintiff can have standing when a statute gives him or her a right to receive information. From there we examine the essential entitlement requirement, explain how informational rights differ from interests in governmental enforcement, and assess the effect of more recent Supreme Court decisions—Spokeo and TransUnion—on the concreteness inquiry. The chapter closes with a focused treatment of OSC’s investigatory authority under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, the Sixth Circuit’s decision in Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel, and the reasons why courts are reluctant to treat investigatory or enforcement-oriented statutes as creating freestanding informational entitlements.

## Foundational cases: Public Citizen v. DOJ; FEC v. Akins; Zivotofsky

Public Citizen v. Department of Justice, FEC v. Akins, and lower-court applications such as Zivotofsky are the classic precedents for informational standing. Each illuminates a different facet of the doctrine.

- Public Citizen v. Department of Justice, 491 U.S. 440 (1989). In Public Citizen the Supreme Court held that a denial of information required by statute can confer standing. The plaintiffs were denied access to Department of Justice (DOJ) records that the statute required, and the Court recognized that being deprived of information that a statute entitles one to receive can constitute an injury-in-fact. Public Citizen thus stands for the proposition that statutory entitlement to information is often sufficient to ground standing—because the right to the information is itself the legally protected interest.

- FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998). Akins reinforces the principle that a statutory disclosure regime creates a legally protected informational interest. There, an association sought to compel the Federal Election Commission to enforce a statute requiring certain political committees to register and disclose information. The Court held that non-disclosure injured the association’s ability to gather and use information necessary to its activities, and that injury was concrete and particularized because the statute created an expectation that the information would be available. Akins underscores that the statutory grant of information to a class of persons can make non-disclosure a cognizable injury.

- Zivotofsky ex rel. Ari Z. v. Secretary of State (D.C. Cir. 2006), 444 F.3d 614. The D.C. Circuit applied the same informational-standing logic in a context where the statutory scheme contemplated a particular factual notation on a passport. The court recognized that a statutory entitlement to particular information (or to a particular governmental act that conveys information) can supply the requisite injury-in-fact. Zivotofsky is often cited for the proposition that a plaintiff can have standing where she is denied information that a statute specifically entitles her to receive or to which she has a statutory entitlement.

Taken together, these authorities establish two central points: (1) an entitlement to information created by statute can independently supply the “injury-in-fact” element of standing, and (2) the plaintiff must be the sort of person the statute protects—i.e., within the class entitled to the information or benefit of the statutory scheme.

## The entitlement requirement: the statute must mandate disclosure to the plaintiff

Not every statutory duty to do something about information creates standing for every disappointed litigant. Informational standing requires an entitlement: the statute must set up a right to receive information (or a right that produces information) for the plaintiff or the class of persons to which the plaintiff belongs.

Why is that entitlement requirement essential? Standing doctrine asks whether the plaintiff has suffered an invasion of a legally protected interest that is concrete and particularized. A statutory obligation creates a legally cognizable interest only if the statute confers the right to the plaintiff or the plaintiff’s class. Mere statutory language that authorizes an agency to act, or that anticipates discretionary enforcement, generally does not create an individual entitlement to receive the fruits of that enforcement. Thus courts draw a line between statutes that mandate disclosure and statutes that create discretionary investigatory or enforcement schemes.

Operationally, courts ask several related questions:

- Does the statute impose a duty to disclose information to a class that includes the plaintiff? If yes, a denial of that information can constitute an injury-in-fact. FOIA is the paradigmatic example: FOIA imposes an obligation on federal agencies to disclose records upon request, subject to enumerated exemptions, and a FOIA requester who is denied responsive records can typically assert a cognizable informational injury (see Public Citizen).

- Is the statutory duty mandatory or hortatory? Mandatory duties are more likely to create entitlements. A statute phrased in mandatory terms (“the agency shall disclose upon request”) is more likely to generate standing than one phrased in discretionary terms (“the agency may investigate”). Where the statute instructs an agency to take a particular act for the benefit of a class, denied beneficiaries can usually show injury.

- Is the plaintiff within the protected class or otherwise the proper party to assert the entitlement? Courts will not confer standing on plaintiffs who are not the intended beneficiaries of the statutory disclosure. Akins is instructive here: the plaintiffs were politically motivated organizations that plainly fell within the class that would benefit from the requested disclosures.

If the plaintiff cannot show a clear statute-based entitlement to receive information, a denial of information will often be characterized as a mere procedural grievance. A “procedural right in vacuo”—a procedural entitlement divorced from a concrete interest the statute seeks to protect—does not create standing (Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488 (2009)). The entitlement requirement prevents private parties from transforming broadly phrased statutory or administrative duties into private causes of action simply by alleging that the government failed to execute its discretionary responsibilities.

## Distinguishing a right to information from an interest in governmental enforcement

Plaintiffs sometimes try to convert an enforcement-oriented statutory provision into an informational right by asserting that an agency’s failure to enforce or investigate deprived them of information they would have obtained following enforcement. That strategy generally founders for two reasons.

First, causation and redressability. To have standing, the plaintiff must show that the defendant’s conduct caused the injury and that a favorable court order would likely redress it. Where the relief sought is an order requiring an oversight or enforcement agency to investigate, the causal chain from that relief to the plaintiff’s receipt of the original records is often too speculative. The oversight agency’s investigation may or may not result in enforcement action; even enforcement action might not result in production of the particular records the plaintiff wants; and independent actors (the agency that holds the records, the adjudicative body, or third-party decisionmakers) may make intervening discretionary choices. Courts decline to find standing where causation would depend on such “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (see FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), and Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), on prosecutorial discretion).

Second, a claim that an agency failed to enforce a statutory duty is often characterized as a challenge to agency inaction. The Supreme Court’s general rule is that agency enforcement discretion is presumptively unreviewable (Heckler v. Chaney), and the remedy for a mere failure to act is not always an order compelling production of records. A plaintiff who really seeks the records should sue the record-holding agency under the statute that governs disclosure (for example, FOIA), not sue an enforcement agency to compel it to take enforcement steps in the hope that those steps will indirectly produce the records.

The America First Legal Foundation v. Greer opinion illustrates these principles. AFL sought to compel OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy, and it argued that OSC’s failure to investigate deprived AFL of the information DOJ had allegedly withheld. The D.C. Circuit rejected that chain of causation: any loss of records was traceable to DOJ, not to OSC, and the possibility that an OSC investigation would eventually cause DOJ to produce the records required too many speculative links (OSC might or might not bring a disciplinary or corrective action; the MSPB or DOJ might or might not choose to sanction or produce; and those entities were not before the court). Because redressability depends on the independent choices of other actors, the plaintiff could not demonstrate that a favorable judgment ordering OSC to investigate “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the informational injury. That analysis underscores the deep doctrinal difference between a direct statutory entitlement to information and a mere derivative interest in governmental enforcement.

## The influence of Spokeo and TransUnion on concreteness in informational harms

Two recent Supreme Court decisions—Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, 578 U.S. 330 (2016), and TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 141 S. Ct. 2190 (2021)—have sharpened the courts’ focus on whether an asserted informational injury is “concrete,” even when a statute has been violated.

- Spokeo: statutory violations are not automatically concrete injuries. In Spokeo, the Court reversed a standing determination based solely on a statutory violation of the Fair Credit Reporting Act’s disclosure requirements. The plurality and the majority’s guidance require courts to consider whether the intangible harm alleged has a “close relationship” to harms historically recognized as providing a basis for lawsuits (e.g., invasion of privacy, defamation, or physical harms), or otherwise is of a type that Congress sought to protect as a concrete interest. Thus a mere statutory formality or a technical statutory violation that does not cause a real-world harm—or at least a harm analogous to historical harms—may not be enough for Article III purposes.

- TransUnion: concreteness requires an actual or imminent harm to a legally protected interest; a statutory violation does not obviate that requirement. TransUnion clarified that some intangible harms (e.g., disclosure of inaccurate information that impedes one’s reputation or credit) can be concrete because they create a real risk of material harm or are the kind of injuries courts have historically treated as harmful. But TransUnion also emphasized that the plaintiff must show an injury that is particularized and concrete: the Court limited standing for a class of individuals whose records had been flagged in a database when most class members suffered no actual harm. In short, even information harms must be tied to a real risk or actual impairment of a legally protected interest.

Applied to informational standing, Spokeo and TransUnion require a two-step assessment: (1) identify the statutory entitlement and the nature of the informational deprivation; and (2) decide whether the deprivation is the sort of concrete, particularized harm that the Constitution requires. A statutory denial of information is more likely to be concrete when (a) the statute confers an affirmative entitlement to particular information, (b) the plaintiff is among the class the statute protects, and (c) the deprivation causes a real-world informational disadvantage that affects the plaintiff’s ability to act or make decisions (for example, the inability to petition, to participate in democratic deliberations, or to vindicate other legal rights). Conversely, a claim that an agency failed to comply with a disclosure statute in a way that produces no actual informational disadvantage to the plaintiff may be non-concrete under Spokeo.

In the America First Legal Foundation case, the D.C. Circuit applied these Spokeo/TransUnion-informed principles. Even if OSC had a duty under § 1216 to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, the court held that § 1216 did not entitle AFL to information about OSC’s investigatory fruits, and thus AFL had not been deprived of a statutory entitlement to information. Under Spokeo, a mere procedural denial of an investigatory process is insufficient unless the deprivation injures a concrete interest protected by statute; AFL could not show that here.

## OSC investigations and the absence of a statutory entitlement under 5 U.S.C. § 1216

The Office of Special Counsel occupies an unusual position: Congress empowered OSC to investigate “prohibited personnel practices” under the Civil Service Reform Act and to investigate certain other misconduct (including alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings) under § 1216. But the question for standing purposes is not merely whether OSC has investigatory authority—it is whether any statute entitles a private requester to receive information from OSC.

Section 1216(a)(3) states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, alleged FOIA violations. Section 1216(c) further provides that OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Read in isolation, § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall . . . conduct an investigation” language looks mandatory; it invites the inference that Congress intended OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholding.

But even if OSC were compelled to investigate in certain circumstances, § 1216 is an enforcement and investigatory provision, not a FOIA-style disclosure mandate. It creates investigatory duties for OSC and processes for discipline or corrective action; it does not unambiguously create a private statutory right to obtain the products of OSC’s investigations. Crucially, § 1214, the remedial and procedural statute for prohibited personnel practices, contains procedures that contemplate notifying complainants and providing certain information during investigations. But § 1216 makes application of § 1214’s procedures to FOIA investigations contingent in a way that courts have treated as discretionary (see Carson, below). In short, § 1216 does not plainly—and thus for standing purposes does not automatically—entitle a FOIA requester to receive OSC investigatory materials or to receive the notification and information that § 1214 requires for ordinary prohibited-personnel-practice complainants.

The America First decision applies these principles. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to invoke § 1216 to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy deprived AFL of information it would have received had OSC acted. The D.C. Circuit held that any informational injury was not traceable to OSC, and that § 1216, even if properly read to impose a duty to investigate, did not create an entitlement to receive information—AFL therefore lacked a cognizable informational injury. The opinion emphasizes that a plaintiff cannot convert an investigatory obligation into a private right to inspection unless the statute clearly confers such a right.

## Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel: discretion to apply § 1214 procedures in § 1216 matters

Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), is the principal appellate decision confronting the interplay between § 1216 and § 1214 procedural protections. In Carson, the Sixth Circuit held that when OSC investigates under § 1216, its decision whether to apply § 1214’s procedural requirements is discretionary. That is, OSC may investigate FOIA complaints under § 1216 but is not statutorily compelled to borrow § 1214’s procedural notice and participation regime.

Carson is important for two reasons. First, it treats § 1216 as creating investigatory authority but not as transplanting, as a matter of law, all of § 1214’s procedural protections into every § 1216 investigation. Second, for standing, that means a complainant cannot necessarily rely on § 1214’s notice and procedural protections as a guaranteed source of information from OSC when § 1216 is implicated. If OSC may permissibly decide not to provide § 1214-style notice and participation, then a litigant who seeks those procedural fruits lacks an entitlement to information unless another statute supplies one.

The D.C. Circuit in America First cited Carson for the proposition that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 investigations is discretionary, and that acknowledgement undermined AFL’s contention that it had a statutory entitlement to receive OSC investigatory notices and information. Carson thus illustrates the limits on construing investigatory statutes as creating private rights to informational fruits of investigations.

## FOIA’s distinct informational entitlement versus CSRA’s discretionary investigatory processes

A clear way to understand the difference between an entitlement to information and a mere interest in enforcement is to contrast FOIA and the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) / § 1216 regime.

- FOIA: FOIA is a disclosure statute. It directly obligates agencies to produce records upon request, subject to narrow exemptions, and it provides a private right of action in federal court to compel disclosure. FOIA’s structure (a party makes a request; the agency must search and either produce or lawfully withhold; the requester can sue) creates a paradigmatic informational entitlement. Public Citizen, Akins, and the long line of FOIA jurisprudence treat FOIA requesters as having a concrete, particularized interest in the requested records.

- CSRA and § 1216: The CSRA and § 1216 create investigative and remedial processes. They allocate responsibilities to OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), and other bodies to investigate prohibited personnel practices and to seek corrective or disciplinary action. Those statutes are primarily aimed at enforcing agency personnel rules and correcting wrongdoing—functions that are valuable but not equivalent to a direct statutory right to receive documents on demand. The CSRA contemplates notice and participation procedures in some enforcement contexts (e.g., § 1214 procedures for complainants), but the extent to which those procedures apply to other statutory investigations—like § 1216 FOIA investigations—is not always mandatory and may be discretionary.

The practical consequence is predictable: where a plaintiff’s injury is the lack of production of documents controlled by a record-holding agency, suing under FOIA will usually present a direct, cognizable claim. Suing a supervisory or enforcement office to compel it to investigate in the hope that such an investigation will lead to production is a roundabout route that often fails the causation/redressability test. If Congress desires that investigative agencies must provide outsiders with the fruits of their probes, it must articulate that entitlement clearly in the statutory language.

## Applying the doctrine: practical guidance and hypotheticals

The doctrine of informational standing is often applied through a simple checklist. A plaintiff who claims an informational injury should be prepared to show:

1. Entitlement: A statute establishes a right to receive the specific information (or an act that produces the information), and the plaintiff belongs to the class of persons the statute protects.

2. Particularization: The harm is particularized to the plaintiff—i.e., the plaintiff has been specifically denied information relevant to his or her interests.

3. Concreteness: The injury is concrete in the Spokeo/TransUnion sense—either historically cognizable or otherwise materially harmful (actual or imminent harm, or a sufficiently substantial risk of harm).

4. Causation: The defendant’s conduct (the refusal to disclose, or the failure to undertake an act that would have produced disclosure) is the cause of the deprivation.

5. Redressability: A favorable judicial decision will likely redress the injury; the relief will be effective against the actor who controls the desired information or will otherwise produce the information.

A few hypotheticals illustrate how courts apply these factors:

- Classic FOIA request: Agency X refuses to produce emails responsive to a request. The requester sues Agency X under FOIA. This is the archetypal informational-standing case: FOIA creates a direct entitlement, the injury is particularized, and a court order compelling production will likely redress the injury. Public Citizen and Akins support standing.

- Enforcement-agency route: The requester instead sues an independent oversight office (Office Y) that has authority to investigate but not to directly produce the requested records. The requester seeks an order compelling Office Y to investigate, reasoning that an Office Y investigation will lead to the production of documents. Courts doubt standing here—the entitlement is missing; causation is speculative; redressability depends on the choices of Office Y and the record-holder. America First v. Greer typifies that result.

- Investigatory notice claim: A private employee complains to OSC and asserts that OSC refused to apply § 1214 procedures and so deprived the complainant of the notice § 1214 promises. Whether the complainant has standing depends on whether § 1214’s procedures apply as a matter of right to that complainant (if so, an informational injury may exist) or whether OSC’s decision to apply § 1214 in § 1216 cases is discretionary (if so, the complainant may lack an entitlement). Carson suggests the latter for many § 1216 matters.

- Statute creating a right to information about third-party conduct: Suppose Congress enacts a statute saying that consumers “shall be entitled to receive” reports from a neutral database about companies’ false representations. Such a statute gives an entitlement, and denial of the reports would likely be a concrete injury under Akins and Public Citizen. Spokeo would still ask whether the sort of information and the harm from its denial are within the realm of historically cognizable harms or otherwise sufficiently real.

## Conclusion: how to plead informational standing and the limits of procedural claims

Informational standing is a powerful doctrine, but it has limits designed by Article III and sharpened by recent Supreme Court precedent. A plaintiff can establish standing by showing a statutory entitlement to information that has been denied, and that denial results in a concrete, particularized injury. But plaintiffs cannot convert every procedural provision or enforcement duty into a private right to receive information. Where a statute confers investigatory or enforcement authority on an agency without clearly conferring a private entitlement to the fruits of that investigation, courts will require a more direct causal connection between the defendant’s conduct and the plaintiff’s informational injury—and a realistic prospect that relief will produce the information.

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer illustrates both the promise and the limits of informational standing. AFL alleged that OSC violated a statutory duty under § 1216 by declining to investigate DOJ’s FOIA policy. But because AFL could not show that § 1216 or any other provision unambiguously entitled it to receive OSC investigatory information, and because any production of documents would have been traceable to DOJ (and contingent on speculative subsequent enforcement steps), the D.C. Circuit concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing. The case therefore is a useful contemporary example: when the information you seek is held by a different actor, and the statutory provision you invoke empowers but does not require a third-party investigatory agency to produce or to disclose the investigatory fruits, standing is unlikely.

For students and practitioners, the takeaways are practical:

- When seeking documents, start with the statute that directly governs the record-holder (e.g., FOIA). That is the most reliable path to create a cognizable informational injury.

- If you must sue an oversight or enforcement agency, plead a clear statutory entitlement to the investigatory fruits, particularized harm from being denied that entitlement, and a plausible causal chain showing that the relief sought will likely produce the desired information.

- Anticipate Spokeo/TransUnion questions about whether the informational deprivation is a real-world harm (or a substantial risk of one), not a bare procedural impropriety.

Keeping these principles in mind will help you assess both the viability of informational-standing claims and the proper defendant and statutory basis for a suit seeking government-held information.

# Independent Actors, Speculative Chains, and Redressability Limits

This chapter examines a familiar but fact-sensitive set of standing problems that arise when a plaintiff’s claimed injury depends on the decisions of independent actors or on a sequence of discretionary choices by multiple public bodies. The controlling question is whether the plaintiff has alleged an injury that is (1) fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct and (2) likely to be redressed by the relief sought. When the causal route from the defendant’s act to the plaintiff’s injury runs through the hands of parties not before the court—or through agency choices that are committed to enforcement discretion—the traceability and redressability inquiries often collapse under the doctrine the Supreme Court and this Circuit have developed. America First Legal Foundation v. Greer, decided by the D.C. Circuit in 2025, furnishes a compact, contemporary illustration: AFL sued the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) for declining to investigate an alleged FOIA policy at the Department of Justice. The court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing because its claimed informational injuries rested on a speculative chain of events—an OSC investigation would (maybe) lead to MSPB proceedings, which (maybe) would lead DOJ to disclose records—rather than on an injury fairly traceable to OSC or likely to be redressed by a judicial order against OSC.

The doctrinal material in this chapter is organized by topic: the independent-actor problem and the general prohibition on standing based on highly speculative causal chains; recent applications of that principle in FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Hecate Energy; the related limit that judgments bind the parties before the court rather than nonparties who might be persuaded by a court’s reasoning (Haaland v. Brackeen); the precedential roots in Clapper v. Amnesty International and Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights; the distinct problem presented by agency enforcement discretion (Heckler v. Chaney); and practical rules for litigators confronting multi-agency remedial paths like OSC → MSPB → agency compliance.

## Independent-actor doctrine and speculation concerns

The core of the independent-actor doctrine is straightforward: an injury cannot be fairly traced to a defendant when the chain of causation depends on the voluntary actions of one or more independent actors not before the court. That is so because standing requires a causal connection between the assertedly unlawful conduct of the defendant and the plaintiff’s injury; speculative or attenuated links supplied by the discretionary choices of third parties break that causal link.

The Supreme Court summarized this prudential and constitutional limit in multiple venues. In situations where the complained-of action is not the proximate cause of the asserted harm, the Court requires plaintiffs to show more than a mere possibility that a favorable ruling will lead third parties to act in ways that redress the injury. The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly applied this principle: when the causal route from the defendant’s conduct to the plaintiff’s injury requires “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts,” the plaintiff lacks the traceability necessary to establish Article III standing.

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer exemplifies these points. AFL alleged that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ’s FOIA practices injured AFL by (1) leaving DOJ’s FOIA production incomplete and (2) depriving AFL of notices and other information an OSC investigation might yield. The court recognized that DOJ’s decision not to produce records is the immediate cause of any FOIA-related informational injury. To link that injury to OSC, AFL needed to show a realistic, non-speculative sequence: an OSC investigation would lead OSC to seek corrective or disciplinary action (under 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214–1215); the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would impose sanctions or order corrective relief; and DOJ would then comply and disclose the records. The court found this chain too speculative: it required successive discretionary choices by two independent actors (OSC and MSPB), followed by unilateral voluntary action by DOJ. Because standing “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts,” the causal link failed.

When applying the independent-actor rule, focus on two questions: (1) Would the third party’s action be compelled by law or purely discretionary? (2) Would the court’s judgment bind the third party or only the defendant? If the third party’s action is mandatory under law or directly ordered by the court’s judgment, the chain is less speculative and traceability may be plausible. By contrast, if the third party has unfettered discretion, courts will require a much more direct nexus between the defendant’s conduct and the injury.

## FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine: redressability and independent choices

The Supreme Court’s decision in FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine (2024) is an important contemporary application of the independent-actor/redressability concern. Although the case concerned FDA regulation of mifepristone, the Court articulated a general rule about causal speculation: when redressability depends on “the unfettered choices” of nonparty actors, that dependence undermines standing. The opinion emphasized that plaintiffs must do more than articulate a chain of contingencies; they must show that a favorable ruling would make the relief “substantially likely” or otherwise “very likely” to occur, not merely possible.

The D.C. Circuit invoked the Alliance principle in America First Legal Foundation, relying on the Court’s admonition that causation “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” That passage was central to the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that AFL’s claim of informational injury was not redressable: even if OSC had been ordered to investigate, the ensuing remedial steps that would obligate DOJ to produce records remained the product of contingent choices by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ.

Alliance underscores that courts will examine the nature of the intervening choices. If a favorable judgment would change only the legal landscape in ways that might persuade an independent agency or tribunal to act, that possibility is usually insufficient. By contrast, if the judgment would alter legal obligations in a way that directly compels third-party action—either because the third party is legally bound by the decision or because the decision would bind an entity that can command the third party’s compliance—redressability is more likely to be found.

For practitioners, Alliance is a reminder that pleading and proof on redressability must attend to the quality of the intervening links. Allegations that a judicial ruling “would persuade” another institution to act are weak; allegations that the ruling would lawfully require that institution to act—or would directly subject it to judicial oversight—are stronger.

## Haaland v. Brackeen: judgments bind parties, not nonparties persuaded by reasoning

Haaland v. Brackeen (2023) contributes a distinct but related principle: when determining redressability, courts consider whether a favorable judgment would bind the relevant actors, not whether it might “persuade” nonparties to act. The Court made clear that the measure of redressability is the court’s ability to alter the legal relations of the parties before it, not its ability to emit a persuasive opinion that induces third parties to change behavior.

The D.C. Circuit relied on that language in AFL: the opinion quoted the Haaland passage to explain why AFL’s attempt to rely on the possibility that OSC’s mandated investigation might “persuade” MSPB or DOJ to act could not satisfy redressability. An order against OSC would bind OSC, but it would not bind DOJ or the MSPB; whether those nonparties would respond by producing documents or imposing sanctions would remain a matter of sovereign discretion beyond the court’s control.

The Haaland principle is particularly important where plaintiffs attempt to vindicate rights by compelling courts to issue purely declaratory or persuasive rulings intended to induce action by nonparties. Such litigants must show that the relief would have some binding effect on the actor that actually controls the injury. If not, the claim faces dismissal for lack of redressability.

## Clapper v. Amnesty International and Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights: causal attenuation

The modern jurisprudence of speculative injury and attenuated causation finds deep roots in Clapper v. Amnesty International USA (2013) and Simon v. Eastern Kentucky Welfare Rights Organization (1976). Both decisions teach that the Article III injury-in-fact requirement cannot be satisfied by conjectural fears or by claims that rest on chains of speculative events.

In Clapper, the Supreme Court rejected standing for plaintiffs who feared surveillance targetting because their claimed future injury relied on a series of independent, discretionary decisions by government actors and foreign entities. The Court underscored that a chain of possibilities, each contingent on future events that may never occur, cannot substitute for a concrete and imminent injury. Similarly, in Simon the Court made clear that a plaintiff cannot manufacture standing through speculative future benefits that depend on governmental actions not yet taken.

America First Legal Foundation’s standing problems echo these precedents: AFL’s alleged informational injury arising from DOJ’s refusal to search employee emails would have required a chain of events—OSC investigation → OSC enforcement action → MSPB action → DOJ compliance—each link contingent on independent judgments. The D.C. Circuit applied the Clapper/Simon logic to conclude that the causal and redressability elements were not met.

These cases teach a practical rule: if a plaintiff seeks to vindicate a present injury by pointing at future, uncertain events, she must show that those future events are not mere possibilities but are substantially likely or legally compelled. Absent that showing, courts will treat the causal story as an unlawful fiction crafted to create standing where none exists.

## Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC: ‘very likely spur’ standard in multi-step causal chains

The D.C. Circuit’s recent decision in Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC (2025) offers a formulation that bridges the traceability and redressability inquiries for multi-step chains. In Hecate the court explained that when redress depends on a sequence of steps, the plaintiff must show that a favorable decision would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury. That standard demands a degree of likelihood higher than mere possibility but lower than mathematical certainty.

In America First Legal Foundation, the D.C. Circuit applied the “very likely spur” formulation and found it unmet. AFL’s narrative required OSC to initiate and pursue enforcement, the MSPB to impose a corrective or disciplinary remedy, and DOJ subsequently to produce documents. Each step involved discretionary judgments; AFL did not (and could not) plausibly allege that those steps were “very likely” to occur in the event of a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate. Thus, under Hecate’s articulation, the redressability prong failed.

The practical import of Hecate is twofold. First, Hecate recognizes that courts should not be hyper-technical in rejecting multi-step causal stories where the sequence is predictable and likely—for example, when a statutory procedure requires certain downstream consequences. Second, it preserves constitutional limits by insisting that plaintiffs show a meaningful probability of redress, not a speculative hope. Litigants seeking to rely on multi-stage causal narratives should therefore plead facts demonstrating how a court’s order would realistically trigger the subsequent steps (e.g., because a statute creates a mandatory enforcement path or because the downstream actor’s discretion is meaningfully constrained).

## Heckler v. Chaney: enforcement discretion as a barrier to judicial compulsion

Heckler v. Chaney (1985) addresses a different but related barrier to standing and judicial relief: the presumption that agency decisions not to enforce are committed to agency discretion and thus generally not subject to judicial review. The doctrine is not framed as a standing rule per se, but the practical consequence is the same: when relief would require a court to command an agency to prosecute, enforce, or otherwise exercise its statutory powers, Heckler warns that courts are ordinarily ill-suited and often lack statutory authority to intrude.

Heckler established that an agency’s decision to decline enforcement is presumptively unreviewable because it typically involves the exercise of broad discretion grounded in agency expertise and policy judgments. Courts will entertain review only where a statute provides meaningful standards to constrain agency action or where an agency’s refusal amounts to an abdication of a ministerial duty. Thus, when a plaintiff’s theory of redress depends on compelling an enforcement agency to act, the combined effect of Heckler and standing doctrine can be decisive: even if the causal link to the plaintiff’s injury were traceable, a plaintiff may still be unable to obtain relief because courts lack the power to force the agency’s hand.

The AFL case illustrates the Heckler problem indirectly. AFL sought to compel OSC to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). But even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, subsequent redress would depend on OSC’s choice to pursue corrective or disciplinary actions (and on MSPB’s further action). To the extent that OSC’s enforcement choices are discretionary, Heckler supports the idea that courts should hesitate to compel specific prosecutorial or enforcement responses. Moreover, even affirmative judicial orders against OSC do not bind the MSPB or DOJ. Heckler thus reinforces the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that AFL’s injury was not likely to be redressed by judicial relief aimed at OSC.

Two clarifications are worth emphasizing. First, Heckler does not render all agency inaction immune from review; where statutory language supplies clear standards for enforcement or where an agency refuses to exercise a nondiscretionary duty, courts may intervene. Second, Heckler concerns reviewability and relief, distinct from Article III standing; nonetheless, the doctrines interact because a plaintiff who cannot obtain meaningful relief against the actor whose conduct causes the injury will often fail redressability as well.

## Applying these principles to multi-agency remedial paths (OSC → MSPB → agency compliance)

The OSC → MSPB → agency compliance sequence presented in America First Legal Foundation is a useful case-study for applying the doctrines described above. The plaintiff sought to compel OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholding and argued that such an investigation would produce the information it sought. The court rejected that argument for traceability and redressability reasons: DOJ’s FOIA decisions, not OSC’s investigatory posture, were the immediate cause of any informational deprivation; the causal chain to connect OSC’s inaction to AFL’s injury required a series of discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ; and the court’s prospective judgment would bind only OSC, not the other actors.

From a doctrinal standpoint, litigators should analyze similar multi-agency scenarios on three axes.

- Which actor actually controls the conduct that causes the injury? If the defendant is not the actor whose behavior produces the injury, the plaintiff must explain why relief against the defendant would nevertheless eliminate the injury. If that explanation depends on the discretionary acts of nonparty agencies, standing will be difficult to establish.

- Are the intervening actors’ responses legally compelled or discretionary? If downstream action is mandated by statute or the judicial order would bind the downstream actor, the chain is less speculative. If the intervening choices are discretionary (as when an enforcement agency may decline to prosecute or an adjudicative body like the MSPB retains remedial discretion), courts will require strong evidence that the favorable judgment would make downstream relief “very likely.”

- Would a judicial order actually change the legal relations of the parties who cause the injury? Haaland reminds us that persuasive authority is not the same as binding authority. A court’s ruling against one agency may alter the legal environment, but unless it imposes a duty on the agency that controls the injury, the judgment may not redress the plaintiff.

Applying these points to OSC→MSPB→agency compliance demonstrates why AFL’s theory fell short. Even assuming OSC had a legal duty under § 1216 to investigate, AFL did not plausibly show that an OSC investigation would predictably produce MSPB sanctions or compel DOJ disclosure. MSPB’s remedial decisions and DOJ’s FOIA choices involve independent exercise of discretion. The court emphasized that redressability turns on how judgments bind parties, not on how persuasive the court’s reasoning might be; that distinction proved fatal to AFL.

There are permutations in which a multi-step path will suffice. For example, suppose statute X requires Agency A to investigate and to refer proven violations to Agency B for enforcement, and Agency B must impose a specific remedial consequence upon Agency A’s referral. If a plaintiff sues Agency A for failing to investigate, a court order compelling Agency A to follow statute X may directly set in motion a series of non-discretionary steps that make redress virtually certain. Under those circumstances, the chain is not speculative, and traceability/redressability problems are less severe. The difference is that the statutory structure removes or constrains the independent discretion that otherwise produces attenuation.

Practical litigation strategies therefore include: (1) suing the actor who actually controls the contested behavior (e.g., suing DOJ directly for FOIA delays or denials rather than an agency that may investigate DOJ); (2) pleading facts that show downstream actions are mandatory or very likely (e.g., statutory referral or non-discretionary remedy rules); (3) seeking relief that directly binds the actor who controls the injury (court orders directed at the entity whose inaction is claimed to cause the harm); or (4) pressing statutory entitlements to information where the statute confers a direct informational right that an award of relief would vindicate (a route sometimes available in “informational standing” cases).

Conclusion

The interplay of independent-actor, causation, and redressability doctrines places significant limits on standing when a plaintiff’s injury depends on a speculative sequence of discretionary acts by nonparties or by multiple agencies. Clapper and Simon teach that courts will not entertain claims premised on conjectural future events; Haaland emphasizes that judicial relief binds the parties before the court, not nonparties whom the court might persuade; Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Hecate reinforce the need for a realistic showing that a favorable judgment will “very likely spur” the remedial steps that will redress the injury; and Heckler signals the judiciary’s limited power to compel enforcement actions. America First Legal Foundation v. Greer synthesizes these principles in a practical setting: to survive a standing challenge when the alleged injury flows through multiple public actors, a plaintiff must do more than hypothesize a chain of events—she must plausibly allege a direct, non-speculative causal pathway from the defendant’s conduct to the injury and a realistic prospect that judicial relief will remedy that injury.

# The Road to the D.C. Circuit: Facts and Procedural History of AFL v. Greer

This chapter traces the factual and procedural path that brought America First Legal Foundation’s (AFL) challenge to the D.C. Circuit. The dispute arises at the intersection of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and the investigatory authority of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC). The issues are fact-bound and statutory, but the litigation trajectory reflects recurring themes in administrative and constitutional law: the scope of agency investigatory powers, the relationship between parallel administrative and judicial remedies, and the limits that Article III standing imposes on judicial review of procedural complaints. We first set out AFL’s FOIA request and the policy it alleged; then describe the parallel FOIA litigation and ongoing productions; recount AFL’s petition to OSC and OSC’s statutory response; recount the district court’s hybrid ruling and remand; and finally narrate OSC’s renewed refusal, the entry of final judgment, and how the court of appeals came to resolve standing on its own motion.

## AFL’s FOIA request to DOJ and the alleged ‘consent to search emails’ policy

AFL’s underlying public-records request to the Department of Justice (DOJ) focused on emails bearing on judicial recusals. Specifically, AFL requested communications between an outside attorney and the Civil Division of DOJ that AFL believed would illuminate decisionmaking relevant to recusal issues. DOJ’s initial response to that FOIA request is the fulcrum of AFL’s complaint: according to AFL, the Civil Division refused to conduct searches of employees’ emails unless the individual employee consented to that search. AFL characterized that response not as a one-off refusal but as evidence of a broader DOJ practice or policy—namely, a policy of not searching employee email accounts without the employees’ consent.

That allegation implicates a discrete doctrinal point under FOIA. FOIA obligates agencies to conduct reasonable searches for records responsive to a request; when records are held in employee email accounts, the agency’s obligation typically extends to reasonable efforts to locate them. The question whether an agency may condition searches on individual employee consent is thus legally significant: if an agency legitimately refuses to search such accounts, requesters may be deprived of information that FOIA is designed to make public. AFL’s framing of DOJ’s reply as a systemic policy rather than a mere ad hoc response is why AFL sought both judicial relief under FOIA and administrative enforcement through OSC.

## Parallel FOIA litigation and ongoing record production

Almost at once, AFL pursued the alternative judicial path FOIA ordinarily provides: it filed a FOIA lawsuit against DOJ to compel production of the requested records. That parallel litigation matters because FOIA contains its own remedial mechanism and makes OSC’s involvement contingent in certain situations. Under FOIA’s fee-and-judgment provision, see 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(E)–(F), certain judicial remedies (including a court’s finding of arbitrary or capricious withholding, plus an award of attorneys’ fees) can trigger a mandatory OSC inquiry into the conduct of the individual government employees who were “primarily responsible” for the withholding. The involvement of the courts, awards of fees, and an explicit judicial finding about arbitrary withholding thus operate as a kind of statutory trigger for OSC activity under FOIA’s separate enforcement provision.

Factually, DOJ did begin to produce records responsive to AFL’s FOIA request; production was ongoing at the time of the OSC complaint and the district-court proceedings. Importantly, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion notes that the sufficiency of DOJ’s production was not the issue presented on appeal—AFL’s challenge instead concerned whether OSC had an independent duty to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy under the Civil Service Reform Act provisions that create OSC’s investigatory powers. But the existence of parallel FOIA litigation and continuing agency production influenced the parties’ positions: OSC relied on FOIA’s own enforcement provision to justify declining to investigate, and AFL emphasized that judicial remedies had not yet been obtained and that OSC’s investigatory authority under § 1216(a)(3) should not depend on FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates.

## AFL’s petition to OSC invoking § 1216(a)(3) and OSC’s reliance on § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates

AFL filed a petition with OSC alleging that DOJ’s asserted practice of declining to search employee emails without consent constituted an “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552” of Title 5—i.e., a FOIA violation. AFL invoked 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), a provision enacted as part of the Civil Service Reform Act that instructs OSC to “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” Section 1216(c) further authorizes OSC to investigate alleged FOIA violations and “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Read in isolation, these provisions appear to impose a mandatory investigatory duty on OSC when it receives allegations that an agency has committed an arbitrary FOIA withholding.

OSC declined to open an investigation. Its stated reason is the heart of the statutory dispute in this case: OSC read FOIA’s separate enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), as imposing three prerequisites before OSC must (or may) initiate disciplinary or corrective proceedings under FOIA: (1) a court must order production of unlawfully withheld records; (2) the court must award attorneys’ fees against the agency; and (3) the court must specifically question whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.” OSC concluded that these three judicial predicates were not satisfied in AFL’s case and therefore that it lacked authority under FOIA to open the kind of enforcement action FOIA contemplates.

AFL rejected OSC’s reading. It argued that § 1216 imposes an independent, mandatory duty to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings even when the FOIA-specific prerequisites in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) have not been met. The statutory text is susceptible to both readings: the “shall … conduct an investigation” language of § 1216(a)(3) suggests a nondiscretionary duty, while § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) looks like an explicit FOIA trigger for OSC’s disciplinary powers in cases where the courts have already acted. The statutory tension between the Civil Service Reform Act’s grant of investigatory power and FOIA’s own enforcement clause thus produced a straight statutory-interpretation dispute that OSC resolved in favor of a narrower, FOIA-contingent role.

## District court’s conclusions: legal error on § 1216 scope; discretion under § 1216(c); remand without ordering investigation

When AFL sued OSC in the District of Columbia, the district court confronted a mixed question of statutory interpretation and judicial review of agency action. The court concluded—contrary to OSC’s position—that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC’s authority under § 1216. In other words, the district court held that OSC was legally mistaken to read FOIA’s three-circumstance trigger as exclusive. The district court viewed § 1216’s “shall … conduct an investigation” language as establishing at least some independent duty to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholding, regardless of whether a FOIA lawsuit had produced the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates.

Yet the district court did not simply order OSC to open an investigation. It recognized another dimension of OSC’s statutory regime: § 1216(c) authorizes OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The court read that cross-reference as preserving OSC’s discretion over whether and how to apply § 1214’s procedural and remedial apparatus when the initial statutory hook derives from § 1216. The court saw OSC’s internal choice about whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary actions—and how to structure any investigation—as entrusted to agency competence and discretion. Because of that discretion, the district court declined to compel a particular remedial course. Instead it remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration in light of the court’s legal interpretation of § 1216.

Two points about the district court’s approach deserve emphasis. First, the court performed the familiar judicial task of correcting a legal error: it set aside OSC’s statutory interpretation that would have tied § 1216 to FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers. Second, the court exercised remedial restraint: it declined to intrude into the agency’s prosecutorial and enforcement judgment by directing OSC to take a particular enforcement step, instead remanding for the agency to reconsider consistent with the court’s statutory ruling. That remedial posture is common in administrative-law litigation where courts find statutory misinterpretations but recognize the agency’s prosecutorial discretion and technical competence in deciding how to proceed.

## OSC’s renewed refusal; entry of final judgment; appeal

On remand, OSC again declined to investigate. The agency apparently considered the district court’s statutory ruling but adhered to a decision not to initiate an OSC investigation of DOJ’s FOIA practices. AFL then sought a final judgment in the district court. The district court entered final judgment dismissing AFL’s remaining claims for failure to state a claim. The dispositive dismissal at the district-court level concluded the case there, and AFL appealed to the D.C. Circuit.

The appellate record reflects that the district court’s disposition left intact two unresolved statutory tensions: whether § 1216 imposes a truly mandatory duty to investigate FOIA complaints in every instance, and whether OSC must—when it does investigate under § 1216—apply the procedural notice and other provisions of § 1214. The district court had answered the first question against OSC but declined to second-guess the agency’s exercise of enforcement discretion. When OSC again declined to act, AFL renewed its attack in the D.C. Circuit.

## De novo standard of review and the court’s sua sponte standing inquiry and supplemental briefing

On appeal, the D.C. Circuit announced that its review of the district court’s legal determinations would be de novo. That is the standard for pure questions of statutory interpretation and for many legal determinations about agency action: appellate courts give no deference to a district court’s statutory interpretations and instead resolve the governing legal questions anew. But before reaching the merits, the court raised a threshold jurisdictional issue sua sponte—whether AFL had Article III standing. Standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite that federal courts must address even if the parties omit it. As the D.C. Circuit explained, Article III confines courts to “Cases” and “Controversies,” and any litigant must show a concrete injury that is fairly traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by the relief sought. See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992).

The D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing on standing because neither party had initially pressed the point. That procedural step is significant: courts can raise standing independently because it bears on subject-matter jurisdiction; and appellate courts are especially exacting in distinguishing a procedural statutory right asserted in a vacuum from a cognizable Article III injury. The court recited the governing framework for cases involving asserted procedural injuries: where a plaintiff alleges that a statutory procedure afforded it was denied, courts may relax the strict requirements of imminence and redressability, but not the core elements of injury-in-fact and causation. See Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488 (2009); Center for Law & Educ. v. Department of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152 (D.C. Cir. 2005). Under that line of authorities, AFL could proceed only if it showed (1) that a procedural right was violated, and (2) that the violation harmed a concrete, particularized interest that the statute at issue was designed to protect.

Two distinct standing theories framed AFL’s supplemental briefing and the D.C. Circuit’s analysis. First, AFL claimed that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused it a concrete informational injury because DOJ refused to produce the records AFL had requested under FOIA. Second, AFL argued that OSC’s failure to investigate deprived it of the information and notices that OSC would provide during a § 1216 investigation—i.e., that AFL had an informational entitlement to the fruits or at least the procedural outputs of an OSC inquiry.

The court treated each theory in turn and found both insufficient. For the first theory, the court stressed the causation and redressability problems. Any present withholding-of-information injury is traceable directly to DOJ’s decisions about whether and how to comply with FOIA, not to OSC’s decision not to investigate. More importantly, to show that OSC’s action was the but-for (or at least proximate) cause of the alleged deprivation would require a speculative chain: OSC might investigate; OSC might decide to seek corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) might impose sanctions; DOJ might, as a result, decide to produce the records. That chain depends on the independent choices of multiple actors (OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ), and courts generally cannot base standing on speculation about the uncabined choices of third parties. The court invoked the principle that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts,” citing recent Supreme Court authority for that proposition (among earlier cases, Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), underscores the non-reviewability of certain exercises of prosecutorial discretion). Because a favorable judgment directing OSC to reconsider would not “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s informational injury, the D.C. Circuit concluded AFL’s first theory failed.

The second theory—informational injury in the form of a right to receive OSC’s investigative notices and results—met a different defect: absence of entitlement. The Supreme Court and this circuit have recognized that a plaintiff can suffer an Article III injury when a statute entitles it to receive certain information and the government withholds that information. See FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998); Public Citizen v. U.S. Department of Justice, 491 U.S. 440 (1989). But § 1216, the D.C. Circuit explained, does not create a statutory entitlement to OSC’s investigative outputs. Even if § 1216 mandates an OSC investigation when allegations of FOIA withholding are presented, it does not compel OSC to provide to the complainant the same notices and periodic reports that § 1214 requires when investigating “prohibited personnel practices.” AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to apply § 1214’s procedural requirements in the § 1216 context is treated as discretionary in existing precedent—Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), being a leading example. Because AFL could point to no statute that unequivocally entitles it to particular investigatory information from OSC, the court found no basis for an informational injury-in-fact.

Synthesizing these holdings, the D.C. Circuit concluded that AFL failed to demonstrate a concrete injury traceable to OSC that would be redressed by a favorable ruling. Because standing is jurisdictional, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. In short, the court never reached the underlying merits of the statutory dispute about the full scope of § 1216 or the remedial consequences of a finding that DOJ had a policy of conditioning email searches on employee consent.

## Teaching points and doctrinal afterthoughts

Several doctrinal lessons flow from the factual and procedural story of AFL v. Greer. First, litigants who pursue parallel administrative complaints and judicial FOIA suits must care for sequencing and causation. OSC’s enforcement authority walks a statutory tightrope: § 1216 appears to require OSC attention to alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides an explicit, court-centered enforcement trigger in some circumstances. How those provisions interact is a pure statutory-interpretation question, but for Article III purposes it matters whether a denial of an administrative investigatory procedure is the proximate cause of an informational deprivation.

Second, standing doctrine imposes substantial barriers for plaintiffs who assert procedural or informational injuries. The Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit have clarified that violations of procedural statutory rights do not automatically confer Article III standing unless the plaintiff shows that the procedural deprivation has harmed a concrete, particularized interest. Summers v. Earth Island Institute and the D.C. Circuit’s own cases place the burden on plaintiffs to show both causation and redressability—in practice, often a hard task when the chain of causation depends on decisions by other agencies or on the exercise of agency prosecutorial discretion. Speculation about how an agency might exercise enforcement tools is typically insufficient.

Third, when statutes create investigatory powers rather than explicit individual entitlements to information, claimants who seek to convert those powers into judicially enforceable rights confront an additional hurdle: informational standing often depends on a statutory entitlement to the particular information sought. Absent such an entitlement, courts are reluctant to hold that a denial of investigatory process itself produces a concrete injury.

Finally, the procedural posture of the district court—correcting a legal error but remanding rather than ordering a specific remedy—illustrates judicial prudence in agency oversight. Courts will sometimes recognize statutory misinterpretation but nevertheless leave agencies room to decide how to proceed, especially where prosecutorial discretion is implicated. But that prudence does not immunize the issue from appellate scrutiny when jurisdictional defects are present; appellate courts must ensure litigants have Article III standing before resolving statutory questions.

For students preparing to apply these principles to new fact patterns, two practical rules of thumb are useful. If a plaintiff’s claimed injury depends on the independent actions of other agencies or tribunals, ask whether the chain from the defendant’s conduct to the asserted harm is speculative. If a plaintiff claims an informational injury rooted in an administrative procedure, search the statute (and regulations) carefully for any express entitlement to particular notices, reports, or information; absent a clear statutory entitlement, courts are likely to find no informational injury sufficient to invoke Article III jurisdiction.

# AFL v. Greer: The D.C. Circuit’s Standing Analysis

This chapter uses America First Legal Foundation v. Greer to teach how Article III standing limits suits that seek to compel administrative investigatory action. The opinion illustrates the interaction of three familiar standing concepts: injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability; the rule that a bare procedural-right deprivation cannot create standing in the absence of a concrete interest (the Summers rule); and the limits on “informational standing” where a statute does not create an entitlement to receive information. The opinion is particularly useful because the plaintiff asserted two distinct theories of informational injury—(1) deprivation of the FOIA records it sought from the Department of Justice, and (2) deprivation of notices and other information that might flow from an OSC investigation—and the court rejected both for separate doctrinal reasons. Students should study how the court parses causation and redressability when the claimed chain of causation runs through multiple independent decisionmakers and how it treats claims that rest on alleged procedural entitlements under statutes that do not expressly create them.

## Background: OSC, FOIA, and the Claim

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is an independent federal agency empowered by the Civil Service Reform Act to investigate prohibited personnel practices and to seek corrective or disciplinary action. Relevant here, 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) directs OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, FOIA withholding. Section 1216(c) then authorizes OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” Separately, FOIA itself, in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), requires OSC to initiate disciplinary proceedings when a court orders FOIA production, awards attorneys’ fees, and specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding.

In the underlying facts, America First Legal Foundation (AFL) submitted a FOIA request to the DOJ Civil Division. AFL also asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s asserted policy of not searching employee emails for responsive records without employee consent, invoking § 1216(a)(3). OSC declined to investigate on the ground that FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) conditions were not met; the district court held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not limit OSC’s authority under § 1216 but nonetheless concluded that the decision whether to apply § 1214’s procedures in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary and remanded. After OSC again declined to investigate, AFL sought judicial relief. The D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits because it concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing. The remainder of this chapter explains that ruling and its doctrinal basis.

## Procedural rights, Summers, and “procedural rights in vacuo”

AFL framed its standing claim as a deprivation of a procedural right—OSC’s statutory obligation to investigate under § 1216. Standing doctrine permits suits that challenge the denial of a procedural protection, but only when the procedural right is intended to protect a concrete, particularized interest and the plaintiff’s asserted injury flows from the statutory violation. The Supreme Court’s decision in Summers v. Earth Island Institute is the touchstone: the denial of a procedural right “in vacuo,” divorced from any concrete interest the procedure was meant to protect, is insufficient to create Article III standing. Summers thus requires more than a bare statutory duty; plaintiffs must show that the procedural deprivation harms some concrete, cognizable interest.

The D.C. Circuit applied Summers at the threshold. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate injured it in two concrete ways (the two theories discussed below). The court first emphasized the legal principle that procedural-right injuries receive a relaxed (but not eliminated) treatment of imminence and redressability only when the procedural right is designed to protect a threatened concrete interest. Thus AFL could not rest on a mere statutory command to investigate; it had to show that OSC’s refusal caused an injury to a concrete interest that the investigation procedure was designed to protect. This framing guided the court’s analysis of both of AFL’s asserted injuries.

Students should note two lessons from this part of the opinion. First, alleging a statutory procedural entitlement (e.g., “OSC must investigate”) is not enough by itself. Plaintiffs must connect the procedural right to a concrete interest—typically an informational or economic interest—that was harmed by the agency’s failure to follow the procedure. Second, the “relaxed” standard for procedural injuries does not collapse Lujan’s other requirements: injury-in-fact, causation, and some showing of redressability still must be plausibly alleged.

## Theory One — The FOIA Records Claim: Traceability and a speculative multi-step causal chain

AFL’s first theory was straightforward: OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ under § 1216 prevented AFL from obtaining the FOIA records that DOJ had withheld. The D.C. Circuit rejected this theory because it failed on both traceability (causation) and redressability grounds.

Traceability requires that the plaintiff’s injury be “fairly traceable” to the defendant’s conduct, not to independent actors. Here the withheld records were in DOJ’s possession and any informational injury from DOJ’s refusal to disclose is, on its face, traceable to DOJ’s conduct, not to OSC’s decision not to investigate. AFL’s attempt to link OSC’s refusal to the withheld records required a multi-step causal chain. As the court summarized it, AFL would have to show that (a) if OSC had investigated under § 1216, it might have initiated corrective or disciplinary proceedings under §§ 1214 or 1215; (b) those proceedings might have led the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to impose sanctions or take corrective action against the responsible DOJ employees; and (c) DOJ might then have decided to produce the requested FOIA records. Each link required a discretionary choice by an independent actor—OSC’s decisions about whether to pursue enforcement and how to do so, MSPB’s decisions about remedies, and DOJ’s decision whether to produce documents. Under controlling Article III precedent, such a chain is too speculative to establish traceability.

This part of the court’s reasoning is an excellent demonstration of how courts analyze causation when the defendant’s conduct is not the most proximate cause of the injury. The court relied on the principle that causation “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” The hypothetical chain required multiple independent choices by agencies not party to the litigation (the MSPB and DOJ), and the court appropriately treated that hypothetical sequence as insufficiently direct to satisfy Article III’s causation requirement.

For students, the key takeaways are practical. When a plaintiff seeks judicial review of a refusal to act by one administrative body and claims that refusal indirectly causes a harm because it would change the behavior of a different agency, courts require a non-speculative link between the challenged action and the disputed harm. Allegations that the challenged agency’s action “might” prompt other agencies to act will usually not suffice. Plaintiffs must either sue the actor that controls the information or show a direct chain of causation requiring fewer discretionary actor choices.

## Independent actors, enforcement discretion, and Heckler v. Chaney

Closely related to the causation problems is the role of enforcement discretion. Heckler v. Chaney stands for the general proposition that an agency’s decision not to enforce is presumptively unreviewable because it often involves policy and resource allocation judgments. The AFL opinion invokes this doctrine in two ways.

First, even if OSC could be ordered to investigate under § 1216, the courts cannot ordinarily control how OSC exercises enforcement discretion in subsequent steps—what remedies it seeks, whether it pursues disciplinary action, or how aggressively it litigates. The speculative path from OSC’s alleged statutory violation to DOJ’s production of documents depends upon OSC making particular enforcement choices that a court would not necessarily be able to compel or predict.

Second, the MSPB and DOJ are independent entities whose future choices could not be bound by a judicial order directed at OSC. That independence amplifies the speculative nature of the causal chain: even if OSC were ordered to investigate, that order would not bind the MSPB or DOJ, and the court would have no reason to assume those actors would react in a way that redresses AFL’s FOIA injury.

Heckler thus supplies a doctrinal backdrop that undercuts efforts to bootstrap standing through threatened enforcement or remedial consequences that rest on discretionary agency choices. For students, the lesson is twofold: (1) the presence of other decisionmakers in the chain of events that would redress the plaintiff’s injury is a barrier to establishing causation and redressability, and (2) when alleged consequential events turn on discretionary agency enforcement decisions, courts are especially reluctant to treat those events as legally compelled or sufficiently likely to confer standing.

## Redressability: binding relief versus persuasive effects on nonparties

The redressability inquiry asks whether a favorable court decision would likely remedy the plaintiff’s injury. The D.C. Circuit emphasized that redressability typically focuses on how a judicial order would bind the defendant, not on whether the court’s reasoning might persuade third parties to act. The opinion invoked recent Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit formulations to show that speculative hopes that an order against OSC “might persuade” the MSPB or DOJ to produce records are insufficient.

This is an important doctrinal nuance. Even if OSC’s investigation could plausibly exert pressure on DOJ, the relevant question is whether a judicial order would make the chain of remedial events sufficiently likely. Absent a mechanism by which an OSC order would bind the MSPB or force DOJ to disclose, redressability fails. The court therefore rejected AFL’s contention that a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its FOIA injury. The appropriate comparison is not between “might” and “might not,” but between a realistic likelihood that the relief will remedy the injury and mere speculative or persuasive effects on nonparties. As Haaland v. Brackeen instructs, persuasive effects on nonparties are not a substitute for legally binding relief.

For practitioners and students, this reinforces the practical rule that the proper defendant in a suit for information is often the entity that controls the information. If the plaintiff wants a court-ordered production of records, the plaintiff should generally sue the record-possessing agency under FOIA rather than seek to compel some other agency to act in ways that might influence the record-possessing agency.

## Theory Two — Information from an OSC Investigation: informational standing and statutory entitlement

AFL’s second theory sought to ground standing in an informational interest: it argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of notices and other information that OSC might provide as part of a § 1216 investigation. The court addressed this claim under the established framework for “informational standing.” The Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff suffers an informational injury if a statute entitles it to receive information and that entitlement is denied. Cases like Public Citizen v. Department of Justice, FEC v. Akins, and Zivotofsky-in-the-circuit (and the D.C. Circuit’s Feld Entertainment decision) are the touchstones: a clear statutory entitlement to information creates a cognizable injury when the information is withheld.

The D.C. Circuit held that § 1216—even if construed to require OSC to investigate—does not create a statutory entitlement for members of the public to receive investigatory notices or other information arising from such investigations. Section 1216 mandates that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of FOIA-withholding allegations, but it does not expressly provide that complainants or requesters are entitled to particular notices or interlocutory information about the investigation. The notice and procedural provisions that do confer such rights appear in § 1214, which governs “prohibited personnel practices,” and those procedural entitlements are invoked only if § 1216’s cross-reference to § 1214 is read to require application of § 1214’s procedures. AFL’s theory depended on the proposition that § 1216 both required an investigation and required OSC to give complainants the § 1214 notice protections.

The court rejected that leap. It observed that courts have treated OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation as discretionary. The Sixth Circuit’s decision in Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel is directly on point: Carson held that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations is discretionary rather than mandatory. Because § 1216 does not itself create an entitlement to the § 1214 notice procedures (and because the procedural importation of § 1214 into § 1216 investigations is discretionary), AFL could not show that a statutory entitlement to informational notices existed. Without that statutory entitlement, AFL lacked the type of informational injury recognized by Akins, Public Citizen, and Feld. As a consequence, AFL failed to show a concrete informational injury traceable to OSC’s refusal to investigate.

This portion of the decision illustrates the particularity required for informational standing. A plaintiff asserting informational injury must identify a statutory provision that not only contemplates relevant information but affirmatively entitles the plaintiff to receive it. Absent such a textual entitlement or a mandatory procedural command creating a right to notice, an informational injury claim will typically fail.

## Failure of injury-in-fact and redressability under the informational-framing

Even were one to accept that a § 1216 investigation could give rise to § 1214-like notices, the court stressed that AFL did not plead a statutory entitlement to those notices and therefore could not show a concrete injury-in-fact. And even if a court could order OSC to investigate, such an order would not necessarily entitle AFL to the § 1214 notices, because the decision whether to apply § 1214 procedures to a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Thus redressability fails in the same way: a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would not be likely to redress AFL’s alleged informational injury (notices) because the statute does not obligate OSC to provide those specific notices in § 1216 investigations.

In short, AFC’s informational-standing theory failed both on injury-in-fact—because AFL lacked an entitlement to the information—and on redressability—because a judicial order would not be likely to produce the information AFL claimed to want. This double failure illustrates how informational-standing claims can founder unless they rest on a clear statutory promise of disclosure.

## Practical implications and doctrinal consequences

AFL v. Greer is a clear example of the limits Article III imposes when plaintiffs seek relief that depends on discretionary acts of multiple agencies. It teaches the following practical points:

- When the injury arises from an agency’s withholding of records, the straightforward route is a direct suit against the record-holding agency under FOIA. Indirect suits against other agencies that might influence production face steep causation and redressability hurdles.

- Procedural statutory duties (e.g., “investigate”) do not automatically create standing. Plaintiffs must show that the procedural duty protects a concrete interest that has been injured, consistent with Summers.

- Informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the information claimed to be withheld. Courts will not create an informational right from a statute that merely authorizes discretionary procedures.

- Where redress requires action by independent agencies not parties to the litigation, courts will generally treat the chain of causation as too speculative to support standing. Persuasive effects on nonparties are not an adequate substitute for binding relief.

- The interplay between statutory cross-references (here, § 1216’s cross-reference to § 1214) is often dispositive. Whether the cross-reference imports mandatory procedures or permissive authorities can determine standing. Here, the D.C. Circuit accepted prior authority treating the importation of § 1214 procedures as discretionary, which undercut any informational entitlement.

For students preparing for litigation practice or exams, the case is a useful problem-solver: given a plaintiff who sues one agency to attempt to change another agency’s behavior, ask whether the alleged injury is traceable to the defendant and whether a judgment would bind the actor who controls the relief. If the answer is no, standing problems will likely doom the claim.

## Conclusion: dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction

Because AFL could not establish an injury-in-fact that was both traceable to OSC’s conduct and likely to be redressed by an order against OSC, the D.C. Circuit concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing. The court vacated the district court’s merits judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The opinion thus reinforces Article III limits on suits seeking to compel administrative investigative action and clarifies the boundaries of “informational” and procedural-right standing in the FOIA/enforcement context.

# Merits Questions Deferred: The Scope of § 1216 and the FOIA Disciplinary Trigger

This chapter examines the difficult substantive questions the district court flagged but the D.C. Circuit ultimately avoided deciding in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer. The panel concluded that the plaintiff lacked Article III standing and therefore vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for want of jurisdiction. Because jurisdictional defects prevented the court from resolving the merits, several important interpretive and remedial questions remain open: whether the FOIA disciplinary-trigger provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), limits or merely supplements the Office of Special Counsel’s independent investigatory duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3); what the statutory phrase “shall … conduct an investigation” in § 1216 means in practice; how § 1216(c)’s cross-reference to §§ 1214 and 1215 affects OSC’s procedural obligations and discretion; whether the Administrative Procedure Act’s (APA) injunction to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld” under 5 U.S.C. § 706(1) can be used to compel OSC to investigate; and why Steel Co. requires courts to resolve jurisdiction before reaching those merits questions. The remainder of this chapter explains each of these issues, illustrates the doctrinal tools courts use to analyze them, and shows how the interplay among statutory text, agency discretion, and judicial reviewability produces hard cases like AFL’s.

## Whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits or supplements § 1216(a)(3) (district court’s view versus OSC’s position)

The core statutory tension in AFL’s suit arises from two different statutory provisions that appear to speak to the same subject—OSC’s role in responding to alleged FOIA withholding. Section 1216(a)(3) provides in sweeping language that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” Section 1216(c) then authorizes OSC to pursue corrective or disciplinary action “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” In contrast, FOIA contains a narrower, more conditional enforcement provision: § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) prescribes that where a court orders production, awards fees, and “specifically questions whether the agency … acted arbitrarily or capriciously,” OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted” against the responsible officer. The interpretive question is whether the FOIA provision is a freestanding, supplementary enforcement mechanism or whether it defines the exclusive circumstances in which OSC must act for FOIA-related misconduct.

The district court resolved that question in favor of the plaintiff. It read § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as a supplemental trigger: FOIA creates a specific, mandatory referral duty in the narrow circumstances listed there, but those circumstances do not exhaust OSC’s independent obligation under § 1216(a)(3) to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholding. That is, the court read § 1216(a)(3)’s unqualified command—“shall … conduct an investigation”—as imposing a broader, statutory investigatory duty that applies even when FOIA’s own forum-triggering conditions are not satisfied. Under that view, OSC’s categorical refusal to investigate FOIA policies or practices on the ground that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is the only available enforcement path would be legally erroneous.

OSC advocated the opposite reading. It argued that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) constitutes the exclusive mechanism for FOIA-driven disciplinary proceedings: FOIA itself prescribes when and how OSC must act with respect to FOIA-related misfeasance, and § 1216 should be read in light of that FOIA structure. On this account the narrower, highly conditioned FOIA referral rule constrains OSC’s FOIA-related duties. OSC’s position can rest on several ordinary-textual interpretive tools: the canon that a specific statutory provision governs a general one when the two conflict (specific beats general), the avoidance of superfluity (if FOIA already provides the mandatory trigger, § 1216(a)(3) should not be read to create duplicative or inconsistent obligations), and contextual reading that harmonizes statutory cross-references.

Both approaches have plausible textual and purposive support. The district court’s reading emphasizes § 1216(a)(3)’s plain language—“shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation”—and Congress’s broader purpose in empowering OSC to police arbitrary withholding as part of the civil-service project. OSC’s reading emphasizes FOIA’s carefully calibrated enforcement layering: FOIA creates a special, court-linked referral when litigation reveals improper withholding and that structure reflects Congress’s intent to link certain disciplinary referrals to judicial findings. The statute does not resolve the matter with a simple rule of construction; instead resolution depends on how one weighs competing interpretive canons and which contextual clues the court deems decisive.

If a court were to decide the issue on the merits, several subsidiary questions would arise. Would § 1216(a)(3) require OSC to investigate any allegation by any member of the public, or only particularized complaints? Would OSC’s duty be triggered the moment a complaint is filed or only after OSC determines a reasonable basis to investigate? How, if at all, do FOIA’s own remedial processes—administrative appeals, litigation, and judicial remedies—interact with OSC’s investigatory role? These practical questions push courts toward assessments of congressional purpose, statutory structure, and the need to preserve agency discretion in enforcement—all considerations that feed into the next topics below.

## The meaning of “shall…conduct an investigation” in § 1216

At first glance the phrase “shall … conduct an investigation” appears mandatory. In statutory construction "shall" commonly imports an obligation: when the statute commands that an officer “shall” perform an act, a court will ordinarily treat that as a nondiscretionary duty. But statutory words do not live in isolation. Context, related provisions, and the broader statutory scheme often alter the force of a “shall” command.

Two interpretive paths lead to different conclusions. One path treats § 1216(a)(3) as imposing a rule-like duty: on receipt of an allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding, OSC must investigate. If a petitioner demonstrates that OSC refused to investigate an allegation within the statute’s scope, the court could hold OSC’s refusal unlawful and could order OSC to initiate an investigation. On that reading “shall” supplies a judicially enforceable duty and the APA’s § 706(1) mandamus remedy could be available to compel it (subject to other limits discussed below).

The alternative path construes “shall” as imposing a policy-directed obligation that is nevertheless bounded by agency judgment about priorities and enforcement choices. Even strong textual commands can be read as subject to agency discretion when they are framed as broad mandates to pursue statutory objectives—especially in contexts where agencies have traditionally exercised prosecutorial or investigatory discretion. Under this approach “shall” signals a general statutory purpose rather than a suite of individually enforceable obligations, and it yields to the commonsense reality that OSC must allocate investigatory resources and determine priorities. The question then becomes whether Congress intended courts to second‑guess those resource-allocation and prioritization decisions.

Deciding between these constructions requires attention to how § 1216 sits within the Civil Service Reform Act and how § 1216(c) qualifies enforcement. The text and structure of § 1216 provide clues: the statute both commands investigation and elaborates how investigations may proceed, by cross-reference to § 1214 and § 1215. Those cross-references could indicate that Congress intended OSC’s investigatory capacity to be meaningful and proceduralized; or they could indicate that Congress left remedial and procedural choices to OSC’s implementation judgment. Because the statutory text supports plausible readings of both kinds, a court resolving the provision confronts a classic choice between an enforcement-oriented reading of “shall” and a discretion-accommodating reading. The Norton line of cases and Heckler v. Chaney further inform how courts treat such commands when plaintiffs seek to compel agency action—topics to which we now turn.

## § 1216(c)’s reference to §§ 1214 and 1215: scope and discretion

Section 1216(c) states that for FOIA-related allegations OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That cross-reference raises two interrelated questions: (1) does § 1216(c) import the full panoply of § 1214 procedural protections and notice requirements into every § 1216 investigation, thus giving complainants a statutory entitlement to particular procedural benefits; and (2) does § 1216(c)’s “may” language preserve agency discretion to decide whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action even if OSC is required to investigate?

On the first question, § 1214 contains a set of procedures that govern how corrective actions are pursued (notification to complainant, progress reports, hearings before the Merit Systems Protection Board, etc.). If § 1216(c) were read to require OSC to adopt all § 1214 procedures as a matter of course when investigating FOIA-related complaints, then the complainant arguably would have a concrete procedural entitlement to notices and timing information that might support “informational” standing claims. But two realities complicate that conclusion. First, § 1216(c) uses permissive language (“may investigate and seek corrective action … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved”): it authorizes OSC to use § 1214 and § 1215 mechanisms; it does not state that OSC must apply § 1214’s every procedural step in every § 1216 investigation. Second, courts have recognized in analogous contexts that Congress’s authorizing cross-references often vest an agency with the option to adopt another regime’s procedures without transforming those procedures into enforceable rights possessed by aggrieved third parties. The Sixth Circuit’s decision in Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel illustrates this point: it held that OSC’s choice whether to follow § 1214’s procedural safeguards in a § 1216 matter is discretionary.

On the second question—the scope of OSC’s discretion with respect to remedial steps—the “may” in § 1216(c) cuts strongly in favor of a remedial discretion reading. Even if § 1216(a)(3) imposes an investigatory duty, § 1216(c) suggests that what happens after investigation—whether OSC seeks corrective action, whether it pursues discipline, whether it follows the precise § 1214 notice regimen—remains largely in the agency’s hands. That leaves open the possibility that a court could compel an investigation but not compel OSC to take a particular enforcement outcome once the investigation is complete. That division between investigatory duty and remedial discretion mirrors other investigative statutes where Congress has required agencies to investigate but left final enforcement judgment to agency expertise.

Heckler v. Chaney intersects with this analysis. Chaney holds that decisions not to take enforcement action are presumptively committed to agency discretion and therefore not subject to judicial review under the APA. If § 1216’s “shall … conduct an investigation” is read as nondiscretionary, Chiltern-like, courts may be able to review OSC’s failure to investigate, but Chaney would counsel against judicial compulsion of particular enforcement results (for instance, directing OSC to bring disciplinary proceedings before the MSPB). Thus the natural reading that emerges from the text can be: § 1216(a)(3) may create an enforceable duty to investigate, while § 1216(c) preserves agency discretion to decide whether, and how, to seek corrective or disciplinary action following that investigation. But a dispositive resolution of that balance—what precisely can be compelled and what remains discretionary—remains unresolved by the courts in this context.

## APA § 706(1): compelling discrete, nondiscretionary action (Norton v. SUWA) and its potential application

When plaintiffs seek to force an agency to act, they ordinarily invoke the APA’s review provision granting courts the power to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.” 5 U.S.C. § 706(1). But the Supreme Court’s decision in Norton v. Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance (SUWA) placed meaningful limits on that power. Norton clarifies two essential points relevant to a § 1216 claim: first, § 706(1) empowers courts to compel agency action only where the statute imposes a “discrete, mandatory duty” upon the agency; second, § 706(1) does not authorize courts to “compel agency action that is committed to agency discretion by law.”

Applying Norton’s framework to a hypothetical § 1216 claim requires a two-part inquiry. The first part asks whether § 1216(a)(3) contains a discrete, mandatory duty: does § 1216 require OSC to investigate “any allegation” of arbitrary FOIA withholding, or is the term “investigate” conceptually porous and dependent on agency judgment? The presence of the word “shall” and the breadth of the phrase “any allegation” point toward a mandatory duty, but statutory context (including § 1216(c)’s permissive “may” and FOIA’s own § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger) complicates that appearance. A court that finds a clear, obligatory investigatory duty under § 1216(a)(3) could conclude that the first Norton criterion is met.

The second Norton limitation—whether the action is “committed to agency discretion”—is often determinative. Even if § 1216(a)(3) is mandatory on its face, Congress may have left substantial implementation choices to OSC; if statutory language and structure indicate that Congress delegated judgment about priorities, timing, and scope to the agency, the action is “committed to agency discretion” and thus beyond § 706(1) compulsion. Norton and Chaney together emphasize that prosecutorial and enforcement decisions—classic exercises of agency judgment—are frequently nonjusticiable. A plaintiff seeking to compel OSC under § 706(1) must therefore show not only a clear duty to investigate but also that Congress did not leave the timing, scope, and processing of such investigations to OSC’s discretion. The permissive language in § 1216(c), and the close analogies to law-enforcement discretion in other contexts, suggest an uphill factual and legal road for a plaintiff.

Norton also requires courts to articulate practical relief. Even when a court finds an agency has unlawfully withheld action, the remedy must be appropriate and appropriate to the right wronged. If a court orders OSC to “investigate,” what does that mean in operational terms? Must OSC follow particular procedures (§ 1214 notice requirements), meet deadlines, or reach particular outcomes? Courts are reluctant to issue open-ended mandamus orders that would involve them in supervising day-to-day agency enforcement choices. For that reason, plaintiffs seeking relief under § 706(1) must both identify a discrete nondiscretionary duty and propose a workable remedy that courts can enforce without impermissibly intruding on agency competence.

In AFL’s case the D.C. Circuit did not reach Norton’s test because it concluded no Article III jurisdiction existed. Were a later court to reach the merits, Norton would be the decisive doctrine shaping the outcome: a finding that § 1216(a)(3) imposes a judicially enforceable, discrete duty would allow the plaintiff to proceed; a finding that OSC retains significant discretion as to whether or how to investigate would foreclose mandamus relief under Norton.

## Steel Co.’s bar on deciding merits without jurisdiction

The procedural posture of America First Legal Foundation v. Greer illustrates a fundamental rule of federal jurisdiction: a federal court cannot resolve the merits of a dispute when it lacks Article III jurisdiction. That rule flows from the Supreme Court’s decision in Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment. Steel Co. establishes that if a court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction the court must dismiss the case rather than proceed to reach the merits. In practical terms, jurisdictional questions are threshold matters that must be resolved before the merits; absent standing, ripeness, mootness, or other jurisdictional prerequisites, the court must refrain from addressing statutory interpretation or the legality of agency choices.

This jurisdictional-first rule serves several functions. It enforces the Constitution’s case-or-controversy limits on judicial power; it protects the separation of powers by avoiding advisory opinions or invalid exercises of judicial authority; and it promotes judicial economy by preventing courts from resolving complex substantive questions that would have no legal consequence for the parties if jurisdiction is lacking. Steel Co. is thus not a technicality about docket hygiene—rather it carries constitutional weight. The D.C. Circuit invoked that principle here: it ordered supplemental briefing on standing, concluded that AFL had not established a concrete and causally connected injury, and therefore declined to reach the statutory interpretation and APA remedies questions the district court had addressed.

The effect of Steel Co. in this case was decisive: even though the district court believed OSC had legally erred by refusing to investigate under § 1216(a)(3), the appellate court could not resolve that legal question after concluding that AFL lacked Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit therefore vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. That disposition leaves the merits questions unresolved and available to a plaintiff who can overcome Article III hurdles in a future case.

## Why the D.C. Circuit avoided these merits questions in light of standing

The D.C. Circuit’s approach—declining to resolve any of the contested interpretive questions about § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)—is explainable both doctrinally and prudentially. Doctrinally the court followed Steel Co. and Lujan: without a plaintiff who has suffered a concrete, traceable, and redressable injury, there is no Article III “case or controversy,” and federal courts lack power to provide the requested relief. AFL’s standing theories were twofold (an alleged informational injury from DOJ’s nondisclosure and an alleged informational injury from OSC’s nondisclosure of investigatory notices). The D.C. Circuit found both theories wanting. The first theory failed because any FOIA-related informational injury is directly traceable to DOJ’s choices and not sufficiently caused by OSC’s refusal to investigate; the causal chain from an OSC investigation to DOJ’s production was speculative and dependent on independent actors and choices. The second theory failed because § 1216 did not, on the court’s reading of the record, create an entitlement to receipt of investigatory information that would confer a cognizable informational injury. Because AFL could not satisfy the basic Article III requirements—even under the relaxed rules for procedural-right claims—the court had no jurisdiction to resolve the underlying statutory disputes.

Prudential considerations reinforce this doctrinal posture. The statutory questions about OSC’s authority and the proper scope of judicial relief implicate separation-of-powers concerns and prosecutorial-prerogative doctrines (Chaney). Courts are rightly hesitant to adjudicate those matters unless presented with litigants who have concrete stakes and whose claims are ripe for adjudication; doing otherwise risks issuing advisory opinions or intruding into core executive functions. By insisting on strict adherence to standing doctrine, the D.C. Circuit avoided deciding questions that would have profound institutional consequences for OSC’s enforcement discretion, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and FOIA enforcement more generally.

Finally, the court’s restraint reflects the prudence of allowing the merits to be litigated in a case where plaintiff’s injuries and remedy prospects are more direct and where a favorable judgment would actually bind a party whose conduct could redress the plaintiff. As the court explained, redressability depends on whether judicial relief would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the plaintiff’s injury; in AFL’s case the speculative chain connecting an OSC investigatory obligation to the ultimate production of FOIA records did not suffice.

Conclusion: unresolved merits and practical consequences

The D.C. Circuit’s decision to vacate and dismiss for lack of Article III jurisdiction leaves several worthwhile issues open for future litigation. A later plaintiff with a more direct injury—perhaps a FOIA requester who obtains a judicial order explicitly satisfying § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)’s predicates, or a complainant who can show that OSC’s refusal to investigate directly caused a concrete and redressable injury—might secure standing and bring the merits into play. If presented with a jurisdictionally proper case, courts would then confront the textual tension between § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” and FOIA’s narrower referral trigger, decide whether § 1216(c) imposes procedural entitlements drawn from § 1214, and apply Norton and Chaney to determine whether any duty is judicially enforceable.

The outcome in any future case will depend on close statutory interpretation and on the difficult boundary between legally enforceable obligations and the executive’s retained discretion over investigations and enforcement. The Norton framework will loom large: plaintiffs must show a clear, mandatory duty and that the agency action sought is not committed to discretionary decisionmaking by law. The practical takeaway for students is that the interplay among clear statutory text, permissive cross-references, enforcement discretion doctrines, and jurisdictional constraints produces finely balanced litigation choices, and that courts will often resolve procedural and standing questions before touching the substantive statutory interpretation issues that animated the dispute.

# Suing Oversight Agencies: Reviewability, Discretion, and Justiciability

The decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (D.C. Cir. 2025) provides a useful vantage point for studying the recurring set of problems that arises when private parties attempt to compel executive-branch oversight or enforcement actions. The case sits at the intersection of four doctrines that together usually foreclose such litigation: (1) the presumption of unreviewability for nonenforcement decisions embodied in Heckler v. Chaney; (2) the limits of statutes’ “shall” language to convert discretionary enforcement authority into a judicially enforceable duty; (3) the practical and doctrinal constraints on mandamus and Administrative Procedure Act relief that would force investigations; and (4) Article III standing’s requirements—particularly for suits alleging procedural harms or informational injuries. Overlaying all of these is a deep separation-of-powers concern: courts must avoid micromanaging executive enforcement, discipline, or prosecutorial choices.

This chapter examines those doctrines with frequent reference to the facts and reasoning of AFL v. Greer. The D.C. Circuit resolved the appeal on standing grounds and did not finally decide whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) must investigate alleged FOIA-withholdings under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. But the opinion illustrates how courts treat claims that an agency has refused to investigate or prosecute alleged misconduct, and it exposes the limits of judicial power in this field.

## Heckler v. Chaney and the Presumption of Unreviewability of Enforcement Discretion

Heckler v. Chaney (1985) is the lodestar for courts’ treatment of agency nonenforcement. There the Supreme Court held that an agency’s decision not to pursue an enforcement action ordinarily is “committed to agency discretion by law” and presumptively unreviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act. The reasons are familiar and weighty: enforcement involves resource allocation, prosecutorial judgment, and assessments of litigation risk and policy priorities that courts are ill-equipped to make. When an agency declines to act, courts risk entangling themselves in sensitive functions reserved to the political branches.

Heckler’s presumption is not absolute. The Court recognized two categories in which a court might review nonenforcement: where Congress has supplied “meaningful standard[s]” to constrain the agency’s choice, or where the decision is alleged to be based on unconstitutional considerations (for example, race). More generally, the Court has signaled that reviewability depends on whether there are judicially manageable standards to apply to the agency’s conduct. If Congress has written concrete, specific, nondiscretionary duties, a refusal to act may be reviewable; if the statute confers broad discretion or lacks standards, the decision is for the agency.

In the OSC context, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion in AFL v. Greer discussed Heckler only implicitly, because the court disposed of the case on standing. But the parties and the district court litigated precisely the question Heckler raises: whether OSC’s statutory text created a nondiscretionary duty to investigate alleged FOIA violations. The district court thought the text did; an OSC refusal to investigate was thus reviewable as a legal error. The D.C. Circuit did not reach that statutory question because it found AFL lacked Article III standing. Nevertheless, the case demonstrates the typical two-step pattern: plaintiffs first try to overcome Heckler by pointing to mandatory statutory language or detailed standards; the agency responds that the language is discretionary, and courts then evaluate whether the statute displaces Heckler’s presumption.

A practical lesson from Heckler is that courts will not lightly substitute judicial judgment for executive assessment. Even when statutory text appears to be mandatory, courts scrutinize whether the statute really furnishes a “meaningful standard” or instead merely sets out goals and general duties that leave core enforcement priorities to the agency.

## When “Shall” Language Overcomes Discretion—and When It Does Not

Plaintiffs commonly hinge their claims on a statute’s “shall”: “shall investigate,” “shall commence,” “shall take” are phrases that seemingly impose command obligations. But courts have long emphasized that “shall” is not dispositive. The Supreme Court’s decisions—most notably in Norton v. Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance (SUWA) and in post-Heckler cases—indicate that a court must look beyond the word to the statutory context to decide whether Congress imposed a judicially enforceable duty.

Norton/SUWA is particularly instructive for APA claims that an agency “failed to act.” SUWA held that § 706(1) of the APA authorizes courts to compel an agency to perform a specific, nondiscretionary duty, but the court cannot create a duty that Congress did not impose. The analysis therefore focuses on whether the statute creates a “discrete, mandatory” obligation that is “not so infused with a high level of discretion” that courts cannot enforce it. A pleading that asserts only a generalized failure to carry out broad responsibilities will not suffice.

Applying that framework, courts ask several questions when confronted with “shall” language: Does the statutory provision contain specific, judicially manageable standards? Does the surrounding statutory scheme contemplate agency discretion in timing, manner, or methods? Is the agency’s task essentially prosecutorial or bound up with judgments that courts cannot review? Where a statutory duty is operationally discrete and administrable—e.g., “within 60 days, the agency must do X”—courts are likelier to treat “shall” as enforceable. Where “shall” exists in a broad remedial or policy context, courts are reluctant.

In AFL v. Greer, the relevant texts illustrate the difficulty. Section 1216(a)(3) of Title 5 provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” On its face that language reads binding. But FOIA itself contains a separate provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), that triggers OSC duties only where (1) a court orders production, (2) attorneys’ fees are awarded, and (3) the court questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. The district court read § 1216 as independent of the FOIA provision, concluding that an OSC obligation under § 1216 exists notwithstanding FOIA’s three-part trigger. OSC and DOJ pressed the contrary view—either that Congress confined OSC to the FOIA-triggered enforcement route or that §1216 left significant discretion in OSC about procedures and use of §1214’s notification provisions.

Even where “shall” seems clear, courts will examine statutory purpose, structure, and implications. A “shall” that would require an agency to pursue investigations in a manner that forces prosecutorial decisions to be reviewed or micromanaged by courts may be treated as aspirational rather than mandatory. The practical upshot is that plaintiffs seeking to convert “shall” into judicially enforceable obligations must show more than a literal command: they must demonstrate that Congress supplied precise standards that courts can apply without intruding into core executive judgment.

## Carson v. OSC and the OSC’s Procedural Flexibility

Carson v. United States Office of Special Counsel (6th Cir. 2011) supplies a circuit-level precedent directly addressing OSC’s discretion within § 1216 investigations. The Sixth Circuit held that OSC retains discretion whether to apply the procedural requirements of § 1214 (which governs “prohibited personnel practices”) when it conducts an investigation under § 1216. That decision recognizes OSC’s twin roles—sometimes playing a corrective or prosecutorial role akin to a personnel-enforcement agency, and sometimes serving as the repository of duties related to FOIA violations—and allows OSC to choose procedures appropriate to the type of claim it is pursuing.

AFL v. Greer cited Carson. AFL had conceded that OSC need not mechanically apply § 1214’s notice and the like to every § 1216 probe; it instead argued that § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” nonetheless imposes an obligation to investigate in some circumstances. The district court disagreed with OSC’s reading and found legal error in OSC’s declination; the circuit court did not reach the statutory question. But Carson illustrates a common doctrinal accommodation: courts often allow agencies procedural flexibility even when statutes impose some substantive investigatory duties.

For students, Carson teaches two lessons. First, even a statute that appears to impose a duty to investigate may have built-in flexibility about what an “investigation” entails. Second, the presence of a discretionary choice about procedures—whether to give notice, whether to apply certain internal rules—makes judicial review more difficult. A statute that imposes a duty but leaves the agency broad control over the form and scope of investigation tends to--for separation-of-powers reasons—conceal the core enforcement judgment within the executive branch.

## Limits of Mandamus and APA Review in Compelling Investigations

When plaintiffs ask courts to compel the executive to investigate, two principal judicial tools are invoked: the writ of mandamus and the APA’s provision allowing review of agency failures to act (5 U.S.C. § 706(1)). Both are available in theory, but in practice they are constrained.

Mandamus (28 U.S.C. § 1361 and traditional equitable writs) requires (1) a clear and indisputable legal right in the plaintiff to the relief sought; (2) a clear and non-discretionary duty in the defendant to perform the act; and (3) no other adequate means to obtain relief. Mandamus is powerful but narrow: it will not issue to compel the exercise of discretion. Courts treat enforcement and investigative choices as quintessentially discretionary; mandamus will not ordinarily be used to direct prosecutorial or disciplinary judgments. Thus, if the duty a plaintiff cites is anything less than an unambiguous statutory command with manageable standards, mandamus relief is unlikely.

APA § 706(1) permits courts to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.” After SUWA, however, courts interpret § 706(1) to require that Congress has imposed a specific, non-discretionary duty. A general statutory responsibility to “enforce” or to “protect” does not create a judicially enforceable mandate. Moreover, even where a discrete duty exists, courts must be able to craft a remedy that does not intrude impermissibly into executive decision-making. The SUWA Court rejected a broad reading of § 706(1) because it would have entitled courts to direct agencies to act in ways that require substantive policy judgments. So when the duty would require courts to dictate how an agency allocates resources, prioritizes cases, or exercises prosecutorial judgments, § 706(1) is an inadequate vehicle.

AFL v. Greer illustrates these limits. The district court attempted a middle course: it found OSC’s declination legally erroneous and remanded for OSC to reconsider, rather than ordering OSC to investigate in a particular way. Remand is a typical judicial response where courts detect legal error in the agency’s denial yet prefer not to micromanage subsequent prosecutorial choices. The D.C. Circuit, by contrast, did not reach the APA/motion-to-compel issues because it concluded AFL lacked Article III standing. But the case shows the doctrinal reluctance to convert Congress’s investigatory aims into automatic, court-enforceable commands.

Practically speaking, a plaintiff seeking mandamus or § 706(1) relief should (1) identify a narrow statutory duty that is concrete in its requirements; (2) demonstrate that the duty is not infused with discretionary considerations; and (3) propose a judicially manageable remedy that does not require day-to-day supervision of executive law-enforcement choices.

## Separation-of-Powers Concerns in Judicial Supervision of Executive Enforcement Choices

Separation-of-powers principles are the constitutional backdrop to judicial restraint in these contexts. The political branches share responsibility for enforcement and oversight—Congress designs mechanisms for enforcement; the executive executes law enforcement and discipline; courts adjudicate legal disputes arising from those processes. When plaintiffs ask courts to review or direct investigations, courts must be mindful that enforcement entails constitutional prerogatives of the executive: prosecutorial discretion, selection of targets, timing, and policy priorities.

Heckler recognized this point explicitly: a judicial command that an agency “prosecute” or “investigate” in particular ways would intrude upon the executive’s sworn duties and undermine the President’s constitutional responsibility to “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.” Courts therefore are cautious about granting relief that would require continuous oversight of executive compliance or that would reduce complex policy choices to judicial orders.

AFL v. Greer underscores a complementary constitutional consideration: standing doctrine itself limits judicial intervention. By insisting on concrete, particularized, and redressable injuries, Article III acts as a gatekeeper that prevents courts from transforming diffuse grievances about executive priorities into federal-court controversies. In the AFL case the D.C. Circuit held that even if OSC committed legal error in declining to investigate, AFL lacked the kind of injury cognizable in federal court—thus avoiding the constitutional entanglement that would otherwise follow.

In short, separation-of-powers concerns operate both directly (through doctrines of nonreviewability and limits on remedies) and indirectly (through standing principles that limit the cases the courts may hear).

## Comparisons to FEC Nonenforcement and Akins-Type Informational Injuries

Two richer contexts for comparison are (1) suits challenging the Federal Election Commission’s (FEC) refusal to enforce campaign-finance laws, and (2) claims that rest on informational injuries—situations in which a plaintiff alleges a statutory entitlement to certain information.

Akins v. FEC (1998) is the seminal “informational standing” case. There the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff had standing to sue the FEC because the statute promised that certain information about contributors would be publicly available; the plaintiff alleged he had been deprived of that information and alleged injury was concrete. Akins teaches two propositions: first, the deprivation of a statutorily bestowed information right can be a cognizable injury; second, that entitlement must be specific and created by statute—an abstract interest in information or “a general interest in government affairs” is insufficient.

Yet informational standing has limits. Post-Akins cases require that the statute create a specific entitlement to information and that the claimed deprivation be traceable to the defendant’s conduct and redressable by the court. In AFL v. Greer the D.C. Circuit applied these principles and rejected AFL’s informational-injury theories. AFL argued that OSC had denied it two informational benefits: production of DOJ records that OSC’s investigation would have spurred, and the procedural notices OSC would have given if it had investigated and followed § 1214. The court found both rationales defective.

First, the causal chain between OSC’s declination and DOJ’s nonproduction was too speculative. Courts will not accept a chain of contingent events involving independent actors—OSC might investigate, then might seek corrective action, MSPB might sanction, DOJ might produce—particularly where the actions of these other actors are discretionary and not before the court. The D.C. Circuit relied on recent standing precedents (including FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine) to stress that redressability cannot rest on speculation about independent decisions.

Second, the court held AFL had no statutory entitlement to the procedural notices it sought. Even if § 1216 requires OSC to investigate, OSC need not apply § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 inquiries; Carson allowed OSC procedural discretion. Because AFL did not allege that § 1216 itself created an entitlement to the specific information it sought, it lacked an Akins-type informational injury.

Similar dynamics recur in FEC cases. Plaintiffs often sue the FEC for alleged nonenforcement and attempt to frame their injury as the absence of required disclosures. Akins-style standing is available if the statute unambiguously mandates disclosure and the defendant’s noncompliance causes the deprivation. But if the alleged injury depends on a chain of discretionary choices—an enforcement action that the FEC might have brought, followed by agency or private compliance—standing falters.

Thus, informational standing can be a route into court, but it demands a clear statutory entitlement and a plausible causal and redressability link to the defendant’s action or inaction.

## Practical Takeaways and Strategic Considerations

AFL v. Greer illustrates the practical difficulties of litigating to compel an oversight agency to investigate or discipline another executive branch component or employee. Some practical—and doctrinal—rules to keep in mind:

- Start with standing. Courts frequently dispose of these suits on Article III grounds. To overcome standing hurdles, plaintiffs should identify a concrete, particularized injury caused by the defendant and likely redressed by a favorable ruling. Procedural-right theories can relax imminence and redressability somewhat, but plaintiffs still must tie the procedural right to a concrete interest that is affected.

- If relying on a statutory “shall,” analyze the provision’s context: Is the duty discrete and judicially manageable? Or is it embedded in a broader enforcement scheme that leaves weighty discretionary judgments to the agency? Absent clear, specific standards, “shall” language frequently will not yield judicially enforceable duties.

- Mandamus and APA § 706(1) are narrow tools for compelling agency action. Mandamus demands a clear, non-discretionary duty; § 706(1) requires a discrete statutory mandate. Both doctrines are ineffective where compliance would require courts to manage or second-guess prosecutorial priorities.

- Consider informational standing where Congress has created a specific entitlement to information. Akins remains a viable route when the deprivation is concrete and the statutory right to information is clear. But do not rely on long causal chains that involve independent actors and discretionary choices, because redressability collapses under such contingency.

- Expect courts to favor remand and reconsideration over intrusive remedies. When a court perceives legal error, a typical remedy is remand for the agency’s reconsideration rather than an order that compels particular investigatory steps.

- Keep separation-of-powers concerns in mind. Courts will avoid remedies and doctrines that unmoor the judiciary from its proper role by commanding the executive’s policy and enforcement judgments.

Conclusion

AFL v. Greer is not surprising in its outcome. Even when a statute appears to mandate an agency investigation, litigants face a trenchant combination of doctrines—Heckler’s presumption of unreviewability, the careful reading of “shall,” the limits of mandamus and § 706(1) relief, separation-of-powers constraints, and strict Article III standing requirements—that jointly limit courts’ ability to compel investigations. The most promising route for plaintiffs is to identify a narrow statutory entitlement to information or to show a specifically mandated, non-discretionary duty with manageable standards. Absent that, courts will usually leave the enforcement or disciplinary judgment within the executive branch.

# Practical Guidance for FOIA Litigants and Watchdog Organizations

This chapter translates the doctrine developed in AFL v. Greer (D.C. Cir. 2025) and its antecedents into practical litigation and advocacy strategy. The D.C. Circuit’s disposition—vacating the district court’s judgment because the plaintiff lacked Article III standing—illustrates a recurring trap for public‑interest litigants: a focus on oversight bodies (OSC, inspectors general, etc.) instead of the agency that controls the records frequently produces jurisdictional defects. The following sections explain the relevant law (injury in fact, causation, redressability, informational standing, and organizational standing) and convert that law into concrete steps that FOIA requesters and watchdog organizations can use to design litigation and administrative campaigns that are likely to survive threshold challenges and to produce the records and public findings the requester seeks.

## Target the source: suing the withholding agency under FOIA to obtain records

At the outset of any FOIA dispute, the most effective and direct strategy is to sue the agency that has custody and control of the records—the withholding agency. The reason is elemental and constitutional: Article III standing requires that the alleged injury be traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by relief against that defendant. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992). Where the injury is denial of information, that injury is normally traceable to the agency that withheld the records and is directly redressable by a judicial order compelling disclosure and awarding fees. See, e.g., Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440 (1989); FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998) (informational injury recognized).

AFL v. Greer shows the peril of suing an oversight body instead of the record-keeping agency. There, the requester sued the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to compel an OSC investigation of alleged FOIA withholding by DOJ. The D.C. Circuit concluded the requester could not show that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused its informational injury—the primary injury (not receiving DOJ records) was plainly traceable to DOJ, not OSC—and any chain of causation through OSC to MSPB and then to DOJ was too speculative to establish redressability. If your principal objective is obtaining records, the common-sense approach is to name and litigate against the agency that controls the records and to seek relief that directly compels production.

Practical steps:
- File your administrative FOIA request to the agency with clear scope, custodians, and date ranges, and preserve administrative exhaustion record (responses, delays, search terms, acknowledgements).
- If the agency denies or fails to respond, sue the agency in federal district court under FOIA—request production and fees and, where appropriate, declaratory relief describing the agency’s liability under FOIA.
- In your complaint and motion practice, frame the injury as the agency’s denial of access so that causation and redressability directly connect to the defendant.

## How to trigger § 552(a)(4)(F): court order, fees, and specific finding on arbitrariness/capriciousness

One common reason to press for judicial relief against a withholding agency is not only to obtain records, but also to trigger FOIA’s disciplinary mechanism. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) requires that, if a court orders production, awards fees, and specifically questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the employee primarily responsible for the withholding. Thus, to put the OSC on automatic footing, litigants should seek three things from the court:

1. A judicial order compelling production of the withheld records;
2. An award of attorneys’ fees under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(E); and
3. A judicial finding—explicit and specific—that the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.

All three predicates are conjunctive; absence of any one undermines the statutory trigger. The D.C. Circuit did not construe § 552(a)(4)(F) on the merits in AFL because standing resolved the case, but the statutory text and practice offer usable guidance.

Practical techniques for securing the three predicates:
- On motions for summary judgment or at the remedial stage after a finding of improper withholding, include a targeted request in your proposed order that the court (a) order disclosure of specified records, (b) award fees under § 552(a)(4)(E), and (c) make a specific finding that the agency’s conduct in withholding the records was arbitrary or capricious. Provide proposed language that mirrors the statutory wording so the court understands the import of making the finding.
- If the agency produces responsive records in litigation, preserve a record and seek a court determination of liability or, at minimum, a contemporaneous finding or statement in the judgment addressing whether the withholding was arbitrary or capricious. Settling in a manner that yields production but no judicial finding will not trigger OSC’s statutory duty.
- Address both merits and remedy. Judges sometimes deem an award of fees to be routine in FOIA cases. If you want OSC’s involvement, explicitly brief why the facts meet the “arbitrary or capricious” standard and why a judicial finding is warranted. Courts may be hesitant to make that finding absent a developed record showing bad faith or willful disregard of FOIA, so develop the record accordingly.

Note the practical difficulty: courts are cautious about labeling agency conduct “arbitrary or capricious” without a developed administrative record that supports such a characterization. Show a pattern (e.g., policy communications, internal guidance, inconsistent search practices) and, where possible, identify facts demonstrating systemic noncompliance rather than isolated errors.

## Avoiding speculative causal chains: seeking relief that directly binds the defendant

The standing requirement of causation and the redressability component together bar reliance on speculative chains of events—especially where the chain involves independent actors not before the court. The AFL opinion emphasizes that a plaintiff cannot establish standing by hypothesizing a multi‑step chain in which an oversight body investigates, a separate tribunal (MSPB) disciplines, and then the original agency changes behavior. See All. for Hippocratic Med. v. FDA, 602 U.S. 367 (2024) (causation cannot rest on speculation about independent actors); Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985) (agency prosecutorial discretion generally not judicially reviewable).

Consequently, plaintiffs should design requests for relief that directly bind the defendant and do not depend on the discretionary choices of third parties. For FOIA litigation, that means seeking court orders that the record-holding agency produce records and award fees, together with a specific finding on arbitrary withholding when appropriate. Avoid crafting claims whose only practical redress would be to prod a separate oversight agency—because such a causal chain is often too attenuated for Article III.

Practical checklist to avoid speculative chains:
- Confirm that the relief you seek (production, injunction, declaratory relief, fees) can be granted directly against the party you name.
- If you want discipline of agency employees, remember federal courts typically cannot order disciplinary action against agency personnel; that relief is the province of the agency’s internal processes or statutory oversight mechanisms (OSC, MSPB). To prompt those bodies, secure the court-connected predicates that confer an institutional duty on the oversight body (e.g., the three § 552(a)(4)(F) elements).
- When asserting injury, tie it to the agency defendant’s conduct (e.g., withheld records, improper policy) rather than the alleged failure of an oversight body to act.
- If you must challenge the conduct of an oversight body, do so only after you have a concrete judgment or administrative record that shows a particularized injury traceable to that body’s conduct.

## Building informational-standing claims: identifying clear statutory entitlements to information

A plaintiff can assert an informational injury when a statute entitles her to receive certain information and the government’s action deprives her of that entitlement. The Supreme Court’s decision in Akins and subsequent cases (Feld Ent., Pub. Citizen) recognize this theory, but courts insist that the statute create a substantive right to obtain information, not merely a subjective or procedural interest.

Key principles:
- The statute must create a concrete entitlement to obtain information. A generalized interest in government transparency is not enough. See Akins (standing where statute required FEC to make certain information publicly available), Pub. Citizen (recognizing right to certain non-classified information), Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc. (standing where statute required disclosure).
- FOIA itself creates a statutory right to records, which can support an informational injury claim if the plaintiff’s request is properly framed and the plaintiff alleges that the agency has denied that right.
- Courts will not generally find an informational injury based on mere procedural rights (procedural right in vacuo) unless the deprivation of the procedure harms some concrete interest the statute aims to protect. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488 (2009).

Practical implications for FOIA litigants:
- Bring your FOIA claim against the agency that controls the records and describe the plaintiff’s statutory entitlement: identify the FOIA request, the date, the agency’s denial or failure to respond, and the statutory basis for production.
- When asserting informational standing, explain concretely how the denial of information harms the plaintiff’s ability to pursue its mission or otherwise causes a concrete injury (e.g., inability to publish a report, to monitor government action, or to litigate another claim). Documentary support—affidavits from organizational principals, declarations of foregone programs or diverted staff time—strengthens the claim.
- Where the legal right to information is not flatly explicit, brief precedents that recognize an informational right under similar statutes and explain why FOIA’s express disclosure scheme creates the required entitlement.

## Why ‘resource diversion’ and organizational standing require concrete, non-self-inflicted harms

Organizations often seek standing by showing that they have diverted staff time and resources to counteract or respond to the defendant’s conduct. The D.C. Circuit and Supreme Court have recognized that resource diversion can support organizational standing, but the diversion must be concrete, particularized, and caused by the defendant—not merely a generalized programmatic preference or self-imposed choice.

Essential legal points:
- An organization can be injured when governmental action forces it to divert resources from its core mission to counteract that action. This kind of harm has been recognized as cognizable in cases such as Havens Realty Corp. v. Coleman and subsequent decisions.
- The diversion cannot be self-inflicted. If an organization could have avoided the diversion by taking different actions under the circumstances, a court may treat the injury as insufficient. Summers emphasizes that procedural rights without a concrete interest are insufficient; similarly, an organization cannot manufacture standing by imposing costs on itself unnecessarily.
- Pleading and proof require quantification and specificity: identify particular projects or staff hours that were diverted, explain why the diversion was necessary because of the defendant’s conduct, and demonstrate that the diversion is a real operational burden.

Practical guidance for organizations:
- Provide declarations that quantify staff time, costs, and lost opportunities attributable to the agency’s withholding or conduct. State who performed the diverted work, the tasks forgone, and approximate hours and dollar values.
- Explain why those efforts were necessary to the organization’s mission and why the organization could not have reallocated tasks without harm to its core programs.
- Avoid vague allegations (e.g., “we will continue to divert resources”) and instead document concrete, non-speculative expenditures already made or imminently required because of the defendant’s specific conduct.
- If the organization litigates on behalf of its members, show member-specific injuries or concrete programs affected; generalized interest in disclosure does not suffice.

## Coordinating parallel FOIA and oversight strategies without undermining standing

Advocates often want a two‑pronged approach: (1) obtain records through FOIA litigation, and (2) prompt oversight or disciplinary bodies (OSC, IGs, MSPB) to investigate. That coordination is sensible in theory, but success depends on sequencing and careful pleading so as not to create standing problems like those the AFL plaintiff confronted.

Strategic sequencing and coordination:
- Primary litigation target: pursue FOIA litigation against the record-holding agency first. This identifies and remedies the immediate injury: the withheld information. It also creates an evidentiary record that can support claims of arbitrary withholding and that can be used to persuade oversight bodies to act or to secure judicial findings that trigger statutory oversight mechanisms.
- Use process to create the predicate findings: in FOIA litigation—or in motions for partial summary judgment or remedies—ask the court not only to order production and award fees, but to make the specific statutory finding that the agency acted arbitrarily/capriciously, if the facts support such a finding (see § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)). Securing these three court actions both redresses the immediate injury and activates OSC’s statutory obligation.
- Parallel administrative complaints: you may file an OSC complaint under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) concurrently, but be cautious about litigating against OSC to compel an investigation before you have the concrete predicate injury or court findings. AFL v. Greer shows that suing OSC without first obtaining production or a judicial finding is a weak approach on standing grounds. Conversely, filing a complaint with OSC after obtaining a judicial finding gives OSC a concrete mandate to act and avoids the standing problem.
- Preserve the record for oversight: assemble the FOIA administrative file, internal agency communications (to the extent you obtain them), declarations describing the agency’s search and processing, and any communications indicating a policy of nonsearch or nonsearch without consent. These materials are persuasive to both courts and oversight bodies.

Sample litigation roadmap:
1. Exhaust administratively and, if denied, file suit against the agency that controls the records.
2. In the complaint and any summary judgment motion, request production, fees, and a finding that the withholding was arbitrary or capricious (with proposed order language).
3. If the court orders production, obtains fees, and makes the § 552(a)(4)(F) finding, notify OSC and encourage them to initiate the disciplinary proceeding; if OSC declines, you will have a stronger claim for its statutory duty. If OSC is recalcitrant notwithstanding the court’s finding, an OSC‑directed suit may then survive standing because the court’s judgment has already created the concrete injury and remedy nexus.
4. If you settle the FOIA case, consider obtaining a settlement provision that acknowledges agency misconduct or grants the requester the right to seek a judicial finding of arbitrary withholding if a dispute arises—this can preserve the ability to trigger oversight procedures.

Practical drafting tips:
- When asking for a judicial finding on arbitrariness, provide specific factual assertions that show the agency’s withholding was not only incorrect but unreasonable or in bad faith (pattern, policy statements, conflicting agency guidance). Courts are reluctant to label government conduct arbitrary on thin records.
- In parallel administrative complaints, rely on the judicial record as evidence and request that the oversight body consider the court’s rulings when determining whether to investigate.

Conclusion

AFL v. Greer reinforces a simple but powerful lesson: Article III requires plaintiffs to connect the injury they allege to the defendant they sue and to seek relief that is likely to redress that injury. For FOIA litigants and watchdog organizations, the practical corollary is equally straightforward: target the agency that controls the records, craft remedies that directly bind that agency, and use litigation to create the specific judicial predicates that activate statutory oversight processes. When challenging an oversight body’s refusal to act, do so only after you have created the concrete, traceable injury and evidentiary foundation necessary to survive a standing challenge. Careful pleading, clear requests for relief, quantified organizational affidavits, and strategic sequencing of FOIA suits and oversight complaints will maximize both the legal viability and real-world impact of transparency litigation.

# Comparative Notes, Circuit Trends, and Open Questions

This chapter takes the D.C. Circuit’s recent decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (2025) as its point of departure for a broader tour of how federal courts are treating suits that seek to compel statutory investigations and that rest on informational or procedural injuries. The decision illustrates the doctrinal cross-currents now shaping litigation about investigatory duties and informational standing: the Court applied the modern, tightened standing framework (Spokeo/TransUnion), emphasized proximate-cause and redressability limits after All. for Hippocratic Med. and Haaland, and relied on the Summers line to reject a procedural-rights-in-vacuo theory. These lines of authority are not unique to the D.C. Circuit, but their interaction has produced different outcomes in different circuits and leaves a set of recurring — and unresolved — questions about the enforceability of congressional mandates to investigate, the remedial reach of “shall investigate” provisions, and the circumstances under which denial of information itself is a cognizable Article III injury.

## D.C. Circuit’s trajectory on procedural and informational injuries post-Spokeo/TransUnion

Since Spokeo v. Robins (2016) and TransUnion v. Ramirez (2021), federal courts have tightened the definition of “injury in fact” required for Article III standing. Spokeo reaffirmed that a statutory violation does not always constitute a concrete injury; TransUnion further explained that plaintiffs must identify a concretely harmful or otherwise historically cognizable injury, not merely a risk of future harm or a bare statutory entitlement. The D.C. Circuit has applied these principles in a string of cases that increasingly scrutinize both the concreteness of informational injuries and the causal connection and redressability required to justify relief.

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer exemplifies the D.C. Circuit’s post-Spokeo posture. There the court accepted, for purposes of its analysis, that AFL alleged violations of a statutory investigatory duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, but concluded that AFL had not shown concrete, redressable informational injuries. The opinion relied on Summers to reject a pure procedural-rights claim (“a procedural right in vacuo”) and on the redressability principles articulated in All. for Hippocratic Med. and Haaland to fault AFL’s reliance on a speculative causal chain linking an OSC investigation to eventual disclosure by the Department of Justice. The result is a framework in which plaintiffs who assert denial of information must do more than point to an abstract statutory right; they also must show a non-speculative causal pathway by which judicial relief would produce the information or otherwise remedy a concrete harm.

The D.C. Circuit’s other recent rulings reflect the same instincts. The court has emphasized that relaxed standards often ascribed to “procedural” injuries are not limitless: procedural violations still must injure some concrete interest, and courts will ask whether a favorable judgment would “very likely spur” remedial action by the relevant actors (Hecate Energy), not merely hope for a sequence of discretionary decisions by independent entities. In short, the D.C. Circuit has become more skeptical of standing theories that rest on either (1) procedural entitlements unconnected to any concrete stake, or (2) long causal chains that require persuasion of non-party actors.

For students, the key takeaways about the D.C. Circuit’s trajectory are (a) demonstrate concrete, historically cognizable harm beyond a bare statutory breach; (b) plead proximate causation and a non-speculative nexus between relief and redress; and (c) if relying on informational standing, identify a statutory entitlement to a discrete category of information or a recognized harm from its absence (e.g., denial of notice, inability to challenge deprivation of a particular right), rather than asserting a generalized interest in government transparency.

## Sixth Circuit’s approach in Carson and implications for OSC practices

Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel (6th Cir. 2011) is the leading circuit decision on the relationship between OSC’s § 1216 investigatory authority and the § 1214 procedural protections that govern investigations of “prohibited personnel practices.” The Sixth Circuit held there was no enforceable right to insist that OSC apply § 1214’s notice-and-procedure regime to § 1216 inquiries; the agency retains discretion whether to follow § 1214 procedures in the § 1216 context. Carson therefore stands for the proposition that Congress’s decision to treat certain FOIA-related allegations under § 1216 as within OSC’s investigatory remit does not automatically import the full panoply of § 1214 procedural protections.

Carson’s practical impact for OSC practice and for potential complainants is significant. First, it preserves OSC’s flexibility: the agency can treat § 1216 investigations operationally differently from prohibited-personnel-practice investigations, tailoring notice, participation, and confidentiality rules to the investigatory context. Second, it narrows the set of remedies available to third-party complainants who want not only an investigation but also the specific procedural participation and notices Congress provided in § 1214. Under Carson, complainants cannot force OSC to adopt § 1214 procedures merely by invoking § 1216; they must instead persuade a court that § 1216 itself or other law requires such procedures.

Carson also gives litigants a concrete roadmap for where to press and where to temper expectations. If the plaintiff’s essential interest is to secure OSC’s application of particular procedural protections, Carson suggests that the courts will be reluctant to second-guess those discretionary choices unless the statute’s text or structure clearly mandates the procedures. On the other hand, if the plaintiff seeks substantive consequences (e.g., disciplinary action that will lead to release of records or corrective action by the agency that controls the records), Carson does not foreclose claiming that § 1216 requires OSC to investigate as a matter of statutory command — but it does leave open whether pursuit of enforcement remedies will suffer from the same redressability and causation problems that doomed AFL’s theories in Greer.

Students should note that Carson’s holding is narrow: it resolves whether § 1214 procedures automatically apply in § 1216 investigations. It does not purport to settle the broader question whether § 1216’s “shall … conduct an investigation” is a judicially enforceable command in every circumstance. That question remains open and has become a focal point for split-line litigation.

## Tensions between Akins-style informational standing and attempts to compel enforcement

Akins v. FEC (1998) established an important doctrinal strand: a plaintiff has standing to sue when a statute entitles the plaintiff to specific information and the denial of that information injures the plaintiff’s interest. Akins’ informational standing rests on the notion that some informational entitlements protect practical, concrete interests (e.g., the electorate’s interest in informed voting) and are therefore more than a bare statutory entitlement.

But the post-Spokeo and post-TransUnion jurisprudence has narrowed the domain of Akins-style standing. TransUnion emphasized that not all statutory information entitlements translate into a concrete injury; the injury must resemble the sorts of harms courts have historically recognized. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in Greer, echoing earlier D.C. authority (e.g., Pub. Citizen, Feld Ent.), shows how courts now parse Akins claims: (1) identify the precise informational entitlement (what statute authorizes the plaintiff to receive, and in what form); (2) explain the concrete interest that the information protects; and (3) show that denial of the information directly impairs that interest.

A tension emerges when plaintiffs attempt to fit that analytical structure onto claims that seek to compel an enforcement agency to act. Many enforcement statutes create informational by-products (reports, notices, or investigative findings) and procedural pathways that could, in principle, supply the requested information. Plaintiffs often attempt to bootstrap standing by claiming a right to those investigatory products or the notices that accompany them. But the courts are alert to two lines of resistance. First, where the informational product is not an express statutory entitlement — that is, where the statute leaves production or notice to agency discretion — Akins-style standing is harder to establish. Second, even when the statute entitles a plaintiff to information, the plaintiff must show that a judicial order compelling an agency to investigate or follow its procedures would in fact produce the information. If the chain linking judicial relief to the desired information requires successive discretionary choices by third parties (other agencies or adjudicative bodies), redressability fails.

These tensions are most acute where plaintiffs sue an enforcement agency (here, OSC) rather than the entity that controls the records (here, DOJ). A successful Akins-style suit should therefore aim to secure either (a) an enforceable legal entitlement to the desired information (e.g., a statutory command that requires disclosure to the plaintiff), or (b) a realistic theory of redressability showing that a judicial declaration or order against the defendant will compel production by the record-holding agency without intervening, discretionary actors. Where neither is present, Akins-based arguments often founder on the twin marshals of modern standing doctrine: concreteness and redressability.

## Potential circuit splits on when ‘shall investigate’ creates judicially enforceable duties

A recurring and consequential doctrinal question is whether a statutory command that an agency “shall … conduct an investigation” creates a judicially enforceable duty. The answer is not uniform across the circuits, and the split turns on two related issues: (1) whether the statutory language supplies a judicially manageable standard, and (2) whether the provision sits in an enforcement context traditionally committed to executive discretion.

Some courts read “shall” as mandatory and susceptible to judicial enforcement unless the statute’s context suggests otherwise. A plainly phrased command in a regulatory setting that addresses rights and duties of private parties will often be treated as judicially enforceable. Other courts, particularly when the “shall” lies in a provision that addresses prosecutorial or discretionary enforcement decisions, will treat the command as non-justiciable or as committed to agency discretion — invoking Heckler v. Chaney (1985) and the APA’s recognition that some agency actions are “committed to agency discretion by law.”

The circuits have applied these principles unevenly in the investigatory-enforcement context. Some decisions emphasize the mandatory language and find judicially enforceable rights; others emphasize the prosecutorial nature of enforcement and refuse review. Carson (6th Cir.) accepted OSC’s discretion to decide whether to apply § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations, signaling deference where procedures would intrude on enforcement prerogatives. By contrast, some district courts (and some circuits in other contexts) have interpreted “shall investigate” language as creating a ministerial duty that courts can compel, particularly where Congress expressly tied an investigatory duty to a complainant’s statutorily ordained process or where the statute supplies an objective trigger (e.g., a court order or a finding of arbitrary withholding).

Greer illustrates the close-run nature of the inquiry. The district court initially read § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” as mandatory and concluded OSC’s non-investigation was legal error; the D.C. Circuit did not resolve whether § 1216 creates a mandatory duty, because AFL failed Article III standing. Thus, a full circuit split could develop — and indeed may be developing — over whether courts should enforce “shall investigate” commands against enforcement agencies, particularly where redressability questions loom. The split will likely turn on how courts classify the statutory duty (ministerial versus prosecutorial) and on whether Congress provided standards that courts can apply without engaging in impermissible second-guessing of prosecutorial priorities.

For practitioners and students, the lesson is that litigation strategy must anticipate both textual and contextual arguments. When pursuing a “shall investigate” theory, litigants should (a) highlight textual mandatory language, (b) identify statutory or regulatory markers that constrain agency discretion (thus creating judicially manageable standards), and (c) minimize the appearance that judicial enforcement would amount to micromanaging executive enforcement priorities.

## Unresolved questions about § 1216’s mandatory scope and the use of § 1214 procedures

Section 1216 sits at the confluence of two statutory schemes: OSC’s general investigatory-and-enforcement authority and FOIA’s separate enforcement pathway. The statute’s text — particularly § 1216(a)(3) (“shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”) and § 1216(c) (allowing OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved”) — lends itself to competing readings.

One reading, embraced by some district courts, is that § 1216 obligates OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholding and authorizes OSC to use § 1214’s procedures as a matter of course when appropriate. Under this view, § 1216 creates a relatively bright-line investigatory duty and gives complainants reasonable expectations of notice and participation analogous to § 1214 protections.

An alternative reading, reflected in Carson and influential in the D.C. Circuit’s practical approach, is that § 1216 creates OSC authority but leaves the application of § 1214 procedural protections to OSC’s discretion. Section 1216(c)’s phrasing — “may investigate and seek corrective action … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” — can be read as permissive or conditional rather than mandatory. The implication is that OSC can choose whether to import § 1214’s procedural apparatus into FOIA-related investigations, depending on operational considerations and enforcement priorities.

Greer leaves several important questions unresolved because the court disposed of the case on standing grounds. These open questions include: (1) whether § 1216’s “shall” creates a judicially enforceable duty in the first instance; (2) whether § 1216(c) authorizes or mandates the importation of § 1214’s procedures; (3) whether a complainant denied § 1214-type notices can sue to compel them; and (4) whether the separate FOIA enforcement-trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) (which obligates OSC to initiate disciplinary proceedings after certain judicial findings) limits § 1216’s scope or coexists with it. Different answers to these questions produce materially different practice regimes for OSC, complainants, and FOIA requesters.

From a doctrinal standpoint, resolving these questions requires more than textual parsing; it requires grappling with separation-of-powers and prosecutorial-discretion concerns. A court that reads § 1216 as creating a judicially enforceable duty must also articulate judicially manageable standards — what facts trigger investigation, what scope is required, and what remedies courts may order — without intruding impermissibly on executive discretion. Conversely, treating § 1216 as largely discretionary preserves executive flexibility but reduces the remedial options available to those seeking the information or procedural protections Congress associated with OSC investigations.

## Prospects for Supreme Court clarification on informational standing boundaries

The Supreme Court is already active in sharpening the boundaries of Article III standing in statutory contexts. Spokeo, TransUnion, and more recent pronouncements have converged on a two-part analytic posture: the plaintiff must (a) identify a statutory or common-law interest that has been invaded, and (b) show that the invasion is a concrete, particularized injury that is fairly traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by the relief requested. The Court has not, however, exhaustively defined what counts as a sufficiently “concrete” informational injury. Akins still stands for the proposition that some denials of information are the sort of concrete injuries the Constitution permits federal courts to vindicate, but lower courts struggle to determine in borderline cases whether an informational deprivation is historical or consequential enough to meet the test.

The Supreme Court is likely to confront these issues again for several reasons. First, as Congress continues to enact statutes that create procedural entitlements, investigatory duties, and information-disclosure regimes, litigants will press standing claims that test the outer fringes of Akins. Second, circuit splits are beginning to form over the enforceability of “shall investigate” language and over whether informational injuries tied to investigatory products suffice absent a proximate causal chain. Third, the Court has shown interest in policing limits on federal jurisdiction, particularly where expansive readings of standing would confer broad and decentralized enforcement power on private plaintiffs.

If the Supreme Court takes a case squarely presenting these questions, possible directions include: (1) reaffirming TransUnion’s emphasis on concrete injury and clarifying what sorts of informational denials qualify (e.g., distinguishing loss of information that frustrates discrete, historically recognized interests from generalized informational grievances); (2) elaborating how redressability analysis applies when relief would be directed at an enforcement agency but would depend on discrete discretionary acts by non-parties; or (3) defining a more categorical rule about when statutory “shall investigate” duties are judicially enforceable, perhaps drawing a clearer line between ministerial duties and prosecutorial discretion. Any guidance from the Court will likely focus on protecting judicial resources and separation-of-powers concerns while preserving meaningful avenues for enforcing statutory informational entitlements.

## Practical implications and litigation strategies

Given the current doctrinal landscape, plaintiffs who seek to compel investigations or to rely on informational standing should adopt a careful, layered strategy:

- Plead concrete, particularized harm. Where possible, connect the absence of information to a classical type of injury (reputational harm, competitive disadvantage, inability to participate meaningfully in a regulatory or political process) rather than asserting a generalized right to know.

- Minimize the redressability gap. Seek relief that would bind the actor who actually controls the information (for example, bring a FOIA suit against the record-holding agency alongside a claim against an enforcement agency), or identify statutory mechanisms by which an order against the defendant would directly compel the desired disclosure.

- Use statutory text aggressively. If seeking to compel OSC action, point to mandatory language, objective triggers, or other textual elements that cabin agency discretion; show that judicial review would require application of neutral standards rather than second-guessing prosecutorial priorities.

- Anticipate Heckler/APA arguments. Be prepared to show that the agency’s decision is not “committed to agency discretion by law” (i.e., that there is “law to apply”), and that meaningful judicial standards exist to judge compliance.

- Consider alternative remedies. Where standing or redressability problems are acute, explore administrative strategies (e.g., pursuing enforcement through OSC’s internal complaint processes, FOIA litigation directly against the agency that controls records) or seek declaratory rather than coercive relief when appropriate.

For agencies such as OSC, the case law counsels clarity and documentation. If OSC wishes to preserve the discretion Carson recognizes, it should promulgate clear guidance explaining when § 1214 procedures will be imported into § 1216 investigations and why, to reduce litigation over alleged arbitrariness. That practice both aids judicial review (by giving courts a discernible basis for review) and reduces the uncertainty that fuels procedural claims.

## Conclusion

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer reinforces two durable lessons for litigation over investigatory duties and informational injuries: statutory entitlements to information are not self-executing invitations to federal court unless the injury is concrete and the relief is likely to redress that injury, and commands framed in the language of investigation or enforcement may be treated as discretionary when their implementation would intrude on executive enforcement judgment. Circuits differ, however, on precisely how those principles apply to “shall investigate” language and to the relationship between investigatory statutes such as § 1216 and procedural regimes such as § 1214. Those differences, together with lingering questions about the limits of Akins-style informational standing, make this an area ripe for further litigation and potentially for Supreme Court clarification. For now, practitioners should plan litigation with an eye to both Spokeo/TransUnion’s concreteness demands and Heckler’s deference to prosecutorial discretion, while exploiting any textual levers in the statute that might convert a discretionary-sounding duty into a judicially manageable obligation.

# Synthesis and Significance: AFL v. Greer in the Standing Canon

Introduction

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is a compact but consequential decision about who may sue to force executive-branch investigatory action and when a plaintiff’s asserted procedural or informational injury will suffice for Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit held that AFL lacked Article III standing to challenge the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC’s) refusal to investigate an alleged FOIA withholding. The court rested its decision on two principal points: (1) a statutory “shall investigate” alone does not necessarily create the kind of concrete informational entitlement that supplies an Article III injury, and (2) redressability is lacking where the causal chain from a judicial order to the desired relief depends on multiple independent actors and discretionary prosecutorial choices. Read together with Summers, Akins/Public Citizen, Heckler v. Chaney, Haaland v. Brackeen, and recent decisions emphasizing non-speculative causation, AFL v. Greer refines the standing limits on suits that seek to spur enforcement or investigatory activity by the Executive.

This chapter unpacks the doctrinal rules the court applied, illustrates how the opinion integrates and clarifies prior precedents, and draws lessons for future watchdog litigation and for legislative drafting aimed at creating enforceable public entitlements.

## Reaffirmation that procedural rights require a concrete, affected interest

AFL’s primary argument was that OSC’s statutory duty to “conduct an investigation” under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) amounted to a procedural right whose violation conferred standing. The court applied the familiar Summers principle: a procedural right, by itself, does not create Article III standing unless the plaintiff also shows that the procedural deprivation affects some concrete interest. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488 (2009), teaches that procedural rights in vacuo—procedural rules taken alone—do not create the injury-in-fact required for standing.

AFL illustrates the point. Even if OSC had a statutory duty to investigate, that duty—standing alone—did not denote an informational or other concrete interest in AFL that was directly invaded by OSC’s refusal. The court emphasized that a plaintiff invoking a procedural right must show that the procedural violation “resulted in injury” to a concrete, particularized interest protected by the statute (or otherwise cognizable under Article III). See Center for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005). In short, AFL could not establish injury-in-fact merely by asserting that OSC failed to perform a statutorily commanded procedural task.

The practical takeaway for future litigants is straightforward: when alleging procedural injury, plead and prove a concrete, particularized harm flowing from the procedural lapse. Examples of such harms include a specific, concrete loss of legally protected information (where the statute entitles the plaintiff to that information), tangible economic losses, or a denial of procedural steps that themselves confer independent benefits (e.g., a right to a hearing that, if denied, causes a distinct deprivation). Absent such a showing, Summers forecloses standing.

## Clarification that not every “shall investigate” yields an informational entitlement

AFL argued that because section 1216 uses mandatory language—OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings—OSC’s refusal created an informational injury: AFL had been denied information that the statute required OSC to produce as part of an investigation. The court rejected that argument, calling attention to the separate requirement for informational standing established by Akins and Public Citizen: to ground standing on the denial of information, the plaintiff must be entitled to receive it under statute.

The jurisprudential rule is clear: an allegation that an agency has failed to investigate does not automatically translate into an entitlement to receive the fruits of a possible investigation. In FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998), and Public Citizen v. DOJ, 491 U.S. 440 (1989), the Supreme Court held that plaintiffs may have standing to sue when a statute gives them a right to obtain certain information and that right is violated. But where a statute merely directs an agency to investigate, without also creating a right to receive specific investigative products or notice, an informational standing theory typically fails.

The D.C. Circuit relied on this distinction. Even if section 1216 requires OSC to investigate, nothing in that provision—or in AFL’s pleading—created a statutory entitlement to the investigative materials or notices AFL sought. AFL attempted to bridge that gap by pointing to the procedures in section 1214 (which include notice and status provisions), arguing that OSC, when conducting a §1216 investigation, would have to follow those requirements and thus generate a right to information. The court noted controlling precedent, however, that OSC’s decision whether to apply section 1214’s procedural rules in a §1216 investigation is discretionary (Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)). Because AFL could not point to a statutory entitlement to the investigative information it sought, Akins/Public Citizen precluded informational standing.

For practitioners, the lesson is that the textual source of the informational right matters. “Shall investigate” language must be parsed to see whether the statute also creates a right to receive particular investigative outputs or notices; without that, an informational injury claim is weak.

## Strengthening of redressability limits where multiple independent actors intervene

A central plank of the D.C. Circuit’s decision was that redressability failed because the asserted causal chain from OSC’s investigation to AFL’s receipt of DOJ records depended on multiple independent actors and discretionary choices. Redressability requires that a favorable judicial decision would likely remedy the plaintiff’s injury. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555 (1992). AFL’s theory required a speculative sequence: OSC would investigate; OSC might initiate corrective or disciplinary proceedings; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) might sanction DOJ employees; and (only then) DOJ might produce the requested FOIA records. The court stressed that such speculation about the future choices of independent agencies is too attenuated to support standing.

This application draws on two related ideas in recent standing jurisprudence. First, courts cannot rely on conjecture about how independent third parties will react to a judicial ruling (Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255 (2023)). Second, where the chain of causation involves discretionary enforcement choices, courts must account for Heckler v. Chaney’s recognition that agency prosecutorial discretion is presumptively non-reviewable and not subject to compulsion. The AFL chain required speculative actions by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ—none of which would necessarily be bound by the district court’s order to OSC (if it were imposed). Thus, a judgment ordering OSC to investigate would likely not be “the thing that causes” DOJ to produce the records AFL wanted.

Recent cases such as FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), and Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC, 126 F.4th 660 (D.C. Cir. 2025), emphasize that causation cannot rest on speculation about the responses of independent actors. AFL v. Greer extends that principle into the investigatory-enforcement context: when successful redress would require several discretionary decisions by multiple agencies, standing is unlikely unless the plaintiff can show a more direct causal link—e.g., a statutory scheme where an agency’s investigatory finding automatically triggers an obligation binding the record-holding agency.

To be successful in this space, plaintiffs should identify statutory provisions that create a direct, non-discretionary chain between the defendant’s act or omission and the injury to be redressed. If the remedy would require successive discretionary acts by others, or if the judgment would merely voice a rationale that might “persuade” but not bind those actors, redressability will almost certainly be lacking.

## Boundary-setting for watchdog litigation against enforcement agencies

AFL clarifies the limits of judicially enforced “watchdog” litigation. Courts are wary about converting the judiciary into a supervisor of prosecutorial and investigatory judgments that Congress left to executive discretion. Chaney established a baseline presumption that attacks on an agency’s decision not to enforce are non-reviewable; the limit is sharpened when plaintiffs seek to force an investigation that could lead to prosecutorial or corrective action. Whether an agency’s decision in that context is judicially reviewable turns on statutory text (does the statute create judicially manageable standards and a right to relief?) and on separation-of-powers concerns about commandeering executive enforcement discretion.

AFL draws a practical boundary. Plaintiffs may not sue merely to press the political branches to act—that is what petitioning, public commentary, and the political process are for. If a plaintiff wants a court to compel an enforcement agency to investigate or prosecute, it must (1) identify a statutory duty that is sufficiently specific and nondiscretionary to be judicially enforceable, or (2) demonstrate a concrete injury directly traceable to the agency’s refusal and likely to be redressed by a court order. Absent those showings, watchdog suits that seek to micromanage or effectively direct prosecutorial choices will often fail for lack of standing and for the additional, related separation-of-powers concerns.

Put practically: suits that target soft or broad statutory phrases (e.g., “shall promote,” “shall investigate” without more) will face a steep uphill fight unless the statute clearly creates rights for private parties (such as a right to notice, specific reports, or specified remedial outputs). Conversely, FOIA suits that challenge an agency’s refusal to disclose records often present a clearer path: the injury (denial of specific records) is traceable to the agency that withheld them, and a favorable order to produce is a direct redress.

## Doctrinal integration with Summers, Akins/Public Citizen, Chaney, and Brackeen

AFL sits at the intersection of several doctrinal strands, and the opinion carefully weaves precedent into a coherent rule about procedural and informational injuries:

- Summers v. Earth Island Institution: Reaffirms that a procedural violation does not confer standing unless it causes a concrete injury. AFL applied Summers to reject standing based only on OSC’s alleged failure to perform an investigatory duty.

- Akins/Public Citizen: Require a statutory entitlement to receive information before an informational injury will support standing. AFL used this principle to hold that §1216’s investigatory command alone did not establish an entitlement to disclosure or notice.

- Heckler v. Chaney: Preserves the presumption that enforcement decisions are committed to agency discretion and typically not subject to judicial compulsion. Although AFL argued OSC’s duty was mandatory, the court recognized that some subsequent enforcement steps (and OSC’s own decision about procedural rules in a §1216 inquiry) involve discretionary judgments that the courts will not ordinarily force.

- Haaland v. Brackeen and FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine: Reinforce that redressability must not rest on speculation about independent actors and that the causal nexus must be reasonably direct and likely. The D.C. Circuit relied on that line to find the causal chain in AFL too attenuated.

Viewed together, these precedents demand that plaintiffs show: a concrete, particularized injury; a clear statutory entitlement where the injury is informational; and a plausible, non-speculative pathway whereby a favorable court order will cause the injury to be remedied. AFL operationalizes that synthesis in the enforcement/investigation context.

## Strengthening of redressability limits where multiple independent actors intervene

AFL underscores a more aggressive application of redressability limits in multi-actor enforcement chains. The court refused to treat a judicial order to OSC as “likely” to prompt production by DOJ because the path from court order to relief required independent exercises of discretion by (i) OSC in deciding whether and how to pursue corrective or disciplinary actions, (ii) the MSPB in adjudicating any such actions and imposing sanctions, and (iii) DOJ in deciding whether a sanction or other result would occasion record production. That constellation of discretionary steps made redress speculative.

This approach emphasizes structural prudence: courts should not assume that favorable judicial rulings will automatically change the behavior of other agencies or actors not before the court. The decision therefore puts real constraints on creative standing theories that attempt to harness the investigatory powers of one agency as a lever to obtain relief from another. Future plaintiffs will need to identify statutory mechanisms that collapse or shorten the chain—e.g., statutory rules that make an agency’s investigatory finding directly binding on the record-holder, or that establish mandatory reporting or disclosure duties triggered by certain investigatory results.

## Doctrinal implications for litigants and enforcement agencies

For litigants who wish to compel investigations or to obtain information indirectly through agency oversight suits, AFL v. Greer requires retooling:

- Focus on direct harms. Challenge the agency whose act or omission directly causes the informational deprivation. FOIA cases that sue the record-holding entity remain the most straightforward route to relief for denied records.

- Show statutory entitlement to information. If relying on informational standing, identify textual language that gives the plaintiff a right to particular notices, reports, or documents. Vague investigatory commands are insufficient.

- Shorten the causal chain. If a plaintiff’s requested relief depends on eliciting action from multiple independent actors, explain why those actors’ responses are non-discretionary or otherwise likely to follow from a judicial ruling.

- Beware of Chaney. If the relief sought would, in practical effect, micromanage prosecutorial or enforcement decisions, the suit will confront both standing hurdles and non-reviewability doctrines.

For enforcement agencies, AFL signals judicial deference to agency discretion in deciding when and how to investigate—so long as the statute lacks clear, private entitlements that mandate different outcomes. Agencies thus retain breathing space to allocate enforcement resources and to decide whether to apply certain procedural rules in cross-authority investigatory contexts.

## Legislative design lessons: crafting enforceable entitlements versus discretionary oversight

AFL v. Greer sends a clear message to Congress about how to draft statutes if legislative actors want private parties to be able to litigate enforcement omissions:

- Create clear, private entitlements. If Congress wants plaintiffs to have standing to sue over a refusal to investigate, it should do more than use hortatory or directory language. Drafting can create a private right to receive specific information (e.g., mandatory notice of investigation initiation, status reports, or final investigatory reports) and specify the content and timing of such disclosures.

- Limit downstream discretion. To ensure redressability, Congress can design consequences that are not purely discretionary. For example, make certain investigatory findings automatically trigger an obligation on other agencies or officials (e.g., an automatic disclosure duty) or require that remedies be implemented unless narrowly specified exceptions apply.

- Provide judicially manageable standards. Where Congress intends courts to review refusals to investigate, it should supply manageable legal standards (e.g., time deadlines, objective criteria for initiating investigations, or minimal thresholds for evidentiary showing) so courts are not asked to second-guess core prosecutorial judgments.

- Consider direct enforcement mechanisms. Where the political goal is oversight, consider private rights of action or statutory mandamus-like remedies that allow courts to compel discrete, nondiscretionary acts (for example, the timely delivery of statutorily required notices), rather than trying to force the broader discretionary exercise of investigatory judgment.

These drafting choices will help avoid the twin problems identified in AFL: procedural rights in vacuo and redressability that depends on speculative actions by other actors.

Conclusion: a refined standing regime for watchdog claims

AFL v. Greer does not foreclose all suits that seek to compel investigatory or enforcement action. It does, however, refine the standing inquiry at that intersection by insisting on (1) a concrete, particularized interest affected by any procedural violation; (2) a statutory entitlement to the information or process claimed; and (3) a non-speculative causal and redressability path to the relief sought. The decision binds together Summers, Akins/Public Citizen, Chaney, and the Court’s recent insistence on non-speculative causation into an operational rule for litigants and legislators alike: if you want courts to police investigatory inaction, make the substantive and remedial contours explicit, narrow the chain of dependency, and supply judicially manageable standards.

For law students and practitioners, AFL provides a useful template for analyzing proposed suits that aim to weaponize investigatory powers: parse the statute for entitlements, map the causal chain from judicial order to the sought-after relief, and identify where discretion or independent actors could frustrate redress. Doing so will separate the plausible claims—those likely to survive threshold standing scrutiny—from the sympathetic but non-justiciable grievances that belong in the political branches rather than the federal courts.
