Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

### Why could the D.C. Circuit raise standing sua sponte and require supplemental briefing, and what factual record governs at this stage?
Question:
Neither party initially addressed standing; the panel ordered supplemental briefing and then dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. Is this because federal courts must ensure Article III standing at all stages? At the motion-to-dismiss posture, are the court’s standing findings constrained to the complaint’s well-pleaded allegations and judicially noticeable materials, or could it consider later developments like DOJ’s rolling FOIA production?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Because Article III standing is jurisdictional, the D.C. Circuit was entitled to raise it on its own and to order supplemental briefing. At the motion-to-dismiss stage the court’s standing inquiry is governed by the facts pleaded in the complaint (and materials properly subject to judicial notice or integral to the complaint); if resolution of standing requires disputed factual development the court must (and sometimes will) convert the case to a later procedural posture (summary judgment or an evidentiary hearing) before making fact-bound jurisdictional findings. The court here applied the motion-to-dismiss standard (requiring plausible allegations of each element of standing), rejected AFL’s causal and redressability arguments as speculative, and did not treat DOJ’s ongoing, rolling FOIA production as a substitute for the pleading required to establish Article III standing.

Why the court could raise standing sua sponte
Standing is a threshold question of subject-matter jurisdiction. The opinion cites Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment for the principle that courts must consider jurisdiction before reaching the merits (Op. § II, opening ¶). Because neither party had briefed standing, the panel properly ordered supplemental briefing rather than proceeding to the merits. A court’s obligation to ensure it has jurisdiction is not waived by the parties’ silence; the court therefore may raise standing on its own initiative and require the parties to address it.

What legal standard governed the court’s standing analysis
The court applied the familiar Article III test from Lujan (injury in fact, causation, redressability) and the pleading-stage rule that “at the motion-to-dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of its standing” (citing Food & Water Watch, Op. § II). It also recognized the “relaxed” burden sometimes applied when plaintiffs assert statutory procedural injuries, but emphasized that even under that relaxed approach plaintiffs still must show a concrete injury and causation (Op. § II, ¶s 2–4).

What factual record governs at this stage
- Pleadings and judicially noticeable materials: At the motion-to-dismiss stage courts look to the complaint’s well-pleaded factual allegations (and materials subject to judicial notice or otherwise integral to the complaint). The opinion explicitly frames the inquiry as whether AFL “allege[d] facts plausibly supporting each element of its standing” (Op. § II).
- Later developments are not automatically controlling: The court rejected AFL’s attempt to base standing on an attenuated chain of events that depended on actions by other agencies (OSC, MSPB, and DOJ) and on how OSC might exercise enforcement discretion. The court treated that chain as speculative and insufficient to show causation or redressability (Op. § II, ¶s 3–5). In short, the court did not treat DOJ’s ongoing FOIA production as supplying the necessary injury or curing AFL’s pleading failures; AFL’s alleged informational injuries remained too indirect and speculative to establish Article III standing.
- When factual development is necessary: The opinion does not undertake factfinding or convert the case to summary judgment. The court could have ordered factual development if it found disputed, material jurisdictional facts; instead it concluded the pleaded facts (and the reasonable inferences from them) were insufficient as a matter of law. If the presence or absence of standing had depended on concrete, provable facts (for example, definitive evidence that an OSC investigation would have compelled production), the court would have had to permit discovery or convert the motion before resolving jurisdictional fact disputes. The court’s approach here follows the usual sequence: resolve standing on the complaint where possible; require further proceedings only if resolution requires factfinding.

How the opinion applied these rules
- Causation/redressability failure: The opinion explains that any informational injury from DOJ’s withholding is directly traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the proposed causal chain (OSC investigation → MSPB enforcement → DOJ production) was too speculative because it depended on independent actors and discretionary choices (citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med. and Heckler v. Chaney) (Op. § II, ¶s 3–4).
- Informational-injury rule: The court also explained that an informational injury requires a statutory entitlement to information; AFL could not identify an entitlement under § 1216 (and conceded OSC’s discretion whether to apply § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations) (Op. § II, ¶s 5–6). Thus a judgment ordering OSC to investigate would not be likely to redress AFL’s asserted informational injuries.

Bottom line
The panel acted correctly in raising standing sua sponte and ordering briefing because jurisdictional defects can be considered at any time. At the motion-to-dismiss stage the court’s standing analysis is limited to the complaint’s allegations (and materials properly noticed); if standing cannot be decided from that record because factual disputes exist, the court must allow factual development before resolving jurisdiction. Here the court concluded the pleaded facts were legally insufficient — AFL’s alleged injuries were speculative and not redressable — and so dismissed for lack of Article III jurisdiction.
