Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

### What counts as the requisite “concrete, particularized interest” tied to the alleged procedural violation in this setting?
Question:
The court says even under the relaxed procedural-injury framework, AFL must show the violation of a procedural right affected a concrete, particularized interest. If the interest is access to DOJ records, the court says that’s traceable to DOJ, not OSC. If the interest is the investigation’s information, the court says there’s no entitlement. What kind of concrete interest could satisfy this requirement in an OSC-investigation context—e.g., would a federal employee whistleblower alleging a prohibited personnel practice have a different result because § 1214 gives them specific notification rights?
Answer:
Short answer
The court applied the familiar “procedural injury” rule: a plaintiff asserting only a procedural right must also show that the procedural deprivation harmed a concrete, particularized interest that the statute was designed to protect. Under America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer, that means the plaintiff needs either (a) a concrete informational entitlement to receive particular information from OSC itself, or (b) some other concrete, particularized interest that OSC’s procedural failure directly injures and that a court order against OSC would likely redress. AFL’s FOIA-requester interest failed both tests; a federal employee who is explicitly entitled by § 1214 to investigatory notices (a “complaining party”) would be a much closer case and could satisfy the requirement if the defendant OSC actually withheld the statutorily mandated notices.

Relevant legal standard (as the opinion states it)
- Article III requires a concrete and particularized injury that is caused by the defendant and likely redressable by the court. Lujan, Summers, etc., quoted in the opinion.  
- The “relaxed” procedural-injury framework only loosens imminence/redressability; it does not eliminate the need for a concrete injury caused by the defendant. (Apln. Br.; see opinion II, citing Summers 555 U.S. at 496 and Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157.)  
- Informational standing exists only when the plaintiff is denied information the statute entitles him to receive. The court cites Feld Ent. and Akins for that point (opinion II).

How the court applied the standard to AFL’s theories
1. Interest in the DOJ records (AFL’s FOIA claim)
- The court treated any injury from DOJ’s nondisclosure as traceable to DOJ, not OSC, so causation fails. OSC’s refusal to investigate would at best create a speculative, multi-step causal chain (OSC → possible OSC enforcement → MSPB action → DOJ production) that depends on the discretionary choices of other, independent actors. The court relied on the principle that standing cannot rest on such speculation about unfettered choices of third parties (citing All. for Hippocratic Med. and Haaland v. Brackeen). Thus AFL’s FOIA-related informational injury is not fairly traceable to OSC and is not redressable by a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate. (See opinion II, paras 2–4.)

2. Interest in getting investigatory notices/information from OSC itself
- Informational standing requires an entitlement to the information. The court observed that § 1214 contains procedures (including notice provisions) for corrective actions and that § 1216 permits OSC to investigate FOIA-withholdings “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” But the court also concluded § 1216 does not itself create an entitlement to receive the § 1214 notices in every § 1216 investigation, and AFL conceded that OSC’s choice to apply § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 investigations has been treated as discretionary (citing Carson). Because AFL had no statutory entitlement to the § 1214 notices in the § 1216 context, it lacked the concrete informational injury necessary for standing. (See opinion II, paras 5–8.)

What kind of “concrete, particularized interest” would satisfy the requirement here?
- A statutory entitlement to particular investigatory information or notices from OSC, coupled with an allegation that OSC (the defendant) actually withheld that information, is the paradigmatic example. The opinion points to § 1214 as an instance of statutory procedures that include notification rights (the opinion notes § 1214 “sets forth procedures … including provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of an investigation”). If the plaintiff is a person whom § 1214 actually entitles to those notices (e.g., a “complaining party” in a prohibited personnel practice inquiry), then being denied those statutorily required notices would be a concrete informational injury under the Feld Ent./Akins line and not merely a procedural right in vacuo.  
- By contrast, a remote interest in records held by another agency (DOJ here) that can be remedied only by a lengthy, speculative chain of enforcement steps (OSC → MSPB → DOJ) will not suffice because of traceability and redressability limits.

Would a federal employee whistleblower fare differently?
- Yes — plausibly. The opinion recognizes that § 1214 contains specific notice and progress-reporting provisions for corrective actions and appears to treat those as the very kind of statutory entitlements that can support informational standing. If a federal employee (or other person) is the sort of “complaining party” to whom § 1214 expressly promises notices and OSC refused to give those notices, then the plaintiff would be asserting denial of information to which the statute entitles them. That situation is much closer to the paradigmatic informational injury the court describes and would avoid the “traceable to DOJ/not OSC” problem that doomed AFL’s FOIA-based theory. But the plaintiff would still have to show that a favorable judicial decree against OSC would redress the injury (i.e., that a court order compelling OSC to provide the statutorily required notices would actually remedy the denial).

Bottom line
- The requisite “concrete, particularized interest” is (most straightforwardly) a statutory entitlement to information or notices from OSC itself — not an attenuated expectation that OSC’s investigation might indirectly produce records from another agency. A whistleblower who is statutorily entitled to § 1214 notice rights and who alleges OSC withheld those notices would present the classic kind of informational injury the opinion says could meet the relaxed procedural-injury test; AFL’s FOIA-requester interest did not.
