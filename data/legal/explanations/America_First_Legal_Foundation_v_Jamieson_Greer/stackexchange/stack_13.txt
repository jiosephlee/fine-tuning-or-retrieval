Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

### Does separation of powers influence the court’s skepticism about compelling OSC investigations?
Question:
The opinion leans on Chaney and emphasizes limits on compelling agency enforcement choices. Is there an underlying separation-of-powers rationale for treating redressability and causation as weak when judicial relief would steer prosecutorial/investigative discretion? How does that constitutional concern intersect with Article III’s injury and redressability requirements in this case?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The opinion grounds its standing skepticism in the same separation-of-powers concerns that underlie Heckler v. Chaney and related precedents, and it applies those concerns at the Article III causation/redressability step. Because compelling an OSC investigation would — directly or indirectly — attempt to steer prosecutorial/investigative choices by OSC (and downstream actors like the MSPB and DOJ), the court treats the causal chain to any FOIA-related relief as too speculative to satisfy redressability and causation.

Why the Court says this (doctrine and how it’s applied)
- Governing standing law. The opinion applies the familiar Article III test (Lujan): plaintiff must show injury in fact, causation, and redressability. It also recognizes the “relaxed” standing rules for statutory procedural rights but emphasizes that even those rules do not eliminate causation/redressability requirements. (Opinion §II, citing Lujan; Summers; Ctr. for Law & Educ.)
- Chaney and prosecutorial discretion. The opinion repeatedly invokes the general rule that courts “generally may not compel” agencies to exercise enforcement or prosecutorial discretion in any particular way, citing Heckler v. Chaney. It treats OSC’s investigative/enforcement choices as the sort of discretionary, prosecutorial-type decisions that courts should not command. (See §I.A describing OSC’s role; §II discussing that “[i]t requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985).”)
- Speculation about independent actors. The opinion frames the causal chain needed to connect OSC’s refusal to investigate to AFL’s alleged informational injury as dependent on multiple independent decisionmakers: OSC might investigate, then might seek corrective/disciplinary action, the MSPB might sanction employees, and DOJ (separately) might then produce records. The Court views that chain as “speculative and attenuated,” and therefore insufficient for causation/redressability. It cites FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for the principle that causation “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” (Opinion §II: “any causal connection…is too speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing” and later “requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit.”)
- How separation-of-powers concerns feed Article III. The opinion does not separately announce a new constitutional separation-of-powers holding. Rather, separation-of-powers concerns operate through established standing doctrine: because a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate or prescribing the manner of investigation would intrude on prosecutorial/enforcement discretion (a core executive function), the Court requires a more direct and non-speculative causal link before finding redressability. In short, constitutional reluctance to usurp executive enforcement judgment makes the court demand a firm chain of causation and realistic prospects of redress — and when that chain depends on discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, or DOJ, Article III is not satisfied. (See §II paragraphs rejecting both of AFL’s standing theories for failure of causation/redressability.)

Key passages the opinion relies on
- On the speculative causal chain and independent actors: “Then, the MSPB might have decided to sanction those employees… Next, DOJ might have decided to produce the requested records. This requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit… causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” (Opinion §II.)
- On prosecutorial discretion: “And it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985).”
- On redressability focus: “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court.’ Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023).”

Bottom line for your question
- The Court’s skepticism is not merely prudential or evidentiary: it is driven by the same separation-of-powers rationale that underlies Chaney — courts should not issue remedies that effectively direct or guarantee particular prosecutorial/enforcement outcomes. That constitutional concern is folded into the Article III inquiry by making causation and redressability harder to establish whenever relief would require or promise that kind of supervisory control over executive enforcement decisions. The opinion therefore denies standing because the required causal path from a judicial order to the plaintiff’s informational relief is too contingent on discretionary actions by executive actors.
