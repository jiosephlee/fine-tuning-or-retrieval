### How did the panel use the 'independent actors' doctrine to find causation and redressability too speculative?
Question:
For AFL’s first theory, the court emphasizes that even if OSC investigated, only a chain of further discretionary decisions (OSC’s enforcement choices, MSPB sanctions, and DOJ’s production) could lead to AFL getting the FOIA records, citing FDA v. AHM and Haaland v. Brackeen. Why is that chain too speculative here? Are there cases where agency investigation or enforcement pressure sufficed for causation/redressability, and what distinguishes those from this context?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel applied the “independent actors” principle to hold that the causal chain from an OSC investigation to AFL obtaining DOJ FOIA records rested on too many discretionary, independent decisions to support Article III causation and redressability. The court relied on three related points in the opinion: (1) OSC’s enforcement choices are discretionary and courts generally cannot compel particular enforcement actions; (2) any relief would depend on the independent, speculative choices of two other agencies (the MSPB and DOJ); and (3) redressability focuses on what a judicial order would bind the defendant to do, not on whether a ruling might persuade non‑parties to act. Because of those contingencies, the panel found AFL’s theory too speculative to satisfy standing.

How the opinion reasons it through (with citations)
- The risky chain the panel isolates. The court explicitly lays out the chain it says is speculative: if OSC had investigated, it “might have decided to pursue a corrective action under section 1214, or a disciplinary action under section 1215”; the MSPB “might have decided to sanction those employees”; and then DOJ “might have decided to produce the requested records.” J.A. (Op.) § II (third paragraph) (pp. 11–12 of the opinion excerpt). The court emphasizes that each step requires discretionary choices by actors not before the court.
- Independent actors / speculation rule. The court invokes FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024): “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” Op. § II (same paragraph). That principle is applied here because the MSPB and DOJ are independent actors whose willingness to act cannot be presumed.
- Limits on judicially compelling enforcement. The panel also notes Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), as authority that courts generally may not require a particular exercise of enforcement discretion by an agency. Op. § II (same paragraph). That cuts against assuming OSC would be forced to pursue the specific actions that would lead to production.
- Redressability focuses on what a judgment binds. The court stresses that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court.’” (quoting Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023)). Here a favorable ruling against OSC would not itself bind MSPB or DOJ to produce the records, so the relief is not likely to redress AFL’s informational injury. Op. § II (same paragraph).
- Procedural/informational standing context. The court contrasts this with situations where a statute creates an entitlement to information (Akins, Pub. Citizen, Zivotofsky), noting that AFL cannot point to a statutory entitlement to the investigative notices or to DOJ’s FOIA records that would make the denial a concrete, redressable injury. Op. § II (second half).

Why some cases differ: when investigation or enforcement pressure suffices
- Cases where investigation/enforcement sufficed typically rest on (a) a statutory entitlement to information or (b) a close, non‑speculative causal relationship between the defendant’s conduct and the plaintiff’s injury.
  - Entitlement to information: In Akins, Pub. Citizen, and Zivotofsky, plaintiffs had statutory rights to particular information and a defendant’s withholding of that statutorily conferred information was the direct cause of the injury. The court here contrasted those authorities because AFL has no statutory entitlement to the investigative notices or to compel OSC to apply section 1214’s notice rules in a section 1216 investigation. Op. § II (second half).
  - Less speculative enforcement link: Some precedents sustain standing when a judgment would directly require the defendant to take steps that remedy the plaintiff’s injury (for example, an agency order that itself produces the records or an order compelling the agency to turn over documents). The panel rejected AFL’s attempt to characterize an OSC investigation as producing that kind of direct, compelled effect because any productive effect would depend on others’ independent choices. Op. § II (middle paragraphs).
- The distinguishing features here: (1) multiple independent decisionmakers (OSC’s enforcement discretion; MSPB’s independent adjudication; DOJ’s discretionary production choices); (2) no statutory entitlement to the investigative information AFL seeks under section 1216; and (3) the impossibility of binding non‑parties (MSPB/DOJ) by a judgment against OSC. All three make the chain too attenuated compared to authorities that permit standing.

Bottom line
The panel found causation and redressability speculative because AFL’s theory required a sequence of discretionary acts by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ—none of which a court could be said to bind—and there is no statutory entitlement that would make the denial of information a concrete, redressable injury. Thus, under the independent‑actors/“speculation” principle (FDA v. AHM), Heckler’s limits on compelling enforcement, and the Haaland rule about binding parties, AFL’s claim fails for lack of Article III standing.
