### How does Heckler v. Chaney factor into a standing analysis rather than just APA reviewability?
Question:
The court cites Chaney to emphasize courts generally may not compel agency enforcement discretion and uses that to undermine causation/redressability. Is the idea that when downstream relief depends on discretionary enforcement choices, a plaintiff cannot show a likely remedy? How is this use of Chaney different from, or overlapping with, the 'committed to agency discretion' bar under the APA?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. The court is invoking the principle from Heckler v. Chaney not to say “this claim is nonreviewable under the APA,” but to show that the causal chain and redressability elements of Article III standing fail here: because OSC (and then MSPB and DOJ) would have to exercise prosecutorial/enforcement discretion, a favorable judicial ruling would not very likely produce the informational relief AFL seeks. That use of Chaney overlaps with, but is logically distinct from, the APA “committed to agency discretion” rule.

Why Chaney helps defeat standing in this case
- Chaney’s core holding: courts ordinarily may not compel an agency to take enforcement or prosecutorial action because such action is presumptively discretionary. See Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985). The opinion quotes Chaney to support the point that “courts generally may not compel agency enforcement discretion” (Op. § II, para on causal chain).
- Standing requires a plaintiff to show an injury that is “likely to be redressed by a favorable decision.” Lujan; Op. § II. If the only plausible way a court’s order could lead to the plaintiff’s requested relief is by triggering a sequence of discretionary decisions by agencies not before the court, that chain is too speculative to supply redressability/causation.
- The opinion applies that reasoning to AFL’s theory: even if OSC were ordered to investigate under §1216, the remaining steps — OSC choosing corrective or disciplinary action, MSPB imposing sanctions, and DOJ producing the FOIA records — all involve discretionary choices by actors not bound by the court’s order. The court cites Chaney in saying it would be speculative to assume those discretionary steps would occur, so AFL has not shown a remedy would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” its injury (Op. § II, paras 3–4).

How this differs from (but overlaps) the APA “committed to agency discretion” bar
- The APA “committed to agency discretion” doctrine is a merits/jurisdictional rule about reviewability: when a statute leaves agency action to agency discretion with no meaningful standard, courts lack statutory authority to review that decision under the APA. That is a limit on judicial review of the agency’s action or inaction.
- By contrast, the court here uses Chaney’s prosecutorial-discretion rationale as part of the Article III standing inquiry — specifically to show that a favorable judicial ruling would not likely produce the plaintiff’s desired result because it would still depend on one or more discretionary decisions. So the focus is on causation and redressability, not on whether the court has statutory authority to review the agency’s choice.
- They overlap because both doctrines rest on the same underlying rule about enforcement discretion: agencies have broad discretion not to enforce, and courts generally cannot force particular enforcement outcomes. But the functional role of the rule differs:
  - APA committed-to-agency-discretion → blocks judicial review on the merits when the statute gives no judicially manageable standards.
  - Chaney-as-used-in-standing → explains why an injunction or declaratory relief would not likely redress the plaintiff’s harm, defeating Article III jurisdiction.

How the opinion demonstrates the distinction
- The district court originally found a legal error but declined to compel OSC to investigate because it thought §1216(c) committed the decision to OSC’s discretion (Op. I.B). That is a classic commitment-to-discretion argument on the merits.
- The appellate court, however, does not rest on that nonreviewability ruling alone. It goes further and holds AFL lacks Article III standing because the causal chain from a court order to the FOIA disclosures is speculative and dependent on discretionary decisions by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ — and cites Chaney in support of that point (Op. § II). The court expressly frames this as a redressability/causation problem, not merely an APA nonreviewability holding.

Takeaway for future standing analyses
- When a plaintiff’s claimed injury would be remedied only if an agency (or other executive actor) exercises enforcement or prosecutorial discretion in a particular way, Chaney’s emphasis on noncompellability can be used to show the necessary causal link is too speculative — and thus Article III standing may fail.
- But that use of Chaney is analytically distinct from invoking the APA’s “committed to agency discretion” bar to defeat review on the merits; a plaintiff can fail for lack of redressability even where review on the merits might theoretically be available.

This is exactly the reasoning the court applied: Chaney supplies the rule that discretionary enforcement choices are generally not compellable, and that rule undermines AFL’s ability to show its FOIA injuries would likely be redressed by a favorable judgment (Op. § II, paras addressing causation/redressability).
