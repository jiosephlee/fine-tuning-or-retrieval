Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

### Does § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” language create a judicially enforceable procedural right for complainants?
Question:
The opinion suggests AFL framed its claim as deprivation of a procedural right to an OSC investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), but the court says a bare procedural right in vacuo doesn’t confer standing (citing Summers). I’m confused: when a statute says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, why isn’t that enough to be a concrete procedural entitlement for the complainant? Is the problem that § 1216 directs OSC’s duties without creating any corresponding right in requesters, or that even mandatory procedures still require a separate concrete interest tied to the procedure?
Answer:
Short answer: No — under this opinion §1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” imposes a statutory duty on OSC but does not, by itself, create a judicially enforceable procedural right for FOIA requesters unless the plaintiff can show a concrete, particularized injury that is caused by OSC’s refusal and likely to be redressed by a court order. The court’s ruling rests on standing doctrine, not on a textual refusal to read “shall” as mandatory.

Why: the governing standing principles
- A plaintiff asserting a procedural right must still plead a concrete and particularized injury traceable to the defendant and redressable by the court. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009) (a “procedural right in vacuo” is not enough).
- Informational-standing rules require a statute to create an entitlement to information; otherwise denial of information is not a concrete injury. See Zivotofsky/Akins line, quoted in the opinion.

How the court applied those principles here
- AFL framed its claim as deprivation of a procedural right to a §1216 investigation. The court accepted that §1216 “shall … conduct an investigation” imposes a duty on OSC, but rejected AFL’s standing for two independent reasons the plaintiff proffered as concrete injuries:
  1. Injury from DOJ’s nondisclosure of records: the court held the alleged informational injury was traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the causal chain connecting an OSC investigation to DOJ producing records was too speculative. That chain would depend on discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ—precisely the sort of independent-actor speculation courts cannot base redressability on (citing Heckler v. Chaney and FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.). Thus a judicial order requiring OSC to investigate would not be likely to “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s FOIA injury.
  2. Injury from deprivation of investigatory notices/information: the court explained that §1216 does not itself entitle a requester to receive the notices that §1214 provides; OSC’s use of §1214 procedures in §1216 investigations has been treated as discretionary (Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)). Because AFL could not show a statutory entitlement to those investigatory materials, there was no concrete informational injury of the sort recognized in Akins/Zivotofsky/Pub. Citizen.

Bottom line
- The problem is twofold in the court’s reasoning: (1) §1216 is framed as a duty owed by OSC (a public-law duty), not as an express statutory entitlement to particularized information or process in favor of FOIA requesters; and (2) even where a statute imposes mandatory procedures, a plaintiff must still show a concrete interest tied to that procedure (Summers). The opinion concludes §1216’s “shall” alone does not satisfy Article III standing requirements when the alleged injuries are speculative or no statutory entitlement to information/process has been shown.
