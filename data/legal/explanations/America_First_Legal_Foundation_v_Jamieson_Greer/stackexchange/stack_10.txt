### Could AFL have established standing via organizational 'resource-diversion' injury, and why might that fail under D.C. Circuit precedent?
Question:
The opinion doesn’t discuss organizational standing. If AFL had alleged it diverted resources to counteract DOJ’s FOIA practices or OSC’s refusal to investigate, would that suffice? My understanding is that mere litigation expenses don’t count, and the organization must show a perceptible impairment to its non-litigation activities—would that theory be foreclosed here because the injury still depends on independent actors and discretionary enforcement?
Answer:
Short answer: Probably not. Had AFL pleaded an organizational “resource diversion” injury, that theory would face two independent and dispositive obstacles under the reasoning in the opinion: (1) D.C. Circuit precedent requires a showing of a cognizable, non‑litigation impairment of the organization’s activities (not mere legal fees or “procedural rights in vacuo”); and (2) even a cognizable diversion injury would still founder on the same causation/redressability problems the court identifies—because any remedy depends on discretionary enforcement and independent actors (OSC’s enforcement choices, MSPB sanctions, and DOJ’s responses), making redress speculative.

Why — with references to the opinion — in a bit more detail

1) The doctrinal baseline for organizational diversion injuries
- The Supreme Court and the D.C. Circuit treat organizational standing for diverted resources as different from a plaintiff’s personal informational injury: an organization can show Article III injury if it has to divert resources from its regular (non‑litigation) programs to counteract a defendant’s conduct (see Havens Realty & D.C. Circuit cases). But the diversion must produce a “perceptible impairment” of the organization’s ability to carry out its mission; litigation expenses alone are generally not enough.
- The opinion presses that AFL’s claims principally rest on alleged procedural rights (the asserted duty of OSC to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216). It emphasizes that “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” (slip op. § II, quoting Summers). That same analytic rule governs organizational diversion claims: the organization must identify a concrete, non‑procedural programmatic harm or diversion (not merely that it sued or spent attorney time).

2) Why a diversion theory would likely fail here under the opinion’s reasoning
- The opinion already rejects AFL’s two informational harms for lack of causation/redressability: (a) DOJ’s nondisclosure of records is traceable to DOJ, not OSC; and (b) any chain from an OSC investigation to MSPB sanction to DOJ disclosure is “too speculative and attenuated” because it depends on “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” and on OSC’s discretionary enforcement (slip op. § II).
- A diversion claim would for the most part run into precisely the same defects. If AFL alleged it diverted staff/time to try to obtain the records or to educate the public because OSC declined to investigate, the causal link between that diversion and a favorable judicial remedy would still rest on the speculative chain the court describes: that an OSC investigation would trigger MSPB proceedings and sanctions that in turn would induce DOJ to change its FOIA practices. The opinion holds that a favorable judgment compelling OSC to investigate would not “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the alleged informational injury (slip op. § II (quoting Hecate Energy)).
- Further, the opinion stresses the barrier of enforcement discretion (citing Heckler v. Chaney): courts generally may not compel the particular enforcement decisions of agencies. A diversion injury premised on the idea that a court order forcing OSC to investigate would cause downstream remedial action is therefore weak: redressability “generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court’ ” (slip op. § II (quoting Haaland v. Brackeen)). If AFL’s diversion allegation depends on persuading MSPB or DOJ to act, that is the kind of speculative chain the court rejects.

3) Practical upshot for pleading strategy
- To survive, AFL would need to allege (and eventually prove) more than legal fees or programmatic activity undertaken in response to OSC’s declination that itself seeks to induce action by other agencies. It would need to plead a concrete, particularized impairment to its regular, non‑litigation programs that is traceable to OSC’s refusal and likely to be redressed by a judicial order against OSC — not by persuading third parties to act. Absent such a tauter causal and redressable link, the D.C. Circuit’s analysis in this opinion indicates a diversion theory would fail for the same reasons the court rejected AFL’s informational theories.

Bottom line: Under the court’s standing analysis, an organizational resource‑diversion theory would be unlikely to save AFL unless the complaint alleged a concrete, non‑litigation impairment directly traceable to OSC and redressable by a judgment against OSC — which the opinion shows is a hard burden here given the intervening discretionary choices of OSC, MSPB, and DOJ.
