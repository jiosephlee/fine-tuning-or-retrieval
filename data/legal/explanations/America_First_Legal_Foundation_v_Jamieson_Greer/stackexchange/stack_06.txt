### What standard of review was applied, and why is it de novo in this procedural posture?
Question:
The opinion states 'our review is de novo,' citing N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf. Is this because the case was dismissed at the pleading stage and standing is a legal question reviewed de novo? Does the same de novo standard apply to both the district court’s interpretation of the statutes and to the Article III standing determination based on the pleadings?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. The court applied de novo review because the appeal principally raises legal questions — (1) whether OSC’s decision was legally correct under the statutes and (2) whether AFL has Article III standing (a jurisdictional/legal question) based on the pleaded facts. The opinion explicitly states “our review is de novo” (citing N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf), explains that standing is a question of subject‑matter jurisdiction, and treats the standing issues as legal (and pleadings‑based) issues appropriate for de novo review. See Opinion at pp. 1–2 (statement of standard), II (standing as jurisdictional and pleading‑stage standard).

Why de novo applies in this posture
- De novo review of legal questions: The court invoked de novo review for the appeal of the district court’s legal rulings. The opinion begins by saying “our review is de novo” and then proceeds to decide statutory‑interpretation and redressability/causation questions as matters of law rather than deferring to the district court’s judgment (Op. at 1–2).

- Standing is jurisdictional and reviewed de novo: The opinion treats Article III standing as a threshold question of subject‑matter jurisdiction — “a question bearing on whether the district court had subject‑matter jurisdiction” (Op. § II). Jurisdictional questions are legal in nature and routinely reviewed de novo on appeal. The court therefore reviews whether the plaintiff’s pleaded facts plausibly support each element of standing at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage (citing Food & Water Watch) and applies settled Supreme Court standards (Lujan, Summers, etc.) to the pleaded allegations (Op. § II).

- Pleadings posture dictates the type of review: Because the case was resolved on the pleadings (dismissal and later dismissal after remand), the factual record is the complaint and related filings. At that stage the relevant standard is whether the plaintiff has plausibly alleged the elements of standing; that is a legal determination about the sufficiency of pleaded facts and is therefore reviewed de novo. The opinion states that “At the motion‑to‑dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of its standing” and then applies legal standards (causation, redressability, and whether any procedural statutory right confers a concrete informational injury) to those allegations (Op. § II).

When de novo would not apply
- If the appeal turned on the district court’s findings of disputed historical facts or credibility determinations, those factfindings would generally receive clear‑error or deferential review. But this opinion contains no such contested factfinding; it resolves standing and statutory questions by applying settled legal standards to the complaint and the statutory text (Op. §§ I–III).

Conclusion
The court’s “de novo” language is appropriate here because the appeal raises purely legal questions (statutory interpretation and whether the pleaded facts suffice for Article III standing). The same de novo standard therefore governs both the district court’s statutory interpretation and its standing determination based on the pleadings, as reflected throughout the opinion (see the “our review is de novo” citation and the standing analysis in Section II).
