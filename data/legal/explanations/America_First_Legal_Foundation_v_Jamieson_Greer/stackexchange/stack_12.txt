### Is there a narrower form of relief that would have satisfied redressability, such as remanding for OSC to reconsider without the legal error?
Question:
The district court remanded to OSC after finding a legal error but declined to order an investigation. On appeal, the panel says AFL lacks standing altogether. Why wouldn’t a limited APA-style remedy (set aside the legal error and require reconsideration) be enough to show redressability, even if the ultimate decision remains discretionary? Does the lack of any statutory entitlement to notices/information under § 1216 defeat redressability for that narrower relief too?
Answer:
Short answer: No. The panel explained that even a narrowed APA-style remedy requiring OSC to reconsider would not cure redressability here, because (1) any path from an OSC investigation to AFL’s receiving DOJ records is too speculative and depends on independent actors, and (2) AFL has no statutory entitlement to the investigatory notices or information it claims to want—so ordering OSC to investigate would not necessarily redress an informational injury.

Why, in the panel’s terms

- Redressability requires that a favorable judgment make it “likely” (not merely speculative) that the plaintiff’s injury will be remedied. Lujan; Hecate Energy, slip op. at ___ (cited in opinion). The court rejected AFL’s first theory (that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused DOJ’s failure to produce FOIA records) because the causal chain is attenuated: an OSC investigation might lead to corrective or disciplinary proceedings (under §§1214/1215), the MSPB might then sanction employees, and DOJ might then produce records. The panel held that this sequence would require speculation about the choices of two independent agencies (MSPB and DOJ) and about OSC’s own enforcement discretion—precisely the kind of “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” that courts cannot rely on to establish causation or redressability. (Opinion II; citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., Heckler v. Chaney, and Haaland v. Brackeen.)

- The court separately rejected AFL’s informational-standing theory. Informational standing is available only when a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive information. The panel pointed out that §1216(a)(3) (if read to require an investigation) does not itself create any entitlement to receive OSC’s investigatory materials or to the notices that §1214 provides; and AFL conceded that OSC’s application of §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations is discretionary. Because AFL has no statutory right to the investigatory notices or information it seeks, a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would not necessarily confer the informational relief AFL claims. (Opinion II, discussion of informational injury; citing APSCA v. Feld Ent., Akins, Pub. Citizen, and Carson.)

Why a remand for reconsideration without other relief fails

- Even if the district court had simply set aside OSC’s legal error and remanded for OSC to reconsider, that remedy would not bind OSC to any substantive outcome (the decision to investigate or to pursue enforcement remains discretionary) nor would it bind the MSPB or DOJ. The panel’s redressability analysis focuses on whether a favorable ruling “would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury; here it would not. The court therefore treated the speculative sequence of events as insufficient to satisfy Article III. (Opinion II.)

Takeaway for future plaintiffs

- If your theory of injury depends on downstream decisions by other agencies or on discretionary enforcement that the court cannot compel, a narrow remand under the APA is unlikely to establish redressability. And if you rely on an informational injury, you need a statute that actually entitles you to the information you claim was withheld; absent that statutory entitlement, courts will find no concrete, redressable informational injury. (Opinion II, paragraphs rejecting both theories.)

In short: the panel explicitly addressed the exact narrower remedy you describe and explained why it would not satisfy Article III redressability in this case.
