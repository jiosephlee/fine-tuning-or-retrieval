Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### Did the court’s finding that defendants aimed to 'comment and criticize' foreclose any secondary liability theories against ABC and Disney?
Question:
The panel affirmed dismissal across the board once fair use applied. If fair use is a complete defense to direct infringement, does that necessarily defeat vicarious/contributory theories against the broadcaster and parent company? Are there scenarios where a secondary-liability theory could survive even if the use is ultimately held fair?
Answer:
Short answer: In this case, yes—the panel’s fair‑use ruling effectively foreclosed Santos’s secondary‑liability theories as pleaded. The opinion treats fair use as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior,” and because the court concluded the JKL segments were fair use it disposed of the copyright claims (and thus left no underlying direct infringement on which contributory or vicarious liability could rest). See pp. 6–8 (fair use as an affirmative defense and application of §107), and the Court’s holding that “fair use bars Santos’s copyright claims even at the pleading stage.”

Why that follows from the opinion
- The panel framed fair use as excusing otherwise infringing conduct (citing Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179) and applied the four §107 factors to conclude the broadcast was fair use. Once the Court concluded the defendant’s use was noninfringing as a matter of law, there was no predicate infringement left for secondary‑liability theories to attach to. The opinion affirms dismissal “of Santos’s copyright claims” because “fair use bars” them. (Opinion pp. 6–8 and conclusion.)

What the opinion does and does not decide
- The opinion does not separately analyze or cite precedent on contributory or vicarious liability; it resolves the case by finding fair use and dismissing the copyright claims. That procedural outcome means the panel did not need to (and did not) adopt a standalone holding about every possible theory of secondary liability.

When a secondary‑liability claim could survive (consistent with the opinion’s reasoning)
- The opinion’s logic points to the limiting principle: secondary liability depends on a viable underlying infringement claim. Thus, under the reasoning here, secondary liability could survive only if there is a plausible predicate direct infringement that fair use does not negate. Put differently:
  - If a defendant’s conduct were held not to be fair use (or if fair use applied to some defendants/uses but not others), then contributory or vicarious claims could proceed.
  - If a secondary actor engaged in independently wrongful acts beyond merely airing or hosting allegedly fair uses—acts that themselves meet the elements of infringement or fall outside §107’s protection—then secondary liability might be viable.
  - Likewise, if factual differences showed the use was not transformative or did supplant Santos’s market (the very tests the opinion applies), the predicate infringement would remain and secondary theories could be pursued.

Finally, note what the panel explicitly rejected here: Santos’s attempt to defeat fair use by pointing to Kimmel’s alleged bad faith. The Court declined to treat subjective bad faith as dispositive and found the complaint itself showed defendants were motivated by “comment and criticism,” a finding that supported fair use (pp. 6–8). That factual posture—use characterized as transformative commentary and thus noninfringing—was the basis for dismissing all copyright claims in this case, including any that would depend on a showing of underlying infringement.

So: the panel’s fair‑use ruling did, in practical effect, foreclose secondary‑liability theories here because it eliminated the predicate infringement. But if the facts or law showed no fair use (or showed additional wrongful conduct by the secondary defendant), secondary‑liability theories could still survive in another case.
