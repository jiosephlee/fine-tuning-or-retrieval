### What is the Second Circuit’s appellate jurisdictional basis here, and could the panel affirm on alternative grounds not reached below?
Question:
The opinion summarily affirms the 12(b)(6) dismissal and says it 'need not resolve' preemption before rejecting the state-law claims on their merits. Is the jurisdictional basis 28 U.S.C. § 1291 (final judgment)? More importantly, is it standard for the court of appeals to bypass a preemption analysis and affirm on any ground supported by the record (even if the district court relied on preemption), and are there constraints on doing so?
Answer:
Short answer
- Jurisdiction: Yes — the Second Circuit is sitting under its general appellate jurisdiction over final decisions, 28 U.S.C. § 1291, because it is reviewing “a final judgment of the United States District Court” dismissing the complaint.
- Affirmance on another ground: Yes — it is routine for an appellate panel to affirm a district court’s judgment on an alternative ground supported by the record (especially on de novo review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal). But the court is constrained: the alternative ground must be legal (or otherwise amenable to appellate resolution), adequately supported by the existing record and briefing, and not require factual development or trial findings the record cannot supply; fairness/preservation considerations may counsel against raising novel issues for the first time on appeal.

Explanation tied to the opinion
- Jurisdictional basis: The opinion opens by identifying the case as an appeal from “a final judgment of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York” dismissing Santos’s claims, and the Court proceeds to review that dismissal. That language (appeal from final judgment) is the paradigmatic § 1291 posture. The Court does not invoke interlocutory jurisdictional statutes (e.g., § 1292), nor any statutory writ — it treats this as a straight appeal from final judgment.

- Ability to affirm on an alternative ground: The opinion itself demonstrates the principle. The District Court dismissed on two principal bases: (1) copyright claims barred by fair use, and (2) state-law claims either preempted or failing on the merits. The Second Circuit stated its review standard up front — “We review de novo the District Court’s dismissal … under Rule 12(b)(6). DiFolco …” — and then (i) agreed with the district court on fair use, and (ii) explicitly declined to resolve the preemption question (“We need not resolve this issue”), instead affirming the dismissal of the state-law claims on their merits. That is a textbook instance of an appellate court affirming on an alternative ground supported by the record and governed by de novo legal review.

- Constraints the opinion reflects: The Court’s approach shows the normal limits on that practice:
  - De novo review: The Court relied on de novo review of legal issues arising on a Rule 12(b)(6) record (as the opinion states), which makes it appropriate to resolve legal defects not reached below so long as resolution does not depend on disputed facts.
  - Record sufficiency: The opinion repeatedly frames its conclusions as based on the complaint and documents incorporated by reference (see the DiFolco quotation). It affirms the state-law dismissals because Santos’s complaint “does not ‘allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms’ ” and “failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss,” i.e., the existing pleadings and exhibits were sufficient to decide those legal failures. That demonstrates the constraint: the alternative ground must be resolvable on the existing record.
  - Avoiding unwarranted factfinding: The Court declined to reach preemption — a question that might have required more layered analysis — but did not invent factual findings; it relied on the pleadings and registrations. If resolution of an alternative ground would require new factfinding or a developed record, the appellate court should not decide it on appeal.
  - Preservation and fairness: Although the opinion does not elaborate on preservation doctrine, its structure (noting de novo review, citing the complaint and incorporated documents, and explicitly stating it “need not resolve” preemption) illustrates the prudential constraint: appellate courts prefer not to decide new issues when doing so would be unfair to a party or would require evidence beyond the record.

Takeaway for future cases
- When a district court enters a final judgment, the court of appeals has jurisdiction under § 1291 and may affirm that judgment on any ground adequately supported by the record and law.
- That flexibility is strongest for pure legal questions decided de novo on the existing record (as here with Rule 12(b)(6) issues); it is limited when meaningful factual development would be necessary or when deciding a new issue would prejudice a party who lacked notice or an opportunity to develop the record.
