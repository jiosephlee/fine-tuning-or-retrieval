Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### How can fair use be decided on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion—what materials can the court consider at the pleadings stage?
Question:
The court affirms dismissal because fair use was clear from the complaint and the works themselves, citing TCA Television and Cariou. Procedurally, what has to be in or attached to the complaint (or incorporated by reference) to let a court consider the original Cameo videos and the JKL segments on a 12(b)(6) motion? Can the court take judicial notice of the videos if they are publicly available online, or must they be exhibits? The panel also took notice of copyright registrations—are there limits on using registration data (e.g., publication dates) at this stage to resolve factor-two issues?
Answer:
Short answer
A court can resolve fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion only when the complaint plus the works before the court make fair use “so clearly established” that dismissal is proper. Santos shows what that means procedurally: the judge may consider (1) the allegations in the complaint, (2) documents attached as exhibits or incorporated by reference, and (3) certain public records (most importantly, Copyright Office registrations). What the panel did not do in terms of broad judicial notice was expand those categories — it treated the original Cameo videos and the JKL segments as the works “before the court” (Cariou) and relied on judicial notice of registrations (Island Software/TCA). So to get a 12(b)(6) fair‑use dismissal, put the works or properly incorporate them and (if relevant) attach or identify the registrations.

Explanation and supporting authority from Santos

1) When fair use can be decided on the pleadings
- Santos reiterates the governing principle: fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal” at the pleading stage. The panel relied on TCA Television and Cariou for that proposition (opinion ¶ I, citing TCA Television, Cariou). That means the court must be able to decide fair use on the basis of the complaint and the works that the complaint places before the court.

2) What materials the court may consider at 12(b)(6)
- The opinion applies the standard list from DiFolco: the court may consider “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” (Santos, quoting DiFolco, ¶ I.)  
- The panel also said it needed only “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” to decide fair use (quoting Cariou), and it resolved fair use based on the complaint plus those works. In other words: the actual works must be before the court either by attachment or by incorporation by reference so the court can compare them.

Practical implication: to enable a 12(b)(6) fair‑use decision, the plaintiff’s complaint should put the defendant’s allegedly infringing work and the plaintiff’s original work before the court — typically by attaching the works (or accurate reproductions/transcripts) as exhibits or by pleading facts that incorporate them and make them integral to the complaint.

3) Judicial notice of online videos?
- Santos does not state that the panel took judicial notice of videos simply because they were publicly available online. Instead, the court proceeded on the ground that the original videos and the JKL segments were the works “before the court” (Cariou) and applied DiFolco’s exhibit/incorporation rule. The opinion expressly notes the court may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations… as published in the Copyright Office’s registry” (citing Island Software and TCA Television), but it does not adopt a general rule in this opinion that publicly available web videos may be judicially noticed without being attached or incorporated.  
- Bottom line from Santos: do not rely on automatic judicial notice of internet videos. If you want the court to compare works at the pleadings stage, the safer route — and the route the opinion contemplates — is to attach or incorporate the relevant video files, transcripts, or other faithful reproductions so the court actually has the works before it.

4) Use of copyright registrations at the pleading stage
- Santos expressly relied on the Copyright Office registrations and the court’s authority to take judicial notice of them: “we may also take judicial notice of ‘copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.’” (Opinion, citing Island Software & TCA Television.) The panel used the registrations to establish that the videos were published the same day they were created (opinion ¶ I).  
- That shows courts will accept registration information from the public registry at the pleadings stage to establish objective facts in the registry (e.g., registration and publication dates), consistent with Island Software/TCA. But Santos does not suggest that registration entries displace disputed factual issues that are not of public record; the opinion uses registration data to supply an uncontested, public‑record fact (publication date) to inform a fair‑use factor analysis.

Practical implication: include registration numbers or certificates (or plainly identify them in the complaint) so the court can take judicial notice of whatever the Copyright Office registry shows (e.g., registration/publication date). Courts will accept those registry facts at the pleadings stage, but they will not convert contested, non‑public factual disputes into judicially noticed facts.

Short checklist for litigants who want a 12(b)(6) fair‑use decision (based on Santos)
- Attach or incorporate the plaintiff’s original work(s) and the defendant’s allegedly infringing work(s) (videos, transcripts, screenshots, etc.) so the court actually has the works for side‑by‑side comparison (Cariou; DiFolco).  
- Plead factual allegations that make the works integral to the claim (so they are properly before the court).  
- Attach or identify Copyright Office registration certificates/numbers if you rely on publication or registration dates; the court can take judicial notice of those registry entries (Island Software/TCA as applied in Santos).  
- Don’t assume the court will judicially notice web videos merely because they are publicly available unless you have placed them before the court by exhibit or incorporation; Santos treats the works as the ones “before the court,” not as newly noticed internet evidence.

Conclusion
Santos confirms that fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage, but only when the complaint and the actual works before the court plainly show fair use. Procedurally, that means you must put the works before the court (attach them or incorporate them) and, if useful, supply registration information for judicial notice. The opinion authorizes judicial notice of Copyright Office registry entries but does not endorse taking judicial notice of online videos as a substitute for attaching or incorporating the works themselves.
