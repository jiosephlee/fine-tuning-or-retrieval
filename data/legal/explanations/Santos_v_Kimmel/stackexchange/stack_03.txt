### Does alleged bad faith or deception in obtaining a copy negate fair use after Campbell and Google v. Oracle?
Question:
Santos argued Kimmel’s use of aliases on Cameo showed bad faith that should weigh against fair use (citing Harper & Row's 'good faith' language). The panel called that language questionable after Campbell and Google v. Oracle and found the complaint showed a purpose of commentary/criticism. In practical terms, does deception ever meaningfully tip the first factor or overall balance against fair use in the Second Circuit, and what kind of record would be needed for that?
Answer:
Short answer: Almost never, and only in a narrow set of circumstances. Since Campbell the Supreme Court and the Second Circuit have treated the Harper & Row “good faith” language as at best of limited weight; alleged bad faith or deception will only meaningfully hurt a fair‑use defense if the evidence shows the secondary use is not transformative and/or is intended to usurp the original author’s market (or otherwise make the use fall within the core proprietary interests the Copyright Act protects). Mere trickery in obtaining a work—without more—does not ordinarily defeat fair use in the Second Circuit.

Why (doctrine and the Santos opinion)
- Harper & Row stated that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” Santos, however, quotes the panel’s rejection of that rigid rule: the court notes Campbell’s “skepticism about whether bad faith has any role in a fair use analysis” and Google v. Oracle’s further observation that that skepticism is “justifiable” because “copyright is not a privilege reserved for the well‑behaved.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. at pp. ___ (citing Campbell, Google). The panel therefore treated Harper & Row’s good‑faith language as weakened.
- The panel then made the practical point: even if bad faith were relevant, Santos’s complaint affirmatively pleaded that Kimmel’s use was for commentary/criticism (a paradigmatic transformative purpose), and the complaint did not plausibly allege an intent to “supplant” Santos’s commercially valuable rights. The opinion: “We reject Santos’s argument regardless of the relevance of good or bad faith,” and the first fair‑use factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use.” (Opinion ¶¶ explaining Harper & Row, Campbell, Google; and the first‑factor analysis.)
- The Second Circuit therefore treated bad faith as at most a subsidiary consideration that cannot override objective evidence of transformation and lack of market usurpation.

What a plaintiff would need to allege/produce to make bad faith matter in practice
If a plaintiff hopes to use deception/bad faith to defeat fair use in the Second Circuit, the record must do more than show mere trickery in obtaining the material. The plaintiff needs evidence linking the deception to one (or both) of two decisive showings:

1) The secondary use is not transformative (or would be improbably viewed as such by a reasonable observer):
   - Evidence that the defendant intended and intended the public to use the work for the same expressive purpose as the original (not a different purpose), undermining any claim of transformation.
   - Internal communications or planning documents showing the defendant commissioned/induced the creation of the content specifically to repackage and broadcast the exact same expressive content, rather than to comment upon it.
   - Admissions or a consistent pattern showing the defendant’s real purpose was to reproduce the original’s expressive value rather than to add new meaning or message.

2) The defendant intended to usurp a traditional or plausible licensing market for the original (market‑usurpation):
   - Evidence that there is (or was foreseeable) a licensing market for the very kind of reuse performed, and that the defendant sought to avoid licensing by deception.
   - Emails, invoices, or business plans showing defendants treated the acquired recordings as a commercial substitute for licensed access (e.g., selling clips, creating compilations sold/licensed to third parties).
   - Proof of actual market harm or a plausible, non‑speculative market substitution (not just reputational harm or criticism).

Put another way: allegations that the defendant lied to obtain material will tip the scales only if those lies are tied to a non‑transformative commercial plan to use the material as a substitute for the original or to strip the work of its original communicative content and exploit it commercially. Absent those ties—where, as here, the complaint itself frames the secondary use as critical commentary and shows no plausible market usurpation—the Second Circuit will not treat deception alone as dispositive.

How that applied in Santos v. Kimmel
- The panel found the complaint itself established a transformative purpose (commentary/criticism) and no plausible market usurpation; thus the alleged aliases/deception did not change the fair‑use outcome. The court explicitly rejected Santos’s attempt to rely on Harper & Row’s good‑faith language and affirmed that even if bad faith were relevant, the complaint’s own allegations undercut any showing that defendants sought to supplant Santos’s rights. (Opinion at the fair‑use discussion.)

Practical tip for litigation
- If you want bad faith to matter, plead and (for summary judgment/trial) prove the link between deception and either (a) a non‑transformative reuse, or (b) an intent and ability to exploit a licensing/market substitute. Scattered allegations of dishonesty without that nexus are unlikely to overcome transformation and lack of market harm.

Conclusion
Deception can matter, but only rarely and only when tied to non‑transformative intent or market usurpation. In the Second Circuit after Campbell and Google, mere bad faith in obtaining material is insufficient by itself to negate fair use.
