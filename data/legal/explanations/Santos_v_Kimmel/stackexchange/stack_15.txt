Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### Why did the court rely on a 'reasonable observer' perception of the Cameo videos’ original purpose rather than Santos’s own stated intent?
Question:
The opinion cites Warhol to discount subjective intent and notes Santos’s own complaint described the videos as conveying 'hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.' How does the 'reasonable observer' approach operate at the pleadings stage when a plaintiff insists his actual purpose matched the defendant’s characterization? Does the court’s ability to view the works themselves override contrary conclusory allegations?
Answer:
Short answer: Because under Warhol/Goldsmith and settled fair-use doctrine the “purpose” inquiry is objective — what a reasonable observer would view the work as doing — and at the 12(b)(6) stage a court may look to the works themselves (and the complaint’s own allegations/exhibits) to resolve an issue that is facially dispositive. Santos’s bare, conclusory assertion about his subjective intent cannot override the reasonable-observer inference the court drew from the videos and the complaint.

Explanation and authority from the opinion

- Objective, not subjective, inquiry. The panel expressly relied on the Supreme Court’s teaching in Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith that “the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.” Santos v. Kimmel, op. at § I (quoting Goldsmith, 598 U.S. at 544). The Second Circuit thus framed the “purpose and character” (transformative-use) factor as what a reasonable observer would conclude about the works’ communicative purpose, not what Santos says he intended.

- The complaint’s own allegations cut against Santos. The court pointed out that Santos’s complaint itself described the videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Op. § I (citing App’x 15). That allegation, combined with the content of the videos and the JKL segments, supported the view that a reasonable observer would not see the originals as already serving the parody/criticism function Kimmel used them for.

- What courts may consider at the pleading stage. The panel reiterated the Rule 12(b)(6) framework: it may consider “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference.” Op. § DISCUSSION (citing DiFolco). Relying on Cariou and TCA Television, the court emphasized that “we need only consider ‘the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue’ to decide ‘the question of fair use’” and concluded fair use was clear on the face of the complaint. Op. § I (quoting Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013)).

- Why Santos’s contrary conclusory allegation lost. The court treated Santos’s claim that he intended the same purpose as Kimmel’s use as a conclusory allegation that cannot overcome an objective reading of the works plus the complaint’s own descriptive language. In short, where the works (and the complaint) plainly show the secondary use is transformative and no plausible competing inference exists, courts can decide fair use on a motion to dismiss. Op. § I (citing TCA Television and Hachette Book Grp.).

Takeaway for pleading practice
- If you want to preserve an argument that your work’s original communicative purpose matches the defendant’s later use, you must plead nonconclusory, fact-specific allegations showing why a reasonable observer would perceive that purpose (and, where possible, attach the works or precise descriptions). Mere assertions of subjective intent will not defeat a fair-use dismissal when the works themselves and other complaint allegations point the other way.
