Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### How did the court treat Santos’s claim that Kimmel ‘instigated’ the creation of the videos when analyzing transformative use?
Question:
Santos argued that because Kimmel submitted the prompts, he effectively designed the videos’ original purpose, undermining a finding of transformation. The court rejected this, emphasizing an objective reasonable-observer perspective under Warhol. Why doesn’t the requester’s role in shaping the original work’s content defeat a finding that the later broadcast use had a different purpose or character? Are there cases where the instigator’s role does matter to the first fair-use factor?
Answer:
Short answer: The Second Circuit rejected Santos’s “instigator/designer” argument. The court held that whether Kimmel prompted the videos does not defeat a finding of transformation because the governing inquiry asks what a reasonable observer would see the original works as conveying, not what the requester or the maker subjectively intended. The opinion relies on Warhol (and Supreme Court guidance) and declines to treat the requester’s role as dispositive.

Why — doctrinal explanation tied to the opinion
- The court frames the first fair-use factor around “transformative” use, which turns on whether the later work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” (citing Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021)).  
- It then rejects Santos’s contention that Kimmel “instigat[ed]” and thus “designed” the originals, explaining that transformation is determined by “what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user.” The court quotes the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision: the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) does not determine the purpose of the use. (Op. citing Warhol, 598 U.S. at 544.)  
- Applying that test, the court accepts the District Court’s factual finding that a reasonable observer would view Santos’s Cameo videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the JKL segments had the contrary purpose (sarcastic criticism — “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money”). Because the public-facing purposes differ, the court concluded the JKL use was transformative and that the first factor strongly supports fair use.

On bad faith and the instigator argument
- Santos also argued Kimmel’s deception (using fake names) showed bad faith that undermines fair use. The court rejects that too. It notes Harper & Row’s old statement that “fair use presupposes good faith,” but observes subsequent Supreme Court decisions have been skeptical about giving bad faith a determinative role (citing Campbell and Google). More importantly, the complaint itself indicates defendants’ purpose was critical commentary, not a commercial supplanting of Santos’s market, so the court saw no basis to deny fair use on bad-faith grounds.

Are there cases where an instigator’s role matters?
- The opinion does not identify any decisions that treat a requester/instigator’s role as dispositive of transformation. Instead, it relies on Warhol’s reasonable-observer rule and the line of authority treating subjective intent (of user or rights-holder) as irrelevant to the characterization-of-purpose inquiry. The court also cites Harper & Row only to note historical language about good faith but explains that later precedent has undercut the significance of bad faith in the fair-use analysis. Thus, within this opinion the court does not recognize the instigator argument as a route to defeat transformation.

Bottom line
- Under this opinion, the fact that Kimmel prompted or “instigated” the Cameo videos did not defeat a finding of transformative use because the test is objective (what a reasonable observer would perceive) rather than subjective (who conceived the original purpose). The court declined to treat the requester’s conduct as controlling, and it pointed to precedent that limits the relevance of subjective intent or bad faith in the fair-use inquiry.
