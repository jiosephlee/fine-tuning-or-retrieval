### How did the court handle the third fair-use factor when Kimmel allegedly used the entirety of the Cameo videos?
Question:
The opinion does not dwell on factor three, but it seems the show aired full videos or substantial portions. In Second Circuit practice, when is taking an entire work permissible because it is 'reasonable in relation to the purpose' (e.g., for commentary or criticism)? What reasoning would justify minimal discussion of factor three here?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel treated factor three (amount/substantiality) as subordinate here because the other factors—especially the first (purpose and character)—clearly favored fair use. The court explained that Kimmel’s uses were transformative criticism/commentary, and that there was no plausible market usurpation under factor four; those conclusions made detailed analysis of the quantity used unnecessary. At the pleading stage, where the complaint itself and attached materials show a transformative, non‑market‑supplanting use, copying even an entire short Cameo video can be permissible.

Why that reasoning fits the law (and the opinion)

- Second Circuit approach to amount/substantiality. The amount-and-substantiality inquiry focuses on whether the portion taken is reasonable in relation to the user’s transformative purpose. The opinion applies the familiar multi‑factor framework (17 U.S.C. § 107) and emphasizes that the “purpose and character” inquiry turns on transformativeness—“whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” Op. at 5–6 (quoting Andy Warhol Found., 11 F.4th at 37). When a use is transformative commentary or criticism, taking the amount necessary to make that commentary can be justified; the court therefore focused on whether Kimmel’s segments added new expressive content rather than on a granular tape‑count of seconds taken.

- How the opinion applied that to the Cameo videos. The panel concluded that a reasonable observer would see Santos’s videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while Kimmel’s broadcasts used them for sarcastic criticism—i.e., a different purpose. Op. at 6–7. The court therefore found the first factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use.” Op. at 7. Because the complaint itself and the attached registrations and clips make that transformativeness apparent, the court treated the other factors (including amount) as less critical to the ultimate fair‑use determination.

- Market effect (factor four) cut the other way as well. The court rejected Santos’s market‑harm theory, explaining that fair use asks whether the second work “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Op. at 8 (quoting Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 and Campbell, 510 U.S. at 592). Finding no plausible allegation that Kimmel’s segments competed with or supplanted Santos’s market for the Cameo videos, the fourth factor supported fair use and reduced the importance of a detailed factor‑three showing.

- Procedural posture matters. The court repeatedly framed its decision as a dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) based on the complaint and materials attached or incorporated by reference. Op. at 4, 8–9. Where the pleadings themselves establish transformativeness and lack of market usurpation, the Second Circuit has allowed fair use to be resolved at the pleading stage without an exhaustive factor‑three analysis. The panel echoed that approach: “Because we need only consider ‘the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue’ to decide ‘the question of fair use,’ Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) … we affirm.” Op. at 9.

Putting it together: minimal discussion of factor three was justified here because (1) the complaint and exhibits showed Kimmel used the videos for a clearly transformative purpose (criticism/sarcasm), (2) the complaint did not plausibly allege market usurpation, and (3) under Second Circuit practice a dominant finding on factors one and four can make a fine‑grained amount/substantiality analysis unnecessary at the pleading stage—even when the secondary user aired entire short works.
