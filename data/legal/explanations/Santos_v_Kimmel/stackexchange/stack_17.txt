### Does the possibility that Kimmel could be a co-author or joint author of the videos (by crafting prompts) affect the fair-use analysis?
Question:
Santos argued Kimmel 'designed' the videos’ purpose by submitting the prompts. If, hypothetically, Kimmel’s contributions rose to the level of joint authorship (or an implied license existed), would that change the fair-use calculus, or simply eliminate infringement altogether? How do courts treat authorship or implied-license arguments when the same facts are used to claim the secondary use is non-transformative?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — but they are different routes to the same end. If Kimmel actually were a joint author of the Cameo videos, or had an (enforceable) implied license from Santos to use them, there would be no infringement to defend against and the fair‑use inquiry would be beside the point. By contrast, arguing that Kimmel “designed” the videos’ purpose is a different kind of argument when used to defeat fair use: courts do not treat a defendant’s claimed role in creating the underlying work as dispositive of whether the defendant’s later use is “transformative.” The Santos opinion illustrates both points.

Why (and how) the opinion draws the line
- The opinion treats Santos’s “designer”/authorship argument as a fair‑use contest about purpose and character, not as an ownership question. Santos argued Kimmel “designed” the videos by submitting prompts and therefore the JKL segments were not transformative. The court rejected that framing: transformation is judged from the perspective of a reasonable observer, not by the subjective creator/secondary‑user intent. See slip op. (Sect. I) (citing Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith and noting “whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user”).
- The court therefore resolved Santos’s argument under the first fair‑use factor (purpose/character) and found the JKL segments strongly transformative (critical/satirical commentary) even accepting Santos’s pleading that he intended uplifting messages. The court relied on the reasonable‑observer standard to reject Santos’s attempt to convert Kimmel’s prompting into a non‑transformative purpose. Slip op. Sect. I.

What the opinion does (and does not) decide about co‑authorship or implied license
- The opinion does not find or adopt a co‑authorship or implied‑license ruling in favor of Kimmel; it reaches only the fair‑use defense on the pleadings. It therefore does not hold that co‑authorship or an implied license existed. See overall disposition (affirming dismissal on fair use and dismissing state claims on other grounds).
- The opinion does, however, treat Santos’s state‑law contract and implied‑contract claims separately and dismisses them for failure to plead the necessary elements under applicable state law (no pleading of enforceable third‑party benefit or essential terms of an implied contract). Slip op. Sect. II. That indicates the court saw the licensing/contract path as a different legal theory from the fair‑use inquiry and concluded Santos hadn’t plausibly stated it.

Practical implication for litigation strategy (as reflected in the opinion)
- If a defendant can prove joint authorship or an implied license in fact, that is a straight‑forward defense: no infringement, no fair‑use determination necessary. The opinion implies that ownership/licensing issues are proper grounds to eliminate infringement where they are adequately pleaded and proven, but it did not reach such a factual finding here.
- If the defendant instead relies on their role in creating the original work to argue the later use is non‑transformative, the court will apply the reasonable‑observer/transformativeness test rather than accept the user’s claimed purpose as dispositive. Santos attempted that strategy and the court rejected it on the pleadings.

Bottom line
- Co‑authorship or an actual (enforceable) implied license, if proven, would negate liability (no need to litigate fair use).  
- But merely arguing that the defendant “designed” the videos or submitted prompts to show the later broadcast use is non‑transformative will not substitute for ownership/licensing proof; courts (as here) will apply the reasonable‑observer standard in the fair‑use analysis and treat authorship/licensing as distinct issues to be pleaded and proven under copyright and contract principles.
