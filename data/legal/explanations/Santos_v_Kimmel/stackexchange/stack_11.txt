### What made Santos’s implied-in-fact contract theory inadequate under New York law at the pleading stage?
Question:
The panel cites Caniglia and DG & A Management to say Santos failed to plead 'essential terms' and a 'meeting of the minds' that Kimmel would adhere to Cameo’s ToS. What specific terms would need to be pleaded to establish an implied-in-fact promise in this context (e.g., scope of permitted use, commercial restrictions, attribution)? Can industry custom or the platform’s standard practices supply those terms without express assent?
Answer:
Short answer
Santos’s implied‑in‑fact theory failed because he pleaded no concrete agreement—no “meeting of the minds”—about the key terms that would convert a Cameo transfer into an enforceable bargain with Kimmel, and he did not allege facts showing those terms were supplied or accepted by the parties. Under New York law (as the panel applied Caniglia and DG & A), an implied‑in‑fact contract must plead the essential terms with non‑conclusory factual detail; Santos pled only the existence of Cameo’s Terms of Service and that videos were submitted, not the specific promises Kimmel allegedly made or accepted.

What the complaint needed to allege (the “essential terms”)
To state an implied‑in‑fact license/promise in this platform context, the complaint must allege the operative terms with enough specificity that a court can identify what was promised and that the parties agreed. That generally includes (at minimum):

- the grant or license scope: what uses Kimmel was permitted to make (e.g., “public broadcast on television,” “use in news/criticism,” “limited personal, noncommercial display,” etc.);
- any temporal or geographic limits: duration of the permission and where it applied;
- commercial restrictions or permitted commercial exploitation: whether resale, editing, broadcast, sublicensing, or monetization were allowed;
- exclusivity or sublicensing rights: whether Santos retained exclusive control or whether defendants could sublicense or transfer the material;
- attribution and moral‑right terms (if relevant): whether Santos required credit or restrictions on derogatory uses;
- consideration or bargained‑for exchange (what Santos received and what was promised in return); and
- any mechanics for acceptance/notice: how a user manifested acceptance (e.g., messages, platform confirmations, negotiated terms).

Why Santos’s pleading fell short under the cited authorities
- Caniglia (cited by the panel): requires pleading “the essential terms of the parties’ ... contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” The panel found Santos “did not ‘allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms’” (App’x 22 ¶¶78–79 and Caniglia citation). Santos therefore failed to identify which provisions of the Cameo ToS (or which additional terms) Kimmel allegedly promised to follow.
- DG & A (cited by the panel): requires factual allegations showing “an indication of a meeting of minds” that the defendant would comply with the asserted terms. The panel held Santos did not plausibly allege any such meeting of minds that Kimmel agreed to adhere to Cameo’s ToS (App’x 22; DG & A citation).

Can industry custom or platform practice supply missing terms?
Possibly, but only if pleaded with factual specificity. Industry custom and platform norms can fill gaps when a plaintiff pleads facts showing:

- those customs are uniform and well‑established in the relevant market;
- the parties knew or should have known those customs applied to their transaction; and
- the defendant acted in a manner consistent with accepting those customary terms (e.g., by following platform workflows that incorporate the terms, communicating acceptance, or negotiating around the terms).

What the opinion requires here is more than asserting “industry practice” or attaching Cameo’s ToS. The panel stressed (1) Santos was not a party to the ToS and (2) the ToS itself contains third‑party beneficiary language only where the drafter expressly intended it—showing courts require clear indicia that non‑parties were intended beneficiaries. Thus, to rely on custom or platform practice, Santos would have needed to plead facts showing either (a) Kimmel affirmatively agreed (by message, express acceptance, or conduct) to be bound by the specific ToS provisions that protect Santos, or (b) the industry/platform practices are so clearly and consistently applied that a reasonable user would understand the particular restrictions and that Kimmel accepted them in ordering and receiving the video.

Practical pleading roadmap (what a viable complaint would allege)
- Quote or attach the exact ToS provisions Santos relies on and explain how they protect a third‑party creator (or allege a separate promise by Kimmel mirroring those provisions).
- Allege concrete communications or conduct from Kimmel showing assent (e.g., messages acknowledging limits on use, platform confirmations visible to both parties, or Kimmel’s acceptance of specific restrictions).
- Plead the specific uses Kimmel made that violated those terms (e.g., “broadcast on national television without permission,” “edited for parody and monetized”), tying each to an identified breached term.
- If invoking industry custom, plead what the custom is, why it governs this transaction, and factual steps by the parties showing they acted consistent with that custom.

Bottom line
Under Caniglia and DG & A (as applied by the panel), Santos failed at the pleading stage because he did not identify the essential contractual terms or allege a plausible meeting of the minds that Kimmel agreed to be bound by the Cameo ToS (or equivalent restrictions). Industry custom or platform practices can supply terms, but only when the complaint pleads facts showing the customs actually governed the parties’ exchange or that the defendant accepted those terms. Without those factual allegations, an implied‑in‑fact contract claim under New York law is inadequate.
