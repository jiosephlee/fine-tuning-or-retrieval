### Why didn’t the court reach any First Amendment analysis beyond fair use?
Question:
Given the expressive, critical nature of the JKL segments, I expected a First Amendment discussion. Instead, the court treats fair use as dispositive. Is fair use considered the constitutional accommodation for speech interests within copyright, making separate First Amendment balancing unnecessary unless fair use is inadequate? Under what circumstances would the Second Circuit add a First Amendment layer beyond the §107 analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel treated fair use as dispositive because, applying the §107 factors to the facts pleaded, fair use plainly barred Santos’s copyright claim. The opinion follows settled Second Circuit practice that fair use is the ordinary, context-sensitive mechanism for accommodating expressive interests in copyright; where the fair‑use inquiry resolves the dispute (as it did here — transformative purpose, little market harm), there is no need to perform a separate First Amendment balancing. The court would add an explicit First Amendment analysis only if the fair‑use framework did not adequately resolve the constitutional free‑speech concern — i.e., if fair use were not clearly dispositive on the record.

Why the court stopped at fair use (with citations to the opinion)
- Fair use is presented in the opinion as the ordinary “excuse” for otherwise infringing uses that protects creativity and commentary: the opinion opens the fair‑use section by describing fair use as excusing “what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” (Opinion, I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use (quoting Hachette), ¶1.)
- The court applied the four §107 factors and found them to “strongly support[]” fair use: it emphasized the transformative nature of the JKL segments (they commented on and mocked Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money), rejected the notion that defendants intended to supplant Santos’s market, and found no plausible market‑usurpation theory pleaded. The opinion relies on the transformative inquiry as the key determinant of the first factor and the market‑usurpation standard for the fourth factor. (See opinion ¶¶ analyzing factor 1 and factor 4; citations to Andy Warhol Found., Hachette, Campbell in the text.)
- The court also noted that fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal . . . at the pleading stage.” It relied on that principle here: because the original videos and the JKL segments themselves (the only works needed for the inquiry) make clear the nature of the use, fair use was resolved on the face of the complaint. (See opinion: “it may be ‘so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal’ . . .” and later “we affirm the District Court’s conclusion that fair use bars Santos’s copyright claims even at the pleading stage.”)

How that relates to the First Amendment
- The opinion treats fair use as the doctrinal vehicle that protects commentary and criticism in copyright disputes. It does not say fair use supplants the First Amendment, but it shows the court’s view that the §107 analysis embodies the relevant accommodation for expressive uses: transformation and lack of market usurpation are the copyright-side standards that vindicate speech interests in many cases.
- The opinion also signals why an additional constitutional balancing was unnecessary here: because the statutory factors (applied to the concrete works) disposed of the case, there was no open constitutional question to resolve. The court thus resolved the dispute within copyright doctrine rather than conducting separate First Amendment analysis.

When the Second Circuit would add an explicit First Amendment layer
- The opinion suggests (through its methodology and citations) that an explicit First Amendment analysis becomes necessary only when the fair‑use framework does not adequately address the speech concern or when fair use is not clearly dispositive on the record. Two indicia in the opinion support this:
  - The court emphasizes that fair use is flexible and context‑sensitive and can resolve disputes on the record where the §107 factors are clear (citing TCA Television and Cariou). That implies that when those factors are not clear, the court may need to consider additional constitutional grounds.
  - The opinion distinguishes statutory fair‑use doctrine from other concerns (e.g., it rejects treating defendant bad faith as automatically fatal), showing a preference to resolve cases within copyright doctrine unless a separate constitutional claim is presented or necessary.
- Put another way: if a claim raised serious, unresolved First Amendment questions that §107 could not answer (for example, where a plaintiff plausibly alleged that a use both enabled market usurpation and chilled speech, or where the statutory factors yield a close result at summary judgment or trial), the court would likely engage in explicit constitutional analysis. The Santos opinion does not reach that point because the statutory factors were one‑sided in the defendants’ favor.

Bottom line
The Second Circuit simply applied §107 and found the JKL segments plainly fair use — transformative criticism and no plausible market usurpation — and so had no occasion to perform an extra First Amendment balancing. The court’s approach is consistent with treating fair use as the primary, often dispositive, doctrinal vehicle for accommodating speech interests in copyright cases; only when fair use cannot resolve the dispute would the court add a separate constitutional layer.
