Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### Why did New York law govern the implied-in-fact contract claim, and what "essential terms" had to be pleaded?
Question:
The court analyzes the implied-in-fact contract claim under New York law (citing Rocky Point, Caniglia, DG & A), while applying Illinois law to the express TOS claim. What choice-of-law principles explain that split—was it because the TOS had an Illinois choice-of-law clause but the alleged implied agreement arose in New York? Substantively, what specific "essential terms" would Santos have needed to allege non-conclusorily to state an implied contract with Kimmel (e.g., no broadcast use, no commercial exploitation, price adjustments), and how would a "meeting of the minds" be shown if there was no explicit discussion beyond the standard Cameo transaction?
Answer:
Short answer

The panel treated the two contract claims under different governing law because they were legally different contracts: the express Terms of Service claim was analyzed under the TOS (which the court says is governed by Illinois law), while the separate implied‑in‑fact contract claim was analyzed under New York law (the court expressly applies New York precedents). The opinion does not walk through a choice‑of‑law analysis in detail; it simply applies Illinois law to the TOS claim and New York law to the implied‑in‑fact claim and cites New York cases for the standards that govern pleading an implied‑in‑fact contract.

What New York law required Santos to plead

The panel relied on New York authorities — Rocky Point, Caniglia, and DG & A — to explain what Santos needed to plead for an implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law. Two key requirements emerge from the opinion:

- Allegations of the essential terms in nonconclusory language. The court quotes Caniglia for the rule that a plaintiff must “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” App’x 22 ¶¶ 78–79 (opinion citing Caniglia).
- An indication of a meeting of the minds. The court cites DG & A and Tjoa for the separate requirement that the complaint plausibly allege “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement.” The panel held Santos’s complaint failed both prongs.

What “essential terms” Santos would have needed to plead (concrete examples)

Under the Caniglia/Rocky Point/DG & A framework the plaintiff must identify the deal’s core terms with factual specificity, not mere legal labels. For this case that would mean alleging, with concrete facts, each of the following elements as to the putative implied contract between Santos and the Kimmel/ABC defendants:

- The parties and the relationship: who made the promise and who accepted it (e.g., that Kimmel, or his agent, directly requested and agreed to limits on use).
- Subject matter / scope of the agreement: a clear statement of what use was permitted and what was forbidden — e.g., “personalized Cameo video for private distribution only; no broadcast, no inclusion in television segments, no commercial exploitation.”
- Duration/territory/exclusivity (if relevant): how long the restriction applied and whether it was exclusive.
- Consideration / price: who paid whom, what payment (or other consideration) was provided, and whether payment was tied to the limited use; if Santos contends the fee was contingent on restricted use, the complaint must allege that factually.
- Performance details / delivery and acceptance mechanism: how the videos were to be delivered and how the requester signaled acceptance of the terms (e.g., platform confirmation, emailed instruction or acknowledgement).
- The specific provision alleged to have been breached: identify the particular promise (for example, “Kimmel agreed not to broadcast or commercially exploit the videos”).
- Damages/remedy tied to that breach.

How to plead a “meeting of the minds” where no explicit bargaining occurred

When there is no express negotiation beyond a standard platform transaction (the factual posture here), New York law requires factual allegations showing mutual assent through words or conduct. The opinion holds Santos failed to do that. Sufficient allegations would include particularized facts such as:

- Direct communications: copies or quoted text of messages, emails, or Cameo request/response content in which the requester (or an agent) expressly limited use (e.g., “please do not broadcast this; it’s for private use”) and the requester or platform acknowledged or accepted those limits.
- Platform confirmations or representations: factual allegations that Cameo or the requester’s account gave affirmative assurances that only private use was intended/allowed, or that a particular request carried an explicit usage restriction visible to both parties.
- Course of dealing or repeated practice: allegations that the requester repeatedly requested videos with the same explicit limitation and the requester consistently accepted them, creating a mutual expectation (with dates, examples, and content).
- Post‑request conduct evidencing assent: conduct by the requester showing assent to limitations (e.g., paying a premium for restricted use; instructing the talent about intended private use and not objecting when talent relied on that instruction).
- Agent/identity facts: if Cameo’s standard process obscured the requester’s identity, allegations that defendants used aliases but the requester’s agents nonetheless were aware of and accepted the restriction, or that the person acting as requester was an agent of the broadcast employer and communicated acceptance on behalf of the employer.

Why Santos’s complaint failed under the opinion

The panel concluded Santos’s complaint contained only boilerplate or conclusory allegations about an implied contract and pointed to nothing like the examples above. The court emphasized that Santos “fails to plead the essential terms” and “does not plausibly allege an indication of a meeting of minds” — the complaint rested on a standard Cameo transaction and did not plead any specific promise by Kimmel (or factual communications evidencing assent) that would create an enforceable implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law.

Bottom line

- The opinion applies Illinois law to the express TOS claim (because that claim is analyzed under the TOS) and New York law to the separate implied‑in‑fact claim (the panel expressly applies New York cases for that claim). The opinion itself does not narrate a full choice‑of‑law exposition; it simply applies those governing bodies of law to the corresponding claims.
- To survive dismissal under New York law Santos needed to plead, in nonconclusory detail, the essential terms of the implied agreement (scope of permitted/forbidden uses, consideration, duration, etc.) and concrete facts showing mutual assent (messages, confirmations, course of dealing, or other conduct); pleading an implied contract based solely on the ordinary Cameo workflow without such factual detail was insufficient, which is why the court affirmed dismissal.
