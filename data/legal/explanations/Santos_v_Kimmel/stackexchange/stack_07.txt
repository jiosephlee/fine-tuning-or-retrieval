### How did the court determine that the Cameo videos were 'published,' and why does that matter to factor two?
Question:
The court took judicial notice of copyright registrations showing the videos were published the same day they were created and cited the §101 definition. Is it proper to treat statements on the registration about publication as true at the 12(b)(6) stage, or just notice of what the registration asserts? Do privately delivered, paywalled Cameo videos qualify as 'published' under §101, and how does their being published affect the factor-two analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
- The court relied on the Copyright Office registrations (App’x 160–69) and the statutory §101 definition of “publication” to find the Cameo videos were published the same day they were created. It did so by taking judicial notice of the registrations—as allowed by Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005) (see opinion at pp. __) — and treating their stated registration information as an adjudicative fact for the 12(b)(6) record.
- At the pleading stage a court may judicially notice the fact of a registration and the contents of the registration as published in the Copyright Office registry; that gives a plaintiff’s complaint less room to dispute the registration date/entry, but it is notice of the registration and its entries, not a merits determination of every factual claim. The Santos opinion treated the registrations as establishing that the works were “published” on the creation date (App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101).
- Whether a given privately delivered, paywalled Cameo video is legally “published” under §101 depends on whether the distribution constituted a general dissemination to the public (or an offering to distribute to a group resulting in dissemination). Private, limited transfers ordinarily do not qualify as “publication” under the statute; the opinion, however, did not analyze Cameo’s access rules in depth — it relied on the registrations’ publication statements.
- Publication matters to fair use factor two because unpublished works have stronger protection against unauthorized appropriation; a finding that the videos were published reduces (but does not eliminate) the second factor’s weight in favor of the copyright holder. Even with publication, the court concluded the overall fair-use analysis favored defendants.

Explanation and authorities from the opinion

1) Judicial notice of registrations at 12(b)(6)
- The opinion follows settled Second Circuit practice allowing courts on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” The opinion cites Island Software, 413 F.3d at 261, and repeats that method (see citation at I; and the court’s statement that it may take judicial notice of registrations). The court therefore treated the registration entries (including the registration/publication date lines in App’x 160–69) as part of the record it could consider in deciding the motion to dismiss.
- Important nuance: judicial notice of the registration and its contents means the court may accept as indisputable the fact that a registration exists and what the registration says (e.g., the listed date of publication). It is not a merits ruling that every factual assertion in a registration is true in the sense of resolving contested factual disputes at summary judgment or trial. The opinion uses the registrations to establish publication timing as a pleaded fact for purposes of the fair-use analysis.

2) §101 “publication” and the Cameo videos
- The opinion expressly links the registrations to the statute: “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101.” (Opinion, I.) That reference invokes the statutory definition of “publication” in §101 as the benchmark for what “published” means for copyright purposes.
- Section 101’s operative concept is dissemination to the public (or an offering to distribute to a group) as opposed to private, limited transfers. The court did not walk through a separate factual inquiry into Cameo’s delivery model (private message, paywalled access, etc.) in this opinion; instead it relied on the registration statements identifying the works as published on a given date.

3) Do privately delivered, paywalled Cameo videos qualify as “published”?
- The opinion does not definitively hold on the general question whether all Cameo-style deliveries are “publication” under §101. It treats these particular registration entries as establishing publication for the record before it. In general doctrinal terms (drawn from §101 as invoked in the opinion): a truly private transmission to a defined, limited recipient group will ordinarily be “unpublished” for copyright purposes; broader dissemination or an offering to distribute that results in public access will be “publication.” Because the court relied on the registrations rather than litigating the underlying access facts, a different factual record (or a registration that did not assert publication) could yield a different conclusion.

4) Why publication matters to factor two (the “nature of the copyrighted work”)
- The court cites the registrations’ publication finding when addressing the fair-use factors (Opinion, I). Factor two asks whether the work is of a type entitled to greater protection; unpublished works traditionally receive greater protection against appropriation than published works. Thus, a finding that a work is published reduces the degree to which factor two favors the copyright owner.
- Even so, publication is only one input. The opinion shows that despite the registrations’ showing of publication the court found the first factor (transformative purpose) and the fourth factor (no market usurpation) strongly favored fair use, and the published status did not prevent the court from concluding that fair use barred the infringement claim. In short: publication moves factor two toward the defendant but does not control the overall fair-use balance.

Practical takeaway for a law student
- At 12(b)(6) a court can and will take judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations and rely on the registration entries (dates, deposited titles, etc.) as record facts. That allows the court to accept a registration’s statement that a work was “published” in evaluating fair use.
- But judicial notice of a registration is not a substitute for factfinding about whether a particular method of delivery legally constitutes “publication” under §101; privately delivered/paywalled material may or may not be “published” depending on the access/dissemination facts. The Santos opinion relied on the registrations rather than resolving that factual question on the merits, and publication remained only one piece of the fair-use analysis.
