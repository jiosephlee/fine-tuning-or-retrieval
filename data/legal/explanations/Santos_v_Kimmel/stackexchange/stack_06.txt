Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### Why did the court find no cognizable market harm under factor four despite Santos’s claim of lost licensing value?
Question:
The panel says we ask whether the secondary use 'usurps the market ... by offering a competing substitute' (citing Hachette) and notes Campbell’s point that creators are unlikely to license criticism. If Santos sells Cameo videos for money, why doesn’t a broadcast that repurposes them for ridicule count as market substitution or at least impairment of a derivative licensing market? What makes the JKL use non-substitutable for buyers of Cameo videos?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the fourth fair‑use factor asks whether the secondary use “usurps” the market for the original work by offering a competing substitute, and the court concluded (based on the nature of Santos’s Cameo videos and the JKL segments) that Kimmel’s broadcasts do not operate as a market substitute for purchasers or licensees of personalized Cameo videos. The opinion relies on Hachette and Campbell to explain that criticism/parody uses occupy a different market function from the original product and therefore ordinarily do not create the kind of cognizable market harm that defeats fair use.

Why the court reached that conclusion (with citation to the opinion)

- Legal standard applied: The court framed the fourth factor as whether the secondary use “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” It quoted Hachette Book Group, 115 F.4th at 189 for that principle and cited Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose, 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994), for the related point that creators of imaginative works are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions (Op. at §I, para beginning “We are equally unpersuaded…” and the immediately following sentences).

- How that standard fits these facts: Santos’s pleading itself described the JKL segments’ purpose as “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money” (Op. at §I). The court emphasized that fair‑use transformation is judged by what a reasonable observer would perceive, not the subjective intent of the parties (citing Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith). The court accepted that Santos intended his Cameo videos to convey “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity” (App’x 15), whereas JKL repurposed those videos into critical, mocking commentary. Because the JKL segments perform a different communicative function — critique/parody — they do not compete with or substitute for the market for Santos’s original personalized videos (Op. §I, para beginning “Santos also contends…” and later where Hachette and Campbell are cited).

- Complaint contradicts a claim of market usurpation: The court also noted the complaint’s own allegations undermined any suggestion the defendants sought to “supplant” Santos’s commercially valuable rights; instead the complaint paints defendants as motivated by criticism and commentary (Op. §I, second paragraph after discussing bad faith; citing Swatch and Authors Guild to the same effect). That factual framing made it implausible to infer the kind of market displacement required by factor four.

- Practical reason why the JKL use is non‑substitutable: Personalized Cameo videos function as paid, individualized messages (sent to specific recipients and conveying positive encouragement). A national broadcast that edits and repackages such videos for ridicule is not a functional substitute for a consumer who would pay for an original Cameo message; it serves a different expressive purpose and a different audience. The court relied on this market‑function distinction (Campbell’s point that creators will not license critical lampoons) to conclude Santos failed plausibly to allege the sort of licensing market harm that defeats fair use (Op. §I, quoting Campbell and Hachette).

Bottom line: The court found Santos’s allegation of lost licensing value insufficient because fair‑use market harm requires usurpation of an actual or likely licensing market by a competing substitute. Transformative criticism/parody that repurposes the work for a different communicative function (as the JKL segments do) does not plausibly function as such a substitute, so factor four did not weigh against fair use.
