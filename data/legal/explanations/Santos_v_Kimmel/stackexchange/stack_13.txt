### Could the district court have declined supplemental jurisdiction over the state-law claims after dismissing the federal copyright claims?
Question:
Because the court held the copyright claims barred by fair use at the pleading stage, could (or should) the district court have dismissed the state-law claims without prejudice under 28 U.S.C. § 1367(c)? On appeal, does the Second Circuit’s merits affirmance of the state-law dismissals implicitly approve the exercise of supplemental jurisdiction, or is that issue waived/harmless here?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes—the district court could have declined to exercise supplemental jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1367(c) after disposing of the federal copyright claims. But the Second Circuit’s opinion does not meaningfully fault that exercise of jurisdiction because the panel affirmed the state-law dismissals on their merits; any error in retaining jurisdiction would therefore be harmless (or moot) here.

Explanation and reasoning

- The statutory rule. Section 1367(c) gives district courts discretion to decline supplemental jurisdiction when the district court has dismissed all claims over which it had original jurisdiction. That is a discretionary doctrine (often applied under the familiar considerations of judicial economy, convenience, fairness, and comity). The question whether a district court should have exercised that discretion is ordinarily a procedural one for the district court.

- What happened in this case. The district court dismissed Santos’s federal copyright claims at the pleading stage (finding fair use). The district court also dismissed his state-law claims (either as preempted or for failure to state a claim). On appeal the Second Circuit (1) affirmed that the copyright claims were barred by fair use, and (2) independently concluded “that each of Santos’s state law claims failed to state a claim” and affirmed the dismissal of those claims. (Op. §§ I–II; conclusion).

- Why § 1367(c) would have been available but is not outcome-determinative. It would have been a permissible course for the district court, after resolving the federal claims, to decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction and dismiss the state-law claims without prejudice under § 1367(c). That is within the district court’s discretion. But the Second Circuit did not reverse the state-law dismissals on jurisdictional grounds — instead it affirmed them on the merits (finding the breach and fraud claims deficient under state-law standards). Because the appellate court independently sustains the state-law dismissals, any error by the district court in retaining supplemental jurisdiction would be harmless for purposes of the disposition: the state claims would be dismissed under the panel’s merits analysis regardless of how jurisdiction had been exercised.

- Practical consequence for appellate review. When an appellate court affirms state-law dismissals on their merits, it effectively obviates any reversible error that might have attended the district court’s decision to retain or decline supplemental jurisdiction. Here the panel’s explicit merits rulings (fair use bars the federal claims; state claims fail to state a claim) mean that plaintiffs obtain no relief even if the district court had instead dismissed the state claims without prejudice under § 1367(c). Thus the jurisdictional issue is not necessary to the Court’s disposition and is functionally harmless in this appeal.

Concluding point: If you were litigating this below, invoking § 1367(c) to seek dismissal without prejudice would be a sensible tactical move (to preserve state claims for refile in state court). But on appeal from a merits dismissal of the state claims, the Second Circuit’s affirmance of those merits rulings renders any question about the district court’s discretionary exercise of supplemental jurisdiction immaterial to the outcome.
