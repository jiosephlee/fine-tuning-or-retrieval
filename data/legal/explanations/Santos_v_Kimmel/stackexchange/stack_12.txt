### Why did the fraudulent inducement claim fail for lack of 'out-of-pocket' loss, and what would count as such a loss here?
Question:
The court, applying Lama Holding and Connaughton, found no actual pecuniary loss. If Santos spent time making the videos and alleges he was tricked into doing so under false pretenses, why isn’t the value of his time or the price differential between a personal Cameo and a commercial broadcast license an out-of-pocket loss? What allegations would satisfy New York’s 'out-of-pocket' rule in a case like this?
Answer:
Short answer
The claim failed because New York law requires a pleaded, concrete pecuniary loss from the fraudulent inducement (the “out‑of‑pocket” rule), and Santos’s complaint did not allege any such loss. Saying he was “tricked” into making videos and spent time doing so is not, as pleaded, an actual pecuniary injury under Lama Holding and Connaughton. To survive, Santos would have needed to allege a specific monetary loss fairly traceable to his reliance on defendants’ misrepresentations — for example, unreimbursed production costs, a forgone licensing fee or cancelled contract he would have obtained but for the deception, or other measurable expenditures or lost revenue caused by the fraud.

Why the complaint failed under Lama Holding / Connaughton
- Lama Holding (cited in the opinion) establishes that New York fraudulent inducement damages are governed by the “out‑of‑pocket” rule: the plaintiff must allege actual pecuniary loss resulting from reliance on the false statement. The panel says Santos “failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss” and therefore the fraud claim was rightly dismissed. Op. § II (last paragraph) (quoting Lama and Connaughton citations).
- Connaughton, also cited, confirms the point: New York law requires a real economic loss caused by the fraud (not merely hurt feelings, wasted time without pecuniary consequence, or an abstract loss of a nonmonetary expectation). The opinion applies that standard and concludes Santos did not plead such a loss. Op. § II.

Why Santos’s alleged losses (time and price differential) did not suffice as pleaded
- Time spent: The opinion treats Santos’s allegations about being “tricked into” producing videos and spending time on them as insufficient because they do not identify a pecuniary outlay or loss. Mere time or effort is not automatically an “out‑of‑pocket” monetary loss under Lama/Connaughton unless the complaint ties that time to a concrete economic harm (e.g., unreimbursed production expenses, loss of billable hours with a quantifiable value, or lost income).
- Price differential between a personal Cameo and a commercial broadcast license: The opinion implicitly rejects the idea that a hypothetical license value alone satisfies the rule unless pleaded as an actual economic loss. Santos did not allege that he was deprived of a licensing fee he actually would have received, that he attempted to license the clips and was precluded from doing so, or that he lost a specific licensing opportunity or revenue stream. Without allegations that defendants’ conduct resulted in an actual, provable forfeited license fee or other monetary loss, the “price differential” is an abstract theory of loss, not an out‑of‑pocket injury.

What allegations would have satisfied New York’s out‑of‑pocket rule here
To plead a viable fraudulent inducement claim under Lama and Connaughton, Santos would need to allege specific, traceable monetary losses caused by his reliance on defendants’ misrepresentations. Examples grounded in the facts of this case would include:
- Unreimbursed production costs: itemized expenses (equipment rental, studio time, props, travel, crew payments) he incurred to make the videos that he would not have incurred but for defendants’ misrepresentations. Allegations should state amounts and causal connection.
- Lost licensing revenue or forgone deal: a plausible allegation that Santos had an extant or likely licensing opportunity for commercial use of the videos (with an expected fee), that defendants’ broadcast preempted or destroyed that opportunity, and a quantifiable amount of lost revenue (or a specific license fee he would have obtained).
- Payment or fee diversion: allegations that Santos would have charged a commercial licensing fee for broadcast use (distinct from Cameo’s personal‑message pricing), that he did not receive such a fee, and a concrete estimate of that fee tied to industry norms or his historical licensing practice.
- Out‑of‑pocket reliance expenditures: money actually paid in reliance on defendants’ promises (for example, if Santos had to hire third‑party editors or pay expedited fees because he believed the request was personal, and those payments were lost).
- Actual diminution in market value: plausible allegations showing measurable diminution in the videos’ commercial value (with a dollar estimate), caused by defendants’ conduct and not by normal market forces.

Form and specificity: the opinion relies on pleading deficiencies. So to survive dismissal the complaint should do more than assert that defendants “tricked” him and that he “lost value.” It must allege concrete expenditures or lost revenues, with factual detail linking those losses to defendants’ misrepresentations — the sort of nonconclusory, quantifiable allegations the panel says were missing. See Op. § II (discussion rejecting Santos’s conclusory pleading and affirming dismissal for failure to allege actual out‑of‑pocket loss).

Bottom line
Under Lama and Connaughton, New York fraudulent inducement damages require an actual pecuniary loss. Santos’s allegations about time spent and speculative price differentials, as pleaded, did not identify a specific monetary loss caused by the defendants. To survive, he would have needed to plead concrete monetary expenditures or a specific lost revenue/licensing opportunity traceable to the alleged fraud.
