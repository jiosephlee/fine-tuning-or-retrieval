ANALYSIS 4 I. Stating a Claim — the Governing Law 5 A. Required Allegations and the Standard of Review 6 The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure require that a claim for relief contain “a short 7 and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2). A complaint may be dismissed to the extent that it “fail[s] to state a claim 9 upon which relief can be granted.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6). This Court reviews such a 10 dismissal de novo. See Bernheim v. Litt, 79 F.3d 318, 321 (2d Cir. 1996) (citation 11 omitted). In doing so, we are constrained to “‘accept[] all factual allegations as true, and 12 draw[] all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor.’” Shomo v. City of New York, 13 579 F.3d 176, 183 (2d Cir. 2009) (quoting Chambers v. Time Warner, Inc., 282 F.3d 147, 14 152 (2d Cir. 2002)). We are also constrained, however, to ascertain that the complaint 15 “contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is 16 plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 129 S. Ct. 1937, 1949 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007)). In other words, a complaint is not 18 required to have “detailed factual allegations, but it demands more than an unadorned, the 19 defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Id. at 1949 (internal quotation marks 20 omitted). In its formulation of the Twombly-Iqbal requirements for a statement of claim, 21 the Supreme Court has established the following order to be followed in determining 1 whether the pleading is adequate: “When there are well-pleaded factual allegations, a 2 court should assume their veracity and then determine whether they plausibly give rise to 3 an entitlement to relief.” Id. at 1950 (emphasis supplied). B. Consideration of Matters Extraneous to the Complaint 5 In considering a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim pursuant to Rule 6 12(b)(6), a district court may consider the facts alleged in the complaint, documents 7 attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the 8 complaint. Chambers, 282 F.3d at 153; Hayden v. County of Nassau, 180 F.3d 42, 54 (2d 9 Cir. 1999). Where a document is not incorporated by reference, the court may neverless 10 consider it where the complaint “relies heavily upon its terms and effect,” thereby 11 rendering the document “integral” to the complaint. Mangiafico v. Blumenthal, 471 F.3d 12 391, 398 (2d Cir. 2006) (quoting Chambers, 282 F.3d at 152–53). However, “even if a 13 document is ‘integral’ to the complaint, it must be clear on the record that no dispute 14 exists regarding the authenticity or accuracy of the document.” Faulkner v. Beer, 463 15 F.3d 130, 134 (2d Cir. 2006). “It must also be clear that there exist no material disputed 16 issues of fact regarding the relevance of the document.” Id. II. DiFolco’s Statements of Claims Examined 18 A. The Contract Claim 19 DiFolco’s complaint on its face clearly alleges a breach of her employment 20 contract with MSNBC. In brief, she alleges that she was subjected to intolerable working 21 conditions and that she was fired when she complained about the conditions. She also 1 asserts that defendants improperly interpreted certain of her e-mails as notices of 2 resignation. The District Court referred to e-mails from DiFolco that were said to be 3 incorporated by reference in the complaint in concluding that DiFolco did indeed 4 repudiate her contract by resignation and therefore that the complaint failed to state a 5 claim upon which relief could be granted. The District Court referred to those e-mails 6 dated August 23 and August 31in arriving at a conclusion that “[b]oth of these e-mails 7 from Plaintiff constituted repudiation of the Contract and relieved MSNBC of future 8 obligations” DiFolco, 2007 WL 959085, at *3. Under New York law 10 “[a] repudiation can be either a statement by the obligor to the obligee 11 indicating that the obligor will commit a breach that would of itself give the 12 obligee a claim for damages for total breach’ or a ‘voluntary affirmative act 13 which renders the obligor unable or apparently unable to perform without 14 such a breach.” Norcon Power Partners, L.P. v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp., 705 N.E.2d 656, 659 16 (N.Y. 1998) (quoting R ESTATEMENT [S ECOND] OF C ONTRACTS § 250) (citing II 17 Farnsworth, Contracts § 8.21; UCC § 2-610, Comment 1 (2001)). However, a 18 repudiation can be determined to have occurred only when it is shown that “the 19 announcement of an intention not to perform was positive and unequivocal.” Tenavision, 20 Inc. v. Neuman, 379 N.E.2d 1166, 1168 (N.Y. 1978); see also De Lorenzo v. BAC 21 Agency, Inc., 681 N.Y.S.2d 846, 848 (App. Div. 1998) (indicating that repudiation occurs 22 where “the repudiating party has indicated an unqualified and clear refusal to perform”); 23 Rachmani Corp. v. E. 96th St. Apt. Corp., 629 N.Y.S.2d 382, 385 (App. Div. 1995) 1 (explaining that repudiation occurs when there is “a definite and final communication of 2 the intention to forego performance”). Generally, “[t]he issue of repudiation or abandonment is an issue of fact.” Bercow 4 v. Damus, 776 N.Y.S.2d 289, 290 (App. Div. 2004). An exception applies where the 5 repudiation is in writing, in which case the court may resolve the issue of repudiation “as 6 a matter of law.” See York Agents, Inc. v. Bethlehem Steel Corp., 318 N.Y.S.2d 157, 7 158–59 (App. Div. 1971). Even if the repudiation is in writing, however, where the 8 written expressions are ambiguous as to their meaning, “then such determination [i.e., 9 repudiation] is to be made by the jury.” Hartford Acc. & Indem. Co. v. Wesolowski, 33 10 N.Y.2d 169, 172 (1973). Because DiFolco referred in her complaint to her e-mails to Kaplan of August 23, 12 2005, and August 24, 2005, the District Court could deem them incorporated in the 13 complaint and therefore subject to consideration in its review of the adequacy of the 14 complaint. Although DiFolco wrote in her e-mail of August 23 that she wished to 15 “discuss [her] exit from the shows” and to give MSNBC “ample time to replace [her],” 16 these statements can be taken as indicative of her desire to get out from under the 17 direction of the people with whom she had problems rather than leaving MSNBC 18 altogether. Also, her statement that she wanted to “be a part of [MSNBC’s] team for a 19 long time to come” and that she asked for a meeting with Kaplan to discuss matters 20 further may point in the direction of her desire to continue employment on a different 21 level. DiFolco’s e-mail to Kaplan on the very next day, August 24, in which DiFolco 1 inquired about the cancellation of her fashion week assignment, included the following: 2 [T]o be clear, I did not resign yesterday and was really giving you 3 significant notice of my intention so you could begin thinking about 4 alternatives for next year. [A]s always, count on my continued 5 professionalism. Kaplan’s e-mail response of August 28, 2005, ignored the foregoing altogether and stated 7 the following: “My complete impression is that you had resigned . . .” and “your obvious 8 intent is to leave.” Kaplan may well have put this “spin” on the earlier e-mail in an 9 attempt to rid MSNBC of an employee he considered troublesome. In any event, we do not agree with the District Court that “[p]laintiff’s 8/23 e-mail 11 is . . . unambiguous in expressing [DiFolco’s] intention to leave,” DiFolco, 2007 WL 12 959085, at *3, as a matter of law. There are at least factual issues as to whether DiFolco 13 had made a final and definite communication of an intent to forego performance or had 14 indicated her refusal to perform in a clear and unqualified way such as to justify a 15 conclusion that she had repudiated her contract. As further support for its conclusion that DiFolco had repudiated her contract, the 17 District Court referred to an e-mail communication from DiFolco to Kaplan, dated August 18 31, 2005, see id., following Kaplan’s August 28 e-mail that included language that it 19 would be “[b]est for all” that DiFolco leave “quickly” and that “sooner is better.” The 20 August 31 e-mail was not attached to the complaint, was not incorporated by reference in 21 the complaint, and was not integral to the complaint. Neither was it relied upon in any 22 way in DiFolco’s claim for breach of contract. Moreover, it was sent after Kaplan’s 1 August 24 e-mail setting forth his “complete impression” that DiFolco had resigned and 2 terminated her employment. The District Court therefore erred in considering the August 3 31 e-mail. Moreover, the August 31 e-mail was ambiguous as well. On the one hand, as the 5 District Court noted, DiFolco wrote: “It was my intention to give you long-term notice 6 that I was terribly disappointed in the way I was treated and was not planning on 7 returning for the second year of my contract.” On the other hand, DiFolco wrote in the 8 same e-mail: “So we went from your agreement to meet with me to discuss the issues to 9 you having a ‘complete impression’ of my resignation, which was never given. . . . Even 10 if you were unclear from my original e-mail, . . . what part of ‘I did not resign yesterday 11 and was merely giving you significant notice of my intention so you could begin thinking 12 about alternatives for next year’ in my follow-up did you not understand? No notice was 13 given, no formal letter of resignation was sent. No time frames were offered.” The August 31 e-mail is a lengthy one and cannot as a matter of law be said to 15 manifest a clear and unequivocal repudiation. In ruling on a motion pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6), the duty of a court “is merely to assess the legal feasibility of the 17 complaint, not to assay the weight of the evidence which might be offered in support 18 thereof.” Cooper v. Parsky, 140 F.3d 433, 440 (2d Cir. 1998) (internal citation and 19 quotation marks omitted). The District Court here “assay[ed] the weight of the evidence” 20 of repudiation and improperly chose between reasonably competing interpretations. Because we find that a claim has been stated for a breach of contract and that repudiation 1 has not been established as a matter of law, we reinstate DiFolco’s breach of contract 2 claim as well as her corresponding claim brought pursuant to the provisions of New York 3 Labor Law §§ 190–199-A for failure to pay wages. B. The Defamation Causes of Action 5 The District Court determined that two of the allegedly defamatory statements — 6 the statement on the “Inside Cable” website that DiFolco had resigned from MSNBC “in 7 the middle of her contract” and the statement on the “NewsBlues” website that Claudia 8 DiFolco has quit MSNBC in the middle of her contract” — “[we]re true . . . and thus not 9 actionable.” DiFolco, 2007 WL 959085, at *5. This determination was based on the 10 court’s conclusion that DiFolco repudiated her contract as a matter of law, a conclusion 11 that we have rejected as premature. Accordingly, we are constrained to reinstate the 12 defamation claims, which are pleaded properly to state claims in any event. The 13 complaint alleges that the statements were untrue and defamatory and were published 14 with malice and “with knowledge of their falsity and/or with a reckless disregard for their 15 truth or falsity.” DiFolco further alleges that these statements were “willful” and 16 “intended to seriously harm [her] . . . career.” According to the complaint, the defamatory statements “impugn Ms. DiFolco’s 18 honesty, trustworthiness, dependability, and professional fitness and abilities by falsely 19 charging her with conduct that would tend to injure her in her trade or business.” These 20 and other allegations in the complaint are adequate to support a claim based on a “writing 21 which tends to disparage a person in the way of [her] office, profession, or trade.” 1 Nichols v. Item Publishers, Inc., 132 N.E.2d 860, 862 (N.Y. 1956). The complaint 2 describes statements that, “if true, would tend to prove [DiFolco] unfit to continue [her] 3 calling.” Id. (internal quotation marks and citations omitted). The complaint sets forth 4 the necessary elements to make out a claim for defamation in New York, including the 5 element of malice. See generally Liberman v. Gelstein, 605 N.E.2d 344, 349–50 (N.Y. 6 1992). As to the third alleged defamatory statement — the TVSpy website statement that 8 DiFolco relied on cleavage and makeup to advance her career, ignored directions, refused 9 “alternate takes,” pouted, and was not a team player — the District Court also was 10 premature in its dismissal. The District Court determined that “a reasonable reader would 11 conclude from both the broader context and the immediate context that the third allegedly 12 defamatory statement consists of opinions, not facts.” DiFolco, 2007 WL 959085, at *8. However, “‘[o]pinions based on false facts are actionable . . . against a defendant who had 14 knowledge of the falsity or probable falsity of the underlying facts.’” Davis v. Ross, 754 15 F.2d 80, 86 (2d Cir. 1985) (quoting Hotchner v. Castillo-Puche, 551 F.2d 910, 913 (2d 16 Cir. 1977). Such knowledge of falsity is properly alleged here, and DiFolco is entitled to 17 show that the “negative characterization” presented on the website “is coupled with a 18 clear but false implication that the author is privy to facts about the person that are 19 unknown to the general reader.” Davis 754 F.2d at 85–86. C. The Tortious Interference with Prospective Business Relations Claim 21 In order to recover for tortious interference with prospective business relations, a 1 plaintiff must plead and prove: 2 (1) business relations with a third party; (2) the defendant’s interference 3 with those business relations; (3) the defendant acted with the sole purpose 4 of harming the plaintiff or used dishonest, unfair or improper means; and 5 (4) injury to the business relationship. Nadel v. Play-By-Play Toys & Novelties, Inc., 208 F.3d 368, 382 (2d Cir. 2000). Noting 7 the statement of counsel “that Plaintiff relied solely on defamation as the wrongful act 8 predicating the claim of tortious interference” and that it had dismissed the defamation 9 claims, the District Court found no further basis for proceeding with the tortious 10 interference claim and dismissed it for failure to state a claim. DiFolco, 2007 WL 11 959805, at *8. Our reinstatement of the defamation claim eliminates the District Court’s 12 rationale for dismissal of the tortious interference claim. However, our review of the 13 District Court’s 12(b)(6) rulings being de novo, “we are free to affirm the decision below 14 on dispositive but different grounds.” Primetime 24 Joint Venture v. Nat’l Broad. Co., 15 219 F.3d 92, 103 (2d Cir. 2000). We affirm the dismissal of tortious interference claim 16 for the reasons given below. In the third claim, entitled “Tortious Interference with Contractual and/or 18 Prospective Business Relations,” DiFolco alleges that “[d]efendants intentionally 19 interfered with Plaintiff’s professional relationships and opportunities for employment” 20 and that “[d]efendants’ actions permanently injured Plaintiff’s business relationships in 21 the [news and entertainment] industry.” According to the complaint, these actions gave 22 rise to damages flowing from harm to DiFolco’s career development, economic harm in 1 the form of lost income and benefits, harm to her professional and personal reputation, 2 and harm consisting of mental anguish and emotional distress. Aside from the fact that these allegations are too conclusory, vague, and lacking in 4 a factual basis to make out DiFolco’s tortious interference claim, see Black Car and 5 Livery Ins., Inc. v. H & W Brokerage, Inc., 813 N.Y.S.2d 751, 752 (App. Div. 2006), the 6 complaint fails entirely to describe any third party with whom DiFolco had prospective 7 business relations to be interfered with, see Nadel, 208 F.3d at 382–83. The lack of such 8 an allegation is fatal to this claim. III. Conclusion 10 We affirm the judgment of the District Court as to the dismissal of the claim for 11 tortious interference and vacate so much of the judgment as dismisses the causes of action 12 for breach of contract (with related New York Labor Law claims) and defamation. We 13 remand for further proceedings consistent with the foregoing.
