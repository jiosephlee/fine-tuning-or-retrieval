Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

# Copyright, Comedy, and the Creator Economy: Setting the Stage

This chapter uses the Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel to introduce—and to illustrate by application—the contemporary contours of fair use in a digital, platform-mediated creator economy and the limits of state-law claims that often accompany copyright litigation. The facts present a familiar modern collision: a public figure records short, personalized videos through a for-hire platform (Cameo); a television host procures those videos under false names; the host broadcasts them with an overlay of commentary and mockery to millions of viewers. The opinion treats that collision as an occasion to apply the classic four-factor fair use test, to explain when courts may resolve fair use at the pleading stage, and to revisit how contract and fraud claims survive (or fail) alongside federal copyright claims. The case thus teaches how courts balance protection for original creators against the First Amendment and the public interest in critical commentary, and how state-law torts and contract theories fare when invoked in disputes about platform-mediated content.

The organization of the discussion follows the familiar doctrinal pieces that the opinion both applies and illuminates: the factual background that gives texture to the legal questions; the discrete claims Santos brought; the procedural posture and standards of review; and a thematic exposition of the doctrinal lessons the opinion offers on transformative use, market harm, pleading-stage disposition on fair use, contract enforceability under platform terms, and the damage requirements for fraud claims.

## Factual background: Cameo requests under fictitious names and broadcast on a late‑night show

The facts of Santos v. Kimmel are straightforward and emblematic of disputes in the creator economy. George Santos, a former Congressman, sold personalized video messages through Cameo, a platform that connects content creators with paying requestors. Jimmy Kimmel, host of a nationally televised late-night program, used various fictitious names to submit requests for personalized video messages from Santos. Santos responded to those requests and produced the videos. Kimmel then selected some of those videos and aired them on Jimmy Kimmel Live! as part of recurring segments titled “Will Santos Say It?” The segments juxtaposed the videos with mocking commentary, casting them as evidence that Santos would “say absurd things for money.”

These facts raise two clusters of legal questions. First, did the broadcast and commentary transform the videos sufficiently to qualify as fair use? Second, did Kimmel’s use of fictitious requestor identities, and the supposed violation of any platform terms, give rise to enforceable contract or fraud claims under state law? The opinion frames both clusters against the background of how courts treat content created on paid-request platforms and later repurposed for mass broadcast and comedic commentary.

When teaching or briefing a dispute with this fact pattern, it helps to isolate three relevant, often-disputed points: (1) the nature of the original works (short, commissioned, published on the same day they were created), (2) the secondary use (televised montage plus critical commentary and mockery), and (3) the method of procurement (fictitious requestors on a user platform). Each point bears on different fair use factors and on the plausibility of contract- or fraud-based claims.

## Claims asserted: copyright infringement, breach of contract, breach of implied contract, fraudulent inducement

Santos’s complaint advanced four primary claims. First, he alleged federal copyright infringement: he claimed copyright in his Cameo videos and argued that Kimmel and the broadcasters reproduced and publicly performed those videos without authorization. Second and third, Santos pleaded state-law contract claims: a direct breach of Cameo’s Terms of Service (TOS) and a claim for breach of an implied contract (the latter based on the proposition that the defendants agreed—expressly or implicitly—to adhere to Cameo’s rules or practices). Fourth, he asserted fraudulent inducement, alleging that defendants used deceit (fictitious names) to procure videos and that this misrepresentation induced him to create content he would not otherwise have created.

These claims present a typical litigation strategy in modern content disputes: combine a federal copyright claim (which supplies statutory damages and injunctive remedies) with state-law contract and tort claims (which may broaden the damages picture or supply alternative theories of liability). The Second Circuit’s opinion evaluates each claim in turn, applying federal fair use law to the copyright claim and state-law doctrines (as governed by choice-of-law provisions) to the contract and fraud claims.

Key doctrinal takeaways from these claims: (1) fair use remains the primary defense in many cases where originally expressive content is repurposed for commentary or satire; (2) litigants often try to convert platform rules into enforceable rights against third parties, but courts are skeptical about conferring third-party beneficiary status to non-contracting users; and (3) fraud claims anchored in procurement by deception must clear jurisdiction-specific damage rules—New York, for example, requires a showing of out-of-pocket loss.

## Procedural posture: Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal and de novo review on appeal

Santos’s complaint was dismissed under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim. On appeal, the Second Circuit reviewed the dismissal de novo. Two procedural principles governed the Court’s analysis.

First, when a court resolves a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, it must accept as true well-pleaded factual allegations, construe them in the plaintiff’s favor, and limit its review to the complaint, documents incorporated by reference, and matters of which the court may take judicial notice. The Second Circuit therefore considered the complaint and several incorporated materials—most notably the copyright registrations themselves, of which courts may take judicial notice.

Second, fair use, while an affirmative defense, can be decided at the pleading stage if the asserted defense is clear on the face of the complaint together with any incorporated materials. The Second Circuit has recognized that fair use is “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” in certain circumstances (citing TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum and related precedent). That is not always the case—many fair-use disputes require factual development—but where the complaint and incorporated exhibits demonstrate the secondary user’s purpose and character, the nature and amount of the portion used, and the absence of cognizable market harm, courts may resolve fair use as a matter of law on a motion to dismiss.

The Court in Santos relied on this procedural posture to assess fair use de novo, taking judicial notice of the registrations showing that the videos had been published the same day they were created, and concluding that, as a matter of law, the JKL segments were fair use. For students, the procedural lesson is concrete: allegations and incorporated documents that establish the secondary purpose (e.g., commentary, satire), the nature of the original works (e.g., brief, published), and the market effects can, in the right case, bring fair use into dispositive view at the pleadings stage.

## Core teaching themes

Below we unpack the doctrinal themes that the Santos opinion brings into relief: transformative use and the reasonable observer test; market harm and licensing markets; the viability of resolving fair use at the pleading stage; the limits of pressing platform Terms of Service into enforceable third-party rights; and New York’s rule on damages for fraudulent inducement. Each sub-theme explains the relevant law, how Santos applies that law, and how to apply the principle in novel fact patterns.

1) Transformative use in commentary and satire

The first fair use factor—“the purpose and character of the use”—is pivotal in modern fair use analysis. The Second Circuit emphasizes that this factor focuses “chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative,” i.e., whether the secondary work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character,” rather than merely superseding the original. In Santos, the Court found that JKL’s use was plainly transformative because the televised segments recast Santos’s sincere-seeming greetings into objects of satire and criticism; the secondary use commented on Santos’s readiness to say outrageous things for hire, an objective distinct from the affirmative, encouraging purpose Santos alleged the videos served.

Two doctrinal refinements are worth underscoring. First, a transformative-use inquiry is an objective one: courts ask what a reasonable observer would perceive, not what the plaintiff subjectively intended or what the defendant claims as intent. The opinion follows the Supreme Court in rejecting an inquiry into the subjective intent of the user as dispositive; instead, transformation is measured by the change in purpose and character visible to observers. Second, transformation may be established even when the secondary work derives from a use of the exact or substantial content of the original, so long as the new work contextualizes, critiques, or otherwise repurposes the original content to serve a different communicative function. Parody and commentary are paradigmatic examples.

In practice: when confronted with a fact pattern in which a broadcaster or commentator uses user-generated content to criticize or mock the content creator, analyze whether the secondary presentation conveys a different communicative purpose. If the host’s segments aim to critique, ridicule, or analyze the original creator, and those aims are reasonably perceivable by the audience, the first factor will likely weigh in favor of fair use. Conversely, if the secondary use merely rebroadcasts the content without meaningful commentary, the use will be less likely to be transformative.

2) Bad faith, deception, and the role (if any) of subjective intent

Santos argued that Kimmel’s use of fictitious names demonstrated bad faith that should preclude invoking fair use. Historically, the Supreme Court once stated that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” (Harper & Row), and lower courts have sometimes considered bad faith evidence. But the Supreme Court and later federal decisions have cast doubt on whether subjective bad faith should play a central role in the fair use inquiry. The Second Circuit in Santos follows that line: it recognizes the historical language about good faith but treats it skeptically and declines to permit alleged deception in the procurement of the videos to defeat fair use when the secondary use was clearly transformative and there is no plausible allegation that the defendants intended to supplant the market for the original works.

The doctrinal lesson is that deception in procurement—while possibly relevant to some equitable remedies or to state-law fraud claims—does not automatically foreclose a fair use defense where transformation is apparent and market usurpation is absent. Courts will consider bad faith in context, but they will not treat every act of deception as sufficient to nullify an otherwise strong fair use showing.

In practice: if a fact pattern includes deceptive procurement, separate the legal consequences. Deception may be probative of motive and may support state-law claims for fraud, but it is unlikely to be dispositive of fair use when the secondary purpose is transformative and the other fair use factors point toward a finding of fair use.

3) Market harm: the displacement/market-substitution analysis

The fourth factor—effect on the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work—focuses less on whether the secondary use “devalues” the original and more on whether it “usurps” or supplies a substitute for the original in any cognizable market. The seminal gloss is that courts consider whether the secondary use serves as a market substitute for the original, thereby harming the copyright owner’s ability to exploit the work. The Supreme Court in Campbell explained that certain critical uses (e.g., parody, borrowing for commentary) are unlikely to supplant the market for the original, and therefore pose little threat to licensing markets.

In Santos, the Court held that Santos had not plausibly alleged that the JKL segments usurped any licensing market for his videos. The videos were personalized greetings sold for individual compensation on Cameo; it was implausible that customers of Santos would, instead of buying personalized messages, choose to license Kimmel’s satirical broadcasts as a substitute. The Second Circuit therefore treated the market-harm factor as favoring fair use.

In assessing market harm in new scenarios, the critical questions are: (a) what is the relevant market for the original work, including potential licensing markets? (b) Does the secondary use function as a reasonable substitute for purchasers of the original? and (c) are there established or potential licensing avenues that the secondary use would supplant? If the answer to (b) or (c) is yes, the fourth factor may weigh against fair use. If not—especially for uses that are critical, transformative, or non-substitutive—this factor will often favor fair use.

4) Amount and substantiality, and the nature of the copyrighted work

The second and third fair use factors—the nature of the copyrighted work and the amount/substantiality of the portion used—require a careful, fact-sensitive balancing. Courts have tended to accord more protection to unpublished, creative works than to published or factual works, and to view the taking of an entire work more skeptically than the taking of excerpts—unless the entire work is necessary for the secondary purpose.

In Santos, the Court took note that Santos’s videos were published on the same day they were created, a fact established by the copyright registrations. Publication reduces the weight of the second factor favoring the plaintiff, and the short, episodic character of Cameo videos may also affect how courts assess substantiality. The Court ultimately treated these factors as not defeating fair use when combined with the strong showing on transformation and the lack of market substitution.

Analytically, ask these questions when evaluating these factors in new fact patterns: Was the original work unpublished or highly creative in a way that makes copying particularly harmful? How much of the original was used, quantitatively and qualitatively? Did the secondary user take the “heart” of the work, or only a necessary portion for commentary? If the secondary use takes an entire short work but recasts it for commentary, this factor must be evaluated in light of the first and fourth factors—the amount taken may be justified by the purpose of critique.

5) Pleading-stage fair use: when courts may dismiss on the papers

A recurrent practical issue is whether fair use can be resolved on a motion to dismiss. The Santos opinion reaffirms the Second Circuit’s prior approach: fair use can be decided at the pleading stage when the complaint and incorporated materials make the defense apparent. The Court relied on the complaint’s allegations (including admissions about Santos’s asserted original purpose for the videos), the registrations showing publication timing, and the observable content of the JKL segments to conclude that no set of facts could plausibly defeat a fair use defense.

The boundary, however, is important to grasp. Courts should not grant dismissal where the facts relevant to fair use are in dispute or where extrinsic evidence (e.g., extensive factual development about editing choices, audience perception, or market behavior) is necessary to determine transformation or market substitution. Only when the materials before the court establish the elements of a fair use defense as a matter of law should dismissal follow.

For law students and litigators: when evaluating whether to move for dismissal or to resist such a motion, carefully inventory the complaint and attachments. If they admit—or minimally permit judicial notice of—facts that establish substantial transformation and no plausible market harm, then a 12(b)(6) dismissal may be defensible. If, by contrast, the record contains unresolved dispute about the secondary purpose or about the market for the original works, fair use may be a fact issue better suited for summary judgment or trial.

6) Contract enforceability through platform terms of service

Santos sought to enforce Cameo’s Terms of Service either directly (as if he were an intended third-party beneficiary of those terms) or indirectly through an implied contract theory. The Second Circuit rejected both approaches. The TOS were governed by Illinois law and presented a presumption against conferring benefits to non-contracting third parties. Under Illinois law, to enforce contract terms as a third-party beneficiary, the implication that the contract applies to non-parties must be “so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” The Court found no such strong implication in Cameo’s TOS; by contrast, the TOS contained explicit third-party beneficiary clauses where the parties intended them to apply—an interpretive clue that absent express language, the agreement was not meant to be enforceable by outsiders like Santos.

The implied-in-fact contract theory fared no better under New York law. An implied contract requires an objective meeting of minds and sufficiently pleaded essential terms. Here Santos failed to plead those elements with the specificity required by New York precedent: he did not identify the terms of the alleged implied contract, show a meeting of the minds with the defendants, or allege facts supporting an understanding that the defendants would adhere to the TOS.

Practical lessons: Platform TOS do not automatically create enforceable rights for third parties. When advising creators or litigants, examine the TOS closely: do they include express third-party beneficiary clauses? Are they governed by a law that presumes against third-party beneficiaries? If you seek to enforce platform rules against a non-party, you must either show express contracting intent or, for implied contracts, allege objective manifestations of mutual assent and identify essential terms with specificity.

7) Fraudulent inducement and the requirement of out-of-pocket loss under New York law

Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim hinged on his allegation that Kimmel obtained videos by deceit, and that he suffered damages as a result. New York law requires a plaintiff asserting common-law fraud to plead actual out-of-pocket loss (or, in some circumstances, benefit-of-the-bargain damages in the case of securities fraud), and to plead reliance and causation with particularity. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal because Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as a consequence of relying on the alleged misrepresentations.

This point underscores that even where deceit is alleged, plaintiffs must satisfy the jurisdiction-specific elements of fraud. In New York, nominal injury or reputational harm unaccompanied by measurable economic loss will not sustain a common-law fraud claim absent special circumstances. By contrast, other jurisdictions may permit different measures of damages or recognize statutory remedies that loosen these requirements; thus choice of law matters.

In applying this lesson to new cases, carefully map the plaintiff’s asserted damages to the legal requirements of the chosen forum. If a plaintiff alleges deceit in procurement (e.g., fake requestors on a platform), think through what out-of-pocket losses—money paid, time expended, lost sales—are pleaded and whether they satisfy the jurisdiction’s rules for fraud damages.

## Putting Santos in broader doctrinal perspective

Santos v. Kimmel typifies the modern intersection of copyright, platforms, and mass media. Several broader lessons emerge:

- Courts remain protective of commentary and satire, particularly where the secondary use repurposes creative material to serve a different communicative function. The reasonable observer standard is the key tool for measuring transformation, not the subjective intent of creators or secondary users.

- Fair use is adaptable to new contexts in the creator economy. Short-form, paid-request content—like Cameo videos—does not always fit squarely into traditional licensing schemas. Courts will scrutinize whether a defendant’s use displaces a plausible licensing market; if it does not, fair use is more likely to apply.

- The procurement method (deception on a platform) may be relevant to certain state-law claims, but it does not automatically defeat fair use. Plaintiffs who wish to pursue state-law remedies must satisfy the particular pleading requirements of the relevant sovereign—third-party beneficiary thresholds for contract claims and damage measurement for fraud claims.

- Procedural posture matters. Fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage when the defense is apparent from the complaint and incorporated materials, but disputes involving factual questions about transformation or market harm should proceed to discovery.

- Platform Terms of Service are powerful instruments but not panaceas. Absent express contractual language or clear manifestations of intent to benefit non-parties, courts will decline to read TOS as creating enforceable rights in strangers.

For students, Santos offers a compact pedagogical case: it ties classical copyright doctrinal principles to the realities of platform-driven content creation and late-night satire. The opinion demonstrates how courts synthesize long-standing precedent (Campbell, Harper & Row, and decisions clarifying bad-faith relevance) with contemporary source materials (user-generated content, TOS, and televised segments) to arrive at predictable, but context-sensitive, outcomes.

## Applying the lessons: sample analytic pathway for a new fact pattern

When presented with a new dispute in which user-created content posted on a platform is later aggregated, edited, and broadcast with commentary, follow this analytic pathway:

1. Identify the works at issue and take judicial notice of any registered facts (date of registration, publication status). These influence the second fair use factor.

2. Pinpoint the secondary use’s purpose and ask the reasonable-observer question: does the secondary work recast the original with a different communicative purpose (criticism, commentary, parody)? If yes, the first factor likely favors fair use.

3. Assess the nature of the original work: is it highly creative and unpublished, or more utilitarian/published? More protection attends to unpublished and highly creative works.

4. Evaluate the amount and substantiality taken: both quantitatively and qualitatively—did the defendant copy the “heart” of the work? If the secondary use needed the entire short work to achieve a critical purpose, that can be a mitigating explanation.

5. Analyze market harm: define the relevant market(s), especially potential licensing markets. If the secondary use is unlikely to function as a substitute, the fourth factor favors fair use.

6. Consider procurement methods: if deception occurred, decide whether that fact requires additional claims (fraud, statutory causes of action) and whether it materially affects fair use in the context presented.

7. If contract claims rest on platform TOS, determine whether the plaintiff is a contracting party or a clearly intended third-party beneficiary; otherwise, expect courts to be skeptical.

8. Finally, decide whether the facts alleged and documents produced to date permit resolution on pleading-stage motions or whether factual development is required.

By following this pathway, a student or practitioner will be able to translate Santos’s lessons to novel settings: from podcasts that clip and comment on user videos, to social-media creators who repurpose follower submissions for critical sketches, and to producers who rely on platform-mediated procurement to build televised segments.

## Conclusion

Santos v. Kimmel is a useful modern touchstone. It confirms the centrality of transformative purpose and market-substitution analysis in the fair use calculus, demonstrates when courts may find fair use at the pleading stage, and clarifies why platform TOS and allegations of deceptive procurement do not by themselves guarantee contractual or tort remedies. The decision thus helps to define the line between protectable creative control and socially valuable commentary—an equilibrium that courts will continue to fashion as the creator economy and mass media intersect.

# Doctrinal Foundations of Fair Use Under 17 U.S.C. § 107

The fair use doctrine under 17 U.S.C. § 107 occupies a central position in American copyright law. It functions as a built-in limitation on the exclusive rights granted by the Copyright Act, permitting uses of copyrighted works that would otherwise be infringing when those uses serve purposes the Act was not meant to suppress — criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research. This chapter explains the statutory framework and the four § 107 factors, traces the modern development of the critical “transformativeness” inquiry (including the role of the “reasonable observer” standard), addresses the place of commerciality and of alleged bad faith, and shows how courts apply these principles in practice. The recent Second Circuit decision in Santos v. Kimmel (2025) is used throughout to illustrate how the doctrine operates at the pleading stage and how courts balance the statutory factors in contexts where a secondary user repurposes material for commentary and ridicule.

## Statutory text and purposes of § 107: preservation of creativity and protection of commentary, criticism, and news reporting

Section 107 lays out a nonexclusive list of fair use purposes — criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research — and directs courts to weigh four enumerated factors in determining whether a particular use is fair. That statutory text reflects Congress’s judgment that copyright should promote creative progress while simultaneously protecting the public’s interest in discourse about works and in further creative activity. The statute therefore rejects an absolutist approach to exclusive rights: not every copying is actionable; some uses are socially valuable precisely because they reuse, comment upon, or build on earlier expression.

Courts have interpreted § 107 against this background. The canonical statement in Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), emphasized that fair use is an “equitable rule of reason” aimed at balancing the interests of copyright owners against the public interest in the free exchange of ideas. The Second Circuit’s phrasing in cases such as Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive stresses that fair use “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Santos v. Kimmel applied the same principle: the district court and the court of appeals viewed the defendants’ use through the lens of § 107’s underlying purposes, concluding that transforming the videos into critical commentary was precisely the type of socially valuable reuse the doctrine protects.

Understanding § 107 therefore begins with its dual purposes. First, the statute preserves a fertile public domain for further expression: later creators may appropriate earlier works for new expressive or informational uses. Second, it protects socially valuable uses — commentary, criticism, news reporting — that serve public discourse even though they rely on preexisting works. Those purposes inform every aspect of the factor-based analysis that follows.

## The four nonexclusive factors and flexible, context-specific application

Section 107 prescribes four nonexclusive factors:

1. The purpose and character of the use, including whether it is of a commercial nature or for nonprofit educational purposes;
2. The nature of the copyrighted work;
3. The amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole; and
4. The effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

These factors are meant to be flexible and context-sensitive; no single factor is dispositive and the relative weight of each depends on the facts. Courts do not mechanically add up scores; rather they ask whether, taken together, the factors support a finding that the secondary use is consistent with the objectives of the Copyright Act.

Factor one — purpose and character of the use — has become the focal point of fair use analysis because of its emphasis on transformation: whether the secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or message, or instead merely supersedes the original. If a use is “transformative,” it is more likely to be fair, even if it is commercial or involves copying large portions of the original. Santos v. Kimmel illustrates the point: the court accepted the district court’s determination that the late-night segments were an attempt to comment on and mock Santos’s propensity to say outrageous things for money. That different communicative purpose — turning earnest, positive-styled videos into a vehicle for critique and ridicule — weighed strongly in favor of fair use under the first factor.

Factor two, the nature of the copyrighted work, asks whether the original is creative or factual and whether it is published or unpublished. Creative, unpublished works receive stronger protection; factual or published materials are more amenable to fair use. But the weight of this factor is often modest relative to the first and fourth factors, and in many modern cases it does not determine the outcome alone.

Factor three, amount and substantiality, considers both quantitative and qualitative aspects of the copying. Courts ask how much was taken and whether the “heart” of the work was copied. But substantial copying may be justified if it is necessary to accomplish a transformative purpose. In Santos the defendants used short, self‑contained video clips; the court found the copying appropriate in light of the use’s commentary function.

Factor four, market effect, focuses on whether the secondary use usurps the market for the original or its derivatives — the so‑called “usurpation” test adopted in Campbell and reiterated in later cases. Courts distinguish between actual market harm and speculative devaluation of the original; they ask whether the secondary work serves as a realistic substitute for the originals in the relevant markets that the copyright owner would otherwise exploit. In Santos the court accepted the complaint’s own allegations (and the context of late-night TV) as showing no plausible market usurpation: viewers were unlikely to substitute a satirical TV segment for authenticated personalized videos the plaintiff sold via an online platform.

Because the factors are nonexclusive, courts may take into account context-specific considerations; for example, whether the secondary use is part of an academic project, a news report, or a satirical routine. The flexible approach prevents doctrinal rigidity and allows fair use to adapt to changing technologies and cultural practices.

## Burden of proof and the nature of the affirmative defense

Fair use is an affirmative defense; the defendant bears the burden of proving it. Practically, however, that burden interacts with pleading-stage doctrine in important ways. A defendant may invoke fair use in a pre-answer motion to dismiss, and courts may resolve fair use questions at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage when the complaint and incorporated materials plainly show that the challenged use is fair as a matter of law. The Second Circuit has repeatedly recognized that fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” (see TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168 (2d Cir. 2016), quoted in Santos).

Santos v. Kimmel is a paradigmatic dismissal-at-the-pleadings case. The court considered the complaint, the registered copyrights (which courts may judicially notice), and the segments aired on television and concluded that the defendants’ use fell within the ambit of fair use even accepting Santos’s factual allegations. Two implications flow from this posture. First, plaintiffs cannot rely on bare allegations of exclusive rights when the publicly observable use obviously presents a transformative, non‑usurping reuse. Second, defendants who reasonably assert a fair use defense should consider early dispositive motions when documentary evidence and the face of the complaint tend to resolve the statutory factors in their favor.

Nonetheless, courts are not free to treat fair use as uniformly susceptible to resolution on paper. If disputed factual issues—such as the extent of market harm, subjective intent in some contexts, or the presence of a licensing market—are material, then summary judgment or trial may be necessary. The key is whether the record attached to the complaint (and appropriate judicially noticed materials) allows the court to weigh the § 107 factors as a matter of law.

## Transformativeness after Warhol v. Goldsmith: the reasonable observer, use‑context, and limits of new meaning alone

Transformativeness has been the most litigated (and litigiously productive) element of factor one. The Supreme Court’s decision in Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), refined the inquiry in important respects. Goldsmith emphasized that the proper focus is how a “reasonable observer” would perceive the derivative work relative to the original, rejecting an analysis that depended on a user’s subjective motives or on an internal judicial view divorced from how the public would interpret the works. In other words, transformativeness is an objective question of whether the secondary use communicates something new or different from the original in the perception of a reasonable observer.

Santos adopts and applies the reasonable-observer framework. The court explained that the question is not what the plaintiff or defendant subjectively intended but what a reasonable observer would think the videos communicated. Santos’s complaint alleged that the videos conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” A reasonable observer, however, would perceive Kimmel’s broadcast use as converting those earnest messages into the subject of ridicule — a sharply different communicative function. That change in expressive purpose supported a finding of transformativeness.

Goldsmith also warned against equating novelty of meaning with automatic fairness. Adding a new message is important, but not dispositive. The secondary work must add “new expression, meaning, or message” in a way that does not simply supplant the original’s market. Goldsmith is most instructive where a secondary work preserves the original’s central expressive content and merely redeploys it; in such cases Goldsmith tends to treat transformativeness with caution. The line is context-dependent and fact-sensitive: visually based appropriations, for example, may be judged more narrowly than textual snippets used for commentary.

Practical application requires the analyst to ask both qualitative and audience-centered questions: What new communicative purpose does the secondary work serve? Does the audience encounter the secondary work as communicating that different purpose? Does the secondary use rely on the original to express the new message in a way that could not have been achieved without copying? Santos illustrates these questions: the defendants used the original videos as raw material to make a point about Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things; the audience would perceive that repurposing as commentary rather than as the original message.

## Parody versus satire and commentary: Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose and beyond

An enduring doctrinal axis in fair use is the distinction between parody and satire. In Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), the Supreme Court explained that a parody — a work that imitates an original to make fun of or comment on that original — is a paradigmatic fair use because it needs to conjure elements of the original in order to comment on it. Satire, by contrast, uses the original to comment on something else; because it does not necessarily target the original, it may weigh less strongly in favor of fair use under factor one.

This distinction matters because parody’s need to “conjure” the original may justify copying more of the original work than would otherwise be tolerable. The Supreme Court in Campbell found that a commercial parody could still be fair where it transformed the original’s message into something new and critical. The distinction is nuanced in practice: courts ask whether the secondary work’s objective is to target the original (parody) or to use the original as a vehicle to critique some other subject (satire).

Santos sits comfortably within this framework. Kimmel’s segments targeted Santos himself — his conduct and statements — rather than using the videos to criticize some unrelated subject. The segments’ purpose was to expose and mock Santos’s willingness to say outlandish things for money; they depended on showing the original videos as the target of the criticism. That relationship to the original is more closely akin to parody or direct commentary, so under Campbell it is strongly compatible with a finding of transformative fair use.

Beyond Campbell, courts have recognized that the parody-satire distinction is not a rigid categorical rule. Rather it is an analytic tool that helps assess the necessity of copying and the degree to which the secondary use adds new expressive content. Courts will probe the secondary work’s communicative target and whether the copy was necessary to accomplish the critical purpose.

## Commerciality within factor one and its interplay with transformativeness

The commercial nature of a use is explicitly part of factor one, and the Supreme Court has admonished that commercialization weighs against fair use. But in practice commerciality is only one element of the first-factor inquiry and can be outweighed by significant transformativeness. Campbell itself held that a “commercial” parody could still be fair because the transformative character of the use was strong.

Santos demonstrates this interplay. Jimmy Kimmel Live! is a commercial television program; airing the videos plainly had a commercial aspect. Yet the Second Circuit treated the commerciality point as subordinate to the more important question of whether the use was transformative. Because the late-night segments added a new critical message — lampooning Santos’s statements and willingness to be paid for them — the court found that the purpose-and-character factor favored fair use despite the commercial setting.

Students should therefore think of commerciality as a factor that matters but rarely dispositive on its own. A highly transformative use may neutralize or overcome the weight of commerciality. Conversely, purely commercial copying that is not transformative will be less likely to qualify as fair use. When analyzing a new fact pattern, evaluate whether the commercial context is incidental to an expressive purpose or whether it is the primary aim; if the latter and there is no substantial transformation, factor one will likely weigh against fair use.

## Good faith and bad faith: Harper & Row’s dictum and subsequent skepticism

Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539 (1985), included language suggesting that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” and for a time lower courts treated subjective bad faith as a potentially relevant consideration. But the Supreme Court’s later opinions, most notably Campbell and Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., 593 U.S. 1 (2021), signaled skepticism about imbuing bad faith with significant doctrinal importance. The concern is that linking fair use to the actor’s moral blameworthiness risks conflating copyright policy with tort-style moral judgments and undermines the objective balancing § 107 requires.

Santos offers a modern application of that skepticism. Santos argued that Kimmel’s alleged deception (using fake names to obtain personalized videos) showed bad faith that should preclude a fair use defense. The court declined to treat bad faith as dispositive. It noted that the complaint itself contradicted any claim that the defendants intended to usurp Santos’s market in the videos, and emphasized that the complaint alleged an expressive purpose — commentary and ridicule — that is squarely within the scope of fair use. The Second Circuit relied on Campbell and Google for the proposition that good or bad faith is not controlling; fair use focuses on objective questions of purpose, transformation, and market impact rather than on a subjective morality test.

That being said, bad faith can sometimes be relevant to subsidiary factual questions — for example, when it bears on whether the secondary user intended to supplant the original or whether the defendant sought to hide the use to evade licensing markets. But courts will not treat bad faith as a per se override of the statutory balancing. Students should therefore treat allegations of deception or bad motives as potentially colorable facts that inform but do not determine the fair use calculus.

## Practical application: a checklist for analyzing fair use in new fact patterns

When confronted with a new fact pattern, a systematic approach will produce a reliable analysis. The following checklist reflects the doctrinal priorities courts apply:

1. Identify the asserted purposes of the secondary use. Ask whether the secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or message and whether the audience would perceive a different communicative purpose (the “reasonable observer” test emphasized in Goldsmith and applied in Santos).
2. Evaluate commerciality as a component of factor one. Is the commercial nature incidental to a transformative purpose, or is the use primarily commercial without added expression?
3. Examine the nature of the original work (factor two): is it creative or factual, published or unpublished? Give appropriate but generally modest weight to this factor.
4. Consider amount and substantiality (factor three): how much was copied and was the copying necessary for the transformative purpose? Was the “heart” of the work taken and if so, was that taking reasonably necessary?
5. Analyze market effect (factor four) with special attention to the usurpation test: does the secondary use act as a realistic substitute for the original or its derivative markets? Distinguish mere critical devaluation from actual substitution.
6. Consider whether parody or direct commentary is at issue: if the secondary work targets the original, that supports the necessity of copying and favors fair use; if the work is satire, the justification may be weaker.
7. Consider any allegations of bad faith as contextual evidence; do not allow those allegations to override the objective statutory balancing but do account for them where they bear on market impact or intent to usurp the market.
8. If a motion to dismiss is contemplated, assemble documentary evidence (the works, registrations, public broadcasts) that may permit the court to decide fair use as a matter of law; if factual disputes about market harm or intent exist, anticipate the need for discovery.

Santos v. Kimmel exemplifies this approach: the court examined the works, read the complaint in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, and concluded, on the face of the materials, that the defendants’ use was commentary-directed and non‑usurping. The defendants met their burden at the pleadings stage because the necessary factual predicates for fair use were either undisputed or apparent from the materials before the court.

## Conclusion — the balancing act at the heart of § 107

Fair use is an exercise in balance. Section 107 gives courts a statutory framework, but the application is inevitably contextual and fact-driven. The modern focus on transformativeness — refined by the Supreme Court’s reasonable-observer test in Goldsmith and applied in the Second Circuit’s Santos decision — requires a careful, audience-centered inquiry into whether a secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or message. Commerciality, the nature of the original work, the amount taken, and market effect are all important, but none operates in isolation.

For students and practitioners, the key lessons are these: (1) begin with the purpose and character of the use; (2) ask how a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary work relative to the original; (3) assess whether the secondary use realistically substitutes for the original in relevant markets; and (4) remember that bad faith allegations are contextually relevant but not dispositive. Santos v. Kimmel confirms that when a secondary use recasts expressive material into an overtly critical or satirical context and does not supplant the original market, fair use can be resolved in favor of the defendant even at the pleading stage. The doctrine remains flexible precisely to accommodate those kinds of socially valuable reuses that the Copyright Act was not designed to suppress.

# Factor One in Depth: Purpose, Character, and Transformativeness

The first factor of the statutory fair use inquiry—“the purpose and character of the use”—is often the most salient and most litigated of the four factors. The Second Circuit in Santos v. Kimmel illustrates why: the court resolved the fair use question at the pleading stage by concluding that a reasonable observer would view the late‑night program’s use of short, personalized videos not as a mere re‑publication of those videos for their original purpose, but as a new work whose aim was commentary and ridicule. This chapter digs into what courts mean by “purpose and character,” how they operationalize “transformativeness,” and how that inquiry is applied to difficult factual situations such as comedy programs, uses procured by deception, and works created at the instigation of the secondary user.

At bottom, the first factor asks whether the secondary use adds something new—“with a further purpose or different character” such that the new use “transforms” the original, or instead merely “supersedes the objects of the original creation.” Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 579 (1994) (emphasis added). A use that truly transforms the original is more likely to be fair, even when it is commercial; conversely, a purely substitutive use that does what the original does will not receive fair use protection regardless of any critical gloss attached to it. The Santos opinion tracks this framework: the District Court and the Second Circuit focused on whether the JKL segments did something qualitatively different—commentary on Santos’s willingness to utter outrageous lines for money—rather than simply re‑publishing the motivational, celebratory tone of the videos Santos recorded.

## Objective versus subjective intent: the reasonable observer standard and disregard of user’s or author’s subjective motives

A recurring lesson in recent Supreme Court and circuit authority—and the lesson the Second Circuit applied in Santos—is that courts evaluate purpose and character through an objective lens. That lens asks what a “reasonable observer” would perceive from the secondary use and its context, not what the copyright owner or the secondary user subjectively intended. The Supreme Court put this point plainly in Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508 (2023): the “subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.” Santos relies on that very premise in rejecting George Santos’s argument that because Kimmel originally instigated the creation of the videos, the use was not transformative.

The reasonable‑observer test requires courts to examine how the later work presents the earlier work, the framing surrounding the display or use, and the new meaning, message, or purpose that the secondary work communicates to the ordinary viewer. Relevant indicia include textual or verbal framing (e.g., host commentary on television), juxtaposition with other material, how the excerpt is edited or excerpted, the medium into which the excerpt is incorporated, and the audience response the secondary work is designed to elicit. Santos makes this operational: the court looked at how Jimmy Kimmel packaged the videos—on a segment explicitly titled “Will Santos Say It?”—and concluded that a reasonable viewer would take the clips as being used to lampoon Santos, not to celebrate or convey the messages Santos intended.

That objectivity has two practical consequences: (1) a plaintiff cannot defeat a fair use defense simply by pleading that she intended the original work for one purpose while the defendant intended it for another; and (2) courts may resolve the issue on the pleadings where the complaint and incorporated materials show, as a matter of law, that the secondary use communicates a different purpose to reasonable viewers. See TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168 (2d Cir. 2016) (affirming resolution at dismissal stage), and the Second Circuit’s treatment of Santos.

## Identifying a distinct purpose: from celebration to critique, from salesmanship to exposure

Determining whether a secondary use has a “distinct purpose” requires a textured, fact‑sensitive inquiry. The paradigmatic transformative uses are expressive: parody, criticism, scholarly analysis, news reporting, and commentary. Those uses take the original for a new communicative end. Conversely, uses that merely repeat the expressive content for the same communicative end—republication, re‑performance, or reuse to sell substantially the same experience—are not transformative.

Consider the following contrasts, which courts frequently rely on to distinguish transformative from nontransformative purposes:

- Celebration versus critique. If the primary work expresses, say, “messages of hope and perseverance,” and the secondary work repurposes the same passages to celebrate those same messages or to elicit the same emotional response, the secondary work likely is not transformative. If, instead, the secondary work quotes or displays those passages to undercut them, to expose hypocrisy, or to illustrate an argument critical of the author, the secondary work more plausibly has a different purpose. In Santos, the complaint itself described the original videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the JKL segment plainly aimed to show Santos’s willingness to say absurd or false statements for money—a different communicative goal.

- Salesmanship versus exposure. A company that posts snippets of a speaker’s paid motivational talk on its website to entice customers to buy access to the full talk is using the excerpts for the same commercial purpose—selling the same expressive product. A news program that airs short excerpts to report on the speaker’s public conduct or to expose inconsistencies in the speaker’s public persona is using them for a distinct, informational purpose.

- Private message versus public commentary. Personalized videos engineered to reach a particular individual—birthday greetings, custom salutations—are originally meant as direct communications. Republishing such messages on a television program with critical commentary recasts them as public exemplars and commentary material; that reframing will often be treated as transformative.

When courts evaluate whether the purpose is distinct, they do not confine themselves to labels (e.g., “parody” or “news”) but look to how the work functions. That functional analysis helps explain why courts find parody (a lampoon that relies on the original for its humor) and criticism (using excerpts to make an argument about the author or subject) similarly protective as transformative uses: both repurpose the original for a different expressive end.

## Transformative commentary on public figures and conduct versus on the work itself

An important distinction arises between (a) secondary uses that comment on the underlying work itself (for instance, an academic critique of a novel), and (b) secondary uses that comment on the subject or the creator—especially when the subject is a public figure. Courts have sometimes been more willing to find transformative purpose when the secondary work targets the public figure’s conduct or persona rather than the copyrighted work as such.

This distinction matters for at least two reasons. First, commentary on public figures often serves the robust, socially valuable role of public discourse—criticism, satire, and exposure—activities the fair use doctrine is designed to accommodate. The Supreme Court in Campbell recognized this policy background in discussing parody and criticism. Second, when the secondary use critiques the person rather than the original work, it is less likely that the secondary use will function as a market substitute for the original work; creators of imaginative works generally will not be in the market to license criticism of themselves, which reduces concerns about market usurpation (a concern treated more fully under factor four).

Santos illustrates how these considerations operate. The JKL segments did not seek to comment on the rhetoric, editing, or artistry of the Santos videos; instead, they used those videos as raw material to comment on Santos himself—his willingness to utter outrageous things for money and the incongruity between the motivational tone of the messages and the celebrity’s conduct. The Second Circuit viewed that as a paradigmatic case of transformative commentary about a public figure, and it treated the commentary’s target (Santos) as central to the analysis under factor one.

But this permissive posture toward commentary on public figures is not limitless. If the secondary work simply republishes a public figure’s speeches or promotional content without meaningful recontextualization, the use will be less likely to qualify as transformative. The key is whether the secondary use “adds” new expression, meaning, or message—that is, whether it allows a viewer to understand the material differently than the original did.

## Commercial late‑night comedy as news/commentary: weighing criticism and humor against revenue motives

A persistent tension in fair use doctrine is the role of commercialization. The Copyright Act does not exclude commercial uses from the fair use defense; Campbell made clear that commercial character is a factor to be weighed, not a dispositive bar. Comedy shows, however, are squarely commercial enterprises: they generate advertising revenue, ratings, and other monetary benefits. How should courts treat the first factor when a commercial comedy program uses copyrighted material to mock or criticize?

The analytical answer accepted in Santos and in prior precedent is that commerciality does not automatically negate a finding of transformativeness. Courts ask whether the commercial secondary use also changes the original with new expression, meaning, or message. A late‑night comedy segment that uses short clips to lampoon a public figure’s statements or behavior may be transformative even though it is shown on a commercial broadcast: the humor and criticism can be the “new” purpose that transforms the original. In Santos, the Second Circuit held that the JKL segments’ purpose—satirical commentary about Santos—was distinct from Santos’s original purpose in producing motivational videos, and thus the commercial nature of Kimmel’s show did not foreclose fair use.

That said, commerciality is still relevant. If a host uses the material in a way that merely repackages the original for commercial gain—say, airing whole videos as filler entertainment without editorial framing—or uses the work as the main attraction in a format that replaces the market for the original (for example, broadcasting the entirety of paid, exclusive performances), commerciality will weigh against fair use. The balancing exercise is contextual: courts look to the scale of the use (how much of the work), the nature of the use (is it presented with new critical framing), and the extent to which commercialization translates into market substitution.

Practical guidance for litigants and judges: examine how the program frames the clips (host introductions, captions, segment titles), the editing choices (do the clips appear embedded within commentary or as standalone), and whether the program’s revenue model depends on providing the same expressive experience the original creator offers (unlikely where the program’s raison d’être is commentary or humor about public figures).

## Bad faith and deception in obtaining works (e.g., pseudonyms, sting operations) and their limited role in factor one

Plaintiffs sometimes argue that bad faith or deceit in procuring copyrighted material should defeat fair use. Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539 (1985), issued language suggesting fair use presupposes “good faith and fair dealing,” and courts historically have considered dishonest procurement as a factor that can weigh against fair use. But later rulings—most notably Campbell, and more recently Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., 593 U.S. 1 (2021), and the Second Circuit in Santos—have cast doubt on the centrality of subjective good faith to the factor one analysis. The modern trend limits the weight of bad faith, treating it as neither necessary nor often decisive.

Two analytical threads underlie this development. First, the core fair use inquiry is about communicative purpose and societal value—whether the secondary use repurposes the original into new expression—rather than the moral character of the secondary user. It is hard to square a rule that a transformative use becomes uncopyrightable simply because the user lied about his name, particularly where the public interest in commentary or news reporting is strong. Second, bad faith often overlaps with other factors: deception may bear on the fourth factor (market harm) or on equitable considerations when a court fashions an injunction, but it does not necessarily negate transformativeness.

Santos applies these lessons concretely. Kimmel allegedly used pseudonyms to obtain the personalized videos through Cameo. Santos argued this deception demonstrated bad faith and thus should nullify the fair use defense. The Second Circuit rejected that view. It noted that the complaint itself described a strong critical purpose for the segments—sarcastic commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things—and that the defendants’ deceptive procurement did not convert a nontransformative use into a transformative one or establish market usurpation. The court also relied on the Supreme Court’s and circuit precedent expressing skepticism about elevating subjective bad faith to a determinative role in the factor one analysis. The result: deception is relevant but often of limited weight; it rarely transforms an otherwise transformative use into an infringing one on its own.

There are caveats. Persistent, egregious misconduct—especially where it has independent legal consequences (fraud, trespass, theft of access)—can affect the case in other ways. For instance, if a defendant obtained material by hacking, breaking into private servers, or committing other criminal acts, courts may be less sympathetic and may consider such conduct when applying equitable remedies or when examining the totality of circumstances. But under current doctrine, the mere fact that a defendant used a pseudonym or leveraged a platform’s submission process to obtain a work is unlikely, by itself, to defeat a fair use finding when the secondary purpose and character are genuinely transformative.

## Applying the first factor: a practical checklist and doctrinal markers

To apply the first factor to new fact patterns, synthesize the doctrinal points above into a practical sequence:

1. Identify the communicative aim of the secondary use from the viewpoint of an ordinary, reasonable observer. Look at titles, host commentary, captions, juxtaposition with other materials, and the medium in which the excerpts appear.

2. Ask whether the secondary use “adds” new expression, meaning, or message. Does it recast the original so that the audience understands the material differently than they would from the original alone? If yes, that points toward transformativeness.

3. Distinguish between commentary on the work itself and commentary on the subject or author. Commentary on public figures’ conduct is often, though not always, more likely to be transformative and less likely to threaten licensing markets for the original.

4. Factor in commerciality: a commercial secondary use is not dispositive against fair use, but analyze whether the commerciality is simply the context of commentary (less harmful) or whether the use essentially performs the original work’s market role (more harmful).

5. Consider procurement conduct: deception or pseudonyms may be relevant but will seldom be outcome‑determinative under the first factor. Reserve assessment of egregious misconduct for other legal consequences or for weighing under equitable doctrines.

6. Remember the pleading threshold: if the complaint and incorporated materials clearly show that the secondary use, as packaged, communicates a different purpose to a reasonable observer, a court may dismiss a copyright claim on Rule 12(b)(6) grounds—Santos is an example.

Illustrative hypotheticals help sharpen the list. A late‑night show that broadcasts a montage of a politician’s speeches intercut with host commentary that mocks inconsistencies is more likely to be transformative than a television channel that rebroadcasts the speeches in full without commentary. A magazine that publishes excerpts from a memoir to review and criticize it likely has a different purpose than a website that posts the same excerpt to entice readers to subscribe to the memoir’s paid access. A journalist who obtains an interview recording under false pretenses and then uses it as the basis of investigative reporting will present a greater factual and ethical minefield than a comedian who uses short, preexisting personalized greetings to make fun of a public figure’s past statements; the latter, per Santos, still may be fair use even if the comedian deceived the speaker about his identity.

Conclusion: The first factor is flexible and contextual. Santos v. Kimmel demonstrates contemporary lines of decision: courts use an objective, reasonable‑observer standard to find transformativeness where the secondary use repurposes material into a different expressive domain—particularly commentary about a public figure—even when the secondary user is commercial and even when the material was obtained via deception. For law students and practitioners, the analytic task is twofold: identify how the secondary use will likely be perceived by the ordinary viewer, and then map that perception to whether it meaningfully repurposes the original into new expression, meaning, or message. If that mapping suggests a new expressive purpose, the first factor will typically weigh in favor of fair use; if not, the plaintiff's copyright claim has survived the first hurdle and the inquiry must proceed to the remaining factors.

# Factors Two and Three: Nature of the Work and Amount/Substantiality

In the fair use inquiry the second and third statutory factors — (2) the nature of the copyrighted work and (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used — are often treated as subsidiary to the first and fourth factors (purpose/character and market effect). Nevertheless, they are important lenses for understanding why some uses that reproduce expressive works will nonetheless qualify as fair. This chapter explains what those factors mean, how courts apply them (with attention to Second Circuit law), and how they operate in the contemporary world of short, personalized videos such as those at issue in Santos v. Kimmel. The goal is to equip a student to analyze new fact patterns where defendants reproduce creative works in service of commentary, criticism, parody, or reporting.

## Creative versus factual works and why factor two often carries limited weight

Factor two asks whether the original work is closer to the poles of expression or fact. The statutory text directs a court to consider “the nature of the copyrighted work” (17 U.S.C. § 107), and cases have long treated imaginative, highly expressive works (novels, songs, films, personal videos) as more deserving of protection under this factor than factual, informational works (news reports, scientific articles). The classic example is Harper & Row, where unpublished portions of a presidential memoir — a highly creative, expressive work — weighed against fair use. By contrast, the Second Circuit and Supreme Court have also recognized that the “creativity” of a work does not by itself doom fair use if the copying is sufficiently transformative or otherwise justified. In Campbell, the Supreme Court observed that factual works are “closer to the core of intended copyright protection” but did not make the factor dispositive.

There are two practical consequences of this doctrine. First, when a defendant’s use is strongly transformative — when the secondary work adds new expression, meaning, or message — factor two tends to carry limited weight. The Second Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that transformative purpose is the focal point of the fair use inquiry, and that the nature-of-work factor rarely outweighs a finding of transformation. Recent decisions (including Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive) stress that transformation and market effect are usually the decisive considerations. In Santos, the Second Circuit described the videos at issue as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity” — clearly expressive — but held that the defendants’ use was transformative because they recontextualized the videos to comment and mock the speaker’s willingness to say absurd things. That recontextualization undercut Santos’s argument that factor two should block fair use.

Second, unpublished status amplifies factor two but does not control. The Supreme Court has treated the use of unpublished works as a special concern because authors have a stronger right to control first publication. Harper & Row is the paradigmatic case: unauthorized quotation of unpublished memoirs weighed heavily against fair use. But later opinions (and the Court’s more recent skepticism about bad-faith considerations) caution that unpublished status is only one ingredient, not a trump card. The courts will weigh unpublished status against whether the use is transformative and whether it usurps any realistic licensing market.

For law students, the practical rule is: identify whether the original work is expressive or factual and whether it is published. Treat that assessment as a contextual input to the larger inquiry, not as dispositive. If the defendant has added new expression, meaning, or purpose, factor two will usually be a weak argument for the plaintiff.

## Publication status and its significance; statutory definition of publication

Publication plays a distinct role in the Second Circuit’s fair use jurisprudence. The Copyright Act defines “publication” in § 101: publication is the distribution of “copies or phonorecords of a work to the public by sale or other transfer of ownership, or by rental, lease, or lending,” or an “offering to distribute copies or phonorecords to a group of persons for purposes of further distribution, public performance, or public display.” Crucially, a public performance or display that does not distribute copies does not itself constitute “publication” under the statute.

Why does publication status matter? Two reasons. First, unpublished works traditionally enjoy greater protection under factor two because the right of first publication is treated as a core interest of authorship; courts ask whether the secondary use infringes the author’s control over the initial dissemination of expressive content. Second, publication affects the market-harm inquiry: if a work is unpublished, there is often no established market for licensing excerpts or derivative uses; yet courts still consider whether the secondary use usurps an actual or potential market.

Santos illustrates the interplay. The Second Circuit took judicial notice of Santos’s copyright registrations, which showed the Cameo videos were published the same day they were created. That fact undercut any argument that defendants had appropriated unpublished material whose publication the author wished to control: the videos were already in the public (or at least distributable) sphere contemporaneous with their creation. The Court therefore treated publication as a reason why factor two should not weigh strongly against fair use in this case. But the Court also made clear that publication does not automatically mandate fair use; rather, publication status is one factor among many.

When analyzing a new fact pattern, ask: was the original work unpublished at the time of the secondary use? If so, be attentive to the plaintiff’s right of first publication. But also ask whether the defendant’s use is transformative or substitutes for the original in a way that would cause market harm. Those latter considerations often eclipse publication status.

## Short, personalized videos: expressive content, personalization, and implications for factor two

Short-form personalized videos present a distinctive context for factor two. They are typically expressive: authors often intend to convey personality, encouragement, or an individualized message for a particular recipient. At the same time, their brevity and platform-driven distribution (for example, via a marketplace like Cameo) influence the expectations of authorial control and the viability of licensing markets.

From the author’s perspective, a Cameo video is both creative (it is a crafted performance) and transactional (it is produced pursuant to a paid request). Courts must decide whether the personalized nature of such content should increase the weight of factor two. The answer developed in modern case law is nuanced. Personalization underscores expressivity, which a plaintiff will press to make factor two unfavorable. But personalization does not substantially differentiate short videos from other expressive works in the fair use calculus: a short film, a song clip, and a short cameo video are all creative products. The critical question remains whether the defendant’s use introduces new meaning or message.

In Santos, the videos were personalized messages that a reasonable observer would understand as conveying positivity. Yet the defendants’ use of those clips as part of a mocking commentary series “Will Santos Say It?” reoriented the videos’ meaning. That reorientation was dispositive. For short videos, the Court’s approach confirms that personal expression does not immunize a work from fair use when the secondary use transforms the message and does not substitute for the original work in a licensing market.

Two practical points for short-form creators and critics. Creators who wish to maintain exclusive control should consider contractual limitations on downstream use and clear license terms; absent such protection, courts will treat short personalized videos like other expressive works. Critics or commentators who use short videos should be mindful that the short length makes the admission of whole works more likely to be necessary and hence more easily defensible (as discussed next), but they should still aim to add distinct context or commentary to show transformation.

## Using the heart of the work or the whole work: necessity for commentary, criticism, or reporting

Factor three requires courts to consider both the quantitative portion taken (how much was copied) and the qualitative importance of what was copied (whether the copied material is the “heart” of the work). The Supreme Court in Campbell and Harper & Row emphasized that taking the “heart” of a work — even if a small portion — can weigh heavily against fair use because it captures what is most valuable about the original. Yet the same doctrines also recognize that some critical uses require quotation of the most salient parts to make the commentary intelligible.

Two guiding principles emerge:

- Necessity relative to the purpose: A copying that reproduces the portion necessary to the secondary purpose is more likely to be fair. Parodies are the classic example: a parody must conjure up the original to succeed as commentary and therefore may lawfully take enough of the original — possibly including its “heart” — as required to “conjure” that original. The Court in Campbell recognized that parodic appropriation of the heart may be acceptable when the purpose is comment.

- Short works and proportion: When the original is itself very short, using the entire work is more likely to be proportionate. A 30-second video’s entire content may be necessary to the secondary work’s point; copying that whole work may not supplant the market because there is little or no market for licensing the same exact clip for the same use (commentary/parody). In Santos, the court accepted that the defendants’ airing of entire brief Cameo videos could be justified by the defendants’ need to show what Santos actually said and how absurd it sounded in context. The Court emphasized that courts must ask whether the secondary use competes with the original work as a market substitute, not simply whether it reproduces the whole.

Applying factor three therefore requires granular inquiry: could the defendant have achieved its purpose with less? If the answer is no — if brevity makes the entire clip necessary to illustrate the commentary — courts are more willing to find the amount used reasonable. Conversely, copying extensive portions of a long work where only minimal quotation is needed will cut against fair use.

A caution for litigants: plaintiffs should allege with specificity both the qualitative importance of the copied material and why its full reproduction is not necessary for the defendant’s purported purpose. Defendants should plead or prove why the particular amount copied was necessary to achieve the secondary, transformative purpose.

## Editing, montages, and contextual framing to establish new meaning or purpose

Transformation is not only a matter of adding text or voiceover; it can be accomplished by editing, montage, juxtaposition, and contextual framing. Courts look at whether the secondary work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” — new meaning, different message, or critical commentary — and editing techniques play a central role in creating that new meaning.

Typologies of transformative editing include:

- Juxtaposition: Placing a clip next to contradictory footage or captions to create irony or commentary. For example, airing a cheerful personalized message next to a title card or voiceover that calls attention to the speaker’s hypocrisy transforms the viewer’s perception of the original.

- Montage and sampling: Combining multiple works or excerpts into a composite to make a larger point about a subject often renders the pieces transformative because they are consumed as components of a new whole.

- Captioning and recontextualizing: Adding explanatory captions, graphic overlays, or surrounding commentary that re-frames the original work’s intent.

Cariou v. Prince and Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith illustrate the point across visual arts contexts: altering photographs or painting over them may create a new meaning, and the court’s focus is the net change in expressive purpose. The Second Circuit’s reasoning in Santos applied that same logic to short video clips: Kimmel’s segments did not merely rerun the Cameo videos; they reframed them as examples of Santos’s alleged willingness to say anything for money, which is a different communicative purpose.

Practically, when editing or montage is used to make a new point, courts will ask whether the additional elements are substantial enough to create a new expression, meaning, or message. Superficial changes (minor cropping, captioning that only repeats the original message) are less likely to be transformative. Robust framing that alters the viewer’s take-away tends to support fair use.

## Applying factors two and three together — analytical checklist and litigating tips

When confronting a fact pattern, work through factors two and three as part of the full fair use mosaic. The following checklist encapsulates the principal inquiries courts undertake:

1. Nature (Factor Two)
   - Is the original work primarily factual or primarily creative/expressive?
   - Was the work unpublished at the time of the challenged use?
   - Does the plaintiff allege special value in controlling first publication?
   - How much weight should be accorded to nature in light of the defendant’s claimed transformative purpose?

2. Amount/Substantiality (Factor Three)
   - How much of the original was taken in quantitative terms?
   - Did the defendant use the “heart” or a qualitatively significant part?
   - Was the amount taken reasonably necessary for the defendant’s purpose (commentary, parody, reporting)?
   - Would a smaller excerpt have sufficed?
   - Does the short length of the original make full copying proportionate?

3. Interaction with other factors
   - Does the use transform the original by adding new meaning, message, or purpose? If so, factor two’s emphasis on protectiveness is diminished.
   - Does the amount taken create a substitute for the original in any actual or likely market? If not, copying of the heart or the whole may be justified.

Litigation advice derived from Santos and related precedents:

- For defendants relying on fair use, early emphasis on transformative purpose is crucial and may allow dismissal at the pleadings stage if the complaint’s allegations themselves show transformation and lack of market harm. Demonstrate how editing, juxtaposition, or commentary changes the original meaning.

- For plaintiffs, plead the nature of the work and the qualitative centrality of the copied portion with specificity. If the work was unpublished, make clear why first-publication interests are implicated. Allegations about market harm should address realistic licensing markets, including whether there existed (or could exist) a market for the exact use the defendant made (e.g., licensing for commentary or parody).

- For both sides in cases involving short personalized videos, remember that shortness can cut both ways: it makes copying the whole work more likely to be necessary, but shortness also means there may be little or no market for the precise use, which supports fair use.

## Conclusion: doctrinal balance in the age of short-form content

Factors two and three are conceptually distinct but practically interrelated. The nature of the work (creative v. factual, published v. unpublished) informs how strongly courts protect the original, while the amount/substantiality question tests whether the secondary user took no more than was necessary and whether the portion taken captures the work’s essence. Modern Second Circuit doctrine — as applied in Santos v. Kimmel — treats these factors as important but subsidiary to whether the secondary use is transformative and whether it usurps the copyright owner’s market. For short personalized videos, courts will pay attention to both the necessity of reproducing short works in their entirety and to the transforming effect of editing, montage, and explicit commentary. The upshot is pragmatic: transformation and market effect remain the decisive elements, and a defendant who recontextualizes short creative clips for commentary or parody will often, though not always, succeed on fair use even when reproducing substantial or qualitatively important parts of the original.

# Factor Four: Market Harm and the Usurpation Versus Criticism Distinction

The fourth statutory fair use factor—the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work (17 U.S.C. § 107(4))—is often decisive. It asks whether the secondary use usurps the market that the copyright owner would normally exploit, or in practical terms whether the secondary use acts as a competing substitute. Over the last several decades the courts have refined the inquiry away from a generalized concern about reputation or devaluation toward a focused analysis of substitution and licensing markets. Santos v. Kimmel, decided by the Second Circuit in 2025, furnishes a clear example of the modern approach: the court affirmed dismissal on the pleadings because the allegedly infringing television segments did not and could not plausibly be shown to usurp Santos’s markets for the underlying personalized videos. This chapter explains the doctrinal underpinnings of factor four, shows how Santos applies them, and draws lessons for litigants and students about what courts will and will not treat as cognizable market harm in the era of platforms, clips, remixes, and short-form content.

## The statutory text and Campbell’s usurpation test

Section 107(4) directs courts to consider “the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” The Supreme Court in Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), distilled the relevant inquiry: courts should look not generally to whether the secondary use “damages” the value of the original through criticism, parody, or devaluation, but whether it “usurps” the traditional or likely markets for licensing the original work. The critical insight from Campbell is two-fold: (1) not all adverse effects on value are cognizable under factor four—an effect that merely “devalues” a work through criticism or parody is often not the type of market injury the Copyright Act protects; and (2) the proper focus is on whether the secondary use functions as a market substitute that deprives the copyright holder of actual or likely licensing opportunities.

This substitutional or usurpation test has practical consequences. A court will scrutinize whether the defendant’s work serves the same functional purpose as the original in relevant markets—e.g., if the defendant’s work is a plausible commercial alternative to the original (for example, a competing background-music track licensed for films), factor four will weigh against fair use. By contrast, if the defendant’s work repurposes the original for a distinct communicative function—commentary, criticism, parody, news reporting—it is less likely to be viewed as a substitute for the original’s market and more likely to be protected as fair use.

Santos v. Kimmel exemplifies this approach. The Santos videos were short, personalized messages that, according to Santos’s complaint, conveyed “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Kimmel used those same clips in television segments that mocked Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for money. The Second Circuit, applying the Campbell/usurpation framework, emphasized that courts “ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Because the television segments served a radically different purpose—satire and criticism—they were not competing commercial substitutes for Santos’s personalized videos, and factor four therefore did not weigh against fair use.

## Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive: substitution over devaluation

Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive (2d Cir. 2024) is a recent Second Circuit restatement of the substitution focus. The dispute involved the Internet Archive’s library-scanning program, which allowed controlled digital lending of ebooks. The plaintiffs argued the Archive’s program harmed their market; the Archive argued for fair use. The Second Circuit emphasized that factor four is concerned with whether the secondary use “usurps” the copyright owner’s market by serving as a substitute—mere devaluation (e.g., critical commentary that might reduce demand) is not the issue. Hachette thus reinforced that the operative question is whether the defendant’s use functions as a commercially reasonable or likely alternative that the copyright holder would otherwise exploit or license.

Hachette also illustrates the interplay between the other fair use factors and the market-harm inquiry. A use that is strongly transformative in purpose and character (factor one) is less likely to be treated as a market substitute because, by definition, it performs a different communicative task. But Hachette also makes clear that evidence about the structure and operation of real markets—what purchasers actually seek and what licensing practices exist—matters. A defendant cannot avoid factor four merely by claiming noncommercial intent; a court will examine whether consumers might use the secondary work instead of acquiring the original, and whether there is a realistic market in which the secondary work displaces licensing of the original.

Santos similarly relied on Hachette’s emphasis: the television use was transformative criticism and therefore unlikely to operate as a substitute for the original personalized videos or for any reasonable licensing market for them.

## Presumptions of harm and their erosion in transformative contexts

Older decisions sometimes treated certain categories of works or uses as raising presumptions of market harm. Two classical examples are (1) the treatment of unpublished works, where the Supreme Court in Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises (1985) found a presumption of market harm from unauthorized publication of unpublished material, and (2) cases where the secondary use is a near replica of a commercially available original.

Harper & Row remains good law to the limited extent that the unauthorized publication of unpublished material can pose special concerns: a copyright owner’s right to control first publication is a significant market right. But courts have been reluctant to allow such special rules to swallow the flexibility of fair use. The Supreme Court and the federal appellate courts have increasingly scrutinized rigid presumptions—especially when the secondary work is transformative. Google v. Oracle (2021), while not a straightforward fair use holding, signaled judicial skepticism about using categorical inferences of bad faith or harm to displace the holistic fair use inquiry. The Second Circuit in Santos explicitly noted that the Supreme Court has expressed “some skepticism about whether bad faith has any role in a fair use analysis” and declined to treat Kimmel’s false identities as dispositive of market-harm concerns, particularly where the complaint itself alleged an explanatory purpose—satirical criticism—that undermined the notion of substitution.

In short, presumptions of harm have been attenuated where the secondary use is transformative: when the new work serves a different function, a court will be less willing to infer that the public would choose the new work instead of the original and thus less willing to find cognizable market harm. Santos demonstrates that tendency: not only did Kimmel’s use perform a different communicative function, but the complaint alleged no plausible licensing market that Kimmel’s broadcasts could have replaced.

## Parody, critical uses, and the unlikelihood of a viable licensing market

Campbell and succeeding cases teach a special principle about parody and critical uses: creators of expressive works ordinarily will not and do not license their works for use as the object of critique, parody, or lampoon. The Supreme Court explained in Campbell that the unlikelihood that authors of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons removes such uses “from the very notion of a potential licensing market.” Consequently, when the secondary work is a critical or parodic use of the original, factor four often weighs in favor of fair use because there is no meaningful licensing market for the use the defendant has made.

This is not an absolute rule. A defendant cannot claim free rein simply because the secondary use includes criticism. The court will ask whether the secondary work is plausibly a substitute for markets the copyright owner actually exploits or could reasonably exploit. A parody that reproduces the heart of the original and functions as a direct replacement for commercially exploited derivatives might still be regarded as substitutive. But in many cases—news reports, commentary, lampoons, reaction videos—those secondary purposes are qualitatively distinct from the markets a creator normally develops.

Santos fits the paradigm: Kimmel’s television segments were (as the complaint itself acknowledged) designed to mock Santos and thereby to comment on his behavior. The Second Circuit followed Campbell in concluding that a television segment that satirizes a person’s paid Cameo messages is not a product for which the copyright owner has a sensible licensing market. The court reiterated Campbell’s instructive sentence: licensing markets for lampoons are unlikely to exist, and an absence of such a market undercuts claims of market usurpation.

## Assessing cognizable derivative and traditional markets in the platform age

Although the core test—would the secondary work act as a substitute for the copyright holder’s market—remains constant, the kinds of markets courts must examine have proliferated in the platform age. Traditional markets once focused on print, film, broadcast, and record sales. Today, new markets and licensing practices matter: short-form clips (TikTok, Instagram Reels), highlight reels, user-generated remixes and mashups, video hosting, ephemeral messaging, and personalized video marketplaces (like Cameo) have created potential licensing opportunities that did not exist a decade ago.

When applying factor four today, courts should (and do) inquire about both (a) traditional derivative markets that the copyright owner actually exploits—e.g., sequels, movie adaptations, soundtrack licensing—and (b) reasonable, non-speculative markets that authors could be expected to develop in light of technology—e.g., licensing short-form excerpts for commercial advertising, compilations, or subscription clip services. The relevant question is not whether any market exists for any conceivable use, but whether the secondary use occupies a market that is “traditional, reasonable, or likely to be developed” by the copyright owner.

This inquiry requires attention to evidence: How does the copyright owner currently monetize the work? Are there established licensing practices for the kind of use at issue? Would consumers accept the defendant’s work as an alternative to acquiring rights in the original (for background use, for commentary, etc.)? Courts are wary of imaginative hypothetical markets conjured merely to defeat fair use. The Second Circuit in Santos relied on exactly this scrutiny: Santos alleged no plausible market that Kimmel’s broadcast could have supplanted, and the complaint’s own allegations (publication on the date of creation; the intrinsic personal nature of the videos) cut against the existence of a substantial licensing market for the particular use.

Specific platform-age examples show how the analysis plays out:

- Reaction and commentary videos. Creators of reaction videos typically use short excerpts for commentary or critique. Courts increasingly see these uses as transformative and unlikely to be substitutes for the originals, so factor four often favors fair use absent evidence of a well-developed licensing market for such clips.

- Highlight reels and sports clips. Sports leagues maintain active licensing regimes for highlights and clips; a commercial service that repackages game footage for mass consumption could usurp those markets. Factor four may therefore weigh against fair use in that context.

- Short-form remixes and musical sampling. If the secondary work functions as a new expressive work and uses limited portions, factor four might favor fair use. But if the secondary work supplants the market for licensing micro-samples—for ringtones, background loops, or other commercial uses—factor four may weigh against fair use.

- Personalized marketplaces (Cameo-style). These marketplaces produce content tailored to individual purchasers. The owner’s conventional markets may be narrow (personalized messages for a fee) and unlikely to be supplanted by broadcast or commentary uses. As Santos shows, when the original content is meant for individualized personal consumption, broadcast of those same clips for a different purpose is not a plausible substitute licensing market.

The platform age thus requires courts to identify not merely whether a secondary work is “market-harmful” in the abstract, but whether it occupies an identifiable licensing market that the copyright holder actually utilizes or reasonably could develop.

## Empirical and pleading-stage considerations

Santos also illustrates how courts handle market-harm allegations at the pleading stage. A plaintiff invoking market harm must allege facts showing plausibly that a cognizable market or class of potential markets exists—mere conclusions that the defendant’s use “damaged” the plaintiff’s market are insufficient. Under Twombly/Iqbal, plaintiffs must offer nonconclusory factual allegations about how the defendant’s use usurped or threatened actual or potential markets.

Several practical evidentiary observations follow:

- Publication timing matters. In Santos the registered videos were published the same day they were created. Publication timing can defeat arguments that the secondary use usurped a first-publication market or that the plaintiff retained exclusive first-publication exploitation rights. When a work is already widely published or publicly available, it is harder to establish that a defendant’s republishing supplanted a necessary market.

- Pleading the existence of licensing markets requires specifics. A plaintiff should plead—realistically and with particularity—the sorts of licenses sought or refused, licensing fees, identified potential licensees, or industry norms that show a market for the use at issue. Vague references to “loss of value” will not survive dismissal.

- Judicial notice and documents matter. Courts may take judicial notice of copyright registrations and published records that contradict a plaintiff’s market-harm theory. In Santos the court considered the registrations and the plaintiff’s own allegations when concluding that no plausible market-harm claim had been pleaded.

- Empirical proof at summary or trial. Where the claim survives pleading, empirical evidence may be decisive. Market analyses, consumer surveys, licensing histories, and expert testimony about consumer behavior and substitution effects can show whether a secondary use reduced demand or substituted for the original. Conversely, market data showing that the secondary use increased interest in the original—or had no impact—can favor fair use.

- Stage-sensitive inferences. Fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage when the complaint and any incorporated documents make clear that the secondary use cannot plausibly have usurped the original’s markets. The Second Circuit in cases such as TCA Television and Santos affirmed that fair use may be a basis for dismissal when the relevant facts are evident from the complaint and judicially noticeable materials.

## Applying the doctrine: lessons from Santos and hypotheticals

Santos v. Kimmel provides a compact lesson:

- Identify the purpose and character of the secondary use. If the use is critical, satirical, or otherwise transformative, it is less likely to be a market substitute.

- Define the relevant markets concretely. Ask: what markets does the plaintiff exploit now, and what reasonable markets could the plaintiff develop? Do the defendants’ uses compete in those same markets?

- Pay attention to publication and licensing facts. If the plaintiff’s work was published and made available in the same channels the defendant used, or if the plaintiff has no licensing history or viable licensing market for the defendant’s type of use, factor four will likely favor fair use.

- At the pleading stage, plead specific facts about licensing and markets. Unsupported assertions of harm are insufficient.

Practical hypotheticals clarify application:

- Parody of a pop song posted on a late-night show. If the show’s parody addresses public conduct and is not a satisfactory substitute for purchasing the original song for listening, factor four likely favors fair use (Campbell).

- Compilation service that packages short-form user clips of a musician’s songs and sells subscriptions. If the service offers a direct market alternative to purchasing licensed song snippets or streaming the artist’s works, factor four may weigh against fair use.

- News program that uses short excerpts of a politician’s personalized paid videos to criticize his public statements. As in Santos, the use is critical, the function differs from the original (public commentary versus personalized messages), and there is no plausible licensing market for criticism; factor four will likely favor fair use.

- Sports highlight app that aggregates entire game clips and sells access without obtaining league licenses. Because leagues have active markets for highlight licensing, factor four may weigh against fair use.

## Conclusion

Factor four anchors the fair use inquiry in market realities. The modern tendency in the courts—exemplified by Hachette, by the Second Circuit’s decision in Santos, and by Campbell’s original formulation—is to scrutinize substitutional effect rather than generalized reputational damage. Transformative critical or satirical uses are less likely to be substitutes for the original’s markets and thus less likely to produce cognizable market harm. In the platform era, courts must carefully identify the specific markets at stake, evaluate plausible licensing practices (not fanciful hypotheticals), and require factual allegations or proof that the secondary use actually occupies or threatens those markets. Santos demonstrates that where the secondary use performs a different function and no viable licensing market for the secondary use exists, factor four will not bar a fair use defense even at the pleading stage.

# Early Adjudication of Fair Use: Motions to Dismiss and Judicial Notice

One of the most consequential practical questions in modern copyright litigation is whether a court may resolve the affirmative defense of fair use at the earliest stages of a case — most commonly on a motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). This chapter explains the doctrinal framework governing early resolution of fair use, describes what materials a court may properly consider at the pleading stage, surveys Second Circuit guidance and its articulation in Santos v. Kimmel, and weighs the competing policy considerations that counsel for and against early disposition. Finally, the chapter offers practical drafting and litigation strategies for lawyers who must plead or defend copyright claims when fair use is in play.

Fair use is an affirmative defense that, by statute and Supreme Court precedent, is highly fact-sensitive. Nonetheless, the Second Circuit has long recognized circumstances in which the defense is so plainly established by the allegations of the complaint and by materials properly considered on a motion to dismiss that dismissal is appropriate without discovery. Santos v. Kimmel (24‑2196‑cv) is a recent example of that line of authority: the court affirmed dismissal where the complaint and publicly available materials — primarily copyright registrations and the videos themselves — made clear that defendants’ televised uses were transformative commentary and did not usurp any plausible licensing market for the original works. Understanding why the court reached that result requires walking through the 12(b)(6) standard, the incorporation‑by‑reference and judicial‑notice doctrines, and the fair use factors as applied in the Second Circuit.

## Rule 12(b)(6) standards and the role of incorporation by reference

A complaint survives a Rule 12(b)(6) motion if it states a claim that is plausible on its face. In evaluating plausibility, courts accept the complaint’s well‑pleaded factual allegations as true and draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. But the plaintiff does not get to rest on conclusory allegations, and courts may consider certain documents outside the four corners of the complaint without converting the motion into one for summary judgment.

Two distinct doctrines govern what extra‑pleading materials are appropriately considered on a motion to dismiss. The first is the incorporation‑by‑reference doctrine: if a plaintiff’s complaint “relies upon” a document and the document is either attached to the complaint or its terms are relied upon in the complaint, a court may treat the document as part of the pleadings. The classic example is a contract that forms the basis of a breach claim. Treating such documents as part of the pleadings prevents a plaintiff from misstating or omitting important terms while shielding the court from factual disputes that are properly resolved at a later stage.

The second doctrine is judicial notice of adjudicative facts. Federal Rule of Evidence 201 permits a court to take judicial notice of facts not subject to reasonable dispute because they are either generally known within the trial court’s jurisdiction or capable of accurate and ready determination from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned. In the copyright context, the Second Circuit has repeatedly allowed district courts to take judicial notice of registrations and recorded information in the Copyright Office’s public database. It has also been willing, in appropriate cases, to consider the content of the works themselves when those works are central to the complaint and are either attached to the complaint or readily accessible to the court.

But these doctrines have limits. Courts must be wary of resolving disputed facts at the pleading stage. If a complaint’s allegations give rise to a plausible dispute about material facts that bear on fair use — for example, whether the secondary work truly has a different communicative purpose or whether the defendant’s use supplants a recognized market — then dismissal is premature. The incorporation‑by‑reference doctrine does not authorize a court to accept as true a defendant’s version of disputed extrinsic facts that undercut the plaintiff’s nonconclusory allegations. Likewise, judicial notice is confined to facts not subject to reasonable dispute; a court may not judicially notice contested factual inferences from a work’s content where interpretation requires further factual development. Santos v. Kimmel illustrates how these doctrines operate together: the court relied on the complaint and on judicially noticeable materials — the Copyright Office registrations and the videos themselves — to conclude that fair use was apparent on the face of the pleadings.

## Judicial notice of copyright registrations and of the allegedly infringing and original works

The Second Circuit has been clear that copyright registrations recorded in the Copyright Office are proper subjects of judicial notice. Island Software & Computer Services, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp. established that courts may take judicial notice of registrations published in the Copyright Office registry, and subsequent decisions have reiterated that such records are admissible on a motion to dismiss. The significance of that authority is twofold. First, a registration and its effective date can establish a predicate fact (e.g., whether a work was published, and when) that bears on fair use factors such as the “nature of the copyrighted work” and market harm. Second, a registration is a public record whose contents are considered reliably ascertainable without discovery.

Even where a registration is not dispositive, the registration’s content — including deposit materials that are publicly accessible — can inform a court’s view of the original work. Where a defendant attaches or where the court judicially notices the original and the allegedly infringing work, the parties and the court can directly compare the two without needing further factual discovery. The Second Circuit has repeatedly held that when the question of fair use can be decided by comparing the two works themselves, a court need not await summary judgment or trial. Cariou v. Prince is instructive in this respect: the court held that a proffered fair‑use defense could be assessed by direct comparison of Prince’s altered photographs with Cariou’s originals, and it remanded for further factual development where such development was necessary. Conversely, when the comparison plainly establishes transformation or absence of market harm, dismissal may follow.

Judicial notice of the works, however, is not an automatic license to resolve all factual disputes. Courts must distinguish between noticing the existence and contents of a work (which is usually permissible) and taking notice of contested inferences about the way a reasonable viewer would perceive the work or about the existence of a licensing market (which is often not). In Santos, the court took judicial notice of the registration records and the videos and concluded as a matter of law that a reasonable observer would understand the shows’ segments to be satirical commentary distinct from the original purpose of Santos's videos. That conclusion was supported by unambiguous contextual indicators in the publicly viewable materials (e.g., the segment titles and the host’s editorial framing) and therefore did not require further factual development.

A final practical point about exhibits: when a defendant attaches the allegedly infringing and original works to a motion to dismiss, the court should be mindful of the potential need to convert the motion to one for summary judgment if those materials give rise to factual disputes outside the pleadings. The safer course for defendants seeking dismissal is to ensure that the works are properly the subject of judicial notice or are relied on in the complaint; otherwise, the remedy may convert to summary judgment procedures, with the attendant burdens of Rule 56.

## Second Circuit guidance: TCA Television, Cariou v. Prince, and related precedents

The Second Circuit has developed a body of case law that explains when fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage and the standards guiding that determination. A few cases merit special attention.

TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum affirmed dismissal of a copyright claim on fair use grounds at the pleading stage. The court recognized that fair use is an affirmative defense but held that when the complaint and attached materials make clear that the defendant’s use was transformative and no plausible market harm existed, dismissal is appropriate. TCA sets an important precedent for the proposition that fair use may be decided early where the record presented to the court is sufficiently complete.

Cariou v. Prince is sometimes viewed as the canonical Second Circuit fair‑use decision. There, the court explained that the inquiry into whether a work is transformative centers on whether the secondary work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, altering the first with new expression, meaning, or message” (internal quotation and paraphrase). Cariou also made clear that courts should not apply a single formula but must evaluate the transformative character in light of the works themselves. Importantly, Cariou remanded certain issues for further proceedings where the court could not conclude, on the record before it, that the alleged transformations were sufficient to constitute fair use. Cariou thus points to both sides of the coin: sometimes the comparison of works resolves the question; sometimes further factual development is necessary.

DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., though not a fair‑use case per se, supplies controlling law about motion‑to‑dismiss practice in the Second Circuit: courts may consider documents attached to, incorporated by reference in, or integral to the complaint. Island Software repeats the proposition that courts may take judicial notice of the Copyright Office’s records. Later decisions such as Authors Guild v. Google, Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive, and the Supreme Court’s decisions in Campbell, Harper & Row, and the more recent Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts v. Goldsmith and Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., supply substantive guideposts for how to analyze transformative purpose and market effect. The Supreme Court’s recent decisions have narrowed the role of subjective intent and have required courts to focus on how an ordinary, reasonable observer would construe the secondary use, rather than on the defendant’s purported motives.

Santos v. Kimmel sits comfortably within this line. The Second Circuit applied the Cariou/TCA approach: it carefully inspected the complaint, the registrations, and the publicly available videos; it framed the first fair‑use factor around transformativeness as judged by a reasonable observer; it rejected reliance on subjective motives or manufactured bad faith as dispositive; and it concluded that the complaint and materials made fair use “so clearly established” that dismissal was proper. The opinion illustrates the point made in prior cases: the defenses of fair use and free expression may justify early resolution when the documentary record is unambiguous.

## Policy considerations: chilling effects versus early disposition of speech‑related cases

Resolving fair use on the pleadings has important policy implications. Two competing values dominate the analysis.

On the one hand, early dismissal can be an important protection for freedom of expression, the efficient administration of justice, and judicial economy. Copyright litigation is expensive. If a defendant’s use is plainly a protected commentary, parody, or other noninfringing exercise, the ability to test the claim early protects defendants (and would‑be speakers) from the chilling effects of protracted litigation. The Second Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that fair use is meant, in part, to prevent the Copyright Act from silencing criticism and commentary; where the record unmistakably shows that the use was for commentary and not a licensing substitute, dismissing the complaint is consonant with the statute’s policy goals.

On the other hand, permitting too broad an early‑dismissal practice runs the risk of improperly short‑circuiting plaintiffs’ rights where factual disputes exist — particularly facts relating to market harm, the extent of copying, or whether purported “commentary” actually serves as an unlicensed substitute for the original. A plaintiff should be able to take limited discovery when the complaint alleges plausible facts that, if proved, would defeat a fair‑use defense. Courts must therefore calibrate carefully: early dismissal is appropriate only where the complaint and materials properly considered demonstrate fair use without the need for contested factual findings.

Santos illustrates the tension. The court emphasized the public‑facing nature of the materials and the unambiguous editorial framing on the television program. Those facts made the second use plainly transformative as a matter of law and eliminated a plausible market‑replacement argument. But Santos also shows the limits to early resolution: the opinion distinguishes between interpreting the communicative purpose of a work — often amenable to resolution from the works themselves — and resolving contested, nonpublic facts about licensing markets or behind‑the‑scenes conduct that might bear on bad faith or market substitution. The Second Circuit further noted the diminishing role of moral‑character in fair‑use analysis: the Supreme Court has questioned whether a defendant’s bad faith should negate fair use, and Santos declined to allow Santos’s allegations of deception to govern the analysis where the complaint otherwise established commentary as the purpose of the use.

A final policy point concerns forum and motion practice: defendants who wish to obtain early dismissal must take care to assemble a clean record for the court’s inspection. Publicly accessible registrations, program transcripts, video clips, and contemporaneous public framing all help the court see the secondary work in its proper communicative context. Plaintiffs wishing to avoid early dismissal should plead nonconclusory facts showing either that the secondary use is not meaningfully transformative, that a plausible licensing market exists for the specific kind of second use at issue, or that disputed factual issues make resolution at the pleading stage inappropriate.

## Practical tips for drafting complaints and motions with the fair use issue in mind

For plaintiffs. A copyright plaintiff seeking to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion must anticipate the defenses that a defendant will present on the face of the complaint and through publicly available records. First, plead with particularity the factual bases for your claim: describe the original work’s purpose, the circumstances under which it was created and registered (attach the registration), and the exact portions alleged to be infringed. Second, allege facts that make fair use less likely, not merely conclusory assertions. Specifically, identify any actual or plausible markets for licensing the type of use at issue and plead facts showing that the defendant’s use could usurp those markets. If the work was unpublished at the time of the secondary use, make that clear, because unpublished status can affect the second fair‑use factor. Third, if the defendant has presented the works or registrations publicly, address those materials directly in the complaint and explain why they do not establish fair use; courts are less likely to treat a defendant’s public record as exculpatory if the plaintiff has already explained contrary plausible interpretations.

For defendants. When moving to dismiss on fair‑use grounds, assemble a concise, self‑contained record that the court may properly consider without conversion to summary judgment. Attach or incorporate by reference the allegedly infringing and original works, the relevant Copyright Office registrations (and their dates), and any contemporaneous public framing (e.g., program episode titles, host statements, or headlines) that reveal the communicative context. On the substantive side, focus on the first and fourth fair‑use factors: show transformation by explaining the different communicative purpose and audience reception of the secondary work, and negate plausible market harm by demonstrating the absence of any realistic licensing market for the particular secondary use. Be prepared to argue that any allegations of bad faith, deception, or behind‑the‑scenes conduct either are not material to fair use or are contradicted by the complaint itself.

For both sides, consider jurisdictional and choice‑of‑law questions at the outset. As the Santos opinion demonstrates, the governing law for non‑copyright claims (for example, contract or fraud) may differ from the law governing the Terms of Service or other agreements presented as support for a copyright claim defense. Those choice‑of‑law determinations can affect the viability of ancillary state‑law claims and should be addressed early.

A procedural tip for plaintiffs is to limit reference to public materials that could be used by a defendant to show fair use. If the defendant’s public materials — for example, a televised segment title that advertises satire of the plaintiff — are integral to the dispute, plaintiff should preempt the defendant’s narrative by pleading facts that mitigate the inference the court might draw from those materials. Conversely, defendants should avoid presenting spurious or disputed materials as if they were uncontested. If a document attached to a motion is likely to generate factual controversy, be prepared to accept conversion to summary judgment or to show that the document is the proper subject of judicial notice.

Concluding guidance: fair use is a flexible, context‑dependent doctrine. Early adjudication is a powerful tool when the public record plainly shows transformation and absence of market harm; but courts must carefully preserve plaintiffs’ ability to develop proof where factual disputes exist. The lawyer’s job in drafting either a complaint or a dismissal motion is to anticipate how the court will view the works themselves and to build a record — through pleading, exhibits, or public records — that supports the party’s preferred legal inference from that record. Santos v. Kimmel exemplifies the circumstances under which a court will accept the defendant’s fair‑use defense at the pleading stage: when the alleged secondary use is publicly contextualized as commentary and when the complaint and publicly available registration data foreclose a plausible market‑replacement theory. When those elements are present, the Second Circuit will not hesitate to dispose of the case early; when they are not, the litigation must proceed to discovery and further factual development.

# Applying the Doctrine in Santos v. Kimmel

This chapter uses Santos v. Kimmel to examine how a court applies the four statutory fair use factors in the concrete context of late‑night satire. The facts are simple: a late‑night host, using fictitious user names, purchased personalized videos from the former Congressman George Santos on a celebrity‑message platform (Cameo). The host then aired those videos on his program as part of a recurring segment whose point was to mock Santos — to test “Will Santos Say It?” — and thereby expose Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for money. On appeal the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal at the pleading stage, concluding that the use was transformative and that, taken together, the four fair use factors compelled judgment for the defendants as a matter of law.

The opinion is useful because it applies each fair use factor to widely recurring questions in media law: when does commentary that uses the entirety of a work qualify as transformative; how much weight should courts give to the copyright holder’s subjective intent; what role does deceptive procurement of the work play in the analysis; how does publication status influence protection; when does using the whole work weigh against fair use; and when does the secondary use usurp a market so as to defeat fair use. Each of these themes appears prominently in Santos, and the court’s reasoning is illustrative of the doctrinal path a court will follow when the record (the complaint and incorporated materials) permits early resolution.

## Factor one: commentary on the willingness to say absurd things for money as a different purpose

The first statutory factor — “the purpose and character of the use” — is the most important in modern practice because courts treat transformativeness as the dominant concern. The statute itself is open: it calls for consideration of “purpose and character,” and caselaw directs courts to focus especially on whether the secondary use “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” rather than merely superseding the original. See 17 U.S.C. § 107; Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation omitted).

In Santos the Second Circuit found rich evidence of transformation. Santos’s videos, on their face, conveyed an encouraging, positive message to viewers — their original communicative purpose was to provide personalized greetings and uplift. Kimmel’s segments, by contrast, used the same material not to replicate that greeting function but to comment on and satirize Santos’s reputation: they revealed and ridiculed the fact that Santos would say off‑the‑wall things on camera for money. The program thereby imposed a new expressive purpose and new meaning. That change in purpose is classic transformativeness: the secondary work recontextualized the originals to produce commentary and criticism, which are paradigmatic fair uses.

Two doctrinal points follow. First, transformativeness does not require a formal “parody” label; it suffices that the secondary use adds a new communicative purpose, whether satirical, critical, or illustrative. Second, transformative uses frequently justify copying much or all of the original, because the point of the commentary is often to present the original material intact for scrutiny or ironic contrast. Santos thus illustrates how purpose and character can weigh strongly in favor of fair use even when the defendant broadcasts the whole original videos.

## Rejecting subjective-purpose arguments and focusing on the reasonable observer

Santos argued that his and Kimmel’s subjective intentions aligned — that both parties intended the videos to show Santos’s willingness to say odd things for money — and therefore the use could not be transformative. The court rejected that line of attack and articulated an important methodological rule: transformativeness is assessed from the perspective of a reasonable observer, not by parsing actors’ subjective motives. The Supreme Court recently emphasized the primacy of that “reasonable observer” lens in Andy Warhol/Goldsmith (and earlier cases) — courts look to how the secondary use would be perceived, not to private, internal intentions.

This shift matters because it prevents a copyright owner from immunizing every critical use by asserting that the original author intended the same message as the critic. If a reasonable viewer perceives the original as heartfelt and the later use as ironic or critical, that change in public meaning supports a finding of transformation. In practice, therefore, a plaintiff’s allegation that he intended the same message as the alleged infringer will often not defeat a fair use defense when the defendant’s recontextualization creates a publicly apparent new meaning.

## Bad faith via pseudonymous requests: limited or no role in fair use after Google v. Oracle

Santos emphasized Kimmel’s use of pseudonymous profiles to procure the videos and argued that this deception evinced bad faith and therefore should prevent fair use. The Second Circuit declined to treat that conduct as dispositive. Historically Harper & Row had suggested that fair use presupposes good faith, but later authorities — including Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose and, more recently, the Supreme Court in Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc. — have made clear skepticism about elevating subjective bad faith into a categorical bar to fair use. The court in Santos followed that trajectory: even if Kimmel’s procurement involved deception, the complaint itself disclaimed any suggestion that Kimmel intended to usurp Santos’s commercial market; instead, his motive was to criticize. Thus, bad faith in procurement did not negate the transformative character of the use.

A student should draw two lessons. First, while deceptive acquisition of material may be relevant to credibility and to some equitable remedies, it no longer functions as a talisman that automatically negates fair use. Second, courts will distinguish between bad faith used to supplant a plaintiff’s market (which tends to undermine fair use) and bad faith used to obtain material for purposes of commentary and criticism (which does not necessarily undermine fair use). The post‑Oracle trend is to treat bad faith as a factor whose weight is factual, not as a per se rule.

## Factor two: published status and its limited weight

The second factor asks about the nature of the copyrighted work, with unpublished works traditionally receiving stronger protection. Harper & Row famously held that unpublished status can be significant because authors traditionally control first public appearance. In Santos the district court — and the Second Circuit — relied on documentary evidence (the registered copyrights and platform metadata) showing that Santos posted and published the videos contemporaneously with their creation. That fact reduced the protective force of factor two: because the works had been published, the traditional reason for extra protection (control of first publication) was absent.

But two qualifications are important. First, this factor is generally less decisive than factor one; courts rarely treat publication status as dispositive. Second, publication is not binary in impact; works that are intimate or highly creative may retain strong protection even when published, while straightforward commercial greetings may be less protected. The Santos videos were, on their face, short, commercial, and intended for broad distribution; those qualities made factor two favor fair use only mildly and left room for factors one and four to dominate.

## Factor three: necessity of using the whole or substantial portions to make the commentary effective

The third factor measures “the amount and substantiality of the portion used.” At first blush, complete copying ordinarily cuts against fair use. But the Supreme Court in Campbell and other cases has recognized that copying the entire work can be justified where the secondary use’s purpose requires it — for example, when criticism, parody, or a comparative news segment needs the full original to make the point. Santos is a textbook example: the point of the JKL segments was to present Santos saying certain phrases and to let the audience judge and laugh. Excerpting or summarizing would have undercut the comedic and critical effect.

Thus, courts apply this factor pragmatically: they ask whether the amount taken is reasonable in light of the transformative purpose. If a commentator must present the original, or a substantial part of it, to “conjure up” what is being criticized, then broad copying can be fair. But the necessity inquiry is exacting: the defendant must show that the use of the whole (or the substantial part) is necessary to accomplish the new purpose, not merely convenient. Santos’s complaint and the attached clips demonstrated that full use was necessary to the satirical sting, so factor three did not preclude fair use.

## Factor four: no usurpation of Cameo’s market; criticism is not a licensable substitute

The fourth factor — effect on the potential market — asks whether the secondary work usurps the market for the plaintiff’s original or its reasonable derivatives. The key legal principle is that a transformative use that merely criticizes or comments on the original is unlikely to serve as a market substitute; creators of original expressive works do not typically license the market for critical reviews or lampoons of their own work, so those markets are not realistically usurped. Campbell framed this as a crucial point: criticism and parody normally cannot be treated as substitutes for the original’s market.

Santos argued market harm, but his pleading did not plausibly allege a cognizable licensing market that Kimmel had usurped. Had Santos claimed that there was an existing market for licensing his cameos for rebroadcast on television as part of entertainment segments, he might have had a different argument. But the complaint on its face established that Cameo’s market is a consumer platform for paid personalized greetings; the JKL broadcasts were critical, public commentary and not a commercial substitute for paid cameos. The court therefore treated factor four as neutral or supportive of fair use.

Students should note the fine distinction courts draw: fair use can fail where the secondary use offers a direct substitute for and therefore harms the licensing market for the original (for example, a streaming site reproducing full films in competition with licensed distributors). But when the secondary use is a critical treatment — evaluative, satirical, or transformative in meaning — the licensing market argument typically loses force because the licensable market at issue is not one that rights holders normally exploit.

## Outcome: fair use established on the pleadings

Taken together, the Santos opinion demonstrates how an integrated, factor‑by‑factor analysis can lead a court to resolve fair use at the pleading stage when the complaint and incorporated materials leave no plausible factual dispute. The Second Circuit emphasized that where the complaint and attachments themselves show the purpose, character, amount used, publication status, and lack of market usurpation, the fair use defense can be obvious enough to support dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6). Cases such as TCA Television, Cariou, and Hachette Book Group provide parallel authority.

For practical exercises: consider three hypotheticals. First, if a late‑night host had edited the clips heavily and used only short clips to support a different critical narrative, factor three would be easier for the defendant but factor one would likely remain strong. Second, if Santos’s videos had been unpublished or intimately personal, factor two would weigh more strongly for Santos and could tip the balance against early dismissal. Third, if the defendants were rebroadcasting the videos as part of a commercial compilation that prospective customers would otherwise buy from Santos or Cameo, factor four could plausibly show market usurpation and defeat fair use.

Santos v. Kimmel thus teaches that (1) meaningful recontextualization that changes public perception of a work is powerful evidence of transformativeness; (2) a reasonable observer standard governs the first‑factor inquiry; (3) procurement bad faith is not a per se death knell for fair use; (4) published status is a modest, fact‑sensitive consideration; (5) copying the whole work may be justified when necessary to the new purpose; and (6) criticism generally does not occupy a licensing market that a defendant can be said plausibly to have usurped. These principles will guide courts evaluating media critiques, satire, and commentary in which original materials are presented intact in order to be analyzed or lampooned.

# Copyright Preemption and State-Law Workarounds

This chapter examines how federal copyright law interacts with state-law causes of action that arise from conduct involving copyrighted works. The litigation between George Santos and Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney provides a compact modern example of how courts apply the Section 301 preemption framework in the Second Circuit, and how litigants attempt — sometimes successfully, sometimes not — to escape federal preemption by pleading state-law “extra elements.” We begin with the doctrinal framework the courts use to decide when the Copyright Act displaces state law, then turn to the line of Second Circuit decisions that give content and limits to the “extra element” test (including Melendez v. Sirius XM). We then catalog the common state-law claims that arise at the margins of copyright disputes — contract, unjust enrichment, fraud, and right of publicity — and explain the typical arguments used to plead them around preemption. Finally, we analyze how the courts in Santos approached preemption and why the Second Circuit nonetheless disposed of Santos’s state-law claims on ordinary pleading grounds, and conclude with practical drafting strategies and common pitfalls.

## Section 301 preemption test in the Second Circuit: subject-matter and general equivalence

Section 301(a) of the Copyright Act contains the principal preemption provision: it withdraws from state law any cause of action that is equivalent to one of the exclusive rights enumerated in the federal statute and that falls within the subject matter of copyright. The Second Circuit applies this two-step inquiry each time a plaintiff advances a state-law claim grounded in conduct that also implicates copyrighted material.

The first step — the “subject-matter” inquiry — asks whether the works at issue are of a type protected by copyright law. This is a relatively straightforward threshold: the pieces must be original works fixed in a tangible medium of expression, falling within the categories listed in Sections 102 and 103 of the Copyright Act. If a plaintiff’s claim relies on material that is not the kind of work the Copyright Act protects (for example, uncopyrightable ideas, facts, or processes), then Section 301 does not provide preemption.

The second step — the “general equivalence” or “extra element” inquiry — asks whether the state-law cause of action asserts rights that are qualitatively different from the bundle of exclusive rights that copyright grants (reproduction, distribution, public performance, derivative works, etc.). The classic phrasing is that a state-law claim is preempted if it is “equivalent to” an exclusive federal right — meaning that it attempts to vindicate the same interests and would be established by proving the same facts as a copyright claim. If, instead, the state-law claim contains an “extra element” that changes the nature of the claim so that it is not merely a copyright substitute, preemption does not apply.

How this plays out in practice: an ordinary claim for copying a protected work — alleging unauthorized reproduction or distribution — will be preempted because it plainly falls within the federal rights and can be redressed under the Copyright Act. By contrast, a claim that depends on some additional factual element not required to prove copyright infringement — for example, a state-law fraud claim that requires proof of a misrepresentation, reliance, and pecuniary loss — may survive preemption so long as the extra element is both necessary to the cause of action and “qualitatively distinct” from rights under copyright.

The line between “equivalent” and “qualitatively different” is often the contested ground of litigation. The Second Circuit emphasizes that the extra-element test is not a formalistic exercise: courts ask whether the extra element alters the nature of the remedy to be provided or requires proof of facts beyond those necessary to establish the copyright claim. If it does, the state-law claim may proceed. If it does not — if the only wrong alleged is the unauthorized use of the copyrighted work and the state-law claim simply repackages that wrong — preemption applies.

## Melendez v. Sirius XM and the “extra element” analysis

Melendez v. Sirius XM, 50 F.4th 294 (2d Cir. 2022), is the Second Circuit’s leading recent exposition of how the extra-element test works in practice. Melendez involved claims by a plaintiff whose recording had been rebroadcast; the plaintiff advanced state-law claims that the court found “qualitatively different” from copyright infringement because they required proof of extra elements beyond what federal law demands.

Melendez is useful for two related propositions. First, it underscores that not every state-law claim that happens to involve copyrighted material is preempted; the presence of an additional, non-copyright element that changes the nature of the duty or the required proof can preserve the claim. Second, Melendez warns courts and litigants not to treat “extra element” analysis as merely a label; the courts examine the substance of the state-law cause and the relief sought to determine whether federal law truly subsumes the state claim.

Two features make an “extra element” persuasive under Melendez: (1) the extra element must be an element of the cause of action itself, not merely an allegation about how the defendant infringed (for example, alleging intent or bad faith does not always create a non-equivalent claim if copyright infringement can be established without that element); and (2) the extra element must be “qualitatively different” from the exclusive federal rights — that is, it must impose an obligation or confer a remedy that copyright law does not encompass.

Melendez leaves open important contours. For example, what counts as “qualitatively different” depends on the context; courts will examine the specific elements required by state law and the relief the plaintiff seeks. If, as in some fraud claims, the extra elements require proof of misrepresentation and reliance, courts often find that those elements create a distinct claim; but per Campbell and later authorities, the relevance of a defendant’s subjective intent or bad faith to fair use is limited, and courts will not automatically treat allegations of bad faith as sufficient to avoid preemption.

Melendez therefore occupies a middle ground. It invites careful pleading by plaintiffs who wish to preserve state-law claims in the shadow of federal copyright, but it also prevents a rote invocation of “extra elements” to avoid preemption when the substance of the claim is simply repackaged infringement.

## Common state-law claims adjacent to copyright: contract, unjust enrichment, fraud, right of publicity

State-law claims that commonly appear alongside or instead of federal copyright claims include breach of contract (express and implied), unjust enrichment, promissory estoppel, fraud (or fraudulent inducement), and the right of publicity. Each has its own doctrinal elements and its own risks of preemption.

Breach of contract. A contract claim will escape preemption if it depends on contract terms and obligations that are independent of the exclusive rights of copyright. A simple example that courts accept is when a licensing agreement contains covenants or conditions that go beyond the mere grant of reproduction or distribution rights — for instance, confidentiality provisions, quality control clauses, or express prohibitions on certain types of subsequent uses. An express breach claim rests on the bargained-for terms; its elements (existence of a contract, specific contractual term, breach of that term, damages) are distinct from a claim that someone infringed an exclusive copyright right.

But contract claims fail when plaintiffs lack privity (they are not parties to the contract) and when the contract does not plainly create enforcement rights in third parties. Courts in the Second Circuit (and elsewhere) apply ordinary state contract doctrines: third-party beneficiary status requires a clear intent in the contract to benefit the third party; implied-in-fact contracts require a meeting of the minds and the essential terms; and unilateral conditions or platform terms of service (TOS) often do not create enforceable duties to non-contracting parties. The Santos case illustrates both pitfalls: Santos attempted to rely on Cameo’s Terms of Service to hold Kimmel and the broadcasters liable, but the TOS were governed by Illinois law and contained no express third-party beneficiary language for non-users; under Illinois law a strong presumption disfavors conferring contractual benefits on noncontracting third parties. Similarly, Santos’s attempt to plead an implied-in-fact contract under New York law failed because he did not plead the essential contractual terms, meeting of the minds, or how consideration was exchanged.

Unjust enrichment. Unjust enrichment is a typical fallback allegation when plaintiffs want relief but lack a written contract. To avoid preemption, unjust enrichment claims must assert a theory that is not equivalent to copyright. Courts are receptive when the unjust enrichment claim rests on an unjust retention of profits for reasons distinct from a copyright violation — for example, where the enrichment arose from an independent wrongful act (misappropriation of confidential information, fraud, or conversion), or where the defendant’s conduct violated duties independent of the exclusive federal rights. But many unjust enrichment claims are preempted because a request for restitution for the value of unauthorized copies simply duplicates the remedy available under federal law.

Fraud and fraudulent inducement. Fraud claims can escape preemption when they require proof of additional elements — a knowingly false representation of material fact, reasonable reliance, and resulting pecuniary loss. Courts often find that these elements make a fraud claim “qualitatively different” from copyright infringement. Nonetheless, the fraud claim will still be preempted if the alleged misrepresentation merely echoes a claim of unauthorized use of the copyrighted material and the damages are coextensive with the value of the copyrighted work. The Santos opinion is instructive: Santos alleged fraudulent inducement against the Cameo requester(s), but under New York law he failed to plead the requisite out-of-pocket loss; without that economic injury, the fraud claim could not survive regardless of preemption.

Right of publicity. The right of publicity — the state-law protection against unauthorized commercial exploitation of an individual’s persona — sits at a complicated intersection with copyright. If an alleged wrong is grounded in the misappropriation of a person’s likeness or identity for commercial benefit, the claim may be separate from copyright and avoid preemption because it protects a different interest (personality/identity rather than creative expression). But if the claim is essentially a complaint about re-use of a copyrighted recording or photograph, courts may find the claim preempted. The survival of a publicity claim often depends on whether the conduct is primarily expressive (invoking First Amendment considerations) or commercial, and on whether the remedy sought targets appropriation of identity rather than copying of expressive content.

Summary. The key lesson across these claim types is consistency: if the state-law claim requires proof of an extra element — an element that changes both the nature of the duty and the proof required — the claim has a chance of surviving preemption. If the state-law cause boils down to “you used my copyrighted work without permission,” it is unlikely to survive Section 301.

## District court’s preemption ruling versus the Second Circuit’s decision to reach the merits

A notable structural feature of the Santos litigation is how the courts handled preemption. The district court dismissed Santos’s contract and implied-contract claims on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. The Second Circuit, however, avoided deciding the preemption question on the merits; instead, the panel concluded that Santos’s state-law claims failed for independent reasons under governing state law: he was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and did not plausibly allege the elements of an implied contract or the pecuniary loss element of fraudulent inducement.

This sequencing matters for two reasons.

First, reaching the merits on ordinary state-law grounds is often a more straightforward and narrow disposition than deciding a federal preemption issue. Courts will sometimes dispose of a case on state-law pleading deficiencies where those defects are clear and do not require a sweeping interpretation of the Copyright Act. That was the Second Circuit’s approach here: because Santos’s pleading failed to establish essential elements of the contract and fraud claims, the court did not need to resolve whether the Copyright Act would have displaced those claims.

Second, the district court’s preemption ruling (though not dispositive on appeal) highlights how defendants often seek to invoke Section 301 as early, broad relief. Preemption can be an attractive defense because, if it applies, it can eliminate state-law claims wholesale. But plaintiffs can thwart preemption by pleading state-law elements that are independent, specific, and well-pleaded. Conversely, plaintiffs who seek to preserve noncopyright causes of action should anticipate preemption challenges and draft their complaints to affirmatively plead the extra elements that make their claims qualitatively different.

One practical corollary is that an appellate court’s choice to decide or avoid a preemption question does not change the underlying doctrine; it simply reflects judicial economy and a preference to resolve cases on narrower, settled grounds when available. Nevertheless, litigants should be mindful that a district court’s preemption ruling may shape discovery and strategy even if the appellate court ultimately resolves the case on state-law pleading grounds.

## Drafting strategies and pitfalls when pleading state claims in copyright-adjacent disputes

For practitioners and litigants working at the interface of copyright and state law, the Santos litigation supplies a set of practical dos and don’ts. Below are core drafting strategies to minimize the risk of preemption and to survive early Rule 12(b)(6) scrutiny.

1. Plead the “extra element” explicitly and with particularity. If invoking fraud, identify the misrepresentation or omission, the speaker, the audience, the time and place, and the specific statements relied upon. Allege reasonable reliance and the causal link to the claimed pecuniary injury. If reliance cannot be plausibly pleaded, the fraud claim will likely fail or be treated as preempted. The Second Circuit in Santos affirmed dismissal of Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim because he failed to allege an actual out-of-pocket loss under New York law.

2. In contract-based theories, plead privity or third-party beneficiary status plainly. If you rely on a Terms of Service or other contract to create a right in a non-signatory, include the precise contract language showing an intent to confer enforceable rights on third parties. If you allege an implied-in-fact contract, plead the essential terms: offer, acceptance, consideration, and a meeting of the minds, describing how those elements were met by the parties’ conduct. The Santos court rejected Santos’s direct and implied contract claims because he neither showed contractual privity nor pled the specific essential terms required under New York law.

3. Quantify and connect damages to the pleaded theory. A state-law cause that survives preemption still requires damages that flow from the extra element, not merely the value of the copyrighted work. For example, if you allege reliance-based damages for fraud, explain how the misrepresentation caused economic loss beyond the lost licensing value of the copyrighted work. If only statutory or discretionary remedies are sought, explain why the state claim provides relief that copyright does not.

4. Avoid pleading purely duplicative legal theories. Do not draft state-law counts that merely restate the copyright infringement allegations in different clothes. Courts look at the substance, not the label. If the gravamen of the plaintiff’s complaint is that the defendant reproduced, distributed, or publicly performed the plaintiff’s copyrighted work, a state-law claim that merely seeks the same relief will be vulnerable to preemption.

5. Use alternative theories carefully and coherently. It is common to plead copyright alongside contract, fraud, and unjust enrichment. But ensure that each count stands on its own by alleging distinct elements and facts. If the fraud count depends entirely on the same proof as the copyright claim and lacks a separate reliance/damage theory, a court may treat it as preempted or dismiss it on pleading grounds.

6. Consider choice-of-law implications in advance. Platform terms of service are often governed by location-specific law and forum-selection clauses; those choices matter for issues like third-party beneficiaries and the existence of reliance-based damages. In Santos, Illinois law governed the Cameo Terms and supplied a strong presumption against conferring third-party benefits; New York law governed Santos’s implied contract and fraud claims and required particular elements that Santos did not plead.

7. Preserve factual evidence and communications that show independent duties. If you want to prove an implied contract, reliance, or misrepresentation, preserve messages, emails, invoices, or testimony that support the alleged elements. Vague assertions about “the parties’ understanding” or “industry custom” seldom withstand Rule 12 scrutiny.

8. Anticipate affirmative defenses at the pleading stage. Fair use is an affirmative defense to copyright claims, and the courts will sometimes decide fair use at the pleading stage when the necessary facts are apparent from the complaint and documents integral to it. If a state-law claim depends on the same materials and would be affected by a fair-use finding, prepare to explain why the state claim asserts an independent obligation.

Pitfalls to avoid

- Do not assume that alleging subjective bad faith automatically creates an “extra element.” Courts have been skeptical about treating bad faith alone as a means to avoid preemption, especially where the gravamen of the complaint remains copying. Harper & Row’s language about good faith has been narrowed by later decisions (Campbell, Google v. Oracle) and the Supreme Court’s skepticism about excluding bad actors from fair use.

- Do not plead damages as conclusory. Courts will dismiss claims that assert generic “lost licensing revenue” or “harm to market value” without explaining how the state claim’s damages differ from what would be recoverable under the Copyright Act.

- Do not neglect to plead causation. Even if you identify an extra element like reliance, you must connect the defendant’s conduct to the specific losses alleged.

- Beware platform terms of service. Plaintiffs often try to use a platform’s TOS to sue downstream users; but many platforms disclaim third-party beneficiaries and limit remedies. Read and quote the relevant provisions, and plead why the TOS were intended to benefit non-users.

Checklist for pleading defensible state-law claims alongside copyright claims

- Identify the specific state cause of action and cite its elements.
- For each element, plead concrete facts showing how the element is satisfied.
- If relying on a contract, attach the contract and quote the precise provisions that create a right in the plaintiff (e.g., third-party beneficiary language).
- If alleging fraud, plead the who, what, when, where, and how, and quantify reliance and damages.
- If alleging unjust enrichment, explain why common-law restitution is justified for reasons independent of copyright (e.g., wrongful retention after fraud).
- Explain why the prospective remedies are not duplicative of copyright remedies (e.g., punitive damages for fraud, disgorgement for unjust enrichment for wrongful behavior unrelated to reproduction rights).
- Anticipate and brief likely preemption and fair-use defenses in initial papers.

## Applying the doctrine to Santos: what the courts did and why the outcome matters

Santos’s complaint illustrates how the rules above operate in a real dispute. Santos alleged that Kimmel had used the Cameo platform to procure videos, which Santos created and published on the same day, and then broadcast those clips on a national television program to mock Santos in a recurring segment. Santos asserted federal copyright infringement claims and state-law claims for breach of contract, breach of an implied-in-fact contract, and fraudulent inducement.

The district court dismissed the copyright claims at the pleading stage, finding on the face of the complaint and the registered copyrights that Kimmel’s broadcast segments were a paradigmatic example of a transformative use — satire or commentary — and therefore were protected by fair use. The Second Circuit affirmed that disposition because fair use is flexible and can be resolved where the relevant facts and registrations are on the record. That disposition shows two things: (1) fair use can be raised and disposed of on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint and attached materials make the relevant facts plain; and (2) if fair use applies, the federal copyright claim itself will fail, which affects plaintiffs’ strategic posture vis-à-vis state claims.

As to Santos’s state claims, the district court concluded that the contract and implied-contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act and that the fraud claim failed to allege the required elements. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal, but not on the preemption ground; rather, the court held that (1) under Illinois law the Cameo Terms of Service did not confer enforceable rights on Santos as a non-signatory; (2) under New York law Santos had not pled the essential terms or a meeting of minds for an implied-in-fact contract; and (3) Santos had not alleged any actual out-of-pocket loss needed to sustain a fraudulent inducement claim under New York law.

This disposition is instructive for two reasons. First, it illustrates how courts will apply ordinary state-law principles to claims that plaintiffs may try to validate as copyright workarounds. Absent clear contractual privity, explicit third-party beneficiary language, or the essential elements of an implied promise, a contract-based route around copyright is fragile. Second, it shows that plaintiffs cannot rely on pleading deficiencies as a fallback; even if preemption could be avoided doctrinally, plaintiffs must still satisfy ordinary elements of the state law causes they invoke.

Finally, Santos underscores a practical device for defendants: in copyright-adjacent disputes, defendants have multiple early-bandwidth defenses (fair use, preemption, failure to plead contract elements, failure to plead damages for fraud). A defendant who can negate one or more elements at the pleading stage can often resolve the case without broad preemption rulings.

Conclusion

Section 301 preemption establishes a broad federal baseline, but courts and litigants operate in the margins where state law can and does remain available. The “extra element” test (as explicated in Melendez and applied in subsequent decisions) is the principal doctrinal mechanism that allows state-law claims to survive federal preemption. Success in preserving state-law claims turns on careful fact-pleading: identify and plead the extra element with specificity, tie damages to non-copyright injuries, and plead contractual or reliance elements in detail. The Santos litigation is a cautionary tale: trying to convert a copyright dispute into a contract or fraud claim without pleading the independent elements will not typically defeat federal preemption or survive the ordinary pleading requirements.

# Platform Terms and Third-Party Beneficiary Doctrine

The dispute in Santos v. Kimmel provides a useful entry point into a recurrent problem in the law of online creator marketplaces: when, if ever, a person who is not a party to a platform agreement may sue to enforce its terms. George Santos alleged that Jimmy Kimmel breached Cameo’s Terms of Service when he used the platform to request personalized videos and then exploited those videos on television. The Second Circuit did not reach whether the Copyright Act preempted Santos’s state-law claims because it concluded, under the choice-of-law clause, that Illinois contract law governed the Cameo Terms and Illinois law disfavors enforcement of contracts by nonparties. The court relied on the Illinois rule that a nonparty may not enforce contract provisions unless the contract manifests an intent to confer a benefit on that third party so strong as to be “practically an express declaration.” That doctrine—its rationale, its mechanics, and its drafting workarounds—is the subject of this chapter.

This chapter explains (1) the Illinois presumption against conferring benefits on noncontracting third parties and how courts apply that presumption; (2) the decision in Barry v. St. Mary’s and the “practically an express declaration” standard; (3) how to read platform Terms of Service (TOS) when there are separate user and talent or creator agreements and why explicit third‑party beneficiary clauses in some parts of a contract can be read as a negative implication for the rest; (4) the strategic role of choice‑of‑law provisions; and (5) practical drafting advice for platform operators and creators who want to allocate rights in user‑uploaded content, authorize broadcast reuse, and preserve enforceable creator protections.

## Third‑party beneficiary presumption against enforcement under Illinois law

At common law, a contract ordinarily confers rights only upon the contracting parties. The law of third‑party beneficiaries creates an exception: where the contracting parties intend that a benefit conveyed by their bargain inures to a third party, that third party may have enforceable rights. But jurisdictions differ sharply on how readily they will infer such an intent from contract language and surrounding circumstances.

Illinois sits on the more restrictive end of that spectrum. Illinois courts begin with a strong presumption that a contract does not confer enforceable benefits on noncontracting third parties. The presumption reflects judicial reluctance to rewrite bargains by imposing contractual obligations for the benefit of persons who did not exchange consideration, negotiate the terms, or assume risks. Practically, the Illinois rule means that a third party who was not a party to the relevant agreement faces an uphill evidentiary burden: the plaintiff must point to language in the contract that unambiguously manifests a direct intent to benefit that third party, not merely to incidental effects that the contract will have on others.

Functionally, courts distinguish two categories of beneficiaries. An incidental beneficiary is someone who will gain from the contract’s performance but whom the contracting parties did not intend to benefit in a legally enforceable way. An intended beneficiary is someone the parties expressly or demonstrably meant to benefit. Illinois requires a clear showing of the latter. Thus, for claimants who rely on a platform TOS to supply legal rights against another platform user (for example, to prevent reuse or to extract compensation), Illinois law will typically demand contractual language that names them or otherwise unmistakably establishes their status as intended beneficiaries.

This rule has concrete consequences in the platform context. Many creator marketplaces operate on a two‑party model: a user (requester) clicks to accept the platform’s TOS when making a request; the creator (talent) separately engages through the platform’s interface but is not a signatory to the requester's acceptance. If the TOS do not manifest an intent to create enforceable third‑party rights in the talent, Illinois law will likely deny enforcement to any nonparty creator who later finds the requester’s conduct objectionable.

## Barry v. St. Mary’s and the requirement of an express declaration of intent to benefit

The Santos court relied on Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, an Illinois appellate decision that is often cited for the proposition that a contractual implication of third‑party beneficiary status must be “so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Barry crystallizes the Illinois approach: courts will not infer a third‑party right from ambiguous or generalized language; rather, the contract must contain wording that shows the parties intended to confer enforceable rights on the third party—language approaching an express naming or unmistakable identification.

Why such strictness? Barry and its progeny reflect several policy concerns. First, enforcing third‑party rights can impose unanticipated obligations on contracting parties who did not bargain with the third party. Second, allowing casual or implied third‑party beneficiary claims would invite litigation from persons affected incidentally by many commercial contracts. Third, the doctrine of consideration and privity historically limits contractual enforcement to parties who have supplied consideration; a strict third‑party‑beneficiary rule preserves that limiting principle unless parties clearly decide otherwise.

Applying Barry to the fact pattern in Santos, the Second Circuit emphasized that the Cameo Terms did not contain an express declaration that certain provisions were intended to benefit creators such as Santos. The court also noted that other provisions of the Terms did, in fact, include explicit third‑party beneficiary language. That asymmetry was important: when a contract explicitly confers third‑party rights in some contexts but omits them elsewhere, courts will often treat the omission as deliberate. Illinois courts will therefore infer that the contracting parties knew how to grant third‑party beneficiary status and did so only where they intended; the absence of such language elsewhere defeats a nonparty’s attempt to enforce the contract.

A few practical lessons follow from Barry. First, the inquiry is text‑centric: a plaintiff who wants to rely on a contract to obtain third‑party enforcement should look first for explicit beneficiary language—naming, identifying, or otherwise unmistakably describing the intended beneficiary—or for similarly strong indicia of intent (e.g., the contract imposes an obligation whose whole purpose is to benefit the third party). Second, courts will scrutinize the contract as a whole: the presence of explicit third‑party clauses in some parts of the TOS weakens arguments that other, silent parts are intended to be enforceable by nonparties.

## Reading TOS structures: separate user and talent agreements; explicit third‑party beneficiary clauses elsewhere as negative implication

Platform agreements are not monoliths. Large creator marketplaces like Cameo typically have multiple agreements and interfaces: a general Terms of Service, a User Agreement governing those who buy services, a Talent Agreement governing creators who offer services, and ancillary policies (privacy policy, content rules, payment terms). These separate documents interact; courts will read them as a whole to determine rights and obligations.

Where a platform sets up separate contracting flows—one for requesters and one for creators—the words used in each flow are material. A common architecture: the requester is required to accept (clickwrap) a User Agreement that grants the platform certain rights and imposes obligations on the requester; creators accept the platform’s Creator or Talent Agreement that grants the platform a license to host and distribute the creator’s work and lays out compensation and dispute procedures. If the requester’s agreement is silent about granting direct rights to creators, and the creator’s agreement is the only place where the creator’s rights are spelled out, Illinois courts will often treat the creator as a contracting party to the creator agreement and not as a third‑party beneficiary of the requester’s agreement.

Santos illustrates a related interpretive rule: when a contract contains express third‑party beneficiary language in some provisions but not in others, that express inclusion can be read as an implicit exclusion elsewhere. The Second Circuit observed that “other provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third‑party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” That negative implication matters: if a platform wants to preserve flexibility to grant certain protections to creators without making them parties to every requester contract, the platform should be deliberate about where it includes express third‑party‑beneficiary language.

For creators, the practical significance is plain: do not assume that clicking through a platform interface entitles you to enforce requester promises. Creators should (a) read both the requester-facing and creator-facing agreements; (b) ensure the creator-facing agreement contains the protections you want (licensing terms, payment obligations, ownership provisions, consent for reuse); and (c) consider using platform features that convert the requester’s assent into an enforceable obligation to you—such as an explicit clause in the User Agreement naming creators as intended beneficiaries, or a mechanism that makes creators co‑parties to the transaction.

From a litigation standpoint, courts will analyze the objective language and structure of the TOS. The presence of an express consent or beneficiary clause will be decisive; in its absence, courts like those in Illinois will be reluctant to impose third‑party rights.

## Choice‑of‑law provisions and their strategic significance

Choice‑of‑law clauses in platform agreements are not mere boilerplate. The governing-law clause determines which state’s substantive contract doctrines will decide whether nonparties may enforce contract provisions. Platforms often select a jurisdiction perceived to be business-friendly or predictable (e.g., Delaware, New York, Illinois); those choices affect a host of doctrinal questions, including third‑party‑beneficiary rules, enforceability of clickwraps, contracting formalities, and statutes of limitations.

The Santos opinion demonstrates the real-world impact of such clauses. The District Court (and the Second Circuit on review) applied the governing Illinois law named in the Cameo Terms to Santos’s claim. Under Illinois law, the strong presumption against conferring benefits on third parties supplied the doctrinal basis to reject Santos’s ability to enforce the requester-facing TOS. Had the TOS specified New York law, the inquiry might have differed; New York courts apply the intended-beneficiary test but have sometimes been more permissive in inferring intent from the contract and surrounding circumstances. Thus, choice‑of‑law selection is a deliberate strategic decision that shapes the enforceability of rights.

Practical implications for drafters:

- Platforms. Select governing law with an eye to the doctrines you want to control. If you prefer a restrictive approach to third‑party enforcement (limiting suits by nonparties), name a state whose law articulates a firm presumption against third‑party beneficiaries. Also consider forum selection and arbitration clauses to reduce litigation risk.

- Creators and requesters. Beware that governing-law clauses can neutralize favorable state doctrines. If you as a creator prefer a jurisdiction more likely to find third‑party beneficiary status, negotiate for that governing law in the creator agreement or an agreement that directly binds requesters to the creator.

Courts sometimes decline to enforce choice‑of‑law clauses when enforcement would violate fundamental public policy or when the clause is procedurally unconscionable. But in ordinary commercial contexts, courts will respect a clear choice‑of‑law clause and apply the chosen state’s law to resolve third‑party‑beneficiary disputes.

## Practical drafting: allocating rights in user‑uploaded content, broadcast reuse, and creator protections

The Santos decision highlights a drafting gap that often exists in creator marketplaces: platforms routinely obtain licenses from creators to host and distribute content but may not secure clear, parallel assurances from requesters that permit downstream uses—broadcast, excerpting, or parody—of content created through the platform. When an improper use occurs, creators who are not parties to the relevant requester‑facing agreement can be left without a contractual remedy unless the TOS affirmatively creates such a remedy.

Below are practical drafting recommendations for platforms, creators, and high‑value requesters to reduce ambiguity and allocate risk.

1. Be explicit about who is a contracting party and who has enforceable rights.

- If the platform intends creators to be able to enforce certain promises made by requesters, include an express third‑party beneficiary clause in the User Agreement that identifies creators (or a defined class of creators) as intended beneficiaries. Sample language:
  “Creator(s) shall be third‑party beneficiaries of the Requester Agreement. Requester acknowledges and agrees that the Creator(s) are express intended beneficiaries of the promises contained herein and may enforce those promises directly against Requester.”

- Alternatively, make creators co‑parties to the transaction (for example, by generating a mini‑agreement that both requester and creator must accept at the point of ordering).

2. Specify the scope of licenses and reuse rights clearly.

- Grant language should be precise about allowed uses: noncommercial/commercial, broadcast, promotional, sublicensing, derivative works, geographic scope, and duration. Example:
  “Creator hereby grants Platform and Requester a nonexclusive, worldwide, transferable license to reproduce, distribute, publicly perform, display, and create derivative works of the Delivered Video in any media now known or hereafter developed, for the term of the copyright, solely for the purposes specified in the Request.”

- If broadcast reuse is permitted, include explicit permission and, where appropriate, a separate compensation mechanism for certain classes of reuse (e.g., television broadcast or network reuse).

- Define the right to edit. If the platform or requester can edit creator content for broadcast or commentary, state whether alterations are permitted and whether the creator’s moral rights are waived.

3. Address publicity, privacy, and right of publicity.

- Ensure that the requester warrants that they have obtained all necessary releases and consents for any person appearing in the video and that the requester indemnifies the creator/platform for claims arising from unauthorized uses.

- Where creators supply content that uses their voice/likeness, include a clause where the creator grants the platform and its licensees rights to use the creator’s name, portrait, or voice, but also provide guardrails on derogatory or demeaning uses if that is a concern.

4. Allocate risk with representations, warranties, and indemnities.

- Requesters should represent that their intended use of the delivered content complies with law and platform policies and that they will not use the content in a way that violates the creator’s rights beyond the scope of the license.

- Include mutual indemnities for breaches of representations: the requester indemnifies for misrepresentations about consents; the creator indemnifies for warranty breaches about originality.

- Consider a “safe harbor” mechanism: if a requester wants to use content beyond the platform’s typical use cases (for example, to include it in a nationally broadcast TV segment), require requester to accept an enhanced license and additional payment and to sign an express acknowledgement.

5. Use express third‑party beneficiary language sparingly but deliberately.

- If the goal is to empower creators to enforce requester obligations, include express beneficiary language. If the goal is to limit creators’ enforcement and keep dispute resolution in a streamlined channel (e.g., arbitration with the platform), do not include such language.

- Make the platform’s intentions clear through definitions and exceptions. For instance, define “Creator” in the User Agreement and expressly state whether creators are intended beneficiaries of particular covenants (e.g., “payment,” “no-derogatory-use”).

6. Design the user experience to create enforceable consent.

- A clickwrap is far stronger than browsewrap. For key rights transfers or third‑party beneficiary grants, require explicit affirmative assent (e.g., checkboxes, signature, or dual acceptance by both requester and creator).

- Consider integrating an in‑transaction confirmation: when a requester orders a video, present a short rider that both parties accept, stating the permitted uses, any fees for certain reuses, and whether the creator will be an intended beneficiary.

7. Consider a modular approach to rights and compensation.

- Permit creators to select a default license for standard marketplace transactions, with options to opt into broader rights (e.g., broadcast, merchandising) for additional fees.

- Provide transparent labeling and pricing for “broadcast rights” or “network rights” to reduce downstream disputes.

8. Protect creators’ moral and reputational interests.

- If creators object to uses that are derogatory or imply endorsement, include contractual protections (e.g., a covenant not to use the creator’s content in a defamatory or falsely endorsing manner) or an opt‑out right for certain sensitive uses, with compensation adjustments.

9. Draft with litigation in mind.

- If you want potential enforcement by nonparties, include clear naming of who may enforce, a statement of intent, and a damages/remedy clause that contemplates enforcement by the beneficiary (including injunctive relief and statutory damages, where applicable).

- If you want to funnel disputes to the platform, include an explicit dispute-resolution clause that requires administrative exhaustion before litigation and clarifies who may participate.

10. Watch choice‑of‑law and jurisdiction language.

- Pick governing law deliberately to reflect the jurisprudence you prefer on third‑party beneficiaries and clickwrap enforceability.

- Coordinate governing law across the suite of documents (User Agreement, Creator Agreement, Privacy Policy). Inconsistent governing-law clauses create interpretive friction and invite forum shopping.

## Practical checklist and takeaways

- For creators who want enforceable protections: do not assume platform TOS protect you unless they expressly say so. Seek a creator agreement, request express third‑party beneficiary language in relevant requester agreements, insist on clear licensing and reuse language, and require affirmative assent where needed.

- For platforms that want to limit creators’ ability to sue requesters: draft requester agreements without express third‑party beneficiary language, but recognize that omitting protections for creators can create reputational and operational risks if content is widely reused. Balance legal insulation with marketplace integrity.

- For both sides: a clear user experience that surfaces key rights to both requesters and creators at the point of transaction reduces disputes. Explicit, narrow grant language (what is licensed, to whom, for what uses, for how long, and for what compensation) is far preferable to ambiguous, catchall phrases.

- Choice of law matters: pick it to align with your policy goals, but be realistic about enforceability and public‑policy limits.

Santos v. Kimmel underscores the practical legal consequence of drafting choices made before any dispute arises. Courts will look first to the text and structure of the TOS: who is named, what language is used, whether the parties used express third‑party beneficiary clauses in some places but not others, and what governing law the contract selects. For creators and platforms alike, the lesson is practical and immediate: rights and remedies in the real world turn largely on careful contracting.

# Implied-in-Fact Contracts in Entertainment and Media Deals

Implied-in-fact contracts occupy a recurring place in entertainment and media litigation. Creators, performers, and studios often deal informally, through emails, platform-mediated exchanges, and industry customs. Yet when a dispute reaches court, plaintiffs who assert that an enforceable promise arose from conduct confront distinct and exacting requirements. This chapter explains how New York law treats implied-in-fact contracts, what a plaintiff must plead to survive a motion to dismiss, how courts identify evidence of a “meeting of the minds,” and how courts distinguish ordinary implied contractual liability from specialized idea‑submission doctrines (the Desny line). We use Santos v. Kimmel as the vehicle for applying these doctrinal rules to a media‑era fact pattern in which a late‑night host obtained and broadcasted personalized videos purchased through a third‑party platform.

## New York standards: intent, surrounding circumstances, and mutual assent

New York treats an implied‑in‑fact contract as a true contract: it is formed by the parties’ conduct rather than their explicit words, and it is governed by the same requirement of mutual assent that underlies express contracts. The courts therefore ask whether the conduct and surrounding circumstances demonstrate that both parties intended to be bound and had a meeting of the minds as to the essential terms. As the Second Circuit summarized in Santos, “[u]nder New York law, we consider ‘the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances’ to determine whether an implied‑in‑fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation omitted).

That inquiry is objective. Courts look to outward manifestations — communications, course of dealing, and conduct — rather than to the parties’ secret subjective intentions. The plaintiff therefore must point to specific factual circumstances that would permit a reasonable finder of fact to conclude that both sides understood and consented to the same bargain. Factors courts consider include: (1) explicit or implicit communications that operate as offers and acceptances; (2) a course of dealing or prior relationship showing habitual practices; (3) industry custom and usage that fill in missing details consistent with reasonable expectations; and (4) the contemporaneous conduct of the parties (for example, performance that only makes sense if a contract existed). But the more essential the omitted term, the more persuasive the evidence of mutual assent must be.

New York also draws a clear line between an implied contract (a consensual agreement inferred from conduct) and a quasi‑contract or restitutionary claim (an equitable remedy imposed to prevent unjust enrichment). An implied‑in‑fact claim requires proof of an objective agreement; restitution does not. Plaintiffs who conflate the two may fail to state a cognizable contract claim if they do not plead the agreement itself.

## Pleading essential terms: Caniglia and the requirement of specificity

The New York authorities emphasize that a complaint alleging an implied‑in‑fact contract must do more than assert, in conclusory fashion, that “the parties had a contract.” A plaintiff must plead the essential terms of the putative agreement with sufficient specificity to allow a court to evaluate both whether a contract could exist and what obligations it would impose. The controlling point is illustrated by Caniglia v. Chicago Trib.‑N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146 (1st Dep’t 1994), relied upon in Santos. Under Caniglia and related decisions, to survive dismissal plaintiffs must identify the essential elements — who promised what, in exchange for what, and on what terms — and allege them in nonconclusory language.

What are “essential terms”? While there is no rigid universal checklist, courts expect allegations adequate to define the parties, the subject matter, performance obligations, consideration or price (if applicable), and timing or scope of performance so that the court can enforce the bargain. In employment or services contracts, for example, courts look for the scope of services, compensation, and duration. In distribution or license contexts, courts look for grant language (exclusive or nonexclusive), territory, duration, and financial terms. A pleading that merely alleges that “defendant agreed to pay plaintiff” without stating the amount, the basis for payment, or when payment was due will typically be insufficient.

Two practical consequences follow. First, plaintiffs who rely on implied agreements should plead the communications and events that supply the missing terms — for example, emails proposing a particular fee, testimony that a customary one‑time license fee applies in the industry, or an invoice reflecting a negotiated price. Second, at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage, courts will not invent contractual terms from generalized allegations of unfairness. When the alleged agreement is too indefinite or the claimed obligations are amorphous, dismissal is appropriate.

Caniglia and its progeny therefore function as gatekeepers: they prevent vague claims of “we had a deal” from proceeding without concrete allegations that can be litigated. In the entertainment context, where informal exchanges are common, plaintiffs must take care to plead with the precision that courts require.

## Evidence of a meeting of the minds: DG & A and similar authorities

Proving a meeting of the minds can be difficult when much of the exchange is nonverbal or mediated through a platform. New York courts have developed several categories of evidence that can support a finding of mutual assent, and a number of decisions illustrate both what works and what does not.

DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305 (3d Dep’t 2008), exemplifies a challenge to an implied‑in‑fact contract where the plaintiff’s allegations did not demonstrate a meeting of the minds. Courts look for objective indicia: (1) offer and acceptance — whether a party made a concrete proposal and the other party accepted it; (2) performative conduct consistent only with acceptance — for example, immediate performance in reliance on a specific promise; (3) contemporaneous statements or writing reflecting agreement; (4) course of dealing or a preexisting relationship that created mutual expectations; and (5) industry custom and usage capable of being proved at trial.

Consider examples that courts find persuasive. An email exchange in which one party proposes to license a short segment for broadcast at a specified fee and the other party replies “agreed” or pays the fee supports a finding of mutual assent. A producer’s invitation soliciting submissions with an express promise of compensation upon selection, coupled with the producer’s selection and public use, can create an implied promise to pay. Conversely, a mere unilateral performance (e.g., a creator uploads a video to a public platform and a broadcaster later uses it) will rarely suffice absent additional communications indicating assent to particular terms.

In practice, courts require more than a plaintiff’s belief that a recipient “should have known” the terms or that the creator expected payment. The complaint must allege tangible facts showing that the recipient knew the terms and intended to be bound by them. Where alleged communications are ambiguous, courts will dismiss. Where the parties’ interactions are mediated by a third‑party platform, moreover, plaintiffs must allege how the platform’s structure, the parties’ use of it, or explicit messages created mutual expectations as to use and payment.

DG & A and similar cases thus set a firm evidentiary floor for implied‑in‑fact claims: proof of an objective agreement, not mere expectation or unilateral conduct.

## Rocky Point and scope of implied terms drawn from conduct and customs

When an implied contract is found to exist, courts often must determine its terms. Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Group, Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269 (4th Dep’t 2002), illustrates the principle that courts may infer or fill in some contractual terms from the parties’ conduct, surrounding circumstances, and established industry practice. But there are important limits. Implied terms can be used to give effect to what the parties manifestly agreed, or to supply reasonable details consistent with their conduct; they cannot be used to fabricate an agreement where no objective assent existed or to create complex economic bargains where the parties left essential terms to future negotiation.

Rocky Point is useful as a guide to when courts will accept implied terms. Where conduct is consistent and continuous — for example, repeated licensing transactions between the same parties, with patterns of payment, scope, and timing — courts are comfortable reading that history into an implied agreement covering subsequent transactions. Where a particular industry has uniform customs that parties reasonably expect to control absent express contrary provision, those customs can inform the content of an implied contract. But the key is that the customs and conduct must be objectively ascertainable and reasonably certain.

This approach recognizes commercial realities: many media and entertainment deals are accomplished through standard forms, oral promises, or customary practices, and the law will enforce those bargains where the evidence shows what was agreed. Yet it also protects parties from having courts impose unbargained‑for obligations based on vague allegations. Therefore, a plaintiff alleging an implied license or implied procedural duty should plead facts showing the consistent practices, prior dealings, or industry norms that supply the missing terms.

## Comparative note: Desny v. Wilder and idea‑submission implied contracts

The idea‑submission line of cases — beginning with Desny v. Wilder, 46 Cal.2d 715 (1956) — addresses a recurring entertainment controversy: when a creator brings an idea to a studio or producer and the producer later exploits that idea, does the submitter have an enforceable right to compensation even without a signed contract? Desny holds that when a defendant solicits ideas or accepts submissions under circumstances that reasonably justify an expectation of payment, an implied promise to pay can be inferred.

Desny has been influential, particularly in California, and courts have distilled elements for an idea‑submission claim: (1) plaintiff disclosed the idea to the defendant under circumstances that create a reasonable expectation of compensation; (2) the idea was novel or of sufficient value; (3) the defendant used the idea and derived benefit; and (4) the defendant’s use was wrongful in light of the expectation of payment. Many courts treat such claims as falling within the realm of implied‑in‑fact contracts because the plaintiff alleges an implied promise to pay arising from the submission context.

But New York courts are more cautious and formalistic. While New York recognizes recovery where a studio solicits materials under an implied promise to pay, courts generally require the same specificity and objective evidence of mutual assent discussed above. New York decisions often scrutinize whether the plaintiff has pleaded facts showing that the recipient in fact solicited or encouraged submissions with an expectation of payment, and whether the parties’ conduct would lead a reasonable person to expect compensation. In short, New York does not reject the idea‑submission theory outright, but it applies its contractual pleading strictures to prevent speculative claims.

A practical upshot for students: when advancing a Desny‑style theory in New York, allege the precise solicitations, the context in which materials were solicited (for example, an open call promising “selected submissions will be paid”), any communications acknowledging receipt and promising compensation, and facts showing the defendant’s actual use and resultant benefit. Without such details, New York courts will treat the claim as an unmoored expectation rather than an enforceable promise.

## Application to Santos: failure to plead specific terms or assent to abide by platform TOS

Santos v. Kimmel provides a contemporary example of the application of these principles to platform‑mediated transactions. The Second Circuit rejected Santos’s claim that an implied‑in‑fact contract arose between him and Kimmel (or the broadcast defendants) that would prevent broadcast of the Cameo videos absent permission or payment. The decision highlights three recurring problems plaintiffs face in this context.

First, the plaintiff was not a party to the Cameo platform’s Terms of Service, and he did not plausibly allege that the parties to the transaction intended to confer enforceable benefits on third parties. When contracts are negotiated by and among platform users and the platform itself, the law presumes that incidental non‑parties are not intended to be third‑party beneficiaries unless the agreement manifests an express declaration to that effect. The Santos opinion notes that the applicable Terms contained explicit third‑party beneficiary clauses in some places and omitted them here — a telling absence under Illinois law for the Terms. The lesson is straightforward: a creator relying on a platform’s terms should plead why those terms, as drafted, were meant to protect him and why the user who uploaded or received content manifested assent to those terms as enforceable against third parties.

Second, Santos’s complaint failed to plead the essential terms of any implied contract with the defendants. The complaint did not identify what specific promises Kimmel made or accepted — there were no allegations that Kimmel agreed to a restricted license, a non‑broadcast covenant, a payment to Santos for further use, or any other concrete obligation. Santos alleged only that he “performed” by making and posting the videos and that Kimmel later aired them. Under Caniglia and New York precedent, that sort of conclusory allegation is insufficient. A court cannot infer from the mere fact of unilateral performance that the recipient agreed to the plaintiff’s preferred terms — especially where the communications occurred through an interaction with a third‑party platform and where the complaint lacks any contemporaneous message or exchange indicating an agreement.

Third, the complaint did not establish a meeting of the minds. Santos argued that the very act of requesting a personalized video through Cameo indicated an understanding about how the video could be used. But the pleadings did not show that Kimmel (or his agents) ever manifested assent to Santos’s expectations. There were no allegations that Kimmel communicated any licensing terms, offered payment beyond the Cameo fee, made promises about limitation of use, or otherwise acted in a manner that could only be rationally explained by acceptance of Santos’s asserted bargain. Performance by Santos — delivering a video — could as easily be interpreted as compliance with the platform’s ordinary purchase flow (a one‑to‑one personalized message for a fee), not as acceptance of a broader permission to restrict dissemination.

Santos also attempted to rely on the notion that a defendant who “instigates” creation cannot later claim fair use or lack of contractual obligation. But even where a defendant solicits content, courts ask whether the solicitation carried an objective promise — for payment or for restricted use — and whether the parties communicated specific terms. A mere anonymous request via a platform, absent messages establishing the requester’s intent to be bound by particular conditions, will not suffice.

Finally, Santos’s pleading problems underscore the practical realities of platform transactions. Many creators using services like Cameo have not bargained for downstream rights or broadcast licenses. Indeed, platform terms often contemplate only a buyer‑recipient relationship, and creators who wish to preserve more restrictive rights must make them explicit in messages or contracts. For a claim that a broadcaster breached a contract by airing platform‑sold content, a plaintiff should allege facts showing (1) that the broadcaster agreed, directly or indirectly, to particular restrictions; (2) what those restrictions were (e.g., no public broadcast, limited noncommercial use only); and (3) how the broadcaster’s conduct violated those specific restrictions.

Practical pleading checklist drawn from Santos and the authorities discussed above
- Identify the parties who actually made the alleged agreement; do not rely on a third‑party platform’s TOS unless you can show the relevant party assented to being bound and the TOS manifest intent to benefit you.
- Plead the essential terms with specificity: scope of the grant or restriction, duration, territory, consideration or payment mechanism, and any relevant conditions or notice requirements.
- Allege objective communications or conduct showing offer and acceptance (emails, text messages, messages on the platform, recorded conversations, or contemporaneous acknowledgments).
- Plead industry custom or prior dealings only where they are concrete and objectively ascertainable; explain how those customs supplied the missing terms.
- If claiming an idea‑submission implied promise (Desny style), plead the solicitation context, any express or implied promise to pay, the novelty and value of the idea, and the defendant’s use and benefit.
- Be prepared to show how unilateral performance (e.g., uploading a video) is not merely compliance with a platform’s ordinary terms but is consistent only with the existence of an enforceable promise.

Conclusion

New York law enforces implied‑in‑fact contracts where the parties’ conduct and the surrounding circumstances objectively demonstrate a meeting of the minds and the complaint alleges the essential terms with specificity. Santos v. Kimmel illustrates how courts apply these principles in the entertainment‑platform context: absent clear allegations of offer, acceptance, specific obligations, or express third‑party beneficiary language, claims that a broadcaster breached an implied agreement by airing platform‑sold content will usually fail at the pleading stage. For creators and practitioners, the case reinforces the importance of creating and preserving objective evidence of the agreement they intend — whether through explicit contractual language, contemporaneous communications, or an industry practice so well established that it can supply missing terms.

# Fraudulent Inducement and the Out‑of‑Pocket Rule

This chapter examines fraudulent inducement under New York law and the distinctive damages rule that often defeats fraud claims where the plaintiff received the agreed consideration. We explain the common‑law tort of fraudulent inducement, develop how New York measures damages for that tort (the “out‑of‑pocket” rule), and then apply these principles to contemporary fact patterns that involve deception by ruse—stings, pseudonymous solicitations, and prank or “gotcha” newsgathering or comedy. The recent Second Circuit decision in Santos v. Kimmel provides a compact illustration: a celebrity alleges he was tricked into producing paid personalized videos, but the court affirmed dismissal because the complaint did not plead any cognizable pecuniary loss.

## Elements of fraudulent inducement under New York law

Under New York law, a claim of fraudulent inducement (commonly called “fraud” or “fraudulent misrepresentation”) has well‑established elements. To state a claim a plaintiff must allege, with the particularity required for fraud claims, the following components:

- a material misrepresentation or omission of fact by the defendant;
- knowledge of the falsity (or reckless disregard for the truth) on the part of the defendant (scienter);
- the defendant’s intent that the plaintiff rely on the misrepresentation;
- reasonable and justifiable reliance by the plaintiff on the misrepresentation; and
- damages proximately caused by that reliance.

These elements track appellate decisions and controlling New York precedent. The “misrepresentation” element ordinarily requires a false statement concerning an existing fact; mere statements of opinion or laudatory puffery will not suffice. Promises about future conduct can support a fraud claim if the plaintiff alleges that the defendant had no present intent to perform (i.e., promissory fraud)—but courts require particularized facts supporting the claim that the promisor intended not to perform at the time of the promise.

Reliance must be reasonable or justifiable under the circumstances. New York applies an objective standard: a plaintiffs’ reliance is not reasonable if the plaintiff knew the truth or if the falsehood was so obviously false that a reasonably prudent person would have investigated further. Finally, damages are not an afterthought in New York fraud claims; a plaintiff must allege actual, pecuniary harm resulting from the reliance. The measure and proof of that pecuniary harm are the focus of the out‑of‑pocket rule discussed next.

Practically speaking for pleadings: because fraud is subject to Rule 9(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, plaintiffs must plead the “who, what, when, where and how” with respect to the alleged misrepresentations, including dates, content, speaker, and the manner in which the misrepresentation induced the plaintiff to act. They must also plead facts supporting scienter and the causal link to damages.

## Lama Holding and Connaughton: out‑of‑pocket loss versus benefit‑of‑the‑bargain

A crucial doctrinal point in New York fraudulent inducement law is the rule that damages for common‑law fraud are measured by the plaintiff’s out‑of‑pocket losses rather than the “benefit‑of‑the‑bargain” or expectation measure.

In Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996), the New York Court of Appeals held that plaintiffs asserting common‑law fraud may recover only their “out‑of‑pocket” losses—i.e., the difference between what they gave and what they received—rather than the difference between the value they were promised and the value actually delivered (the expectation or benefit‑of‑the‑bargain measure). Lama thus narrowed the recoverable damages in fraud actions: a plaintiff who was induced to enter a transaction and actually received what was bargained for generally cannot recover the hypothetical value of what was promised.

Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017), clarified how the out‑of‑pocket rule operates in pleading and proof. Connaughton emphasized that to sustain a fraud claim, a plaintiff must allege a cognizable pecuniary loss—concrete, economic injury caused by the defendant’s misrepresentations. Claims that assert only non‑pecuniary injury (e.g., damage to pride, embarrassment, or reputational harms absent a link to a monetary loss) or claims which allege that the plaintiff “would not have entered the transaction had he known the truth” but nonetheless received the promised consideration, fail to plead the required economic harm. In short, New York requires a tangible, monetary injury to accompany the other elements of fraud.

Two points follow from this doctrine. First, rescission and restitution operate differently from tort damages: a plaintiff who timely elects rescission may be able to recover the return of consideration (a form of restitution). But absent rescission, the tort claim for fraud seeks out‑of‑pocket losses. Second, New York’s limitation means that many complaints asserting deceit in contexts where the plaintiff actually received the contracted good or service are vulnerable to dismissal unless the complaint identifies separate, compensable economic losses (out‑of‑pocket expenditures, consequential losses, lost business opportunities, diminution in value, etc.).

## Stings, pseudonyms, and deception in newsgathering and comedy: injury analysis

Modern media, comedy, and investigative “stings” often involve deception: a reporter or entertainer induces a subject to speak or act under false pretenses. These contexts raise two distinct legal questions: (1) whether the deception establishes the elements of fraud (misrepresentation, scienter, reasonable reliance, etc.), and (2) whether the deception produced a cognizable, pecuniary injury under the out‑of‑pocket rule.

On the first question, courts recognize that deliberate misrepresentations of identity or purpose can satisfy the misrepresentation and scienter elements of fraud. If a defendant uses a pseudonym and knowingly misstates their identity or purpose to induce the plaintiff to act (for example, to secure a paid personalized video, an interview, or access), those facts may satisfy the “who/what/when” requirements of a fraud pleading—so long as the complaint alleges that the misrepresentation was material and intended to deceive.

But the second question is decisive in many modern cases. Courts routinely distinguish between economic and non‑economic harms. Deceptions that lead only to embarrassment, reputational damage, or moral injury, without proximate, pecuniary consequences, do not satisfy New York’s damages requirement for common‑law fraud. Frequently in “sting” or prank contexts the alleged injury is humiliation when the recorded content is later broadcast or mocked. That humiliation may support other claims in narrow circumstances (invasion of privacy, intentional infliction of emotional distress, or state privacy statutes), but it is not, by itself, the pecuniary loss New York requires for a fraud claim.

A related point concerns transactions in which the subject is paid for the requested performance. If a subject is paid the full agreed fee for their performance (for example, a paid Cameo request), the out‑of‑pocket rule generally forecloses fraud damages unless the plaintiff pleads additional, monetary harm caused by the deception—expenses undertaken because of the deception, loss of business due to the misuse, lost endorsement opportunities, diminution in the value of the plaintiff’s intellectual property, or similar measurable losses. These consequential losses must be pleaded with specificity and a clear causal link to the defendant’s misrepresentation.

First Amendment considerations also often arise in these contexts (especially when the defendant is a media organization or entertainer). While the First Amendment does not change the elements of fraud or the out‑of‑pocket requirement, it colors judicial fact‑finding about motive, public‑interest defenses, and whether a defendant’s use of the plaintiff’s content is privileged (e.g., parody, commentary, or fair use in a copyright case). But even if First Amendment doctrines justify the publication, they do not eliminate the statutory pleading requirements for a fraud tort, nor do they alter the need for a pecuniary injury for fraud damages in New York.

## What qualifies as cognizable pecuniary loss and how to plead it

Because the availability of damages frequently decides the viability of a fraud claim, plaintiffs must understand what counts as a cognizable pecuniary loss in New York and how to plead those losses.

1. Typical forms of cognizable pecuniary loss
- Out‑of‑pocket monetary expenditures made in reliance on the misrepresentation (for example, payments made to the defendant, costs incurred to prepare and perform services, travel expenses, fees paid to agents, and replacement costs).
- Consequential economic losses proximately caused by the misrepresentation, such as lost profits, lost business opportunities, or diminution in the value of the plaintiff’s property or goodwill—if those are alleged with particularity and causation is plausibly pleaded.
- Restitutionary recovery (return of consideration) where rescission is sought and permitted; restitution is a remedy distinct from tort damages and may entitle a plaintiff to the return of monies paid or the value of services rendered in some circumstances.
- In limited cases, a decline in market value (e.g., loss of licensing opportunities, reduced fees for future performances) that is foreseeable and causally connected to the deception.

2. What does not ordinarily qualify
- Mere embarrassment, humiliation, mental anguish, or reputational harm unconnected to an identifiable monetary loss are not recoverable as damages for common‑law fraud in New York.
- The “benefit‑of‑the‑bargain” expectation loss (the subjective value of what the plaintiff hoped to obtain) is not recoverable in a tort fraud action absent an alternative basis (e.g., contract damages or a statutory scheme that provides a different measure).

3. Pleading practice and particularity (what plaintiffs must allege)
- Specific dollar amounts: pleaders should specify amounts paid, expenses incurred, or lost profits claimed. General assertions of “millions lost” are insufficient without factual underpinning.
- Causation and timing: describe the chain of causation showing how the defendant’s misrepresentation directly produced the monetary loss (for example, “as a direct result of D’s misrepresentation, P incurred X in travel expenses and forfeited Y in scheduled paid appearances”).
- Reasonableness of reliance and foreseeability: allege facts supporting the reasonableness of the plaintiff’s reliance and the foreseeability of the claimed losses.
- Particularity as to fraud: under Rule 9(b), allege with particularity the content of the misrepresentation, who made it, when and where it was made, and the means by which it induced the plaintiff to act.

4. Examples of successful pleadings (illustrative)
- A plaintiff induced to sell stock on the basis of false financial statements who alleges specific dollar losses (price at which sold versus true value) and documents showing reliance.
- A performer who alleges that because of a defendant’s fraudulent representations they performed free of charge and incurred quantifiable expenses (travel, time‑based fees) and lost specific subsequent bookings directly traceable to the broadcast use.
- A business that alleges, with invoices and contractual documentation, that because of a defendant’s false statements it made specific expenditures it would not otherwise have made, and those expenditures were wasted.

In short, to survive a motion to dismiss a fraudulent inducement claim in New York, plaintiffs must do more than allege that they were tricked: they must allege concrete, pecuniary harm with specificity and show it was proximately caused by the defendant’s deception.

## Application to Santos: no out‑of‑pocket loss where plaintiff was paid for requested work

Santos v. Kimmel applies these principles in a contemporary media setting. The operative facts, as alleged, are straightforward: Jimmy Kimmel (using pseudonyms) submitted requests through the Cameo platform that solicited personalized videos from George Santos; Santos produced the videos and was paid for them; Kimmel then broadcast some of the videos on his late‑night program as part of a series mocking Santos. Santos alleged that Kimmel’s false representations induced him to create the videos under false pretenses, and he asserted a common‑law fraudulent inducement claim under New York law.

The Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege a cognizable out‑of‑pocket loss. Applying Lama and Connaughton, the court explained that New York requires the pleading of an actual pecuniary loss and that Sanchez’s complaint did not allege any such loss. Critically, Santos was paid for each video—the core transaction was completed, and the complaint did not identify any out‑of‑pocket expenditures, consequential monetary loss, or diminution in value that could be ascribed to Kimmel’s deception. Although the complaint alleged deception and a resulting humiliation when the videos were aired and mocked, humiliation and reputational injury are non‑pecuniary harms and, without an accompanying monetary loss, do not sustain a common‑law fraud claim in New York.

The opinion illustrates several practical points for plaintiffs and practitioners:

- If a defendant obtains work or performance from the plaintiff but pays the agreed fee, the plaintiff cannot rely on a bare claim of deception and embarrassment to recover fraud damages. They must instead allege specific monetary losses caused by the deception (for example, if the broadcast caused cancellation of future paid engagements, with invoices or booking records showing such losses).
- Where plaintiffs seek restitution (return of the payment), they must plead and timely pursue relief in that form (rescission or restitutionary equitable relief), which differs from compensatory tort damages. Santos did not plead an effective rescission claim that would have justified a restitution remedy.
- Rule 9(b) imposes particularity requirements for fraud allegations; in contexts like stings where covert recordings and pseudonymous communications are used, a well‑pleaded complaint should attach or describe the communications and specify dates, methods of contact, and amounts paid to demonstrate both the misrepresentation and any consequent out‑of‑pocket loss.

Finally, the Santos decision underscores the policy behind New York’s out‑of‑the‑pocket rule: tort damages are intended to redress actual economic losses caused by deceit, not to provide a windfall or to substitute tort recovery for disappointments unaccompanied by economic harm. In a media age in which people are often induced to participate in recorded performances that may later be used for satire or criticism, the out‑of‑pocket rule limits recovery in fraud to genuine pecuniary injuries—unless the plaintiff can plead (and ultimately prove) a monetary nexus between the deception and concrete economic harm.

Conclusion — doctrinal takeaway for practice and litigation strategy

When evaluating or drafting potential fraudulent inducement claims under New York law, remember the following checklist:

- Allegations: plead the false statement(s) of fact, identify the speaker(s), and state when and where the misrepresentations were made. Show scienter with particular facts.
- Reliance: plead facts demonstrating that reliance was reasonable and that the plaintiff actually relied on the misrepresentation.
- Damages: plead an identifiable, concrete pecuniary loss—amounts paid, expenses incurred, lost profits, lost opportunities, diminution in value, or restitution sought via rescission. Specify amounts and causal links.
- Rule 9(b): plead with the particularity Rule 9(b) requires for fraud claims.
- Remedies choice: consider whether rescission/restitution is more appropriate than tort damages where the plaintiff seeks return of consideration.
- Alternative claims: when pecuniary loss is absent or hard to allege, consider other causes of action (privacy torts, defamation, contractual claims where a party has enforceable contractual rights, or statutory claims) that may address non‑pecuniary injuries or provide different remedies.

Santos v. Kimmel shows how the out‑of‑pocket rule operates in media and entertainment contexts: deception alone is insufficient in New York to convert non‑pecuniary injury into recoverable tort damages; without tangible economic loss, the fraudulent inducement claim will likely be dismissed.

# Ethics, Deception, and Fair Use: Beyond Doctrine

This chapter uses Santos v. Kimmel as a starting point to explore the uneasy intersection of a court’s legal analysis of fair use and the ethical questions that surround deceptive practices used to obtain material for commentary. Santos involved a familiar modern fact pattern: a late‑night television host, posing under fictitious names, obtained short personalized videos from the former Congressman George Santos through a commercial platform (Cameo) and then broadcast those videos in a recurring segment that mocked Santos. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of Santos’s copyright and state‑law claims, holding that the broadcast use was a paradigmatic fair use and that the state law claims failed for independent reasons. The court’s opinion, and the district court’s reasoning that it adopts, illuminate several doctrinal points about how courts treat deception in fair use cases. But the opinions also highlight the limits of legal analysis: conduct that a court deems legally protected may still be ethically fraught, reputationally costly, and risky in other legal fora.

The goal of this chapter is twofold. First, it explains, with attention to the controlling precedents, what role — if any — deception and bad faith play in the fair use analysis and in related equitable defenses. Second, it steps beyond doctrinal black‑letter to give practical guidance to journalists, comedians, producers, and media organizations about risk management and ethical decisionmaking when deceptive tactics are contemplated as a means to obtain material for commentary.

## Harper & Row’s good‑faith dictum revisited by Campbell and Google v. Oracle

Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises (1985) contains the oft‑quoted statement that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” — language that many courts and litigants have seized upon as though bad faith automatically dooms a fair use defense. In Harper & Row the Nation magazine published excerpts of President Ford’s unpublished memoirs after acquiring them from a source who had been given advance access. The Supreme Court emphasized the Nation’s purposeful appropriation of verbatim, unpublished material that directly supplanted an exclusive right the copyright holder intended to exploit. In that context, Harper & Row’s remark about good faith served to underscore the wrongfulness of Nation’s conduct in connection with the other fair use factors, particularly the fourth (market effect), and the third (amount and substantiality of the portion used).

But the subsequent doctrinal course has been to resist an absolutist reading of Harper & Row’s dictum. Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc. (1994), while not expressly overruling Harper & Row, made clear that bad faith is not a categorical bar to fair use. Campbell emphasized that whether a use is transformative and whether it usurps the copyrighted work’s market are the primary concerns; the Court expressed skepticism that purpose and subjective intent should be determinative. More recently, the Supreme Court in Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc. (2021) described that skepticism as “justifiable,” observing that copyright is not a privilege reserved for the “well‑behaved.” Campbell and Google therefore instruct that bad faith can be a relevant factual circumstance to be considered in the fair use balance, but it is not an independent legal trump card that automatically defeats the defense.

Santos v. Kimmel is a concrete illustration of those principles in application. Santos argued that Kimmel’s use of deceptive identities on Cameo negated fair use because it showed bad faith. The Second Circuit declined to give that contention dispositive weight. The court explained that the relevant inquiry looks to the reasonable observer’s appraisal of the purpose and character of the secondary use and whether that secondary use is transformative — not to the subjective intent of the secondary user or the creator. The court also observed that Santos’s own complaint described the videos he produced as conveying messages of “hope” and “encouragement,” whereas the JKL segments plainly used the materials for satirical commentary — a form of expressive activity historically favored as transformative. In short, the Second Circuit followed Campbell and Google: bad faith is a contextual factor, not an independent categorical bar, and transformation and market effect remain the central concerns.

For students asked to apply these precedents to new facts, the practical takeaway is this: when faced with deceptive procurement of copyrighted material, analyze bad faith as a factual input that may influence the first and fourth fair use factors (purpose/transformativeness and market effect), but do not treat bad faith as dispositive without regard to transformation and substitution. If the secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or message (i.e., is transformative) and does not function as a market substitute for the original, courts are reluctant to allow the fact of deception to outweigh those considerations.

## Unclean hands and equitable defenses in copyright cases

Fair use is an affirmative defense grounded in statute and doctrine; it is not an equitable doctrine like laches or unclean hands per se. Nonetheless, equitable doctrines sometimes appear in the copyright context — particularly when the remedy sought is equitable (for example, injunctive relief). Courts have long recognized that equitable defenses, such as unclean hands, may bar equitable remedies where the plaintiff’s own misconduct is intimately related to the claim. Conversely, unclean hands and related equitable defenses generally do not override statutory rights to monetary relief when the legislature has provided such remedies; courts will not lightly refuse to enforce statutory rights because of unrelated wrongdoing by the rights holder.

The relevance of unclean hands or other equitable defenses to a defendant’s deception depends on how closely tied the plaintiff’s asserted harm is to the defendant’s misconduct and the nature of the relief requested. If a defendant seeks only to avoid an injunction and is willing to pay damages, a court may be more inclined to consider equitable defenses in denying prospective relief. But for that defense to succeed, the plaintiff’s misconduct must be directly related to the controversy and must have offended the conscience of the court. Unclean hands is not a catchall that absolves wrongful copying; it is a narrow equitable weapon.

In Santos, the defendants were not seeking equitable relief to restrain Santos from doing something; they were defending against a statutory claim for infringement. The court therefore analyzed fair use directly under the statutory factors. The district court and the Second Circuit viewed the deceptive procurement as a factor to be weighed mainly in the purpose-and-character inquiry; they did not treat it as grounds for applying equitable doctrines against Santos. That aligns with the broader trend: courts will consider deceitful conduct in the fair use calculus but rarely invoke unclean hands to nullify statutory remedies unless there is an affirmative, direct equitable relationship that makes such a defense appropriate.

Students should remember how remedy choice affects defenses. If a defendant seeks a declaration or an injunction (equitable relief), the plaintiff’s inequitable conduct might defeat that relief. But in ordinary infringement suits seeking damages, equitable defenses like unclean hands are seldom dispositive. Moreover, even if unclean hands were invoked by a copyright plaintiff, it would not automatically excuse what otherwise is an infringing use where the statutory factors do not favor the defendant.

## Journalistic and comedic ethics versus legal permissibility

Law and ethics are distinct inquiries. A courtroom determines whether conduct is legally protected or actionable; a profession, newsroom, or comedy show must also ask whether the conduct comports with ethical obligations, standards of professional behavior, and institutional norms. Santos v. Kimmel epitomizes the disjunction: Kimmel’s use of fictitious identities to secure Cameo messages may be legally defensible as fair use, but many observers will instinctively view the tactic as unethical or at least morally dubious.

Journalistic ethics codes (for example, the Society of Professional Journalists’ Code of Ethics) counsel against misrepresentation, urging reporters to be honest about their identity and purpose except in rare circumstances when the public interest overwhelmingly justifies deception and no alternative means exist. The rationale is threefold: misrepresentation can harm subjects, erode public trust in the press, and create slippery slopes that broaden the acceptability of deceit. Comedic practice, by contrast, often embraces provocation, satire, and undercover stunts as legitimate tools of social comment. The tension is most acute where comedy borrows commonly journalistic devices to expose wrongdoing or hypocrisy. The difference, therefore, is largely normative — what our institutions ought to do — rather than legal.

Practically, the distinction matters for two reasons. First, audience perception and reputation are central to a media entity’s business model. If viewers perceive that a program routinely misrepresents its methods to produce humiliation, the program may lose credibility and advertisers, even if courts would permit such methods. Second, some deceptive tactics may produce legal exposure outside of copyright: trespass laws, recording statutes, fraud, or breach of contract claims may arise depending on jurisdiction and facts. Santos tried to bring state causes of action (breach of contract, fraudulent inducement) and the state law path can sometimes be the more promising route for a plaintiff harmed by deception. In Santos, the state law claims failed on pleading and preemption grounds. That outcome, however, is fact- and jurisdiction‑specific; other plaintiffs in other circumstances might succeed under state tort or contract theories.

Ethical analysis also requires balancing competing values, such as the public’s right to know versus individual privacy and dignity. If the commentary exposes criminal behavior, systemic corruption, or matters of significant public concern, an ethical justification for deception may be stronger. If the stunt targets private failings or aims primarily to humiliate for entertainment, ethical justifications are much weaker. These normative judgments will not always align with legal outcomes, but they should inform editorial decision‑making by professionals.

## Risk management for media entities: reputation, audience trust, and litigation exposure

Risk management for media institutions must treat legal defensibility and ethical acceptability as overlapping but distinct risks. A defensible fair use argument is necessary but not sufficient; reputational injury and the prospect of costly litigation — even if ultimately unsuccessful — often motivate organizations to avoid certain tactics.

First, reputation and audience trust are intangible but real assets. Losing them can inflict immediate and long‑term harm: dropping viewership, advertiser withdrawal, and difficulty attracting talent. Before authorizing deceptive procurement, editorial leadership should conduct a reputational assessment weighing the likely editorial gain against the foreseeable backlash. This includes considering how the target’s identity, the subject matter, the potential victims (e.g., vulnerable persons), and the distribution context will shape public response.

Second, litigation exposure goes beyond copyright. Probable areas of legal risk include state claims for fraud, negligent misrepresentation, breach of contract (especially if the deceptive conduct involves misrepresenting assent to a platform’s terms), invasion of privacy, intentional infliction of emotional distress, and, in narrow circumstances, criminal statutes depending on the method used (for example, impersonation or wire fraud). Even if fair use will likely defeat a copyright claim, defending against parallel state claims — or even a preliminary injunction application — can be expensive and distracting. Organizations should budget for pre‑publication legal review when deception is contemplated, and consider whether insurance policies cover the relevant exposures.

Third, internal processes can substantially reduce risk. That includes establishing clear approval protocols for stunts and undercover approaches that require senior editorial and legal signoff, documenting the decisionmaking process, and creating fallback plans. If a segment is intended to be satirical commentary, producers should ensure the final product clearly bears a critical or transformative message that will be obvious to a reasonable observer; this is both a fair use consideration and a reputational safeguard.

Finally, swift post‑publication response strategies are crucial. If a segment provokes backlash or litigation, transparent explanation of editorial intent, prompt correction of factual errors, expressions of regret when appropriate, and offers of remediation can mitigate reputational damage. Litigation is often as much about public perception as it is about legal rights; a thoughtful communications strategy should be part of the risk plan.

## Guidelines for responsible use of pranks and stings in creative commentary

Where an organization chooses to use pranks, stings, or deception in service of commentary, a disciplined framework helps align ethical standards with legal defenses and risk management. The following guidelines distill best practices that flow from both ethical norms and the practical realities courts have articulated about the role of deception in fair use.

First, establish and document a strong editorial justification. Deception should be used only when it meaningfully advances legitimate public interest objectives — exposing wrongdoing, illustrating hypocrisy, or revealing a matter of political or social importance — and when non‑deceptive alternatives are inadequate. The stronger the public interest, the more ethically justifiable the tactic.

Second, favor transparency in editorial framing. When the resulting content is published or broadcast, editors should make clear the purpose of the segment and how the material was obtained, to the extent that disclosure does not vitiate the investigative value. Not only does this practice foster audience trust, but it also helps courts evaluate purpose and transformation by making the critical context explicit.

Third, avoid means that create new, actionable legal risks. Do not engage in trespass, hacking, or interception of communications that violate statutes. Be cautious about impersonation where state law criminalizes the conduct. Check platform terms of service and consider the legal implications of creating false accounts; while breach of a platform’s terms is not always a cognizable private right of action, it can be relevant evidence and may trigger contractual or tort liability in some jurisdictions.

Fourth, limit the scope of humiliation and harm. Even when the subject is a public figure, calibrate the content to avoid gratuitous personal attacks or disclosure of private information that has no bearing on the public interest. Avoid repeated use of a tactic that systematically harms particular individuals or vulnerable populations. This consideration is important ethically and can affect market harm and public reaction.

Fifth, document consent and preservation of evidence. If the plan contemplates later public use of material, preserve transcripts, unedited recordings, and contemporaneous editorial logs that explain the decisionmaking process. If consent is obtained after the fact, preserve proof. Those records are invaluable both for internal review and in litigation.

Sixth, conduct a legal pre‑publication review that considers likely claims beyond copyright — privacy, contract, fraud, and publicity rights — and estimates the litigation exposure and potential damages. If legal counsel advises that certain aspects create high risk, consider alternatives or modifications.

Seventh, adopt remedial and restorative practices. If a segment causes identifiable harm, be prepared to retract, apologize, or redact offending content. Offer opportunities to subjects to respond on the same platform. These steps mitigate reputational damage and may reduce the incentive to litigate.

Eighth, create and enforce a proportionality test for stunts. The potential social or editorial benefit must be proportional to the intrusion entailed by the deception. Assign senior editors the authority to veto stunts that fail the proportionality test.

Applying these guidelines to the Santos context: a producer who contemplated posing under false names on Cameo should have documented the editorial imperative for doing so; assessed whether Santos’s statements were of public interest and whether equivalent commentary could be drawn from publicly available statements; considered obtaining post‑hoc consent or offering Santos an opportunity to respond on air; and prepared for the reputational and legal fallout. The Second Circuit’s legal conclusion in Santos does not diminish the value of these precautions.

## Applying doctrine to hypotheticals: when deception will matter in fair use

To help students synthesize the doctrinal rules, consider two hypotheticals that show when deceptive procurement of copyrighted material is more or less likely to influence the fair use inquiry.

Hypothetical A: A documentary filmmaker covertly records a corrupt corporation’s senior executive admitting to illegal conduct during an off‑the‑record meeting. The filmmaker uses the recording in an exposé that contextualizes the admission, adds analysis, and changes the communicative purpose of the raw material. The use is transformative, the public interest is substantial, and the secondary use is unlikely to be a market substitute for any plausible commercial market in the audio clips. Though the procurement involved deceit, courts following Campbell and Google will likely treat that deceit as a contextual fact but not an override of fair use. Deception strengthens the public interest case ethically (arguably) and does not likely create market harm.

Hypothetical B: A comedy troupe uses false pretexts to obtain personalized greeting videos from a set of independent performers on a platform where such videos are monetized. The troupe then compiles the videos and packages them, minimally edited, as a commercial special that functions as an entertaining substitute for the original personalized videos. Here, the plaintiff can plausibly show market substitution: consumers could conceivably prefer the troupe’s compilation instead of ordering their own custom greetings. Deceptive procurement aimed at securing the precise commercial content that the musicians or creators intended to sell is more likely to produce adverse conclusions on the third and fourth fair use factors. Bad faith in this context is relevant because it aligns with a nontransformative use and with a risk of usurping a licensing market.

The difference between these hypotheticals illustrates the analytic pathway courts follow: examine transformation first — does the secondary use add new expression or message? — and then evaluate whether the use supplants the original’s market or potential licensing markets. Deception enters the analysis as a factor that informs both inquiries, but its significance depends on its relation to transformation and market substitution.

## Conclusion: law permits what ethics may condemn

Santos v. Kimmel illustrates a current, pragmatic reality of media law: courts increasingly view deceptive procurement as one among many contextual facts that can be outweighed by a strong transformative purpose and an absence of market substitution. Harper & Row’s admonition about “good faith” survives as a caution, but Campbell and Google v. Oracle make clear that bad faith is not a per se bar to fair use. Unclean hands and other equitable defenses remain available in narrow circumstances, primarily as limitations on equitable relief.

Yet the legal permissibility recognized in Santos does not eliminate ethical concerns or other legal exposures. Journalists, comedians, and media organizations should not equate a likely successful fair use defense with imprimatur to employ any tactic. Decisionmakers should assess editorial value, public interest, potential legal claims beyond copyright, reputational consequences, and proportionality before deploying deception. Clear editorial policies, legal review, and post‑publication remediation plans are prudent means to balance the creative and critical ambitions that fair use protects against the moral and practical costs that deception may impose.

For law students, applying these lessons requires careful, balanced analysis: identify the transformative character of the secondary use; evaluate market substitution risk; treat evidence of deceptive intent as a contextual factor that may influence those inquiries; and remember that remedies and defenses will vary depending on whether the dispute is statutory, tort‑based, or equitable. The law gives breathing space for robust commentary, satire, and investigation. Ethical judgment determines how that space ought to be used.

# Comparative Case Law and Adjacent Doctrines

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel sits at the crossroads of several strands of fair use and related doctrine: appropriation art and transformative use, news reporting and full‑work copying, searchability and snippets, and the special problems posed by functional broadcast‑monitoring services. This chapter situates Santos within that case law, explains how courts weigh the statutory fair‑use factors in these contexts, and sketches the ways in which non‑copyright claims (contract, fraud, and the right of publicity) interact with—and sometimes are displaced by—the Copyright Act. Throughout, the goal is doctrinal clarity: to give students a usable map for applying these precedents to new fact patterns.

## Swatch v. Bloomberg: news reporting, complete copying, and market effects

Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P. is frequently cited for the proposition that complete copying is not automatically fatal to a fair‑use defense when the secondary use serves a news‑reporting function and does not usurp a licensing market. In Swatch, Bloomberg ran an article about Swatch that included photographs of watches. Bloomberg’s republication of the images in full was found to be fair use in light of the purpose of the use and the lack of a plausible licensing market for the precise use Bloomberg made.

What Swatch teaches about the first and fourth fair‑use factors is especially relevant to Santos. The first factor—purpose and character—puts a heavy thumb on the scale when the secondary use is commentary, criticism, or news reporting. The court evaluates whether the secondary use is “transformative,” meaning it adds a new purpose, message, or context that differs from the original. Swatch recognized that news reporting often repurposes copyrighted material to inform the public and that such uses can be transformative even when they incorporate entire works.

But Swatch also illustrates the limits of a form‑over‑substance approach. The fourth factor—market effect—asks whether the secondary use usurps a likely market for licensing the copyrighted work. Swatch found no plausible market harm because news reporting of the sort Bloomberg did was not a market for which copyright holders typically license images; there was little risk Bloomberg’s use would supplant an existing or potential market for Swatch’s images. Courts thus distinguish “devaluation” (where the secondary work diminishes the value of the original by disparaging it) from “usurpation” (where the secondary work is a close market substitute). Swatch was a classic example where complete copying did not translate into market usurpation.

Santos parallels Swatch: Kimmel aired Santos’s cameo videos in full as part of a televised segment that mocked their content and the circumstances in which they were created. The Second Circuit relied on the same structural reasoning as Swatch—transformative commentary and the absence of a plausible licensing market for the exact expressive use Kimmel made—to conclude that complete copying of the videos could be fair use. The takeaway for students is that wholesale copying in a media context can be fair when the secondary user’s purpose (reportage or commentary) is different in kind from the original and when no competing derivative market is plausibly being replaced by the secondary use.

## Authors Guild v. Google Books: searchability, snippet view, and transformativeness

Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., decided by the Second Circuit in 2015, is the paradigmatic case for a very different category of reuse: indexing and search. Google’s Library Project scanned millions of books and made “snippets”—small excerpts—available through search results. The court held that Google’s use was a transformative one because it created a new, useful function (searchability and access) that did not exploit the expressive content of the books in the way that the market for books normally does.

Google Books sharpens three lessons about fair use:

- Transformative purpose may derive from providing information services rather than commentary. Courts will recognize a transformation when the secondary use performs a new informational or functional role, such as enabling discovery or search, even if the underlying copyrighted text is not itself altered.

- The amount and substantiality inquiry is contextual. Small snippets reviewed in search results weigh in favor of fair use because they are proportionate to the new function (discoverability) and because they do not reveal the “heart” of a work in a way that substitutes for reading the book.

- Market effect analysis focuses on whether the secondary use is a substitute for the original or for a market for derivatives. Google Books’ snippet view was not a substitute for buying or licensing full books; indeed, it arguably enhanced book sales and scholarship by making works discoverable.

Compare Google Books to news programs and entertainment shows. The Google Books paradigm is useful where the secondary use is functional, informational, or infrastructural—search, indexing, or archiving. But the court in Santos did not rely on a Google‑Books style analysis because Kimmel’s use was expressive commentary, not an indexing function. Nonetheless, the Google case is instructive as a reminder that “transformative” covers more than parody and criticism; it also embraces new utilitarian functions.

## TCA Television v. McCollum and Cariou v. Prince: early‑stage resolution and visual‑art transformations

Two strands of Second Circuit authority are helpful when litigation touches appropriation art and when courts face the question whether fair use can be resolved before discovery.

TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum and related cases demonstrate that fair use can be decided at an early stage—sometimes even on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion or on summary judgment—when the complaint and materials subject to judicial notice make the necessary facts plain. The key is whether, viewing the complaint and incorporated materials in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, the use is so clearly transformative and the market‑effect considerations so weak that no reasonable jury could find infringement. Santos follows that approach: because Santos’s complaint itself described the original videos’ expressive content and the defendants’ contrasting purpose (satirical commentary), and because Santos’s own copyright registrations were before the court, the Second Circuit concluded that fair use was resolvable on the pleadings.

Cariou v. Prince offers a model for handling visual‑arts appropriation. There, a photographer sued an artist who had used and altered photographs. The Second Circuit announced a pragmatic, picture‑by‑picture assessment of transformativeness: a work is transformative when it “add[s] new expression, meaning, or message” to the original. Cariou reversed the district court’s finding for many of the appropriation pieces, remanding for a more nuanced, context‑sensitive analysis. Cariou showed that courts must compare the original and the secondary artwork to determine whether the latter has a different aesthetic or communicative purpose.

Santos’ reliance on these precedents is twofold. First, the case exemplifies the propriety of early dismissal when the pleadings and publicly available registrations make the outcome inevitable. Second, like Cariou, Santos confirms the visual‑comparative inquiry: a reasonable observer’s perception of what the original work communicated compared to the new context created by the secondary use is central to the transformativeness inquiry. In Santos the court emphasized that transformation is assessed from the reasonable observer’s perspective—not on the subjective intent of the copyright holder or of the secondary user—a point sharpened by recent Supreme Court decisions.

## TVEyes and broadcast monitoring cases: functional uses and limits

Broadcast monitoring and clipping services present a distinct challenge. These services typically provide subscribers with recorded broadcasts, searchable clips, and alerts; the use is often commercial and may substitute for obtaining licensed clips from a broadcaster. Courts have been wary of treating such functional, market‑serving services as fair uses when the secondary use effectively fills the market role of licensing or distribution—especially when the defendant operates a subscription business.

The central doctrinal pivot is functionality. If the secondary use is essentially utilitarian—indexing broadcasts, delivering verbatim clips to customers for their monitoring or competitive needs—courts ask whether the service supplants a licensing market or otherwise harms the owners’ revenue alternatives. The more a use is a mere vehicle for duplication and sale or subscription income, the less likely it is to be transformative in the expressive sense that favors fair use.

This functional/market analysis helps explain why courts treat entertainment‑oriented reuses (parody, critique, news commentary) differently from monitoring services. Santos falls on the expressive side of this divide: Kimmel’s program used the clips as material for commentary in a mass‑audience entertainment format, not as a subscription service that simply furnished raw clips to paying customers. Thus the broadcast‑monitoring cases operate as cautionary precedents—warning courts not to conflate all broadcast copying with fair use—rather than as direct impediments to expressive repurposing.

When confronting a fact pattern involving broadcast clips, ask first: is the secondary use primarily expressive/commentary or primarily functional/commercial? If the latter, market‑substitution concerns will loom larger. If the former, transformative purpose and a lack of a licensing market for the expressive use will be likely to tip the balance in favor of fair use.

## Parody lineage: Campbell and its progeny; satire and commentary in modern media

Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc. is the lodestar of parody‑related fair use doctrine. The Supreme Court held that a commercial parody—2 Live Crew’s version of “Oh, Pretty Woman”—could be fair use. Campbell instructs courts to focus first on transformativeness: does the secondary work use the original to comment on or criticize that original (parody), or does it merely use the original as a vehicle to comment on something else (satire)? The Court noted that parodies that mimic an original to make a critical point about it are more likely to be transformative, and it also observed that market harm from parodies is less likely because license markets do not typically cover critical distortions of originals.

Two connected principles run through the lineage from Campbell to the present:

- The reasonable‑observer test for purpose: courts evaluate whether the secondary use conveys a different message or purpose to viewers or readers. The test is objective; subjective intent matters less than public perception.

- The reduced weight of market‑harm concerns for critical works: where the secondary use is criticism or parody, the market‑harm factor often counts for less because licensing markets do not customarily include rights to lampoon an original.

Santos’ “Will Santos Say It?” segments fall within Campbell’s orbit but track modern nuances. Kimmel used Santos’s cameo videos to ridicule and comment on Santos’s public persona and the peculiar commercial arrangement (paid cameos). Santos argued he created the videos for inspirational messaging; Kimmel repurposed them for satire. Under Campbell and later cases, courts will find a transformative purpose where the secondary user’s new message is readily discernible to a reasonable observer—which is precisely the Second Circuit’s reasoning in Santos.

Important doctrinal distinctions remain. Satire directed at society, not the original, is less likely to be transformative in the Campbell sense because it does not rely on the original to make its critical point; instead it uses the original instrumentally. Courts view such uses with more skepticism as to fair use. In practice, modern media often blurs the line: late‑night segments that lampoon public figures generally use originals in service of social commentary about the figures themselves—and so have been treated as transformative.

Finally, Harper & Row’s old language about “good faith” has been deemphasized by later decisions. Although bad faith can in some contexts be relevant to the first factor, the Supreme Court and the Second Circuit have cast doubt on giving good or bad faith dispositive weight in the fair use calculus. Santos invoked Harper & Row’s formulation, but the Second Circuit treated the defendants’ deceptive procurement of the videos as immaterial to the fundamental transformativeness and market‑effect inquiries.

## Right of publicity overview and preemption intersections (brief note)

Copyright is not the only legal regime that protects certain exploitations of a person’s likeness or creative work. The right of publicity—an aspect of state law—permits a person to control commercial exploitation of her name, image, or persona. But when a plaintiff asserts state law claims that depend on copying expressive works, § 301 of the Copyright Act may preempt those claims if they are “equivalent” to the exclusive rights within copyright and do not contain extra elements that make them qualitatively different.

Two basic preemption principles are worth stressing:

- If a state‑law claim protects rights equivalent to copyright—i.e., it seeks to prevent unauthorized reproduction, distribution, or display of a work fixed in a tangible medium—it is likely preempted unless it contains an element that is qualitatively beyond mere copyright protection.

- Right‑of‑publicity claims often survive preemption because they require an extra element—use of identity for commercial exploitation—that is distinct from copyright’s bundle of exclusive rights. Nonetheless, survival in the preemption sense does not guarantee success on the merits: First Amendment defenses, newsworthiness exceptions, and other limitations may bar liability.

Santos did not prevail on his state law breach and fraud claims, and the Second Circuit disposed of them on ordinary contract and tort pleading grounds without resolving the preemption question. But the opinion underscores the practical point that plaintiffs who seek to litigate identity‑based harms arising from expressive use must craft causes of action that contain elements beyond simple copying and must be prepared to confront First Amendment defenses and newsworthiness exceptions. In the television‑broadcast context, courts often balance the publicity interests of speakers and public figures against a plaintiff’s right to control commercial exploitation of identity.

## Practical synthesis and applying the precedents

Putting the doctrinal pieces together yields a practical approach when faced with secondary use of expressive works—particularly in media contexts:

1. Identify the nature of the secondary use. Is it expressive commentary/parody/news reporting, or is it functional/indexing/commercial redistribution? Expressive uses favor the first factor; functional uses raise market‑substitution concerns.

2. Ask what a reasonable observer would perceive as the new work’s purpose. The inquiry is objective and focuses on the new message, context, or function—not merely the defendant’s subjective intent. Cases from Cariou through Andy Warhol and Santos emphasize this point.

3. Compare the works. For visual or audiovisual materials, courts perform a side‑by‑side comparison to assess whether the secondary work adds new expression, meaning, or message. Partial or full copying does not decide the matter in isolation—context and purpose do.

4. Consider the amount used in context. Even wholesale copying can be fair if it is appropriate to the secondary purpose and does not reveal or substitute for the heart of the original in a way that harms legitimate markets.

5. Evaluate market effect narrowly: ask whether the secondary use is a likely substitute for the original or for a derivative market that the copyright holder would reasonably enter. News reporting and parody often will not be such substitutes. Functional monitoring services, however, are more likely to be seen as market substitutes.

6. Remember that fair use can be resolved at an early stage when the complaint and incorporated materials make the analysis straightforward. TCA, Cariou, and Santos illustrate that judges need not wait for discovery where the operative facts relevant to fair use are apparent.

7. If a plaintiff brings state‑law claims alongside copyright, parse those claims for extra elements that could avoid preemption; but be ready for First Amendment and newsworthiness defenses. Contract and fraud claims will often founder if they are not pleaded with precise factual and legal elements beyond mere copying.

Santos v. Kimmel thus fits squarely into a developing jurisprudence that treats expressive transformation—whether in the form of parody, satire, news commentary, or informative repurposing—as a powerful defense to copyright claims, provided the secondary use does not simply substitute for the original in a relevant market. At the same time, the case reminds litigants and courts to respect the line between expressive reuse and functional commercial replication: when the secondary use is primarily a marketplace substitute (indexing, clipping for customers, or raw redistribution), fair use is a harder sell.

# Significance, Strategy, and Future Litigation

This chapter draws out the doctrinal lessons and practical consequences of Santos v. Kimmel, situating the Second Circuit’s decision within the larger fair use landscape and considering how creators, platforms, and media companies should respond. The opinion is notable for the court’s early (12(b)(6)) resolution of a copyright dispute on fair use grounds, its emphasis on an objective “reasonable observer” test for transformativeness, its reiteration that market-usurpation—rather than mere reputational or expressive harm—is the critical inquiry for the fourth factor, and its refusal to give dispositive weight to subjective bad faith. Each of those positions carries consequences for litigation strategy, platform drafting, and the emerging market for personalized creator content. Below we unpack those doctrinal takeaways, explain tactical choices for litigants, propose contract-drafting best practices for platforms, analyze implications for the creator economy, and identify unresolved questions likely to surface in future cases.

## Doctrinal takeaways: objective transformativeness, market-usurpation focus, and limited role of bad faith

Santos v. Kimmel is best read as a practical application of the modern fair use framework with three reaffirmed themes.

First, transformativeness is judged objectively from the perspective of a “reasonable observer,” not by the subjective intent of either the original creator or the secondary user. The court accepted the district court’s characterization that the JKL segments’ purpose was to lampoon Santos’ willingness to utter absurdities for payment, and it rejected Santos’s argument that the videos were created for the same purpose. The Second Circuit emphasized that what matters is how a reasonable observer would understand the videos: if the original videos communicated “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” repurposing them into a sarcasm-laden segment that highlights performative willing-to-say absurd-things-for-money alters the communicative purpose sufficiently to be transformative. For law students, the lesson is twofold. One must distinguish (a) the creator’s stated intent and (b) the reasonable perception of the work in context; fair use evaluation privileges the latter. In practice, courts will compare the original work’s communicative content with the secondary use’s message and purpose to determine whether something “new” and different has been added.

Second, the opinion underscores the centrality of market-usurpation in analyzing the fourth fair use factor. The court reiterated that not all deleterious effects on value qualify as cognizable markets to be protected—copyright does not give creators a monopoly on criticism, parody, or critical commentary that would otherwise be outside the relevant licensing market. The inquiry asks whether the secondary use functions as a market substitute for licensing the original work, not whether the secondary use damages the original’s reputational or expressive value. That distinction matters at both pleading and trial stages: a complaint that alleges reputational devaluation or general “harm” will not survive if the complaint lacks plausible allegations that the defendant’s use substituted for a likely licensing market.

Third, Santos illustrates that evidence of bad faith or deception (Kimmel used pseudonyms on Cameo) is not automatically fatal to a fair use defense. The court noted Harper & Row’s statement that fair use presupposes good faith but observed that subsequent Supreme Court decisions have been skeptical about elevating good faith to a determinative factor. The Second Circuit applied that skepticism: even if Kimmel’s tactics were deceptive, the complaint itself portrayed defendants’ conduct as motivated by critical commentary rather than a desire to supplant Santos’s market. Thus, misrepresentations by a secondary user do not necessarily defeat fair use where the other factors—including transformativeness and lack of market usurpation—favor the defendant.

Taken together, these points push doctrinal analysis toward an objective, context-driven assessment of what meaning a secondary use conveys and whether the secondary use competes with a cognizable licensing market for the original work. Fair use is flexible, but courts will not accord decisive weight to a creator’s subjective intent nor to vague allegations of reputational harm.

## Litigation strategies: positioning for or against early fair use adjudication

Santos was resolved at the pleading stage on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion because the materials necessary to decide fair use were before the court and the complaint itself established the facts the court needed to resolve the defense. That outcome provides a strategic roadmap for both plaintiffs and defendants.

For defendants seeking early resolution:
- Assemble the judicially cognizable record. The Second Circuit relied on materials properly before the court: the complaint’s allegations, the alleged works themselves, and Santos’ registrations (which courts may take judicial notice of). Attach the contested materials and any registrations to the motion to dismiss or make them part of the complaint by incorporation. Where the fair use analysis depends on a side-by-side comparison of the works, provide those comparisons to show transformativeness and limited market impact.
- Emphasize an objective, reasonable-observer account of meaning. Build the argument that the secondary use materially alters the original’s message or purpose. Use visual or transcript comparisons to show how the secondary use recontextualizes the original content.
- Address market effects head-on. Show why the secondary use cannot serve as a substitute in any existing licensing market: is the work already published? Would licensees ever pay for a critical or parodic use? If the challenged use appears in short clips excerpted for commentary rather than for entertainment or replacement, stress those facts.
- Anticipate and deflect bad-faith allegations. Acknowledge misconduct where necessary but frame conduct as tactical or theatrical and emphasize that transformativeness and lack of market substitution mitigate any misconduct’s significance.

For plaintiffs trying to avoid early dismissal:
- Avoid conclusory allegations. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal in part because Santos’s complaint lacked nonconclusory allegations about market harm and failed to plead essential terms for the contract claims. Plaintiffs must plead concrete facts—specific markets that defendants’ use has or will usurp, particular licensing opportunities lost, or how the secondary use competes commercially with the original.
- Preserve the record about intent and use. If the defendant’s conduct involves deception or targeted inducement to create content for a broadcast, plead those facts in detail—who used what account, the timing of requests, communications showing intent to broadcast, and the creator’s expectations about permissible use. Those facts may undercut a defendant’s transformativeness argument or support a claim of bad-faith inducement in narrow circumstances.
- Distinguish your work and its markets. A plaintiff who can plausibly allege a preexisting or realistic licensing market for the specific kind of work (e.g., individualized paid videos licensed for broadcast) will be in a stronger position. Allegations that the creator explicitly intended to license the works for certain uses and had been negotiating such licenses will help resist dismissal.

Both sides should remember that fair use is fact-intensive; early resolution is possible where the record is complete enough for an objective comparison, but when contested facts (about meaning, market, or intent) are plausibly in dispute, defendants may face a harder path on a motion to dismiss and might prefer summary judgment once discovery establishes the context.

## Platform drafting: making (or avoiding) third-party beneficiary rights explicit

Santos also illustrates the risks and limits of relying on platform Terms of Service (ToS) as a vehicle to confer or avoid third-party rights. Santos attempted to assert direct breach claims against the Cameo ToS and an implied-in-fact contract; the court rejected both theories because Santos was not a party to the ToS and did not plausibly allege an implied contract.

For platforms and creators, the key drafting and business-design lessons are practical, not merely academic.

For platforms and purchasers who want to ensure that noncontracting creators can sue third-party abusers:
- Include express third-party beneficiary language. If a platform wishes to enable creators to enforce usage restrictions against platform users or platform licensees, the ToS should say so explicitly: name classes of intended beneficiaries, state the specific rights conferred to them, and provide a clear mechanism for enforcement (e.g., notice-and-cure, indemnities).
- Draft clear licensing grants and restrictions. If user-generated content may be used by purchasers for broadcast or other public-facing uses, define the permitted and prohibited uses clearly. If a creator sells a personalized video only for private, non-broadcast use, the sales interface and purchase confirmation should limit broad downstream uses. Conversely, if purchasers are to be permitted broad redistribution, the platform should require affirmative checkbox consent from the creator.
- Design a separate, maker-facing contract. Platforms can offer creators a distinct creator agreement that sets out resale, redistribution, and enforcement rights. Making creators affirmatively opt into these terms reduces ambiguity about expectations.
- Preserve audit trails and consent records. When disputes arise, the identity of buyers, timestamps, IP addresses, consent checkboxes, and message logs often determine whether a purchaser intended to broadcast a video. Maintain logs that can be produced in litigation.

For platforms and media companies who prefer to avoid creating third-party enforcement rights:
- Use explicit disclaimers and narrowly tailored licenses. If the platform’s ToS are not intended to create enforceable rights in nonparties, the drafting should avoid broad beneficiary language and explicitly state that no third-party has enforcement rights absent an express written agreement.
- Provide transparent notice to creators about possible downstream uses. Platforms that obscure likely downstream uses increase litigation risk; transparent notices help manage creator expectations and reduce claims of reliance or inducement.

Two additional points follow from Santos’s analysis. First, governing law and forum-selection clauses matter. Cameo’s ToS were governed by Illinois law, and the court applied Illinois contract doctrines (strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries) to reject Santos’s claim. Drafters should choose governing law with an eye toward third-party-beneficiary doctrines. Second, an implied-in-fact contract claim requires specific pleading about the essential terms and a meeting of the minds. Platforms should avoid relying on ambiguity to preserve flexibility; clarity reduces litigation risk.

## Creator economy implications: personalized content, licensing expectations, and public-figure commentary

Santos arose in the new economy of micro-payments for personalized content. Cameo and similar services allow fans or media to commission bespoke videos. The case illuminates how courts will treat such content when it migrates from a private, paid interaction to public commentary.

Expectations and default rules. Many creators who produce paid personalized content do not contemplate or are not told that purchasers can use the content in broadcast contexts. Santos’s dispute underscores the need for creators to set expectations explicitly. Without explicit licensing terms, courts may view broadcast or critical reuse as transformative commentary protected by fair use, especially when the secondary use recontextualizes the content into satire or criticism. Creators who want to preserve broad exclusive rights must contract for them.

Pricing and licensing markets. Santos failed on the market-harm theory because the complaint did not plausibly allege a realistic licensing market that the JKL segments could usurp. But the creator economy is evolving: as platforms mature, a specialized licensing market for personalized videos might develop (e.g., news programs pay for permission to show user-created content, or aggregators license clips for compilations). Creators who believe that a licensing market could exist should plan for licensing-pricing mechanisms and express licensing opt-ins/opt-outs. Conversely, broadcasters relying on fair use should monitor market developments; if creators successfully establish a licensing market for these sorts of clips, the fourth-factor analysis could shift.

Public figures and commentary. The Santos fact pattern is particularly favorable to defendants because the secondary use was commentary about a public figure’s behavior—one of the classic contexts courts have treated as protected by fair use. In practical terms, producers of news, comedy, and commentary have more latitude to repurpose creator-produced content when the repurposing is itself expressive criticism or satire aimed at a public figure. That latitude shrinks if the broadcast is merely a straight reproduction that commodifies the content without changing its message.

Monetization choices. Creators should consider tiered offerings: private/personal-only deliverables at one price point, and broad-license versions at higher rates. Platforms should provide easy paths for creators to elect licensing terms and for purchasers to purchase broader rights when needed.

## Open questions: boundaries of satire/commentary, platform-induced reliance, and evolving markets for critical uses

Santos resolves certain issues but leaves others open—many of which will shape future litigation and industry practice.

Where is the boundary between satire/commentary and nontransformative reuse? Santos treats recontextualization for satirical commentary as transformative. But how far must recontextualization go? Two arrays of fact patterns will test the line:
- Close derivative reuse: Suppose a broadcaster shows a complete personalized video verbatim, with a narrator saying, “see how absurd this is?” but without editing, juxtaposition, or new meaning. Is mere juxtaposition sufficient? Courts will likely analyze whether the audience reads the original work differently in the new context or whether the new use merely repackages the original for entertainment. The reasonable-observer test gives courts a tool, but its application will turn on nuanced fact-finding about audience perception.
- Entertainment vs. commentary: A late-night show that uses clips to mock a figure is classic commentary. But a compilation program that uses similar clips for entertainment without any explicit critical framing poses a closer question. The higher the secondary use’s commercial character and the closer its function is to replacing the original work’s market, the less likely a finding of fair use.

What is the role of platform-induced reliance or instigation? Santos touched on the notion that Kimmel “instigated” the original works by requesting them through Cameo. Santos argued that this makes the secondary use nontransformative because Kimmel designed the videos for his later purpose. The court rejected that line because the relevant question remains what the reasonable observer would perceive. But future cases may arise where secondary users’ conduct goes beyond mere solicitation and into directed production—e.g., a broadcaster communicates precise scripting, phrases, or staging to induce creators to perform, with the explicit intention of later broadcast. When the secondary user clearly directs the creative choices, courts may be less willing to treat the secondary use as truly transformative since the original work was created at the instigation of the secondary user for the secondary user’s purpose. To preserve defenses, platforms and creators should document who set the creative parameters.

How will evolving licensing markets change the fourth-factor analysis? The Second Circuit stressed that critical or satirical uses are unlikely to be the kind of uses creators would license. But if a robust licensing market arises—platforms that broker broadcast rights for personalized videos, or subscription services that compile creator content—then an argument that broadcasters are usurping a cognizable market becomes stronger. Fair use is fact-sensitive and partly market-driven; courts will revisit the fourth factor if and when transactional evidence shows a realistic market for such licenses.

Will courts ever elevate bad-faith or deception to a decisive role? Santos reiterates the contemporary judicial skepticism about treating bad faith as determinative. Still, egregious conduct—targeted deception to obtain copyrighted works and then reproduce them verbatim for profit—could sway a court’s fair use calculus in close cases. Litigation exploiting egregious tactics (e.g., fabricated identities, explicit pre-broadcast promises that are broken) might produce a different result than the Second Circuit reached in Santos.

What are the preemption and contract-law boundaries? The opinion avoided deciding whether state contract claims are preempted by the Copyright Act, instead dismissing them on their merits. Future cases might squarely present whether state-law claims that enforce contractual restrictions on use of copyrighted material (distinct from copyright statutory claims) are preempted. Thoughtful drafting—express third-party beneficiary clauses, explicit waivers, or separate licensing agreements—can reduce litigation risk, but the interplay between federal copyright and state contract law remains a fertile question for appellate courts.

## Practical checklists and closing guidance

For creators:
- Do not rely on a platform’s default settings; negotiate or select licensing options when you want to preserve broadcast rights.
- Keep contemporaneous records of the creator’s expectations, communications with purchasers, and any license sales.
- Consider tiered offerings (private-only vs. broad-license) and price accordingly.

For platforms:
- Draft ToS with clear, explicit language about third-party enforcement rights, permissible uses, and licensing pathways.
- Provide creator-facing agreement options and affirmative consent mechanisms for broad redistribution.
- Preserve logs and evidence of buyer identities and purchase confirmations.

For media companies:
- Before broadcasting user-created content, assemble the record: the original clip, registration status, evidence of recontextualization, and a clear statement of how the broadcast transforms the original work’s meaning.
- Where possible, secure express licenses for uses that might be nontransformative or that could operate in a developing licensing market.
- If relying on fair use, prepare to show how the use changes the message and why it does not substitute for a licensing market.

For litigators:
- Defendants: move early when the record is complete; attach contested works and registrations; craft a strong reasonable-observer argument.
- Plaintiffs: plead market-harm facts and detailed allegations of inducement, direction, and reliance; preserve discovery to probe intent and market effects.

Santos v. Kimmel reiterates that fair use is both flexible and capacious; in the era of personalized creator content, courts will continue to balance the competing objectives of protecting creators’ markets and allowing robust public discourse and criticism. The case guides litigants and industry actors toward clarity—both in factual presentation in court and in contractual clarity in the marketplace—while leaving open important line-drawing questions that future litigation will have to resolve.

# Notes, Questions, and Hypotheticals

This chapter uses Santos v. Kimmel as the launching point for exercises that test how small factual changes can push a dispute across the line from fair use dismissal and state-law failure-to-plead into viable claims. The Second Circuit’s opinion supplies the governing legal framework on fair use and on when state-law claims survive (or are displaced) by federal copyright law. Below we explore six fact-variants that commonly arise in practice, explain the doctrinal pivot points, and pose the precise factual showings a plaintiff would need to survive early dismissal.

## What if the show aired the videos without commentary, in full, and monetized them exclusively on social platforms?

Santos was dismissed because Kimmel’s use was overwhelmingly “transformative” — the purpose and character of the JKL segments was to mock and comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money — and because the other fair-use factors cut in favor of the defendants. That conclusion rests on the particular mixture of transformation (criticism, satire) and the limited market-harm risk created by the segments.

Change the facts so that the show instead runs each Cameo video in its entirety, with no critical framing or commentary, and republishes them on defendant-controlled social accounts where the defendants monetize views (ads, sponsorships, or paid promotions). Under Section 107, this factual shift affects at least three of the four fair-use factors in predictable ways.

First, purpose and character: a verbatim broadcast of the entire video, without commentary or context, is far less likely to be viewed as “transformative.” The Second Circuit emphasizes the reasonable-observer inquiry: what does the secondary use add, and does it alter the original with a different purpose or message? When the secondary use merely republishes the work to reach an audience and extract ad revenue, it “supersedes the objects of the original creation” rather than adds new expression or meaning. That pushes the first factor strongly against fair use.

Second, amount and substantiality: airing the whole work weighs against fair use. The use of the entire creative work is presumptively non‑fair when the entire expression is copied and there is no strong justification for doing so to accomplish a transformative purpose.

Third, market effect: republishing the videos on monetized social platforms creates a real risk of usurpation. If viewers can access the same content on the defendants’ high‑traffic platforms, the secondary distribution functions as a substitute for the creator’s licensing opportunities (e.g., the market to license clips, or direct-pay opportunities for full videos). The Supreme Court and the Second Circuit draw a line between critical uses (which courts have recognized as unlikely to form a conventional licensing market) and untransformed republishing that competes with the original creator.

Taken together, these changes make fair use a much weaker defense. A complaint alleging that the defendant ran the unaltered, monetized videos could survive a motion to dismiss if the plaintiff alleges that defendants extracted commercial value by offering the same expressive content to the public and that the defendants’ platforms were a realistic substitute for plaintiff’s marketplaces. In short: airing in full and monetizing likely tips the balance away from fair use and toward liability.

Questions for students: What evidence would work to show that the defendants’ social posts served as substitutes for plaintiff’s market? Would view counts or advertising revenues be necessary at the pleading stage, or could plausibly pled allegations about platform reach suffice? How would a plaintiff allege licensing value or lost licensing opportunities plausibly?

## What if Santos included a visible license term or watermark restricting broadcast use in each video?

Embedding a license term (for example, a short text on the video — “Not for broadcast” — or a watermark stating “Licensed for private use only”) changes the legal texture of the dispute, but it does not automatically convert a copyright defense problem into a win for the copyright holder. The effect of such notice operates on several doctrinal axes: fair use, bad-faith inquiries, and contract or licensing claims.

On fair use: fair use remains an objective legal doctrine. Courts look to the use’s purpose and character, the amount copied, the nature of the work, and market effect. A visible “no-broadcast” notice does not negate fair use automatically, but it can be relevant evidence. The Supreme Court has been skeptical of treating subjective bad faith as dispositive in the fair-use calculus (see Campbell’s and later decisions’ caution about treating “bad faith” as decisive), but objective evidence of purposeful evasion can still matter. A notice makes it harder for a defendant to credibly argue that it thought the use was noncopyrightous or that it believed it had a right to republish. Thus notice tends to cut against a defendant on the first factor (purpose/character) insofar as it undermines claims of benign intent, and it may influence a court’s view of overall fairness.

On contract/licensing: an embedded license notice could function as a unilateral license term, but the enforceability of that term depends on the parties’ relationship. If the recipient of the video (or the platform through which the video is delivered) expressly accepts the license term in a way that satisfies contract formation requirements — offer, acceptance, and consideration — then the license may be enforceable. A platform-wide Terms of Service, however, is typically a contract between the user and the platform; a notice within a media file does not by itself create privity between the uploader and every downstream viewer. Where a defendant individually negotiated with the creator or explicitly agreed to license restrictions in messages or a separate contract, the embedded term has more force.

On remedies: a clear visible license or watermark strengthens claims for breach of contract or breach of an implied license if the defendant’s conduct created the requisite contractual relationship or if the defendant’s acceptance of the uploader’s conditions can be plausibly alleged. It may also reinforce equitable claims (e.g., injunction) because it lessens any equitable defense based on laches or reasonable misunderstanding.

Practical note for a plaintiff: plead the notice’s text, its location on the video, and the recipient’s subjective knowledge (e.g., that the defendant watched the video and the watermark was visible) to make it concrete. The more the complaint plausibly shows the defendant saw and ignored the notice, the stronger the argument that fair use should not be resolved in their favor at the pleading stage.

## Would a narrowly tailored right of publicity claim avoid preemption and survive fair use in this context?

Right-of-publicity claims are conceptually distinct from copyright. Copyright protects original expression; the right of publicity protects the commercial appropriation of an individual’s identity (name, persona, likeness) to exploit a commercial value. Whether a narrow right-of-publicity claim survives (1) copyright preemption and (2) any “defenses” available to the defendant (including expressive‑use defenses) turns on two questions.

First, preemption: federal copyright law preempts state-law claims that are equivalent to copyright’s exclusive rights (17 U.S.C. § 301). The test asks whether the state claim protects rights that are qualitatively the same as those protected by copyright and whether the state remedy is no broader than the rights granted by copyright. A right-of-publicity claim that does not seek to control expressive elements that are the same as the copyrighted expression — for example, it seeks to redress commercial exploitation of a person’s persona distinct from any claimed exclusivity in the creative content itself — is less likely to be preempted. Put differently, if the complaint centers on unauthorized commercial exploitation of Santos’s identity (separate from the expressive content of the particular videos), it may survive preemption because it vindicates a different interest: control of commercial use of one’s persona.

Second, survivability against an expressive‑use defense (sometimes manifesting as First Amendment protection): courts often balance the individual’s publicity right against the public’s interest in free expression. In the 2d Circuit, and in many other jurisdictions, when the use is expressive (commentary, parody, news reporting), courts apply a balancing test — sometimes characterized as a “transformative use” inquiry similar in flavor to the copyright test — to determine whether the public‑interest value outweighs the commercial appropriation. Where the use is highly transformative (adds significant new expression, meaning, or message), the First Amendment tends to protect the defendant; when the defendant uses the plaintiff’s identity primarily for commercial exploitation and the work is not meaningfully transformative, the plaintiff’s publicity interest prevails.

Applying these principles to a “Will Santos Say It?” scenario: if Santos pleads a narrow publicity claim alleging that Kimmel used Santos’s name and likeness as a commercial draw for ad‑supported segments and social posts — independent of any claimed ownership in the videos’ expressive elements — that claim may be pled as qualitatively different from a copyright claim and therefore avoid preemption. Whether it survives summary judgment or trial depends on the expressive character of the broadcast. The use at issue in Santos was, arguably, expressive (satirical commentary), which is the exact context where defendants frequently win under First Amendment balancing. But if the defendant’s broadcast was unaccompanied by commentary, was used as pure advertisement bait, and was monetized directly as a commercial substitute for Santos’s own market, the publicity claim would be stronger.

Takeaway: a well‑pleaded, narrow publicity claim that seeks to protect the commercial value of the plaintiff’s persona and does not attempt to assert exclusive rights over the expressive content of the video has a plausible path around copyright preemption. It then faces — and may lose to — First Amendment expressive‑use defenses depending on the particular application’s transformative character and commercial nature.

## If Kimmel edited the videos to fabricate statements Santos did not make, how would that affect factors one and four?

Fabrication is a complicating fact with implications across multiple doctrines. Two fair-use factors are most directly implicated: purpose/character (factor one) and market effect (factor four). Fabrication also raises separate torts (defamation, false-light privacy, and possibly fraud) that may provide independent remedies.

On factor one (purpose and character): editing a creator’s videos to fabricate statements changes the second work’s relationship to the original. If the edits are designed to misrepresent Santos’s own expression — e.g., welding unrelated clips together to make him appear to say things he did not — a reasonable observer might not view the secondary use as commentary but as a deceptive creation that supplants or supplants the authentic message. The court’s transformative inquiry asks whether the new work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” Fabrication may add a new (false) message, but that message is not a commentary on the original work; it is an altered portrayal that distorts the author’s expressive intent. Courts have been skeptical of protecting mischievous or misleading uses under the banner of “transformation” when the new work merely manipulates the original to create a false impression.

Therefore, fabrication tends to undermine claims of transformativeness. The defendant cannot evade liability simply by asserting that the altered work offers “new meaning” if that new meaning is a falsification of the original speaker’s message. That is especially true where the fabricated content damages the speaker’s public reputation or supplants his authentic expression.

On factor four (market effect): fabrication increases the risk of market harm in two distinct ways. First, it can diminish the market for the original creative works because consumers will not rely on or purchase the creator’s authentic messages if misleading, fabricated versions proliferate. Second, the fabricated versions may usurp licensing opportunities: for example, news outlets or social channels may choose to share the fabricated clip rather than seeking out or licensing the original content. Fabrication therefore increases the plausibility of a substitution argument and corresponding market harm.

Aside from copyright, fabrication can open the door to other tort claims that the Santos opinion did not reach. If a defendant edits content to create false statements, a plaintiff may have a viable defamation or false‑light privacy claim (depending on jurisdiction and whether the plaintiff is a public figure) and possibly a claim for fraudulent inducement if the defendant’s pre-broadcast misrepresentations caused the plaintiff to act to his detriment. Those tort claims are not precluded by copyright because they vindicate distinct interests.

In short: fabrication weakens a fair-use defense and strengthens both the case for market harm under the fourth factor and allegations that the defendant acted deceptively, inviting non‑copyright remedies.

## Assume a detailed, mutually acknowledged message exchange agreeing to abide by TOS — does an implied-in-fact or express contract claim now lie?

The Santos opinion rejected a breach-of-TOS claim largely because Santos was not a party to the platform’s Terms of Service and because the complaint lacked facts showing a binding implied‑in‑fact agreement between the creator and the defendants. But change the factual record to include a detailed, mutually acknowledged message exchange in which the defendants explicitly state that they reviewed the Cameo Terms of Service, agree to abide by them, and promise not to broadcast or distribute the videos beyond the intended private audience. How do contract doctrines apply?

Express contract: If the messages constitute an offer (defendant’s promise), acceptance (creator’s assent), consideration (the creator’s promise to deliver a video in exchange for payment by the defendant or otherwise provided consideration), and sufficiently definite terms, an express contract likely exists between the creator and the defendant. Courts look to objective manifestations of assent; modern case law treats electronic messages like other writings: an exchange of emails or texts can create a binding contract if the parties agree on essential terms. A message in which the defendant acknowledges the platform’s TOS and expressly promises not to broadcast would be strong evidence of express agreement. The presence of consideration is typically satisfied where the creator provides a paid service.

Implied‑in‑fact contract: Where the communications are less formal, courts evaluate whether the parties’ conduct and surrounding circumstances evidence a meeting of the minds. If the defendant requests a personalized recording, the creator records and delivers it, and the defendant pays (or promises to pay), courts can infer an implied-in-fact contract whose terms include customarily expected conditions like the intended audience and license scope. Explicit message acknowledgements strengthen the inference; indeed, messages that incorporate TOS terms or refer to them can supply the terms of an implied contract.

Third‑party beneficiary issues: An alternative route is showing that Santos was an intended third‑party beneficiary of the platform’s TOS. The Santos opinion noted the strong presumption against conferring rights on nonparties under Illinois law (the choice-of-law that applied to the platform Terms). But if the TOS expressly designates third‑party beneficiaries or the messages between Kimmel and Santos state that the TOS protections apply for Santos’s benefit, the “strong presumption” can be overcome. Express language in the TOS or in the bilateral messaging creating an express contractual obligation to individuals like Santos would be decisive.

Practicalities and enforcement: If there is a clear, bilateral contract between Kimmel and Santos limiting use to non-broadcast or private distribution, a breach-of-contract claim can proceed independently of copyright law. The defendant’s fair‑use defense to a copyright claim is irrelevant to breach-of-contract unless the contract purports to surrender rights that are not within the parties’ power to bargain away. Contract remedies (damages, injunctions) are available subject to standard defenses (consent, waiver, lack of consideration). Thus, a detailed, mutually acknowledged message exchange can create an express contract, or at minimum supply the facts needed to plead an implied‑in‑fact contract with particular terms that will survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

Questions for students: What specific terms must the messages contain to make the contract sufficiently definite? How would formation be affected by platform intermediaries or by TOS provisions that purport to disclaim third‑party obligations? How would you plead waiver or modification by subsequent conduct?

## What out-of-pocket loss could be plausibly alleged if Santos declined higher-paying requests due to time spent on Kimmel’s prompts?

The Santos court affirmed dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim because the complaint failed to allege any out‑of‑pocket loss — a necessary element under New York law for such a claim. But a different set of allegations about foregone opportunities could plausibly state an out‑of‑pocket loss.

Under New York law, a fraudulent inducement plaintiff must plead a pecuniary loss caused by reasonable reliance on the defendant’s misrepresentation. Out‑of‑pocket damages are the classic form of reliance damages: money the plaintiff actually spent, or money the plaintiff could have earned but lost because of reliance.

In the hypothetical where Santos alleges that, based on Kimmel’s solicitations (and perhaps false representations about how the videos would be used), he declined other offers that would have paid more, the complaint must do more than plead a conclusory statement that he “lost” opportunities. To be plausible, the complaint should allege:

- Specific offers that were declined: identify the other requesters (if possible), the dates and approximate times, the higher fees that were offered, and the fact that the creator declined those offers in reasonable reliance on the defendant’s representations or promises.
- Causation and reliance: show that Santos reasonably relied on the defendant’s representations (e.g., the defendant said the videos would only be used privately, and Santos relied on that promise) and that reliance caused him to decline other paid opportunities.
- Quantifiable loss: plead the amount of the foregone fees (e.g., “Santos declined a $5,000 Cameo request on April 1 because he had agreed to produce Kimmel’s request that evening”); if there is a pattern, aggregate the lost sums.
- Non-speculative connection: explain why Santos could not have done both (time constraints, scheduling, or exclusivity commitments), and why the lost opportunities are not merely hypothetical.

If Santos can plausibly allege the foregoing, courts have allowed out‑of‑pocket reliance damages to survive. The complaint must avoid vague or conclusory statements about lost business; specificity is required to show a real pecuniary injury. Alternative measures of damages could also include costs incurred in producing the Kimmel videos (equipment, assistants, studio hire) that would be recoverable as out‑of‑pocket expenditures if the defendant’s fraudulent inducement caused the creator to undertake the costs.

One caveat: if the plaintiff is a public figure (as Santos was), damages for reputational harms are harder to recover under certain torts; but out‑of‑pocket, pecuniary reliance losses do not require a showing of reputational injury and so are the most straightforward path to survive a motion to dismiss on fraud claims.

Question for students: How would you plead reliance where the defendant’s misrepresentation was implicit (e.g., use of a fake account to solicit videos), rather than an explicit promise about distribution? What kinds of documentary evidence would strengthen the plaintiff’s claim at summary judgment?

---

These hypotheticals illustrate how relatively subtle factual changes alter the balance between competing doctrines: copyright fair use, contract formation, preemption, right-of-publicity law, and tort damages. The Santos decision highlights several recurring lessons. First, courts resolve fair use by focusing on what a reasonable observer would see as the new use’s purpose and market effects. Second, statutory preemption presents a barrier to state-law claims that merely duplicate the rights of copyright; narrowly tailored claims that vindicate distinct interests (persona, contract promises) can survive. Third, pleading matters: specificity about the defendant’s conduct, the plaintiff’s losses, and any contemporaneous agreements will often be dispositive at the Rule 12 stage. Use these hypotheticals to practice translating fact patterns into the legal elements each cause of action requires, and to think about the types of evidence — messages, view counts, licensing offers, and payment records — that will matter as the case develops.
