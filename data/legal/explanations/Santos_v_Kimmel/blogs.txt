## Fair use in the gig economy: Second Circuit greenlights late‑night mockery of Cameo videos

Why this case matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel is a concise, pragmatic application of contemporary fair‑use doctrine to a very 21st‑century fact pattern: short, paid “personalized” videos created via the Cameo platform and then excerpted and broadcast by a late‑night host as material for satirical commentary. The opinion matters for creators and content platforms across the gig economy because it signals that courts will treat publicly distributed short‑form content—especially content sold or made available for a particular audience—as fair game when reused to add a critical or comedic overlay. That holding lowers the risk that mainstream media outlets (and arguably social platforms) will face debilitating copyright suits simply for repurposing user‑submitted clips in critical or parodic contexts.

How the court reached its result

The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims at the pleading stage, applying the familiar four‑factor fair‑use inquiry but emphasizing how each factor pointed toward fair use here.

Purpose and character. The court placed primary weight on transformativeness, asking whether the re‑use “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” rather than merely superseding the original. Kimmel’s use—airing the Cameo clips as part of a recurring segment to mock Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things on camera for money—was the paradigmatic critical use the fair‑use doctrine protects. The panel rejected Santos’s argument that the original purpose behind his videos (as boosterish, encouraging messages) was the same as Kimmel’s, explaining that “transformative” inquiry looks to how a reasonable observer perceives the two works, not the creator’s subjective intent. That reliance on an external, reasonable‑observer framing follows the Supreme Court’s recent emphasis in Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and is consistent with the Second Circuit’s post‑Warhol approach.

Bad faith. Santos argued that Kimmel’s deceptive procurement of the videos (using fictitious names) showed bad faith and therefore undermined fair use. The court declined to treat alleged bad faith as fatal. While acknowledging Harper & Row’s historic language suggesting fair use presupposes good faith, the panel observed that more recent high court guidance has been skeptical of giving bad faith determinative weight. Moreover, the complaint itself described a use squarely in the realm of commentary and ridicule—not a commercial substitute that would usurp Santos’s market—so the alleged deception did not convert commentary into actionable infringement.

Nature, amount, and market effect. The opinion treated the second and third factors as non‑problematic given the short‑form, published nature of the Cameo clips and the limited portions used. On market effect—the fourth factor—the court applied the substitution test: fair use looks to whether the secondary use usurps a licensing market for the original, not whether it merely damages the reputation or “devalues” the original through criticism. Because creators of imaginative, personal messages do not typically license them for critical commentary or late‑night satire, the court concluded there was no cognizable market that Kimmel’s segment had supplanted.

Taken together, the panel concluded that the fair‑use defense was strong enough to support dismissal at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage—underscoring that, in some contexts, fair use can be resolved before discovery when the pleaded facts and the works themselves make the defense apparent.

Analytical perspective: strengths and limits of the opinion

The opinion is forceful in its application of settled fair‑use principles to a modern medium. Two features deserve particular emphasis.

First, the court’s reliance on the “reasonable observer” perspective helps prevent the inquiry from devolving into dueling assertions of intent by creators and re‑users. That is sensible: when a clip is uploaded for one audience and later reframed on national television as an object of ridicule, an objective assessment of how the materials are perceived offers a workable, administrable test for judges. It also aligns with the Supreme Court’s broader push to measure transformativeness by public perception and effect rather than undisclosed subjective motives.

Second, the decision resists letting allegations of bad behavior (here, deceptive user identities) swallow the fair‑use inquiry. That is consistent with a line of cases—most recently signaled in Google v. Oracle—that treat bad faith as at most a contextual factor, not a talisman that defeats fair use. The court’s approach reasonably draws a line between moral opprobrium and legal disqualification: wrongdoing can have independent tort consequences, but it does not automatically negate copyright defenses when the secondary use is plainly commentary.

There are limits and tensions to note, however. The decision resolves fair use at the pleading stage because the parties’ submissions and the works themselves supposedly made the issue clear. That gatekeeping function is valuable to avoid costly discovery in weak infringement suits, but it raises the risk that subtle factual disputes—about, for example, the extent of editing, the context in which clips were presented to viewers, or downstream licensing markets—might sometimes be prematurely decided. The court’s dismissal is defensible here because the complaint and copyright registrations were explicit, but litigants should not assume all takedown or reuse disputes will be so straightforward.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Santos sits comfortably with a recent trend in the Second Circuit favoring fair use for critical, scholarly, or comedic repurposing of published materials. Cases like Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive and Authors Guild v. Google established that transformative, socially beneficial reuses—especially those that do not serve as market substitutes—deserve robust protection. Santos extends that logic into the short‑form, platformized content economy.

Practically, the decision has several foreseeable effects. Media entities and entertainers will read Santos as confirmation that they can repurpose user‑supplied clips for commentary without automatically courting copyright liability, provided their use meaningfully reframes the material. Platforms like Cameo and TikTok, where users produce content for narrow audiences or individualized consumption, may also see fewer copyright constraints on third parties that sample those clips for critical or satirical ends.

For content creators who sell personalized media, Santos is a caution: making material public (or publicly accessible via a platform) weakens exclusive control when others transform the work for commentary. Creators concerned about commercial exploitation can rely on platform terms, licensing arrangements, or contract remedies, but those instruments will not necessarily block fair‑use defenses if a broadcaster or commentator repurposes clips for criticism or comedy. On the other hand, where a re‑user attempts to appropriate content as a market substitute (e.g., by repackaging whole works as-is in a way that competes with the original’s licensing market), Santos does not protect that appropriation.

Finally, Santos reinforces a doctrinal boundary between copyright and other areas of law. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of Santos’s state‑law contract and fraud claims on independent grounds, emphasizing traditional third‑party beneficiary, contract formation, and out‑of‑pocket loss doctrines. Those rulings foreshadow limits on using state causes of action to circumvent federal copyright preemption or to garment‑bag creative reuse into more palatable tort claims.

Conclusion

Santos v. Kimmel is an instructive, relatively short opinion that applies established fair‑use principles to an emblematic gig‑economy dispute. By focusing on a reasonable‑observer test for transformativeness, downplaying bad faith as dispositive, and sticking to a substitution‑centered market‑harm analysis, the Second Circuit affirmed that mainstream commentary and satire—including late‑night television segments that repurpose Cameo videos—remain squarely within fair use. The ruling should reassure commentators and discussants who rely on short user‑supplied clips for criticism or comedy, while reminding creators that the decision does not immunize uses that actually compete with a legitimate licensing market.



# From Harper and Row to Google v. Oracle: Bad faith keeps shrinking in fair use

## Why this matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel is short on novelty but long on signal. At the heart of a dispute that reads like modern political theater—Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names on the Cameo platform, soliciting personalized videos from former Congressman George Santos and then airing them on his show—the court had to confront a recurring question in fair use doctrine: when, if ever, should a defendant’s deceptive conduct in acquiring or creating copyrighted material defeat a fair use defense?

Santos argued that Kimmel’s use of false names and the resulting deception should weigh against fair use, invoking the venerable but diffuse Harper & Row dicta that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” The Second Circuit, however, followed the trajectory the Supreme Court has sketched in recent years—most conspicuously in Google v. Oracle—and treated bad faith as, at best, a diminished or limited consideration in the fair use analysis. The court affirmed dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims at the pleading stage, holding that Kimmel’s broadcast of the Cameo videos was transformative political commentary and that the alleged deception did not meaningfully alter the fair use calculus.

That posture—tucking deception toward the margins of fair use—matters for journalists, satirists, documentary makers, and those who run undercover sting operations. It also raises doctrinal and policy questions about incentives, licensing markets, and the role that noncopyright causes of action might play when copyright law declines to police bad faith conduct.

## The doctrinal arc: from Harper & Row to Google v. Oracle

Harper & Row (1985) is often invoked for the proposition that fair use assumes “good faith and fair dealing.” But Harper’s factual focus—and its concern with a prepublication market for a memoir—meant that the good-faith sentence was not a categorical rule so much as contextual observation. Subsequent Supreme Court decisions, and increasingly the lower courts, have been skeptical of treating subjective bad faith as determinative in the statutory four-factor inquiry.

Campbell v. Acuff-Rose (1994) acknowledged Harper’s language but also suggested skepticism about folding a pure “bad faith” inquiry into the mechanical determination of fair use. The trend toward skepticism accelerated in Google v. Oracle (2021), where the Court described suspicion of the Harper dictum as “justifiable,” observing that copyright is not a privilege reserved for the well-behaved. Google thus signaled that transformation and market effects deserve primary attention, while moralistic assessments of motive are a poorer fit for the statutory factors.

Santos v. Kimmel applies that trajectory in a familiar fact pattern: Kimmel allegedly obtained content by deception and used it for comic criticism. The Second Circuit, leaning on Google and its own precedents (Andy Warhol, Hachette, Authors Guild, TCA Television), treated the deception as legally immaterial to the key question—whether Kimmel’s use was transformative and whether it usurped any licensing market for Santos’s videos. The court emphasized the “reasonable observer” test: fair use looks to how the accused work functions to the public, not to the secret intentions of the parties. Santos’s own complaint described his original videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance,” whereas Kimmel’s segments repurposed them as fodder for satirical commentary—classic transformative use.

The Second Circuit also followed existing law that market-harm analysis requires showing that the secondary use serves as a market substitute for the original. Parody or lampooning rarely displaces a licensing market for the original material; thus, the court was unconvinced that Kimmel’s mockery harmed any cognizable market for Santos’s Cameo videos.

## What Santos teaches us about bad faith in fair use

There are three takeaways about the current doctrinal role of “bad faith”:

First, bad faith is an attenuated consideration in the transformation-centric fair use framework. Where a defendant’s repurposing adds new expressive content or commentary that a reasonable observer would perceive as distinct from the original, courts are likely to treat the use as transformative regardless of the means of acquisition. Santos fits squarely in this lane: sarcastic commentary on a public figure’s willingness to say outrageous things is paradigmatic of speech the fair use doctrine protects.

Second, courts will be especially skeptical of tying bad faith to the fourth factor (market effect) unless the plaintiff can plausibly allege that the secondary use substitutes for the original in some licensing market. The Second Circuit reiterated the longstanding distinction: market-devaluation (aesthetic or reputational) is different from market usurpation (offering a direct substitute), and only the latter tends to weigh against fair use.

Third, Google v. Oracle gives lower courts a doctrinal foothold to treat subjective intent as either irrelevant or, at best, a minor, context-dependent consideration. The Second Circuit’s opinion explicitly cites the Supreme Court’s skepticism and declines to allow allegations of deception to overcome the otherwise strong showing of fair use at the pleadings stage.

## Practical implications: journalism, satire, and stings

The Santos ruling is good news for late-night shows and satirists who repurpose candid or solicited recordings for commentary. It suggests that deceitful acquisition—creating false personas on a platform to obtain a recorded statement—will not, in itself, doom a fair use defense when the end use is transformative commentary about the speaker.

That said, the decision doesn’t give license to any and all deception. The opinion leaves room for limits in at least two settings. First, where the deception is designed to supplant a licensing market—e.g., a defendant obtains exclusive rights to audiovisual clips and then rebroadcasts them in a way that functions as a market substitute—courts may find market-usurpation and deny fair use. Harper’s core concern—protecting a prepublication news market for an unpublished manuscript—is an example where motive and market interplay mattered. Second, cases involving other legal doctrines (privacy, fraud, trespass, contract) remain live avenues for challenge. Santos’s state-law claims were dismissed for independent reasons, but a future plaintiff who can show concrete out-of-pocket harm or other tortious consequences of deception may succeed outside of copyright.

For journalists and documentary makers who use undercover techniques, Santos is both a reassurance and a reminder. Fair use may protect the republication of undercover-acquired material when used for commentary, but the same conduct can trigger liability under noncopyright rules—fraud, contractual privity breaches, platform terms violations, or privacy torts. And legal risk will be higher where the target is not a public figure or where the deception is part of a larger scheme to commercialize the content directly.

## Broader doctrinal and policy consequences

Doctrinally, Santos v. Kimmel is another step in the “transformation-first, motive-second” evolution of fair use. It reinforces the message that copyright’s primary function is to balance incentives for creation against the free flow of ideas and commentary; courts appear reluctant to let moral judgments about methods trump that balance.

Policy-wise, the ruling can be expected to have mixed effects. It lowers the legal bar for broadcasters and media platforms seeking to expose or lampoon public figures, which aligns with free-speech values—especially where the work is clearly commentary. But it also potentially lowers the cost of deceptive acquisition strategies, which could pressure platforms and content creators to adopt stricter contractual and technological controls (e.g., platform verification, tighter terms-of-service enforcement, or paywalls for personalized content) or to rely more on state-law claims when deception produces tangible harm.

Finally, the decision underscores a recurring theme in modern copyright litigation: when the copyright remedy proves inadequate or unpalatable, plaintiffs will turn to other causes of action. Santos’s state-law claims were rejected on their merits (or preempted questions left unresolved), but nothing in the court’s fair use analysis prevents creative litigants from pursuing fraud, breach, or privacy theories in future cases where the facts support them.

## Where courts might still credit bad faith

While the Second Circuit’s tone is clear, it is not absolute. There are at least three doctrinal spaces where bad faith can still matter:

1. Market usurpation: If deceptive conduct produces a work that functions as a ready market substitute for the original (e.g., repackaging the original service’s product for the same customers), courts will weigh market effect heavily against fair use. That is the concern animating Harper & Row and Swatch-style rulings.

2. Unpublished works and prepublication markets: Courts remain sensitive where the copyright holder has a recognized prepublication market—again, Harper & Row’s factual core. Deception that defeats a publisher’s exclusive prepublication right is more likely to be met with an adverse fair use inference.

3. Interplay with other legal rules: Bad faith may not negate fair use, but it can trigger other liability—fraud, breach, trespass to chattels, invasion of privacy, or even criminal statutes. Plaintiffs with plausible noncopyright harms may find success outside the Copyright Act.

## Conclusion

Santos v. Kimmel is a tidy reaffirmation of a trend: courts are downplaying the doctrinal weight of “bad faith” in fair use when the use is plainly transformative and does not usurp a licensing market. That line makes intuitive sense within a transformation-centered copyright regime, but it also pushes disputes about acquisition methods onto other legal terrains. For practitioners and content platforms, the decision encourages a bifurcated strategy: rely on fair use for expressive repurposing, but don’t ignore contract, privacy, and fraud exposure where deception is involved. For the law, Santos is another waypoint on the journey from Harper & Row’s cautionary words to Google v. Oracle’s pragmatic tilt toward functional analysis of use and market effect.



# Pleading-stage fair use is now a feature, not a bug

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters

The Second Circuit’s opinion in Santos v. Kimmel is another clear sign that federal courts are increasingly willing to resolve fair-use disputes at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage when the complaint and the works themselves permit a clean comparison. That willingness is doctrinally significant and practically consequential for media defendants: when the alleged infringing use and the copyrighted works are before the court, fair use can be judged as a matter of law without months of discovery. Santos reinforces a line of Second Circuit authority — TCA Television, Cariou, and most recently Hachette — that treats fair use as a flexible, context-driven doctrine that can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal” where the record is front-loaded into the pleadings.

The case also illustrates how the combination of judicial notice of copyright registrations and the court’s ability to consider the works themselves as integral to the complaint supplies the factual substrate for early resolution. For media defendants, Santos is a roadmap for how to assemble a motion-to-dismiss record that stands a good chance of success. For plaintiffs, it is a reminder that claims hinging on editorial use, parody, or commentary may be vulnerable at the pleadings stage if the defendant can show transformation and lack of market substitution on the face of the works.

## How the Second Circuit reasoned

Santos arose from Jimmy Kimmel’s use, on his late-night show, of personalized videos that George Santos had posted to Cameo. The District Court dismissed the complaint, and the Second Circuit affirmed. The court followed the familiar four-factor fair-use framework in 17 U.S.C. § 107 and placed primary emphasis on the first factor — purpose and character — asking whether the JKL segments were transformative. Relying on precedent that transformation is assessed from an objective “reasonable observer” perspective, not by the subjective intent of either the copyright claimant or the secondary user, the court concluded that Kimmel’s use repurposed Santos’s videos from inspirationalized personal messages into objects of critical commentary and mockery. That conclusion pushed the first factor strongly in favor of fair use.

The court then disposed of the remaining factors without extended factual inquiry. It took judicial notice of the registrations and the publication dates and relied on the complaint’s own allegations to find that (1) the videos were published contemporaneously with their creation, undermining any argument that the works were unpublished, and (2) the JKL segments did not usurp any likely licensing market for the original Cameo videos. The court cited Hachette’s focus on actual market substitution — not mere devaluation through parody or criticism — and found no plausible pleading that the JKL segments served as a market substitute for Santos’s original works. On that basis, the Second Circuit held that fair use barred the copyright claims and affirmed dismissal at the pleadings stage.

## Placing Santos among TCA, Cariou, and Hachette

Santos fits squarely into a developing Second Circuit practice of early fair-use resolution, and it is useful to compare its approach to the relevant precedents.

- TCA Television (2016) set the proposition that dismissal is appropriate where the complaint and attached materials make fair use plainly apparent. The court in TCA emphasized that when the necessary facts are undisputed and the works are before the court, fair use can be resolved as a matter of law.

- Cariou (2013) elaborated on the transformation inquiry in the visual-arts context, cautioning that courts must compare the works themselves and that transformation can be established by changes in purpose, meaning, or message.

- Hachette (2024) further fleshed out the fourth factor by rejecting a broad “devaluation” test and clarifying that the relevant inquiry is whether the secondary use usurps the market for the original, i.e., offers a competing substitute that creators would plausibly license.

Santos synthesizes these lines: the court considered the primary materials (the videos and the broadcast segments) and the publication records; evaluated transformation from the standpoint of how a reasonable observer would perceive the works; and applied Hachette’s market-substitution lens to the fourth factor. The result is predictable in the doctrinal arc: when the defendant’s use is plainly transformative and does not replace the original market, fair use can be resolved without discovery.

## What this means doctrinally

There are three doctrine-level takeaways from Santos:

First, courts are comfortable treating fair use as a mixed (sometimes purely legal) question that can be resolved on the pleadings where the relevant facts are either judicially noticeable (registrations, publication dates) or evident from the content of the works themselves. That reduces fair use’s previously perceived status as mostly a fact-intensive inquiry that always requires discovery.

Second, the Second Circuit continues to apply the “reasonable observer” standard for transformation, insulating the analysis from the copyright holder’s contrary subjective intent. That standard advantages defendants in media contexts (commentary, parody, critique) where the new work plainly repurposes meaning.

Third, Santos confirms Hachette’s narrower reading of the fourth factor: courts will not treat any perceived devaluation as market harm; they look for substitution risk. That doctrinal choice reduces the scope of the market-harm inquiry at the motion-to-dismiss stage and increases opportunities for early dismissal where substitution is implausible.

## Practical guidance for media defendants

Santos is a practical playbook for defendants seeking an early win:

1. Attach the works. Where possible, attach the original copyrighted works and the allegedly infringing works to the complaint (or to the motion) or incorporate them by reference in the complaint. If the plaintiff’s own complaint relies on the works, emphasize that they are integral to the allegations and thus properly before the court.

2. Seek judicial notice of registrations and publication data. Copyright registrations and the Copyright Office’s public records are proper subjects for judicial notice and can be decisive on issues like published/unpublished status and timing.

3. Frame the use as transformative. Present a clear, objective account of how the secondary use repurposes the original (e.g., commentary, parody, criticism). Emphasize what a reasonable observer would perceive as the new purpose or message.

4. Address market-substitution head-on. Show — on the face of the works and the complaint — that the secondary use is not a substitutable product. Use Hachette’s framing: the relevant question is whether the secondary work offers the same market that the copyright owner would reasonably exploit.

5. Dispel reliance on bad faith. Don’t invite unnecessary disputes over intent. Post-Harper & Row skepticism and Supreme Court signals (noting that copyright is not “a privilege reserved for the well-behaved”), courts will not treat alleged bad faith as fatal where the use is otherwise transformative and non-substitutive.

6. Build an early record. If the defendant can supply the relevant recordings, transcriptions, and public context (e.g., program descriptions indicating satire or commentary), do so in the motion. The clearer the transformation on the face of the materials, the stronger the motion.

## Limits and when discovery will still be needed

Santos does not mean the end of discovery in fair-use cases. There remain circumstances in which factual development is necessary:

- Unpublished works. If the plaintiff plausibly alleges that the original work was unpublished and the status is contested, the second factor (nature of the work) can weigh heavily and may require factual development.

- Market substitution disputes. Where there is a plausible allegation that the secondary use functions as a market substitute — for example, where the secondary work is commercially packaged in a way that could supplant sales or licensing of the original — courts are likely to find factual issues about licensing markets and damages that require discovery.

- Quantitative and qualitative “amount used.” When the amount and substantiality of what was taken cannot be assessed from the works as presented to the court, discovery may be necessary to resolve the issue.

- Credible allegations of covert conduct with commercial intent. If the plaintiff alleges, with plausible detail, that the defendant’s conduct was part of a larger scheme to supplant or monetize the plaintiff’s market (as opposed to criticism or commentary), courts may decline to resolve fair use at the pleadings stage.

- Complex remixing/transformation. Some transformation claims rest on nuanced or contested claims about meaning and context that are not easily resolved on paper; such disputes may require fact development or expert evidence.

In short, defendants should not assume every fair-use case is ripe for dismissal, but they should seize the opportunities where the works and public records make transformation and lack of substitution obvious.

## Concluding perspective

Santos v. Kimmel confirms what many defense counsel have suspected and litigators have quietly urged for years: fair use is not a procedural afterthought that must always await discovery. Where the defendant can put the works and the relevant public records before the court, and where the new work’s purpose is transparently transformative (with little risk of market substitution), the Second Circuit will not hesitate to treat fair use as a dispositive legal question at the pleading stage. Media defendants should prepare their motions accordingly. Plaintiffs, meanwhile, must plead with care if they hope to avoid an early dismissal — particularly by alleging specifics about market harm, unpublished status, or other facts that make fair use genuinely contestable.



## Can platform terms rescue creators from fair use? Santos’s contract theories run aground

Why this case matters: the Santos litigation sits at the intersection of two hot-button issues in contemporary media law — the scope of fair use for broadcast commentary and the ability of creators to enforce platform contracts against third-party users of those platforms. The Second Circuit’s short, pragmatic opinion resolving Santos v. Kimmel confirms what many practitioners already suspected: when a creator’s copyright claim is likely to be defeated by a robust fair‑use defense, attempts to route around that problem by enforcing platform terms or asserting implied contracts face steep doctrinal hurdles. The decision is a useful reminder that platform terms do not automatically give creators a private enforcement route, and that pleading an implied-in-fact contract or third‑party beneficiary claim requires far more than alleging injury and citing terms posted by a platform.

What the court held and why it matters in practice
The panel affirmed dismissal of Santos’s copyright and state‑law claims. On the copyright side the court followed familiar Second Circuit lines: the use at issue — late‑night television segments that aired Cameo videos Santos had recorded in response to requests — was, as a matter of law at the pleading stage, a transformative, critical use that falls within fair use. That conclusion effectively foreclosed Santos’s copyright theory.

Santos then tried to vindicate his interests under state law, asserting (1) breach of Cameo’s Terms of Service (a third‑party beneficiary theory governed by Illinois law), (2) an implied‑in‑fact contract with Kimmel and the broadcasters (governed by New York law), and (3) fraudulent inducement. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of each claim, but not by relying on one catch‑all rationale: it rejected the third‑party beneficiary theory because Illinois law presumes contracts do not confer benefits on non‑parties absent an express declaration that the contracting parties intended third‑party enforcement; it rejected the implied‑in‑fact contract theory because Santos’s complaint did not plead the essential terms or a meeting of the minds required under New York law; and it rejected the fraud claim for failure to allege out‑of‑pocket loss. Importantly, the court declined to resolve whether the state‑law claims were preempted by the Copyright Act, instead disposing of them on the state‑law merits.

Why the court’s analysis on the state‑law claims is important
Two doctrinal points are worth emphasizing.

First, courts will not use platform boilerplate to manufacture a contractual remedy for nonparties unless the contract itself clearly contemplates such enforcement. The Illinois presumption against third‑party beneficiaries is a demanding one: when the contracting parties intend third‑party enforcement, “they said so expressly,” and the absence of such language dooms the claim. That principle protects platforms from creative attempts by content creators to treat posted Terms of Service as an open-ended source of third‑party rights. From a policy perspective, the Second Circuit’s application of the presumption prevents a flood of litigation in which every user whose content was reused in ways they dislike seeks to invoke platform terms that were never drafted with third‑party enforcement in mind.

Second, alleging an implied‑in‑fact contract with a person who solicited content through a platform (here, Kimmel) is not a free pass around formalities. New York law requires pleading the essential terms and a meeting of the minds; an allegation that a user requested a video and a creator complied, without more, does not plausibly establish the mutual assent necessary to form an enforceable agreement imposing the platform’s terms on the requester. The court’s approach preserves the distinction between transactional bargains (which can create enforceable duties) and the informal interactions that characterize much of platform commerce.

Analytical perspective: fair use remains the real gatekeeper
The court’s readiness to treat fair use as dispositive at the pleading stage is significant. Fair‑use defenses have been increasingly adjudicated early in litigation when the facts necessary to evaluate the statutory factors are supplied by the complaint and incorporated documents. Here, the videos were short, published immediately, and they were used in a clearly critical, contextual manner on national television. Those facts enabled the court to find transformative purpose and lack of market usurpation without discovery. For creators, the lesson is stark: if the defendant’s reuse is plainly commentary or criticism (and especially when it is unlikely the creator would have a licensing market for critical uses), copyright law may not offer a remedy, and state‑law contract theories face their own obstacles.

Where the decision leaves unresolved law: preemption and the limits of state law
Although the Second Circuit disposed of Santos’s state‑law claims on the merits, it expressly avoided resolving whether they were preempted by the Copyright Act. That question remains consequential. If state‑law claims that hinge on ownership and use of copyrighted material are consistently held preempted, creators could be shut out of state law avenues entirely, leaving them to rely solely on copyright — which, as this case shows, may be unusable in many commentary contexts. Conversely, an approach that allows carefully tailored state‑law claims to proceed could provide creators some options when fair use blocks copyright relief. Practitioners should expect future litigation to test the preemption boundary: claims that focus on contractual promises distinct from copyright interests (for example, confidentiality or express licensing promises made outside the scope of the Copyright Act) might fare differently than third‑party enforcement theories that essentially repackage infringement claims as contract disputes.

Practical takeaways for platforms and creators
For platforms that host paid-recorded messages or personalized content (Cameo and dozens of others), the opinion suggests three practical drafting moves.

1) If platforms want users to be able to confer enforceable rights on content recipients or other noncontracting third parties, they should include explicit third‑party beneficiary clauses identifying who may enforce what promises. Silence or general statements will not be enough under jurisdictions like Illinois; clarity encourages predictability and reduces litigation.

2) Where platforms intend to create licensing regimes or to preserve certain markets (for instance, to allow creators to control broadcast uses), they should draft clear licensing terms and provide mechanisms for creators to opt into or out of particular uses. If a platform’s business model involves facilitating dissemination to media outlets, platforms should consider explicit licensing provisions that spell out who can reuse content and under what conditions.

3) Platforms should also consider enabling express, bilateral agreements between creator and requester when the parties wish to limit downstream uses. The Santos decision highlights the weakness of implied bargains in the platform context; providing lightweight workflows that capture assent to specific terms (checkbox consents, toggleable restrictions, or explicit “do not broadcast” certificates) will create better evidence of mutual assent than the nebulous interactions the court rejected.

For creators, the practical lesson is similarly firm: do not assume that platform terms (or informal interactions) will provide a dependable legal remedy if your content is reused in unwelcome ways. Where possible, capture express agreements about permitted uses at the time you create content, and if preventing broadcast or reuse is important, consider contractual language that binds the requester directly — not just a claim against the platform.

Downstream litigation and policy effects
Expect litigants on both sides to draw from Santos. Media defendants will point to it as a model for early dismissal when their reuse of platform‑originated content is critical or parodic. Creators and their counsel, recognizing the limits of both copyright and informal contract claims, may push platforms to redesign user workflows so that enforceable licenses are created at the point of sale. Legislatures and regulators paying attention to creator economics might also see in this line of cases an argument for disclosure rules or default licensing regimes that protect small creators who cannot negotiate bespoke contracts.

A final note on temperament: the court did not reach for expansive pronouncements about the shape of online contracting generally, nor did it treat the platform as a safe harbor for bad actors. Instead, it applied traditional doctrines — third‑party beneficiary presumptions, meeting‑of‑the‑minds requirements, and pleading standards for fraud — to a contemporary fact pattern. That methodological restraint means the decision is a dependable guidepost rather than a doctrinal earthquake, but it leaves open important questions (notably preemption) that will surface again as platforms and creators continue to collide in new forms of expressive reuse.



# Market harm in the meme era: Why mockery does not substitute

Why this matters: The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel is a fast, clear application of the fourth fair-use factor in a modern setting where short, paid, personalized videos—so‑called “shout-outs” or Cameo-style messages—are repurposed for broadcast satire. The court held that Jimmy Kimmel’s on‑air lampooning of George Santos did not “usurp” any realistic licensing market for those videos. That conclusion rests on a familiar but philosophically important principle in copyright law: criticism and parody are rarely the kind of downstream uses that copyright owners can plausibly claim to have lost licensing revenue for. As content creation migrates into platforms that sell short, personalized clips for money, the Santos decision clarifies how courts are likely to treat claimed market harm when the secondary use is criticism or mockery.

The court’s reasoning, focused on factor four
Under Section 107’s fourth fair‑use factor, courts ask whether the secondary use has the effect of “usurp[ing] the market for the original by offering a competing substitute.” The Second Circuit leans on Campbell, Hachette, and recent Second Circuit precedent to draw a bright line: uses that are critical, lampooning, or otherwise “transformative” in the sense of adding commentary do not ordinarily create a plausible licensing market that could be usurped. In Santos, the complaint itself described the Kimmel segments as sarcastic commentary aimed at exposing Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for money. Because the reasonable observer would read the original Cameo videos as inspirational promotional messages—not as invites to be lampooned—the show’s use added a different communicative purpose. That difference matters: a creator of an imaginative or expressive work is not understood to intend or to commercialize permission for others to license praise or parody of that work back to them.

This is not just a matter of subjective intent. As the court emphasized, fair‑use analysis asks what an ordinary observer would perceive. If the broadcast use “adds something new” — humor, satire, or critical commentary — that use is not a market substitute for the original. The Santos court therefore held that Santos’s allegations fail to show a cognizable lost market for licensing the videos for critical broadcast uses. Because alleged market harm must be about a likely, cognizable market in the ordinary course of exploitation, the court concluded Kimmel’s lampooning did not qualify.

Why courts generally exclude licensing markets for criticism and lampooning
The Santos decision follows a long line of cases that refuse to equate every conceivable monetizable downstream use with a realistic licensing market. There are two interlocking reasons for that refusal.

First, the nature of the original work matters. Authors of creative works are not traditionally understood to have a licensing market for critical reviews or parodies of their work. Campbell made that explicit: the Court observed that creators of imaginative works will not be expected to sell licenses to critics or satirists, and thus those secondary uses are removed from the “notion of a potential licensing market.” The Second Circuit in Hachette and others have applied the same logic when the secondary use is commentary and does not substitute for the original’s market.

Second, and practically, permitting rights‑holders to claim lost licensing fees for criticism would let copyright become a tool to chill speech. If every critical mashup, parody clip, or mocking excerpt were treated as market usurpation meriting damages, copyright would confer an unwieldy control over public discourse. Courts are therefore circumspect about recognizing licensing markets that would effectively give authors veto power over commentary and satire.

Is there a cognizable licensing market for paid shout‑outs at all?
That begs the narrower empirical question Santos raises indirectly: is there any realistic, cognizable market in which a rights holder licenses others to rebroadcast the very shout‑outs they sell? The answer is usually no, for multiple reasons.

Cameo‑style clips are sold with a particular communicative context: a personalized message from a celebrity to a named recipient, often produced on the understanding that the clip will be used privately or shared among the recipient’s network. That market—personalized, one‑off engagement for fans—differs in kind from a market for syndicated, editorial, or parodic uses. To be a plausible licensing market, there must be an ordinary course of exploitation by which rights holders routinely license their works in the relevant secondary market. There is little evidence that sellers of personalized shout‑outs habitually license those clips for use in televised satire or commentary. Where such licensing exists (for example, when a studio licenses a film clip for use in a documentary or compilation), it is because the parties operate in an industry where such permissions are standard. Cameo‑style transactions are generally not part of that industry practice.

Edge cases: where substitution might approach plausibility
That does not mean factor four is a talisman that always favors fair use. There are plausible edge cases where a secondary use might more closely approach a market substitute, and the court’s analysis leaves room for those scenarios.

One important edge is scale and systematicity. A single brief clip used for a short comedic bit is different from a program that systematically harvests paid shout‑outs to assemble a monetized compilation. If a broadcaster were to create a regular series that repackages dozens or hundreds of paid personalized videos, monetizes the series, and thereby competes with platforms or creators who might sell compilation rights, a court might treat that as closer to market substitution. The risk is especially acute if the secondary use is functionally identical to what the copyright owner would license—same format, same audience, same monetization mechanism.

Another edge is the nature of the secondary product. Suppose a broadcaster takes paid messages and rebroadcasts them unaltered as a paid subscription channel, or sells a DVD or streaming package that is essentially a rebroadcast of the shout‑outs. That would look a lot more like usurpation: the secondary product would be a direct commercial substitute. Similarly, uses that transform the clips only marginally or repurpose them for the same expressive function (e.g., selling personalized messages as part of a greeting-card service or compiling them into a “best of” collection marketed to the creators’ original fans) are more plausibly within the rights holder’s market.

A third edge is contractual: when the original seller has explicitly reserved the right to control secondary uses, or when the platform’s terms and the industry’s expectations create a clear licensing market, the fourth factor may cut the other way. The Santos court did not reach contract preemption questions (or, where it did, it rejected Santos’s state law claims on other grounds), but a rights holder with a carefully drafted contractual regime that restricts redistribution and monetization could have stronger non‑copyright remedies—and, potentially, a more plausible claim of a licensing market, if the contract reflects an actual market practice.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Santos v. Kimmel is doctrinally unsurprising but practically significant. It reinforces the existing principle that criticism and mockery are paradigmatic fair uses for the purposes of market analysis under factor four. For creators and platforms, the decision signals that claims of lost licensing revenue will not easily survive a pleading-stage challenge when the secondary use is commentary and the alleged market would require rights holders to license criticism.

We should expect a few practical consequences. Broadcasters and creators who produce satirical compilations of user‑generated content will find some breathing room under the fair‑use doctrine, particularly when their use is clearly transformative in communicative purpose. Rights holders who value control over secondary uses will, conversely, have stronger incentives to rely on contract and platform design—explicit licensing terms, technological access controls, and clear notice to users—rather than on copyright claims. Plaintiffs will need to plead concrete facts showing either a recognizable licensing market for the secondary use or that the secondary use functionally substitutes for the primary market. Bare allegations of lost licensing opportunities will no longer pass muster where the secondary use is critical or parodic.

At the margins, litigants will test the limits: large‑scale aggregators that routinely repurpose paid content into profitable catalogs, repeat broadcasters that make a business of rebroadcasting paid messages, or novel business models that monetize repurposed clips in the same way the original seller monetizes them could push courts to distinguish those cases from the conventional satire example. Those are the fact patterns in which the fourth factor could plausibly favor a rights holder.

Conclusion
Santos v. Kimmel is a timely application of the old idea that copyright should not be transplanted into a policing power over criticism and parody. In an era of viral clips, platforms, and monetized bite‑sized content, courts will continue to ask whether the secondary use is truly a market substitute or instead a different communicative use that the copyright owner has not traditionally exploited. When the latter is true—when the use is satire or commentary, and when the market posited by the rights holder would require licensing criticism—factor four will ordinarily favor fair use. Where downstream uses become functionally identical to the original market—through scale, replication, or explicit industry licensing—copyright’s protection may reach further, but those are the high‑threshold cases that the Santos opinion left for another day.



# No harm, no fraud: The out-of-pocket rule as a shield in prank litigation

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters

The Second Circuit’s decision affirming dismissal of George Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC turned on a seemingly dry pleading point with outsized practical effect: under New York law a fraud plaintiff must allege an actual out‑of‑pocket pecuniary loss to state a claim for fraudulent inducement. In a world where late‑night shows routinely use social‑media‑sourced clips and celebrities sometimes obtain content through deception or prank, the court’s application of Lama Holding and Connaughton shows how a longstanding damages rule can bar headline‑grabbing suits that seek to turn embarrassment, reputational injury, or the indignity of being tricked into a legal theory of fraud in the absence of quantifiable economic harm.

The decision is important for two audiences. For media defendants, it is an affirmation that not every prank or deceptive request that ends up on television opens the door to fraud claims. For would‑be plaintiffs, it is a reminder that pleading the elements of fraud is not enough; you must plausibly allege measurable economic loss caused by the misrepresentation.

## The doctrinal core: Lama Holding and the out‑of‑pocket rule

New York law recognizes fraudulent inducement as a species of common‑law fraud, requiring a false representation, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance by the plaintiff, causation, and damages. Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney is the foundational case that crystallized the rule for damages in misrepresentation claims: the plaintiff must allege an out‑of‑pocket loss. Connaughton v. Chipotle later emphasized this requirement by disallowing recovery where the plaintiff had not alleged actual pecuniary loss resulting from the misrepresentation.

The Second Circuit applied those precedents straight‑on. Santos alleged that Kimmel — using fictitious Cameo accounts — procured personalized videos from him and then aired them on Jimmy Kimmel Live! in a mocking segment. Santos alleged he created videos that conveyed hopeful, positive messages and argued he had been fraudulently induced to make them. But the complaint did not allege an out‑of‑pocket loss: Santos did not contend that he paid money, expended quantifiable outlays, surrendered a valuable right or license, or otherwise suffered a pecuniary detriment traceable to Kimmel’s misrepresentations. On that basis the court concluded Santos failed to state a viable fraudulent inducement claim under New York law.

The court’s reasoning is narrow and familiar: pleading the non‑economic injury of being duped or the reputational sting of being lampooned does not replace the need for a concrete economic loss if a plaintiff invokes the tort of fraudulent inducement under New York law.

## What the holding means in practice

Two practical implications follow.

First, fraud claims are a poor vehicle for litigating pranks or editorial conduct unless the plaintiff can tie the deception to concrete economic harm. Typical media uses of user‑generated content — where a creator receives payment from a platform, posts content, and the content later is repurposed in commentary or satire — will not, by themselves, support fraud claims if the creator cannot show pecuniary loss arising from the deception.

Second, the ruling reinforces a sensible gatekeeping function: it prevents courts from turning every embarrassing episode of “gotcha” television into a fraud litigation bonanza. Requiring quantifiable loss narrows the universe of actionable cases to those in which there is real economic injury (for example, a third party misrepresenting themselves to obtain copyrighted content and then licensing it commercially so as to deprive the creator of licensing fees, or inducing a creator to transfer rights for inadequate consideration).

## Alternative pleading strategies plaintiffs might consider

The Second Circuit’s dismissal does not mean no lawsuit is ever viable when someone is duped into creating content for broadcast. But it should guide plaintiffs in crafting complaints that survive a Rule 12(b)(6) challenge. Possible approaches include:

- Alleging specific out‑of‑pocket expenditures traceable to the misrepresentation. If the plaintiff incurred production costs, travel expenses, or other quantifiable outlays made necessary by the defendant’s false representations, those costs can satisfy the Lama Holding rule if causation and reliance are adequately pleaded.

- Pleading lost economic opportunities or diminished future earning capacity with specificity. A plaintiff who can plausibly allege that the broadcast caused the loss of a contract, booked appearance, sponsorship, or other measurable income stream may state a damages component for fraud. That requires factual allegations tying the loss to the broadcast or the defendant’s conduct, and, ideally, evidence of the market value of what was lost.

- Framing the claim as one for rescission or restitution where appropriate. If a defendant procured a transfer of rights or a license through deceit, a plaintiff might seek rescission or equitable restitution. That path depends on showing a transfer of a valuable right (not merely reputational affront) and that rescission is available under the circumstances.

- Asserting ancillary claims that present different damages regimes. Depending on the facts, state statutory claims (e.g., right of publicity, invasion of privacy) or conversion/unjust enrichment theories might permit different remedial frameworks. But plaintiffs should be cautious: statutory claims also have their own elements and defenses (and, as the Second Circuit shows elsewhere in this case, copyright and fair use doctrines can preempt certain contract or tort theories).

- Alleging a market‑substituting use where the defendant’s conduct supplanted a genuine licensing market and caused quantifiable lost licensing revenue. This is more likely in commercial exploitations than in late‑night satire, but it is a recognized theory when a defendant’s use functions as a market substitute.

Any of these strategies requires specificity in the complaint. General assertions of reputational harm, emotional distress, or “loss of value” without numeric or transactional anchors are unlikely to satisfy Lama Holding and Connaughton.

## Why reputational or expectation harms usually won’t do

Courts — particularly under New York law — have drawn a line between economic loss and non‑economic harm in fraud cases. Reputation, embarrassment, and affront to dignity are real harms, but they normally do not translate into recoverable damages in a fraudulent inducement suit unless they produce measurable pecuniary consequences. The interest protected by the tort is the economic one: the plaintiff’s expectancy interest, their out‑of‑pocket loss in reliance, or the restitutionary interest in preventing unjust enrichment.

This distinction has several policy justifications. First, it guards against speculative damage awards based on emotional or reputational injuries that are notoriously difficult to quantify with fidelity. Second, it steers litigants seeking redress for non‑economic harms toward more appropriate torts — such as defamation or certain privacy causes of action — where the law has developed different rules for compensating reputational injury. Third, it helps avoid chilling robust public commentary and satire by limiting the class of plaintiffs who can transform a media prank into an economically motivated fraud suit.

## Broader implications and downstream effects

The Second Circuit’s opinion will be cited as a useful precedent by media defendants facing fraud claims arising from deceptive content procurement or pranksters whose content winds up on air. It highlights how pleading choices — especially when damages allegations are sparse — are decisive.

The ruling also dovetails with other lines of law that protect expressive activity. In Santos, the court resolved the related copyright claims against Santos on fair use grounds, and its handling of damages here complements that protective posture by removing an alternative route to recovery that could otherwise enable plaintiffs to circumvent doctrinal limits on copyright and speech claims.

Finally, the decision may influence plaintiffs’ tactical decisions. Where reputational injury is the centerpiece, plaintiffs will increasingly consider alternative or complementary claims (privacy, publicity, defamation) and will focus on gathering and pleading concrete economic consequences — lost deals, rescinded sponsorships, quantifiable production costs, or demonstrable licensing losses — from the outset.

## Takeaway

Santos v. Kimmel is a reminder that pleading a fraud claim in New York is more than alleging a lie and hurt feelings. Lama Holding’s out‑of‑pocket requirement remains a robust gatekeeper: without plausible allegations of quantifiable economic loss caused by the defendant’s misrepresentation, a fraudulent inducement claim will likely fail at the pleading stage. For plaintiffs tempted to convert pranks and public embarrassment into fraud claims, the lesson is clear: plead the money. For defendants in the media and entertainment space, the decision is a reaffirmation that the law does not readily convert satire and deception into recoverable fraud absent real pecuniary injury.



# The missing publicity claim and the newsworthiness backstop

Why this case matters: Santos v. Kimmel is poised, in most retellings, as a copyright case about fair use. But the litigation also highlights an equally salient—yet conspicuously absent—legal front: the right of publicity. George Santos’s Complaint alleged copyright infringement and state-law contract and fraud theories, and the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal on fair-use and state-law pleading grounds. The opinion is notable not only for its fair-use analysis but for what it did not decide. In an era when celebrities sell personalized greetings and public figures monetize their persona across platforms, broadcast programs that repurpose those materials are increasingly likely to prompt right-of-publicity claims. Santos’s choice not to pursue that route (or, if he did, not to press it on appeal) leaves open important doctrinal questions about how publicity law interacts with robust First Amendment and “newsworthiness” principles—questions that the Second Circuit’s reasoning on copyright and fair use indirectly illuminates.

Why a publicity claim would have been the natural next step
The facts are straightforward: Santos sold personalized videos on Cameo; Kimmel, using pseudonyms, purchased several and then aired clips on Jimmy Kimmel Live! as part of a recurring segment mocking Santos. A publicity claim would have alleged unauthorized commercial exploitation of Santos’s likeness or persona—precisely what many state statutes and common-law doctrines guard against. In New York, the statutory provision (Civil Rights Law §§ 50–51) and related common-law privacy/publicity principles prohibit using a person’s name, portrait, or picture for advertising or trade purposes without written consent. A plaintiff in Santos’s position could plausibly assert that clips of his personalized salutations—commercial products sold on a platform—were lifted and broadcast for a profit-making television program.

But pleading proximity is not the same as winning. New York courts, and many others, treat “newsworthiness” and related First Amendment-driven defenses as powerful backstops against publicity claims—especially when the plaintiff is a public figure or the use is part of commentary or criticism. The Second Circuit’s disposition on copyright fair use underscores precisely those considerations.

How the Second Circuit’s fair-use analysis illuminates publicity-law limits
The court’s conclusion that Kimmel’s airing of the videos was a prototypical fair use centered on transformative purpose: the clips were used to comment upon and satirize Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for money, not to replicate and supplant the market for the Cameo videos themselves. That inquiry—would a reasonable observer see the secondary use as adding new expression or purpose—echoes the core of many publicity defenses. Courts confronting publicity claims frequently ask whether the defendant’s use is communicative (news reporting, commentary, or parody) or primarily commercial in the narrow advertising-for-products sense. If a broadcast segment employs a person’s image to communicate satire, news, or commentary about the person’s public conduct, courts are often reluctant to allow a publicity remedy that would chill protected expression.

Several doctrinal connections are worth highlighting:

- Transformative purpose in fair use is analytically akin to the communicative function in publicity defenses. Both lines of doctrine distinguish uses that “simply trade on” an identity (classic publicity harm) from uses that use identity as a vehicle for expression (parody, critique, news). The Santos opinion treats the television use as the latter.
  
- The Fourth fair-use factor—market substitution—maps onto a publicity plaintiff’s need to show actionable commercial exploitation. If airing the videos is not a market substitute for the Cameo product (and the court found it unlikely to be), it weakens the claim that the broadcaster has misappropriated the commercial value of the persona in a way publicity law is meant to redress.

- The court’s dismissal of Santos’s allegations of deceptive procurement (Kimmel’s pseudonymous purchases) as immaterial to transformative purpose further suggests publicity plaintiffs cannot rely on bad faith alone to overcome First Amendment-protective uses. Courts often view the identity of the purchaser as, at most, marginal where the broadcast function is commentary.

New York’s newsworthiness doctrine and public-figure status
If Santos had asserted a publicity claim under New York law, he would have run into two significant obstacles. First, New York’s newsworthiness exception is broadly read to immunize reporting, commentary, and other uses of a person’s image when the subject pertains to matters of public interest. The threshold for what counts as newsworthy is low when the person is a public figure—former members of Congress fit squarely into that category—and when the broadcast’s expressive content is plainly directed at public conduct or controversy.

Second, New York courts have been particularly protective of speech about public figures. Even where a program is profitable (late-night shows are commercial enterprises), courts recognize the constitutional value of satire, parody, and critical commentary and often decline to allow publicity claims that would confer a veto on such speech. The interplay of statutory language (which mentions “advertising or trade”) and First Amendment concerns means that many broadcast uses—even if televised on a profit-making program—are characterized as nonactionable news/commentary uses.

Preemption and overlap with copyright
Santos litigated copyright issues; defendants prevailed under fair use. But if Santos had sued under publicity law, Defendants likely would have mounted a different defensive arc: First Amendment, newsworthiness, and perhaps preemption concerns. On the latter, preemption typically runs the other way—copyright can preempt state-law claims that are “equivalent” to exclusive rights protected by copyright—but publicity claims are qualitatively different in protecting personality rights rather than expression. That said, the Second Circuit’s analysis shows some conceptual overlap: when courts find a secondary use transformative and non-market substitutive, they are signaling that the secondary use is expressive rather than pure commerce. That expressive character, in turn, bolsters the First Amendment defense against publicity claims and reduces the risk that a court will treat the use as classic misappropriation.

First Amendment defenses that typically doom publicity claims in satirical segments
Three First Amendment tropes tend to defeat publicity claims in broadcast satire:

1. The newsworthiness/expressive exemption: Uses that contribute to public discourse or commentary on a public figure are protected even when distributed commercially.

2. Parody and satire protections: When a segment uses a person’s likeness to ridicule or critique, courts frequently treat the appropriation as noncommercial expression.

3. Low likelihood of market harm: Publicity claims are designed to police commercial appropriation of identity for endorsement or advertising. A satirical segment about a public figure rarely serves as a substitute for the plaintiff’s commercial persona or endorsement market.

Taken together, these defenses make it difficult for a publicity plaintiff—especially a public figure who monetizes speech—to prevail against televised satire.

Where the law might go from here
Santos v. Kimmel is unlikely to be the last time commercialized personalized speech (Cameo-style products, influencer shout-outs, etc.) collides with traditional media. A few potential developments to watch:

- Plaintiffs with stronger publicity claims may try to distinguish Santos by alleging uses more closely tied to commercial endorsement (e.g., if the broadcaster used clips in advertising or to endorse products, or repackaged videos as a commercial product). The line between satire and commercial exploitation will remain litigable.

- Legislatures and platforms may respond. As more monetized persona products proliferate, some states may consider adapting publicity statutes to address platform-mediated transactions, although First Amendment constraints will limit extreme revisions.

- Courts may further refine the overlap between copyright’s fair-use transformative inquiry and publicity/newsworthiness analysis. The doctrinal convergence in Santos suggests a pragmatic synthesis: if a use is transformative enough to be fair use, it is likely expressive enough to survive publicity-based suppression.

Conclusion
The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel settles the copyright front for this case but leaves open the publicity terrain that practitioners and plaintiffs often see as the more potent remedy in unauthorized uses of persona. Yet the opinion’s reasoning—centered on transformation, non-substitutive market effect, and the expressive function of satire—points in the same direction as the First Amendment-friendly press and publicity precedents that have repeatedly squashed publicity claims brought by public figures after critical or mocking broadcasts. For plaintiffs who monetize personalized speech, the ruling is a reminder that creating paid speeches or greetings does not ironclad their control over future uses for commentary or criticism; for broadcasters and satirists, it reaffirms the durability of expressive protections even when the forum is commercial television.
