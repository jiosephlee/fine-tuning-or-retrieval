Title: Blog about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

# Objective Purpose After Warhol: The Second Circuit Calls Kimmel’s Cameos Transformative Commentary

## Why this case matters

Santos v. Kimmel is the latest Second Circuit decision to grapple with how to apply the “purpose and character” prong of fair use after the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision. On a straightforward fact pattern—personalized Cameo videos created by George Santos and later aired by Jimmy Kimmel as part of a mocking segment—the court held at the pleading stage that Kimmel’s use was a paradigmatic instance of transformative commentary and therefore protected by fair use. The opinion illustrates three important trends in contemporary fair-use doctrine: the emphasis on an objective, reasonable‑observer inquiry into purpose; continued judicial skepticism that deceptive or scripted conduct automatically defeats fair use; and a reiteration of Campbell’s market‑harm frame that criticism is generally not a licensable substitute for the original. For creators, broadcasters, and platforms alike, the decision signals that courts in this circuit will view critical repurposing of user‑generated audiovisual content through a fairly protective lens.

## How the court reasoned

The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), relying chiefly on the first fair‑use factor. The court framed the inquiry under the familiar transformative‑use rubric: does the second work merely supersede the original, or does it “add something new, with a further purpose or different character”? Drawing on the Supreme Court’s Andy Warhol Foundation decision, the panel rejected Santos’s insistence that subjective intent controls. Instead, it asked what a reasonable observer would understand the original videos to convey. Santos’s complaint itself pleaded that the Cameo works communicated “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” By contrast, the JKL segments plainly repurposed those videos into a textual and visual joke: Kimmel solicited videos using fictitious prompts and then aired them to lampoon Santos’s alleged willingness to “say absurd things for money.” The court concluded that a reasonable observer would see the JKL segments as commentary or criticism rather than as merely restating the originals, and that conclusion strongly favored fair use.

On Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s use should be disqualified because Kimmel made false representations when procuring the videos, the court was unpersuaded. It acknowledged Harper & Row’s language that “fair use presupposes good faith,” but observed that the Supreme Court has since softened that statement, and emphasized that the complaint did not plausibly allege an intent to “supplant” Santos’s market for the videos. Rather, the complaint itself depicts the defendants’ objective as sarcastic criticism—classic transformative speech.

The court also disposed of the remaining fair‑use factors without difficulty. The registered videos were published contemporaneously with their creation, and the complaint contained no plausible allegation that JKL’s use usurped a licensing market for Santos’s videos. Relying on Campbell and the Second Circuit’s recent Hachette decision, the court reiterated that a critical or parodic use that merely lampoons an original is unlikely to be a substitute in any cognizable market and so cannot satisfy the fourth factor.

Finally, the panel affirmed dismissal of Santos’s state‑law claims as inadequately pled: he was not a party to Cameo’s Terms of Service, he failed to allege an enforceable implied‑in‑fact contract with the defendants, and he did not plead the requisite out‑of‑pocket damages for fraudulent inducement under New York law.

## Analytical perspective: Warhol’s reasonable‑observer test in action

What is striking about the opinion is how cleanly the Second Circuit applies the Warhol principle that fair‑use purpose is an objective inquiry. Santos’s parade of subjective facts—Kimmel as the instigator, the scripted prompts, and the alleged deception in procuring the videos—does not change how the repurposed works register to an ordinary viewer. That matters because it prevents copyright owners from converting every critical or lampooning reuse into a factual contest over user intent. The court’s approach reduces fair‑use disputes to the observable expressive differences between the works, which is precisely the point of the transformative‑use inquiry.

At the same time, the opinion recognizes limits. The court was careful to ground dismissal on the specific record available at the pleading stage: the complaint’s own allegations, the attached registrations, and the obvious content of the JKL segments. The Second Circuit invoked its TCA and Cariou precedents to underscore that where the relevant features are apparent on the face of the complaint and would be seen by any reasonable observer, fair use can be resolved early. But that procedural point is not a license for premature rulings where the factual picture is disputed; it simply affirms that clear cases of commentary or parody—especially when the defendant’s use is publicly observable in the same form as the original—need not survive to discovery.

## Bad faith after Harper & Row

Santos leaned on Harper & Row’s line that fair use “presupposes good faith,” arguing that Kimmel’s deceit should be dispositive. The Second Circuit rejects a categorical role for bad faith, consistent with post‑Harper precedents. The court treated allegations of deceptive procurement as insufficient unless they plausibly show an intent to supplant the market for the original work. That reflects a pragmatic balancing: courts will police uses that aim to appropriate market share or sidestep licensing, but will not extend that policing so far as to chill parody, journalism, or satire simply because the commentator used artifice in putting together their piece.

This does not mean bad faith is irrelevant. In a closer case—where false pretexts are tied directly to an attempt to undercut a licensing market, or where the deception is integral to copying rather than to gathering material for commentary—bad‑faith evidence could tip the scales. But Santos v. Kimmel marks another decision in which deceptive procurement, standing alone and paired with otherwise clearly critical repurposing, fails to defeat fair use.

## Market‑harm framing: criticism is not a licensable substitute

The court’s treatment of the fourth Campbell factor follows the Second Circuit’s current line of cases like Hachette: the question is not whether the secondary use devalues the original as art, but whether it functions as a market substitute—that is, whether consumers would choose the secondary use instead of the original, or whether the secondary use usurps a licensing market that the original owner reasonably would have exploited. Parody and critical commentary rarely create such a market, because authors of original expressive works typically do not license for lampoons or critical reviews. Applying that frame, the court found Santos’ pleading silent on any plausible licensing market that JKL’s segments could have appropriated. That analysis keeps fair use faithful to the twin aims of copyright: incentivizing creativity while preserving space for commentary and criticism.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Santos v. Kimmel advances a coherent, somewhat protective posture toward commentary in the Second Circuit. First, it reiterates that the Warhol objective test is the operative lens for the first factor and that outcomes will often flow from an ordinary‑observer comparison of expressive content. Second, it further minimizes the potency of bad‑faith allegations unless they tie directly to market usurpation. Third, it reinforces the idea that fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage when the expressive difference is obvious.

For practitioners, the decision signals that broadcasters and commentators who repurpose user‑generated content for criticism, satire, or journalistic purposes will often win pretrial on fair‑use grounds, so long as the reused material is clearly being reframed rather than replayed verbatim as a substitute. For creators who sell personalized content through platforms like Cameo, the decision is a caution: copyright will not necessarily protect against public lampooning of those materials. Creators concerned about reuse should pursue contract and platform protections—though Santos shows those remedies are not automatic, particularly for noncontracting third parties.

Finally, platforms themselves may take notice. Cameo‑style marketplaces that facilitate paid personalized content face a tension: they must enable transactions while clarifying the reuse risks. Clear, express third‑party beneficiary clauses or bespoke licensing options could help creators retain more control, but as the Second Circuit’s contract analysis shows, the precise drafting and choice of governing law matter.

## Bottom line

Santos v. Kimmel is a tidy application of post‑Warhol fair‑use doctrine: look objectively at what the works communicate, protect critical repurposing that adds new expressive meaning, and treat market‑harm claims with Campbell’s substitution test in mind. The decision does not create a freewheeling safe harbor for all deceptive procurement or repurposing, but it does make clear that parody and critical commentary—especially when the repurposed work is plainly being used to lampoon the original—remain robustly protected in the Second Circuit.



# Fair Use at the Pleading Stage: Santos v. Kimmel as a Roadmap for 12(b)(6) Dismissals

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel (Oct. 6, 2025) is a clear and pragmatic statement about when courts may dispose of copyright suits at the pleading stage: if the complaint, the works themselves, and other materials properly before the court make plain that the defendant’s use is fair, a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal is appropriate. For media defendants fighting infringement suits based on parody, satire, or commentary, the opinion crystallizes the tactical posture and evidentiary showing that can win an early exit. For plaintiffs, it highlights what must be pleaded — and what risks turning a complaint into a self‑defeating record — if they want to push past the pleadings into discovery.

## How the panel reasoned

The panel affirmed dismissal for two linked reasons: the challenged uses were objectively transformative and the other fair‑use factors did not weigh against the defendants. The court relied on materials properly before it — the complaint, the videos themselves (incorporated by reference), and judicially noticed copyright registrations — to compare the original works with the secondary use, applying the familiar four‑factor test in 17 U.S.C. § 107.

On the first factor, the court focused on the “reasonable observer” standard from Andy Warhol Foundation and related Second Circuit precedents: transformation is an objective inquiry about how the secondary work presents the original material. Santos’s complaint itself acknowledged the videos’ original emotional tone (e.g., “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement”), and it also admitted that Kimmel’s program used the videos to lampoon Santos — a use the complaint could not plausibly recast as non‑transformative. The panel treated Santos’s contrary assertion about authorship or Kimmel’s role in instigating the videos as insufficient to undercut that objective comparison.

The court likewise disposed of the remaining factors on the face of the complaint and related records. The registrations showed the works were published contemporaneously with their creation, and the complaint failed to allege any plausible market substitution: the program’s satirical use did not usurp a real licensing market for critical or lampooning uses. The panel also rejected an argument that alleged deception by Kimmel (false identities on Cameo) negated fair use, noting that Harper & Row’s once‑quoted requirement of “good faith” has been narrowed by subsequent authority and that Santos’s pleadings did not show an intent to supplant the market for his videos.

Importantly, the opinion follows Cariou and TCA Television in recognizing that courts can decide fair use as a matter of law where the comparison between the original and the challenged work — together with the complaint’s own allegations — makes the outcome plain.

## Practical takeaways for litigants

For defendants in media, commentary, or satire cases, Santos supplies a practical playbook. First, bring the full record to the motion: attach or incorporate the works at issue, include transcripts or clips showing context, and seek judicial notice of registrations or other public records. The law permits courts to consider such materials on a 12(b)(6) motion where they are integral to the complaint or otherwise subject to judicial notice (DiFolco; Island Software). Once the works are before the court, the transformative inquiry becomes concrete and objective — no amount of conclusory pleading by the plaintiff will suffice to contest it.

Second, emphasize the lack of market substitution. Courts continue to treat the market‑harm factor as asking whether the secondary use competes as a substitute in a licensing market, not whether it merely devalues the original by criticizing or parodying it (Campbell; Hachette Book Group). Plaintiffs rarely have a cognizable licensing market for being lampooned; defendants should underscore that point early.

For plaintiffs, Santos is a cautionary tale about pleading choices as much as legal theory. When a complaint itself supplies the very facts that doom infringement claims — e.g., admissions about the original work’s emotive content or about the purpose of the defendant’s use — courts can and will take those admissions into account. Plaintiffs who want discovery should therefore plead around fair use, not hand the defendant a record that makes transformation obvious. Concretely, that means pleading nonconclusory facts that the defendant’s use was not transformative, alleging plausible market harm (showing a specific licensing expectation or actual demand for licensing the particular kind of use), and resisting incorporation of the works where doing so would allow an early comparison that defeats the claim.

A few tactical notes follow. Plaintiffs may avoid attaching the works to the complaint, but courts can still consider them when defendants properly present them on a 12(b)(6) motion if they are integral to the claims or otherwise judicially noticeable. And even careful pleading will not always save a case: when the works themselves objectively show a transformative use, no amount of alternative labeling in the complaint will likely generate a plausible inference of infringement. Conversely, defendants should not treat Santos as a universal license to dismiss all parody disputes: where the parties’ pleadings and the works create factual disputes about purpose, the degree of transformation, or plausible market effect, dismissal at the pleading stage will not follow.

## Doctrinal and downstream implications

Santos reinforces a trend within the Second Circuit that fair use is not merely an affirmative, fact‑finder‑oriented defense but can be a jurisdictionally appropriate threshold determination when the record is complete. Cariou and TCA Television pointed the way; Santos applies that guidance in the modern media context where short video clips, social media posts, and rapid‑fire commentary are the norm.

The opinion also tightens the interplay between pleading doctrine and copyright law. Federal pleading standards require nonconclusory factual allegations, and this decision shows how a plaintiff’s own facts (or failure to supply contrary facts) will be decisive. The upshot is likely to be more early motions in contamination cases: defendants will see a clearer path to 12(b)(6) dismissal if they can present the works and related public records; plaintiffs will try to structure complaints to preserve contested facts for discovery.

That said, Santos does not convert fair use into a predominantly legal question divorced from fact. The court’s exercise depended on the particular factual record before it — notably the complaint’s own admissions — and left intact the long line of authority that factual disputes will preclude dismissals. Expect close cases to continue to go past the pleadings, especially where the parties dispute what a reasonable observer would perceive, where the secondary use is more derivative than critical, or where plaintiffs can credibly allege a licensing market for the disputed usages.

## Bottom line

Santos v. Kimmel is a valuable procedural roadmap: when the complaint, the works, and registries are before a court and together make fair use apparent, Rule 12(b)(6) can and should dispose of the case. For media defendants, assemble the record and move early; for plaintiffs, plead carefully to avoid handing the court the very materials that will doom your claim. The decision neither collapses fair use into a pure question of law nor invites permissible gamesmanship; it simply reiterates that the intersection of pleading standards and objective comparison of works can end a suit before discovery when the law and the record point in one direction.



# When Platform Terms Don’t Save You: Why Santos’s Contract and Fraud Theories Failed

## Why this case matters

Santos v. Kimmel might have grabbed headlines because of its celebrity plaintiff, but the Second Circuit’s October 6, 2025 decision is more consequential for anyone who creates content and expects platform terms to provide a backstop. Beyond the court’s fair‑use disposition of Santos’s copyright claims, the panel’s treatment of his state‑law contract and fraud theories is a compact primer on why platform Terms of Service (TOS) rarely translate into enforceable rights for non‑contracting third parties. The opinion also illustrates the practical pleading traps that doom many post‑hoc contract and fraud claims against platforms and their users.

## What the court held (and why)

The panel affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Santos’s breach‑of‑contract, implied‑in‑fact contract, and fraudulent inducement claims — not on preemption grounds, but because the state‑law claims failed on the merits.

1. Direct breach of the Cameo Terms of Service (governed by Illinois law). Santos was not a party to Cameo’s TOS and claimed the defendants had breached the TOS provisions that supposedly protected his videos. Under Illinois law — which the court applied to the TOS here — there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” The panel found no express declaration in the TOS that would convert Santos into an intended third‑party beneficiary. Indeed, the court pointed out that the TOS included explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language in other provisions, undermining any inference that Cameo intended broader third‑party enforcement. The upshot: absent clear contractual language, non‑users cannot piggyback on platform agreements.

2. Implied‑in‑fact contract (governed by New York law). Santos separately pleaded an implied‑in‑fact contract claim under New York law. The Second Circuit reiterated New York’s requirement that a plaintiff plead the “essential terms” of the alleged contract and facts showing a meeting of the minds. Santos’s complaint failed to identify the indispensable contract terms or non‑conclusory facts demonstrating mutual assent. On those pleading deficiencies, the implied‑contract claim could not survive.

3. Fraudulent inducement (governed by New York law). Santos alleged that Kimmel (using fictitious names) induced him to record videos by misrepresenting the intended use. New York law requires a showing of out‑of‑pocket loss to recover for common‑law fraud. The panel agreed with the district court that Santos did not plausibly allege any actual, pecuniary out‑of‑pocket loss as a result of the alleged misrepresentations, so the fraud claim failed as pleaded.

Importantly, the panel expressly avoided resolving whether the Copyright Act preempted Santos’s contract claims. Although the district court had made preemption arguments, the appeals panel concluded that it need not reach the preemption question because the state‑law theories failed independently under state law. That posture — resolving state claims on the merits rather than on federal preemption grounds — is both pragmatic and doctrinally significant.

## Analytical perspective

The panel’s approach reflects a familiar judicial instinct: where ordinary contract doctrines dispose of a case, courts will typically avoid the broader, federal preemption question. That moderation reduces federal‑state friction and confines decisionmaking to well‑trodden contract principles. But the opinion also reinforces a more concrete message to creators and litigants: platform design (choice‑of‑law clauses, express beneficiary language, and who actually contracts with whom) and careful pleading matter hugely.

The court’s reliance on the presumption against third‑party beneficiaries is doctrinally orthodox. Platforms typically draft TOS to govern the relationship between the platform and its users; courts will not read those agreements to confer rights on non‑parties without an unambiguous statement to that effect. The Second Circuit highlighted that the TOS’ inclusion of explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language in some places cuts strongly against implying such a remedy elsewhere — a classic application of the maxim that express inclusions imply exclusions.

The implied‑contract ruling illustrates the practical difficulty of converting a platform interaction into a bilateral contractual relationship with a creator who never negotiated or signed the platform agreement. Absent some specific interaction or contemporaneous agreement showing mutual assent to concrete terms, courts will treat such claims as conclusory.

Finally, the fraud ruling is a reminder that tort theories commonly invoked in platform disputes — fraud, misrepresentation, unjust enrichment — are subject to well‑established damages doctrines. In New York, the requirement of out‑of‑pocket loss for common‑law fraud is a high bar for plaintiffs who suffered reputational or non‑pecuniary harms.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several implications flow from this relatively compact opinion:

- For creators, the decision underscores that the architecture of platform agreements — and who signs them — determines enforceability. Expect courts to decline third‑party enforcement absent express language.
- For litigators, the ruling is a cautionary tale about pleading. Courts in the Second Circuit will dismiss contract claims at the 12(b)(6) stage when the complaint lacks factual allegations showing essential terms and a meeting of the minds.
- The court’s decision to sidestep preemption leaves unsettled questions about the interplay between state contract theories and federal copyright law. The panel’s approach reduces the likelihood of a near‑term Supreme Court showdown on preemption in similar factual settings, but it also leaves open lower‑court variance about when preemption should be invoked.
- Platforms can take comfort: carefully drafted TOS that make clear who may enforce provisions and that delineate rights and remedies will be strong shields against third‑party claims. Conversely, platforms that leave ambiguities may find courts interpreting those ambiguities narrowly against third‑party enforcement.
- Finally, the opinion signals that plaintiffs seeking to hold platforms or platform users accountable for downstream uses of content should be prepared to show a contemporaneous contractual relationship or concrete, pecuniary reliance losses, not merely disappointment or reputational harm.

## Practical takeaways for creators and practitioners

There are three practical lessons the Second Circuit’s decision makes vivid:

First, if you want enforceable use restrictions or licenses, get your own written agreement. Relying on a platform’s TOS — particularly when you are not a signatory — is risky. A bilateral license or a platform contract that expressly names you as an intended beneficiary is far more likely to create enforceable rights.

Second, preserve evidence of a meeting of the minds. If you expect to license content to a third party (including a platform user), contemporaneous communications that establish the essential terms (scope, duration, consideration, permitted uses) and mutual assent will be critical both to enforcement and to surviving early motions to dismiss.

Third, for fraud and inducement claims, plead and preserve concrete, out‑of‑pocket damages. Generalized reputational harms or the abstract claim that content was “used improperly” will not satisfy jurisdictions like New York that require pecuniary loss for common‑law fraud. Think in terms of measurable reliance — money paid, costs incurred, or a demonstrable loss of a pecuniary opportunity tied to the misrepresentation.

## Conclusion

Santos v. Kimmel is a useful reminder that platform dynamics and contract drafting matter as much as, if not more than, the substantive rights creators imagine they have. The Second Circuit didn’t need to—and wisely did not—decide thorny federal preemption questions when time‑tested contract and tort rules resolved the dispute. For creators, the message is straightforward: if you want enforceable control over how content is used, don’t rely on passive expectations about platform terms; secure clear, express agreements and preserve evidence of reliance and damages.



# Satire, Stings, and the Creator Economy: The Broader Stakes of Santos v. Kimmel for Cameo and Political Speech

## Why this case matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel does more than dispose of one vexed lawsuit by a disgraced former congressman. It signals how courts will treat the recycling of short, commissioned creator content when that recycling is deployed as social or political commentary. In an age when public figures monetize personalized videos through platforms like Cameo, and when late‑night hosts and online journalists regularly repurpose those clips for satire, Santos v. Kimmel clarifies that copyright is not a backstop against all second‑use mockery. The ruling therefore affects not only the familiar constitutional turf of parody and commentary, but also the practical bargaining power of creators, the drafting of platform terms, and the risk calculus of media producers who procure content by ruse or disguise.

## What the court did

Applying settled Second Circuit fair‑use law, the panel affirmed dismissal of George Santos’s copyright claim at the pleading stage. Santos had produced short personalized videos for requests placed via Cameo; an impersonating user obtained those videos from Santos, and Jimmy Kimmel aired them on Jimmy Kimmel Live! as part of a recurring segment lampooning Santos. The court agreed with the district court that the JKL segments were transformative commentary — they repurposed Santos’s upbeat messages to critique and ridicule his willingness to say outrageous things for money — and therefore strongly favored fair use under the first statutory factor. The panel found the other factors likewise unhelpful to Santos: the videos were published contemporaneously with their creation; there was no plausible allegation that Kimmel’s use usurped a market for licensing critical commentary; and assertions of bad faith, born of Kimmel’s use of fictitious requestors, did not defeat fair use as alleged. The court also affirmed dismissal of Santos’s state‑law contract and fraud claims for failure to plead a valid third‑party beneficiary or an out‑of‑pocket loss.

## Reading the court’s reasoning

Two doctrinal points stand out. First, the panel hews to the modern, First Amendment‑inflected understanding of fair use in which transformative purpose carries heavy weight. The court relied on the reasonable‑observer standard from Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith to assess whether the secondary use added new meaning or message; once that transformation is clear on the pleadings, dismissal was appropriate. Second, the opinion treats Harper & Row’s suggestion that fair use presupposes good faith as of diminished force; allegations that a defendant secured clips by deception are relevant facts but do not automatically override a strong transformative purpose or convert commentary into substitutional use.

The court’s failure to credit Santos’s argument that Kimmel “instigated” the original videos is also telling. A plaintiff’s claim that a defendant prompted creation does not, by itself, nullify a later use’s transformative character. That aligns with prior decisions recognizing that the same raw materials can serve different expressive ends — and that copyright will not necessarily police the tactics by which those materials are obtained when the use is criticism, commentary, or parody.

Finally, the panel was willing to resolve fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage because the pleadings and incorporated materials left no plausible dispute about the key facts. That procedural posture makes the decision particularly significant: it suggests that obviously transformative reuses — even of commissioned, personalized content — can be dismissed early as non‑actionable.

## Doctrinal and downstream implications

The most immediate legal takeaways are unsurprising to fair‑use watchers: satire, criticism, and political commentary deployed by media outlets remain robustly protected, and courts will not reflexively allow copyright owners to block lampooning even when the underlying clips were created for pay. But the case also crystallizes several ripples worth noting.

First, monetized personalized videos are more vulnerable than their creators may expect. A celebrity or politician who sells a short clip on Cameo may understandably think copyright gives them control over reuse. Santos shows that copyright enforcement may not succeed if a media defendant reframes the clip for commentary and does not compete in a licensing market for satire. That does not mean creators have no recourse, but it does reduce the deterrent effect of copyright.

Second, the decision implicitly places greater emphasis on contract and platform governance as practical tools to control reuse. Because Santos was not a party to Cameo’s Terms of Service and could not plausibly show third‑party beneficiary status, contract law offered no rescue. That gap pushes creators to rely on the platform’s enforcement tools or to negotiate bespoke licensing arrangements when control over future dissemination is essential.

Third, the opinion strengthens the predictability of fair use for late‑night shows, journalists, and creators of critical content. Media defendants who can demonstrably add commentary, transformation, or new meaning to short clips should feel more confident that a copyright suit will not survive a motion to dismiss. This is particularly true in the political sphere, where courts remain attentive to the public‑interest value of commentary on public figures.

At the same time, Santos does carve out limits. The court’s analysis repeatedly invokes the market‑substitution test from Campbell: fair use is far less likely to shield a secondary work that functions as a market substitute for the original. If a defendant’s reuse offers the same product the plaintiff markets — or if the secondary use strips the creative work of its expressive core without adding new meaning — fair‑use protection will wane. And while the court was skeptical that Kimmel’s deception alone defeated fair use, egregious or unlawful conduct in obtaining material (for example, hacking or theft) may have different consequences, potentially implicating other causes of action or equitable relief.

## What this means for creators, platforms, and media

Creators who monetize personalized content should update their expectations. Copyright is not an absolute shield against recontextualization for commentary; transformation and the absence of market substitution can be dispositive. Accordingly, creators who want durable control should consider licensing regimes that expressly restrict downstream uses, require attribution, or provide for additional compensation if material is republished in broadcast or mass‑media contexts. Those arrangements are most enforceable when entered into directly with the requesting user or the hosting platform, not as hopes embedded in generic platform terms that do not name third‑party beneficiaries.

Platforms like Cameo will be under pressure to clarify and harden their contractual architecture. Santos underscores that a platform’s Terms of Service can be an important locus of control — but only if the terms are carefully drafted, prominently presented, and, where appropriate, drafted to confer enforceable rights on creators. Platforms can also offer tiered licensing options (personal use only; promotional use; broadcast license) and mechanisms for creators to flag sensitive requests or to require identity verification for requesters.

For journalists and entertainers, Santos is a favorable reminder that transformative reuse for commentary enjoys strong protection. Still, media producers should continue practice‑wise precautions: obtain releases where feasible, limit the amount used to what is necessary for the commentary, and be prepared to explain the transformative justification if litigation ensues. And while bad‑faith tactics to obtain material may not be dispositive of fair use, they can produce other liabilities and reputational costs, so they are not risk‑free.

Finally, the ruling has a political‑speech dimension. Courts remain attentive to protecting commentary about public figures, and the fair‑use doctrine will often overlap with First Amendment values when the secondary use adds critical or satirical meaning to otherwise promotional content.

## Risk‑management steps for creators and platforms

Creators who sell personalized content should insist on clear, upfront licensing terms. If a creator wants to preclude reuse for broadcast, commentary, or commercial publication, that restriction should be explicit in the contract with the purchaser and, where possible, registered or recorded with the platform. Creators may also consider watermarking or short‑form edits that preserve their expressive control, and they should weigh the costs of immediate copyright registration when they foresee potential high‑value disputes.

Platforms should reexamine their Terms of Service to ensure that they protect creators’ legitimate expectations. That means offering selectable licensing options at the point of sale, drafting third‑party beneficiary clauses where the business model requires them, and improving requester verification to reduce fraudulent or anonymous solicitations. Platforms should also build operational tools — takedown, licensing marketplaces, and fast‑track dispute resolution — to give creators meaningful remedies without litigation.

Media producers and hosts should continue to rely on fair use for legitimate commentary but also adopt best practices that reduce collateral legal and reputational risk: minimize the quantity of original material used, make clear the transformative purpose on the face of the segment, secure consent when feasible, and avoid unlawful methods of procurement. Even where fair use is strong, litigation costs and public backlash can be consequential.

## Conclusion

Santos v. Kimmel reconfirms an intuitive but consequential principle: when a media actor repurposes a short, commissioned clip and adds a new critical or satirical message, copyright law will often bow to the expressive and informational interests that fair use protects. The decision will comfort late‑night shows and journalists, worry creators who monetize personalized content, and push platforms to refine contractual and operational safeguards. It does not, however, leave creators helpless; contract design, platform policy, and careful licensing remain the practical levers to manage risk in a media environment where pranks, political stings, and rapid recontextualization are now routine.
