# Market harm in the meme era: Why mockery does not substitute

Why this matters: The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel is a fast, clear application of the fourth fair-use factor in a modern setting where short, paid, personalized videos—so‑called “shout-outs” or Cameo-style messages—are repurposed for broadcast satire. The court held that Jimmy Kimmel’s on‑air lampooning of George Santos did not “usurp” any realistic licensing market for those videos. That conclusion rests on a familiar but philosophically important principle in copyright law: criticism and parody are rarely the kind of downstream uses that copyright owners can plausibly claim to have lost licensing revenue for. As content creation migrates into platforms that sell short, personalized clips for money, the Santos decision clarifies how courts are likely to treat claimed market harm when the secondary use is criticism or mockery.

The court’s reasoning, focused on factor four
Under Section 107’s fourth fair‑use factor, courts ask whether the secondary use has the effect of “usurp[ing] the market for the original by offering a competing substitute.” The Second Circuit leans on Campbell, Hachette, and recent Second Circuit precedent to draw a bright line: uses that are critical, lampooning, or otherwise “transformative” in the sense of adding commentary do not ordinarily create a plausible licensing market that could be usurped. In Santos, the complaint itself described the Kimmel segments as sarcastic commentary aimed at exposing Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for money. Because the reasonable observer would read the original Cameo videos as inspirational promotional messages—not as invites to be lampooned—the show’s use added a different communicative purpose. That difference matters: a creator of an imaginative or expressive work is not understood to intend or to commercialize permission for others to license praise or parody of that work back to them.

This is not just a matter of subjective intent. As the court emphasized, fair‑use analysis asks what an ordinary observer would perceive. If the broadcast use “adds something new” — humor, satire, or critical commentary — that use is not a market substitute for the original. The Santos court therefore held that Santos’s allegations fail to show a cognizable lost market for licensing the videos for critical broadcast uses. Because alleged market harm must be about a likely, cognizable market in the ordinary course of exploitation, the court concluded Kimmel’s lampooning did not qualify.

Why courts generally exclude licensing markets for criticism and lampooning
The Santos decision follows a long line of cases that refuse to equate every conceivable monetizable downstream use with a realistic licensing market. There are two interlocking reasons for that refusal.

First, the nature of the original work matters. Authors of creative works are not traditionally understood to have a licensing market for critical reviews or parodies of their work. Campbell made that explicit: the Court observed that creators of imaginative works will not be expected to sell licenses to critics or satirists, and thus those secondary uses are removed from the “notion of a potential licensing market.” The Second Circuit in Hachette and others have applied the same logic when the secondary use is commentary and does not substitute for the original’s market.

Second, and practically, permitting rights‑holders to claim lost licensing fees for criticism would let copyright become a tool to chill speech. If every critical mashup, parody clip, or mocking excerpt were treated as market usurpation meriting damages, copyright would confer an unwieldy control over public discourse. Courts are therefore circumspect about recognizing licensing markets that would effectively give authors veto power over commentary and satire.

Is there a cognizable licensing market for paid shout‑outs at all?
That begs the narrower empirical question Santos raises indirectly: is there any realistic, cognizable market in which a rights holder licenses others to rebroadcast the very shout‑outs they sell? The answer is usually no, for multiple reasons.

Cameo‑style clips are sold with a particular communicative context: a personalized message from a celebrity to a named recipient, often produced on the understanding that the clip will be used privately or shared among the recipient’s network. That market—personalized, one‑off engagement for fans—differs in kind from a market for syndicated, editorial, or parodic uses. To be a plausible licensing market, there must be an ordinary course of exploitation by which rights holders routinely license their works in the relevant secondary market. There is little evidence that sellers of personalized shout‑outs habitually license those clips for use in televised satire or commentary. Where such licensing exists (for example, when a studio licenses a film clip for use in a documentary or compilation), it is because the parties operate in an industry where such permissions are standard. Cameo‑style transactions are generally not part of that industry practice.

Edge cases: where substitution might approach plausibility
That does not mean factor four is a talisman that always favors fair use. There are plausible edge cases where a secondary use might more closely approach a market substitute, and the court’s analysis leaves room for those scenarios.

One important edge is scale and systematicity. A single brief clip used for a short comedic bit is different from a program that systematically harvests paid shout‑outs to assemble a monetized compilation. If a broadcaster were to create a regular series that repackages dozens or hundreds of paid personalized videos, monetizes the series, and thereby competes with platforms or creators who might sell compilation rights, a court might treat that as closer to market substitution. The risk is especially acute if the secondary use is functionally identical to what the copyright owner would license—same format, same audience, same monetization mechanism.

Another edge is the nature of the secondary product. Suppose a broadcaster takes paid messages and rebroadcasts them unaltered as a paid subscription channel, or sells a DVD or streaming package that is essentially a rebroadcast of the shout‑outs. That would look a lot more like usurpation: the secondary product would be a direct commercial substitute. Similarly, uses that transform the clips only marginally or repurpose them for the same expressive function (e.g., selling personalized messages as part of a greeting-card service or compiling them into a “best of” collection marketed to the creators’ original fans) are more plausibly within the rights holder’s market.

A third edge is contractual: when the original seller has explicitly reserved the right to control secondary uses, or when the platform’s terms and the industry’s expectations create a clear licensing market, the fourth factor may cut the other way. The Santos court did not reach contract preemption questions (or, where it did, it rejected Santos’s state law claims on other grounds), but a rights holder with a carefully drafted contractual regime that restricts redistribution and monetization could have stronger non‑copyright remedies—and, potentially, a more plausible claim of a licensing market, if the contract reflects an actual market practice.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Santos v. Kimmel is doctrinally unsurprising but practically significant. It reinforces the existing principle that criticism and mockery are paradigmatic fair uses for the purposes of market analysis under factor four. For creators and platforms, the decision signals that claims of lost licensing revenue will not easily survive a pleading-stage challenge when the secondary use is commentary and the alleged market would require rights holders to license criticism.

We should expect a few practical consequences. Broadcasters and creators who produce satirical compilations of user‑generated content will find some breathing room under the fair‑use doctrine, particularly when their use is clearly transformative in communicative purpose. Rights holders who value control over secondary uses will, conversely, have stronger incentives to rely on contract and platform design—explicit licensing terms, technological access controls, and clear notice to users—rather than on copyright claims. Plaintiffs will need to plead concrete facts showing either a recognizable licensing market for the secondary use or that the secondary use functionally substitutes for the primary market. Bare allegations of lost licensing opportunities will no longer pass muster where the secondary use is critical or parodic.

At the margins, litigants will test the limits: large‑scale aggregators that routinely repurpose paid content into profitable catalogs, repeat broadcasters that make a business of rebroadcasting paid messages, or novel business models that monetize repurposed clips in the same way the original seller monetizes them could push courts to distinguish those cases from the conventional satire example. Those are the fact patterns in which the fourth factor could plausibly favor a rights holder.

Conclusion
Santos v. Kimmel is a timely application of the old idea that copyright should not be transplanted into a policing power over criticism and parody. In an era of viral clips, platforms, and monetized bite‑sized content, courts will continue to ask whether the secondary use is truly a market substitute or instead a different communicative use that the copyright owner has not traditionally exploited. When the latter is true—when the use is satire or commentary, and when the market posited by the rights holder would require licensing criticism—factor four will ordinarily favor fair use. Where downstream uses become functionally identical to the original market—through scale, replication, or explicit industry licensing—copyright’s protection may reach further, but those are the high‑threshold cases that the Santos opinion left for another day.
