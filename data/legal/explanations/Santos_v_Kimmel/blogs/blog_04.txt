# No harm, no fraud: The out-of-pocket rule as a shield in prank litigation

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters

The Second Circuit’s decision affirming dismissal of George Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC turned on a seemingly dry pleading point with outsized practical effect: under New York law a fraud plaintiff must allege an actual out‑of‑pocket pecuniary loss to state a claim for fraudulent inducement. In a world where late‑night shows routinely use social‑media‑sourced clips and celebrities sometimes obtain content through deception or prank, the court’s application of Lama Holding and Connaughton shows how a longstanding damages rule can bar headline‑grabbing suits that seek to turn embarrassment, reputational injury, or the indignity of being tricked into a legal theory of fraud in the absence of quantifiable economic harm.

The decision is important for two audiences. For media defendants, it is an affirmation that not every prank or deceptive request that ends up on television opens the door to fraud claims. For would‑be plaintiffs, it is a reminder that pleading the elements of fraud is not enough; you must plausibly allege measurable economic loss caused by the misrepresentation.

## The doctrinal core: Lama Holding and the out‑of‑pocket rule

New York law recognizes fraudulent inducement as a species of common‑law fraud, requiring a false representation, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance by the plaintiff, causation, and damages. Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney is the foundational case that crystallized the rule for damages in misrepresentation claims: the plaintiff must allege an out‑of‑pocket loss. Connaughton v. Chipotle later emphasized this requirement by disallowing recovery where the plaintiff had not alleged actual pecuniary loss resulting from the misrepresentation.

The Second Circuit applied those precedents straight‑on. Santos alleged that Kimmel — using fictitious Cameo accounts — procured personalized videos from him and then aired them on Jimmy Kimmel Live! in a mocking segment. Santos alleged he created videos that conveyed hopeful, positive messages and argued he had been fraudulently induced to make them. But the complaint did not allege an out‑of‑pocket loss: Santos did not contend that he paid money, expended quantifiable outlays, surrendered a valuable right or license, or otherwise suffered a pecuniary detriment traceable to Kimmel’s misrepresentations. On that basis the court concluded Santos failed to state a viable fraudulent inducement claim under New York law.

The court’s reasoning is narrow and familiar: pleading the non‑economic injury of being duped or the reputational sting of being lampooned does not replace the need for a concrete economic loss if a plaintiff invokes the tort of fraudulent inducement under New York law.

## What the holding means in practice

Two practical implications follow.

First, fraud claims are a poor vehicle for litigating pranks or editorial conduct unless the plaintiff can tie the deception to concrete economic harm. Typical media uses of user‑generated content — where a creator receives payment from a platform, posts content, and the content later is repurposed in commentary or satire — will not, by themselves, support fraud claims if the creator cannot show pecuniary loss arising from the deception.

Second, the ruling reinforces a sensible gatekeeping function: it prevents courts from turning every embarrassing episode of “gotcha” television into a fraud litigation bonanza. Requiring quantifiable loss narrows the universe of actionable cases to those in which there is real economic injury (for example, a third party misrepresenting themselves to obtain copyrighted content and then licensing it commercially so as to deprive the creator of licensing fees, or inducing a creator to transfer rights for inadequate consideration).

## Alternative pleading strategies plaintiffs might consider

The Second Circuit’s dismissal does not mean no lawsuit is ever viable when someone is duped into creating content for broadcast. But it should guide plaintiffs in crafting complaints that survive a Rule 12(b)(6) challenge. Possible approaches include:

- Alleging specific out‑of‑pocket expenditures traceable to the misrepresentation. If the plaintiff incurred production costs, travel expenses, or other quantifiable outlays made necessary by the defendant’s false representations, those costs can satisfy the Lama Holding rule if causation and reliance are adequately pleaded.

- Pleading lost economic opportunities or diminished future earning capacity with specificity. A plaintiff who can plausibly allege that the broadcast caused the loss of a contract, booked appearance, sponsorship, or other measurable income stream may state a damages component for fraud. That requires factual allegations tying the loss to the broadcast or the defendant’s conduct, and, ideally, evidence of the market value of what was lost.

- Framing the claim as one for rescission or restitution where appropriate. If a defendant procured a transfer of rights or a license through deceit, a plaintiff might seek rescission or equitable restitution. That path depends on showing a transfer of a valuable right (not merely reputational affront) and that rescission is available under the circumstances.

- Asserting ancillary claims that present different damages regimes. Depending on the facts, state statutory claims (e.g., right of publicity, invasion of privacy) or conversion/unjust enrichment theories might permit different remedial frameworks. But plaintiffs should be cautious: statutory claims also have their own elements and defenses (and, as the Second Circuit shows elsewhere in this case, copyright and fair use doctrines can preempt certain contract or tort theories).

- Alleging a market‑substituting use where the defendant’s conduct supplanted a genuine licensing market and caused quantifiable lost licensing revenue. This is more likely in commercial exploitations than in late‑night satire, but it is a recognized theory when a defendant’s use functions as a market substitute.

Any of these strategies requires specificity in the complaint. General assertions of reputational harm, emotional distress, or “loss of value” without numeric or transactional anchors are unlikely to satisfy Lama Holding and Connaughton.

## Why reputational or expectation harms usually won’t do

Courts — particularly under New York law — have drawn a line between economic loss and non‑economic harm in fraud cases. Reputation, embarrassment, and affront to dignity are real harms, but they normally do not translate into recoverable damages in a fraudulent inducement suit unless they produce measurable pecuniary consequences. The interest protected by the tort is the economic one: the plaintiff’s expectancy interest, their out‑of‑pocket loss in reliance, or the restitutionary interest in preventing unjust enrichment.

This distinction has several policy justifications. First, it guards against speculative damage awards based on emotional or reputational injuries that are notoriously difficult to quantify with fidelity. Second, it steers litigants seeking redress for non‑economic harms toward more appropriate torts — such as defamation or certain privacy causes of action — where the law has developed different rules for compensating reputational injury. Third, it helps avoid chilling robust public commentary and satire by limiting the class of plaintiffs who can transform a media prank into an economically motivated fraud suit.

## Broader implications and downstream effects

The Second Circuit’s opinion will be cited as a useful precedent by media defendants facing fraud claims arising from deceptive content procurement or pranksters whose content winds up on air. It highlights how pleading choices — especially when damages allegations are sparse — are decisive.

The ruling also dovetails with other lines of law that protect expressive activity. In Santos, the court resolved the related copyright claims against Santos on fair use grounds, and its handling of damages here complements that protective posture by removing an alternative route to recovery that could otherwise enable plaintiffs to circumvent doctrinal limits on copyright and speech claims.

Finally, the decision may influence plaintiffs’ tactical decisions. Where reputational injury is the centerpiece, plaintiffs will increasingly consider alternative or complementary claims (privacy, publicity, defamation) and will focus on gathering and pleading concrete economic consequences — lost deals, rescinded sponsorships, quantifiable production costs, or demonstrable licensing losses — from the outset.

## Takeaway

Santos v. Kimmel is a reminder that pleading a fraud claim in New York is more than alleging a lie and hurt feelings. Lama Holding’s out‑of‑pocket requirement remains a robust gatekeeper: without plausible allegations of quantifiable economic loss caused by the defendant’s misrepresentation, a fraudulent inducement claim will likely fail at the pleading stage. For plaintiffs tempted to convert pranks and public embarrassment into fraud claims, the lesson is clear: plead the money. For defendants in the media and entertainment space, the decision is a reaffirmation that the law does not readily convert satire and deception into recoverable fraud absent real pecuniary injury.
