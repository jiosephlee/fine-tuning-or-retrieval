Title: Blog about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

# From Harper and Row to Google v. Oracle: Bad faith keeps shrinking in fair use

## Why this matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel is short on novelty but long on signal. At the heart of a dispute that reads like modern political theater—Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names on the Cameo platform, soliciting personalized videos from former Congressman George Santos and then airing them on his show—the court had to confront a recurring question in fair use doctrine: when, if ever, should a defendant’s deceptive conduct in acquiring or creating copyrighted material defeat a fair use defense?

Santos argued that Kimmel’s use of false names and the resulting deception should weigh against fair use, invoking the venerable but diffuse Harper & Row dicta that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” The Second Circuit, however, followed the trajectory the Supreme Court has sketched in recent years—most conspicuously in Google v. Oracle—and treated bad faith as, at best, a diminished or limited consideration in the fair use analysis. The court affirmed dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims at the pleading stage, holding that Kimmel’s broadcast of the Cameo videos was transformative political commentary and that the alleged deception did not meaningfully alter the fair use calculus.

That posture—tucking deception toward the margins of fair use—matters for journalists, satirists, documentary makers, and those who run undercover sting operations. It also raises doctrinal and policy questions about incentives, licensing markets, and the role that noncopyright causes of action might play when copyright law declines to police bad faith conduct.

## The doctrinal arc: from Harper & Row to Google v. Oracle

Harper & Row (1985) is often invoked for the proposition that fair use assumes “good faith and fair dealing.” But Harper’s factual focus—and its concern with a prepublication market for a memoir—meant that the good-faith sentence was not a categorical rule so much as contextual observation. Subsequent Supreme Court decisions, and increasingly the lower courts, have been skeptical of treating subjective bad faith as determinative in the statutory four-factor inquiry.

Campbell v. Acuff-Rose (1994) acknowledged Harper’s language but also suggested skepticism about folding a pure “bad faith” inquiry into the mechanical determination of fair use. The trend toward skepticism accelerated in Google v. Oracle (2021), where the Court described suspicion of the Harper dictum as “justifiable,” observing that copyright is not a privilege reserved for the well-behaved. Google thus signaled that transformation and market effects deserve primary attention, while moralistic assessments of motive are a poorer fit for the statutory factors.

Santos v. Kimmel applies that trajectory in a familiar fact pattern: Kimmel allegedly obtained content by deception and used it for comic criticism. The Second Circuit, leaning on Google and its own precedents (Andy Warhol, Hachette, Authors Guild, TCA Television), treated the deception as legally immaterial to the key question—whether Kimmel’s use was transformative and whether it usurped any licensing market for Santos’s videos. The court emphasized the “reasonable observer” test: fair use looks to how the accused work functions to the public, not to the secret intentions of the parties. Santos’s own complaint described his original videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance,” whereas Kimmel’s segments repurposed them as fodder for satirical commentary—classic transformative use.

The Second Circuit also followed existing law that market-harm analysis requires showing that the secondary use serves as a market substitute for the original. Parody or lampooning rarely displaces a licensing market for the original material; thus, the court was unconvinced that Kimmel’s mockery harmed any cognizable market for Santos’s Cameo videos.

## What Santos teaches us about bad faith in fair use

There are three takeaways about the current doctrinal role of “bad faith”:

First, bad faith is an attenuated consideration in the transformation-centric fair use framework. Where a defendant’s repurposing adds new expressive content or commentary that a reasonable observer would perceive as distinct from the original, courts are likely to treat the use as transformative regardless of the means of acquisition. Santos fits squarely in this lane: sarcastic commentary on a public figure’s willingness to say outrageous things is paradigmatic of speech the fair use doctrine protects.

Second, courts will be especially skeptical of tying bad faith to the fourth factor (market effect) unless the plaintiff can plausibly allege that the secondary use substitutes for the original in some licensing market. The Second Circuit reiterated the longstanding distinction: market-devaluation (aesthetic or reputational) is different from market usurpation (offering a direct substitute), and only the latter tends to weigh against fair use.

Third, Google v. Oracle gives lower courts a doctrinal foothold to treat subjective intent as either irrelevant or, at best, a minor, context-dependent consideration. The Second Circuit’s opinion explicitly cites the Supreme Court’s skepticism and declines to allow allegations of deception to overcome the otherwise strong showing of fair use at the pleadings stage.

## Practical implications: journalism, satire, and stings

The Santos ruling is good news for late-night shows and satirists who repurpose candid or solicited recordings for commentary. It suggests that deceitful acquisition—creating false personas on a platform to obtain a recorded statement—will not, in itself, doom a fair use defense when the end use is transformative commentary about the speaker.

That said, the decision doesn’t give license to any and all deception. The opinion leaves room for limits in at least two settings. First, where the deception is designed to supplant a licensing market—e.g., a defendant obtains exclusive rights to audiovisual clips and then rebroadcasts them in a way that functions as a market substitute—courts may find market-usurpation and deny fair use. Harper’s core concern—protecting a prepublication news market for an unpublished manuscript—is an example where motive and market interplay mattered. Second, cases involving other legal doctrines (privacy, fraud, trespass, contract) remain live avenues for challenge. Santos’s state-law claims were dismissed for independent reasons, but a future plaintiff who can show concrete out-of-pocket harm or other tortious consequences of deception may succeed outside of copyright.

For journalists and documentary makers who use undercover techniques, Santos is both a reassurance and a reminder. Fair use may protect the republication of undercover-acquired material when used for commentary, but the same conduct can trigger liability under noncopyright rules—fraud, contractual privity breaches, platform terms violations, or privacy torts. And legal risk will be higher where the target is not a public figure or where the deception is part of a larger scheme to commercialize the content directly.

## Broader doctrinal and policy consequences

Doctrinally, Santos v. Kimmel is another step in the “transformation-first, motive-second” evolution of fair use. It reinforces the message that copyright’s primary function is to balance incentives for creation against the free flow of ideas and commentary; courts appear reluctant to let moral judgments about methods trump that balance.

Policy-wise, the ruling can be expected to have mixed effects. It lowers the legal bar for broadcasters and media platforms seeking to expose or lampoon public figures, which aligns with free-speech values—especially where the work is clearly commentary. But it also potentially lowers the cost of deceptive acquisition strategies, which could pressure platforms and content creators to adopt stricter contractual and technological controls (e.g., platform verification, tighter terms-of-service enforcement, or paywalls for personalized content) or to rely more on state-law claims when deception produces tangible harm.

Finally, the decision underscores a recurring theme in modern copyright litigation: when the copyright remedy proves inadequate or unpalatable, plaintiffs will turn to other causes of action. Santos’s state-law claims were rejected on their merits (or preempted questions left unresolved), but nothing in the court’s fair use analysis prevents creative litigants from pursuing fraud, breach, or privacy theories in future cases where the facts support them.

## Where courts might still credit bad faith

While the Second Circuit’s tone is clear, it is not absolute. There are at least three doctrinal spaces where bad faith can still matter:

1. Market usurpation: If deceptive conduct produces a work that functions as a ready market substitute for the original (e.g., repackaging the original service’s product for the same customers), courts will weigh market effect heavily against fair use. That is the concern animating Harper & Row and Swatch-style rulings.

2. Unpublished works and prepublication markets: Courts remain sensitive where the copyright holder has a recognized prepublication market—again, Harper & Row’s factual core. Deception that defeats a publisher’s exclusive prepublication right is more likely to be met with an adverse fair use inference.

3. Interplay with other legal rules: Bad faith may not negate fair use, but it can trigger other liability—fraud, breach, trespass to chattels, invasion of privacy, or even criminal statutes. Plaintiffs with plausible noncopyright harms may find success outside the Copyright Act.

## Conclusion

Santos v. Kimmel is a tidy reaffirmation of a trend: courts are downplaying the doctrinal weight of “bad faith” in fair use when the use is plainly transformative and does not usurp a licensing market. That line makes intuitive sense within a transformation-centered copyright regime, but it also pushes disputes about acquisition methods onto other legal terrains. For practitioners and content platforms, the decision encourages a bifurcated strategy: rely on fair use for expressive repurposing, but don’t ignore contract, privacy, and fraud exposure where deception is involved. For the law, Santos is another waypoint on the journey from Harper & Row’s cautionary words to Google v. Oracle’s pragmatic tilt toward functional analysis of use and market effect.
