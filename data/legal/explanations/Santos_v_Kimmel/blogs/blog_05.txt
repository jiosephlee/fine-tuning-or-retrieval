# The missing publicity claim and the newsworthiness backstop

Why this case matters: Santos v. Kimmel is poised, in most retellings, as a copyright case about fair use. But the litigation also highlights an equally salient—yet conspicuously absent—legal front: the right of publicity. George Santos’s Complaint alleged copyright infringement and state-law contract and fraud theories, and the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal on fair-use and state-law pleading grounds. The opinion is notable not only for its fair-use analysis but for what it did not decide. In an era when celebrities sell personalized greetings and public figures monetize their persona across platforms, broadcast programs that repurpose those materials are increasingly likely to prompt right-of-publicity claims. Santos’s choice not to pursue that route (or, if he did, not to press it on appeal) leaves open important doctrinal questions about how publicity law interacts with robust First Amendment and “newsworthiness” principles—questions that the Second Circuit’s reasoning on copyright and fair use indirectly illuminates.

Why a publicity claim would have been the natural next step
The facts are straightforward: Santos sold personalized videos on Cameo; Kimmel, using pseudonyms, purchased several and then aired clips on Jimmy Kimmel Live! as part of a recurring segment mocking Santos. A publicity claim would have alleged unauthorized commercial exploitation of Santos’s likeness or persona—precisely what many state statutes and common-law doctrines guard against. In New York, the statutory provision (Civil Rights Law §§ 50–51) and related common-law privacy/publicity principles prohibit using a person’s name, portrait, or picture for advertising or trade purposes without written consent. A plaintiff in Santos’s position could plausibly assert that clips of his personalized salutations—commercial products sold on a platform—were lifted and broadcast for a profit-making television program.

But pleading proximity is not the same as winning. New York courts, and many others, treat “newsworthiness” and related First Amendment-driven defenses as powerful backstops against publicity claims—especially when the plaintiff is a public figure or the use is part of commentary or criticism. The Second Circuit’s disposition on copyright fair use underscores precisely those considerations.

How the Second Circuit’s fair-use analysis illuminates publicity-law limits
The court’s conclusion that Kimmel’s airing of the videos was a prototypical fair use centered on transformative purpose: the clips were used to comment upon and satirize Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for money, not to replicate and supplant the market for the Cameo videos themselves. That inquiry—would a reasonable observer see the secondary use as adding new expression or purpose—echoes the core of many publicity defenses. Courts confronting publicity claims frequently ask whether the defendant’s use is communicative (news reporting, commentary, or parody) or primarily commercial in the narrow advertising-for-products sense. If a broadcast segment employs a person’s image to communicate satire, news, or commentary about the person’s public conduct, courts are often reluctant to allow a publicity remedy that would chill protected expression.

Several doctrinal connections are worth highlighting:

- Transformative purpose in fair use is analytically akin to the communicative function in publicity defenses. Both lines of doctrine distinguish uses that “simply trade on” an identity (classic publicity harm) from uses that use identity as a vehicle for expression (parody, critique, news). The Santos opinion treats the television use as the latter.
  
- The Fourth fair-use factor—market substitution—maps onto a publicity plaintiff’s need to show actionable commercial exploitation. If airing the videos is not a market substitute for the Cameo product (and the court found it unlikely to be), it weakens the claim that the broadcaster has misappropriated the commercial value of the persona in a way publicity law is meant to redress.

- The court’s dismissal of Santos’s allegations of deceptive procurement (Kimmel’s pseudonymous purchases) as immaterial to transformative purpose further suggests publicity plaintiffs cannot rely on bad faith alone to overcome First Amendment-protective uses. Courts often view the identity of the purchaser as, at most, marginal where the broadcast function is commentary.

New York’s newsworthiness doctrine and public-figure status
If Santos had asserted a publicity claim under New York law, he would have run into two significant obstacles. First, New York’s newsworthiness exception is broadly read to immunize reporting, commentary, and other uses of a person’s image when the subject pertains to matters of public interest. The threshold for what counts as newsworthy is low when the person is a public figure—former members of Congress fit squarely into that category—and when the broadcast’s expressive content is plainly directed at public conduct or controversy.

Second, New York courts have been particularly protective of speech about public figures. Even where a program is profitable (late-night shows are commercial enterprises), courts recognize the constitutional value of satire, parody, and critical commentary and often decline to allow publicity claims that would confer a veto on such speech. The interplay of statutory language (which mentions “advertising or trade”) and First Amendment concerns means that many broadcast uses—even if televised on a profit-making program—are characterized as nonactionable news/commentary uses.

Preemption and overlap with copyright
Santos litigated copyright issues; defendants prevailed under fair use. But if Santos had sued under publicity law, Defendants likely would have mounted a different defensive arc: First Amendment, newsworthiness, and perhaps preemption concerns. On the latter, preemption typically runs the other way—copyright can preempt state-law claims that are “equivalent” to exclusive rights protected by copyright—but publicity claims are qualitatively different in protecting personality rights rather than expression. That said, the Second Circuit’s analysis shows some conceptual overlap: when courts find a secondary use transformative and non-market substitutive, they are signaling that the secondary use is expressive rather than pure commerce. That expressive character, in turn, bolsters the First Amendment defense against publicity claims and reduces the risk that a court will treat the use as classic misappropriation.

First Amendment defenses that typically doom publicity claims in satirical segments
Three First Amendment tropes tend to defeat publicity claims in broadcast satire:

1. The newsworthiness/expressive exemption: Uses that contribute to public discourse or commentary on a public figure are protected even when distributed commercially.

2. Parody and satire protections: When a segment uses a person’s likeness to ridicule or critique, courts frequently treat the appropriation as noncommercial expression.

3. Low likelihood of market harm: Publicity claims are designed to police commercial appropriation of identity for endorsement or advertising. A satirical segment about a public figure rarely serves as a substitute for the plaintiff’s commercial persona or endorsement market.

Taken together, these defenses make it difficult for a publicity plaintiff—especially a public figure who monetizes speech—to prevail against televised satire.

Where the law might go from here
Santos v. Kimmel is unlikely to be the last time commercialized personalized speech (Cameo-style products, influencer shout-outs, etc.) collides with traditional media. A few potential developments to watch:

- Plaintiffs with stronger publicity claims may try to distinguish Santos by alleging uses more closely tied to commercial endorsement (e.g., if the broadcaster used clips in advertising or to endorse products, or repackaged videos as a commercial product). The line between satire and commercial exploitation will remain litigable.

- Legislatures and platforms may respond. As more monetized persona products proliferate, some states may consider adapting publicity statutes to address platform-mediated transactions, although First Amendment constraints will limit extreme revisions.

- Courts may further refine the overlap between copyright’s fair-use transformative inquiry and publicity/newsworthiness analysis. The doctrinal convergence in Santos suggests a pragmatic synthesis: if a use is transformative enough to be fair use, it is likely expressive enough to survive publicity-based suppression.

Conclusion
The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel settles the copyright front for this case but leaves open the publicity terrain that practitioners and plaintiffs often see as the more potent remedy in unauthorized uses of persona. Yet the opinion’s reasoning—centered on transformation, non-substitutive market effect, and the expressive function of satire—points in the same direction as the First Amendment-friendly press and publicity precedents that have repeatedly squashed publicity claims brought by public figures after critical or mocking broadcasts. For plaintiffs who monetize personalized speech, the ruling is a reminder that creating paid speeches or greetings does not ironclad their control over future uses for commentary or criticism; for broadcasters and satirists, it reaffirms the durability of expressive protections even when the forum is commercial television.
