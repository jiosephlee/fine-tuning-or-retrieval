Title: Blog about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

# Pleading-stage fair use is now a feature, not a bug

## Why Santos v. Kimmel matters

The Second Circuit’s opinion in Santos v. Kimmel is another clear sign that federal courts are increasingly willing to resolve fair-use disputes at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage when the complaint and the works themselves permit a clean comparison. That willingness is doctrinally significant and practically consequential for media defendants: when the alleged infringing use and the copyrighted works are before the court, fair use can be judged as a matter of law without months of discovery. Santos reinforces a line of Second Circuit authority — TCA Television, Cariou, and most recently Hachette — that treats fair use as a flexible, context-driven doctrine that can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal” where the record is front-loaded into the pleadings.

The case also illustrates how the combination of judicial notice of copyright registrations and the court’s ability to consider the works themselves as integral to the complaint supplies the factual substrate for early resolution. For media defendants, Santos is a roadmap for how to assemble a motion-to-dismiss record that stands a good chance of success. For plaintiffs, it is a reminder that claims hinging on editorial use, parody, or commentary may be vulnerable at the pleadings stage if the defendant can show transformation and lack of market substitution on the face of the works.

## How the Second Circuit reasoned

Santos arose from Jimmy Kimmel’s use, on his late-night show, of personalized videos that George Santos had posted to Cameo. The District Court dismissed the complaint, and the Second Circuit affirmed. The court followed the familiar four-factor fair-use framework in 17 U.S.C. § 107 and placed primary emphasis on the first factor — purpose and character — asking whether the JKL segments were transformative. Relying on precedent that transformation is assessed from an objective “reasonable observer” perspective, not by the subjective intent of either the copyright claimant or the secondary user, the court concluded that Kimmel’s use repurposed Santos’s videos from inspirationalized personal messages into objects of critical commentary and mockery. That conclusion pushed the first factor strongly in favor of fair use.

The court then disposed of the remaining factors without extended factual inquiry. It took judicial notice of the registrations and the publication dates and relied on the complaint’s own allegations to find that (1) the videos were published contemporaneously with their creation, undermining any argument that the works were unpublished, and (2) the JKL segments did not usurp any likely licensing market for the original Cameo videos. The court cited Hachette’s focus on actual market substitution — not mere devaluation through parody or criticism — and found no plausible pleading that the JKL segments served as a market substitute for Santos’s original works. On that basis, the Second Circuit held that fair use barred the copyright claims and affirmed dismissal at the pleadings stage.

## Placing Santos among TCA, Cariou, and Hachette

Santos fits squarely into a developing Second Circuit practice of early fair-use resolution, and it is useful to compare its approach to the relevant precedents.

- TCA Television (2016) set the proposition that dismissal is appropriate where the complaint and attached materials make fair use plainly apparent. The court in TCA emphasized that when the necessary facts are undisputed and the works are before the court, fair use can be resolved as a matter of law.

- Cariou (2013) elaborated on the transformation inquiry in the visual-arts context, cautioning that courts must compare the works themselves and that transformation can be established by changes in purpose, meaning, or message.

- Hachette (2024) further fleshed out the fourth factor by rejecting a broad “devaluation” test and clarifying that the relevant inquiry is whether the secondary use usurps the market for the original, i.e., offers a competing substitute that creators would plausibly license.

Santos synthesizes these lines: the court considered the primary materials (the videos and the broadcast segments) and the publication records; evaluated transformation from the standpoint of how a reasonable observer would perceive the works; and applied Hachette’s market-substitution lens to the fourth factor. The result is predictable in the doctrinal arc: when the defendant’s use is plainly transformative and does not replace the original market, fair use can be resolved without discovery.

## What this means doctrinally

There are three doctrine-level takeaways from Santos:

First, courts are comfortable treating fair use as a mixed (sometimes purely legal) question that can be resolved on the pleadings where the relevant facts are either judicially noticeable (registrations, publication dates) or evident from the content of the works themselves. That reduces fair use’s previously perceived status as mostly a fact-intensive inquiry that always requires discovery.

Second, the Second Circuit continues to apply the “reasonable observer” standard for transformation, insulating the analysis from the copyright holder’s contrary subjective intent. That standard advantages defendants in media contexts (commentary, parody, critique) where the new work plainly repurposes meaning.

Third, Santos confirms Hachette’s narrower reading of the fourth factor: courts will not treat any perceived devaluation as market harm; they look for substitution risk. That doctrinal choice reduces the scope of the market-harm inquiry at the motion-to-dismiss stage and increases opportunities for early dismissal where substitution is implausible.

## Practical guidance for media defendants

Santos is a practical playbook for defendants seeking an early win:

1. Attach the works. Where possible, attach the original copyrighted works and the allegedly infringing works to the complaint (or to the motion) or incorporate them by reference in the complaint. If the plaintiff’s own complaint relies on the works, emphasize that they are integral to the allegations and thus properly before the court.

2. Seek judicial notice of registrations and publication data. Copyright registrations and the Copyright Office’s public records are proper subjects for judicial notice and can be decisive on issues like published/unpublished status and timing.

3. Frame the use as transformative. Present a clear, objective account of how the secondary use repurposes the original (e.g., commentary, parody, criticism). Emphasize what a reasonable observer would perceive as the new purpose or message.

4. Address market-substitution head-on. Show — on the face of the works and the complaint — that the secondary use is not a substitutable product. Use Hachette’s framing: the relevant question is whether the secondary work offers the same market that the copyright owner would reasonably exploit.

5. Dispel reliance on bad faith. Don’t invite unnecessary disputes over intent. Post-Harper & Row skepticism and Supreme Court signals (noting that copyright is not “a privilege reserved for the well-behaved”), courts will not treat alleged bad faith as fatal where the use is otherwise transformative and non-substitutive.

6. Build an early record. If the defendant can supply the relevant recordings, transcriptions, and public context (e.g., program descriptions indicating satire or commentary), do so in the motion. The clearer the transformation on the face of the materials, the stronger the motion.

## Limits and when discovery will still be needed

Santos does not mean the end of discovery in fair-use cases. There remain circumstances in which factual development is necessary:

- Unpublished works. If the plaintiff plausibly alleges that the original work was unpublished and the status is contested, the second factor (nature of the work) can weigh heavily and may require factual development.

- Market substitution disputes. Where there is a plausible allegation that the secondary use functions as a market substitute — for example, where the secondary work is commercially packaged in a way that could supplant sales or licensing of the original — courts are likely to find factual issues about licensing markets and damages that require discovery.

- Quantitative and qualitative “amount used.” When the amount and substantiality of what was taken cannot be assessed from the works as presented to the court, discovery may be necessary to resolve the issue.

- Credible allegations of covert conduct with commercial intent. If the plaintiff alleges, with plausible detail, that the defendant’s conduct was part of a larger scheme to supplant or monetize the plaintiff’s market (as opposed to criticism or commentary), courts may decline to resolve fair use at the pleadings stage.

- Complex remixing/transformation. Some transformation claims rest on nuanced or contested claims about meaning and context that are not easily resolved on paper; such disputes may require fact development or expert evidence.

In short, defendants should not assume every fair-use case is ripe for dismissal, but they should seize the opportunities where the works and public records make transformation and lack of substitution obvious.

## Concluding perspective

Santos v. Kimmel confirms what many defense counsel have suspected and litigators have quietly urged for years: fair use is not a procedural afterthought that must always await discovery. Where the defendant can put the works and the relevant public records before the court, and where the new work’s purpose is transparently transformative (with little risk of market substitution), the Second Circuit will not hesitate to treat fair use as a dispositive legal question at the pleading stage. Media defendants should prepare their motions accordingly. Plaintiffs, meanwhile, must plead with care if they hope to avoid an early dismissal — particularly by alleging specifics about market harm, unpublished status, or other facts that make fair use genuinely contestable.



## Can platform terms rescue creators from fair use? Santos’s contract theories run aground

Why this case matters: the Santos litigation sits at the intersection of two hot-button issues in contemporary media law — the scope of fair use for broadcast commentary and the ability of creators to enforce platform contracts against third-party users of those platforms. The Second Circuit’s short, pragmatic opinion resolving Santos v. Kimmel confirms what many practitioners already suspected: when a creator’s copyright claim is likely to be defeated by a robust fair‑use defense, attempts to route around that problem by enforcing platform terms or asserting implied contracts face steep doctrinal hurdles. The decision is a useful reminder that platform terms do not automatically give creators a private enforcement route, and that pleading an implied-in-fact contract or third‑party beneficiary claim requires far more than alleging injury and citing terms posted by a platform.

What the court held and why it matters in practice
The panel affirmed dismissal of Santos’s copyright and state‑law claims. On the copyright side the court followed familiar Second Circuit lines: the use at issue — late‑night television segments that aired Cameo videos Santos had recorded in response to requests — was, as a matter of law at the pleading stage, a transformative, critical use that falls within fair use. That conclusion effectively foreclosed Santos’s copyright theory.

Santos then tried to vindicate his interests under state law, asserting (1) breach of Cameo’s Terms of Service (a third‑party beneficiary theory governed by Illinois law), (2) an implied‑in‑fact contract with Kimmel and the broadcasters (governed by New York law), and (3) fraudulent inducement. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of each claim, but not by relying on one catch‑all rationale: it rejected the third‑party beneficiary theory because Illinois law presumes contracts do not confer benefits on non‑parties absent an express declaration that the contracting parties intended third‑party enforcement; it rejected the implied‑in‑fact contract theory because Santos’s complaint did not plead the essential terms or a meeting of the minds required under New York law; and it rejected the fraud claim for failure to allege out‑of‑pocket loss. Importantly, the court declined to resolve whether the state‑law claims were preempted by the Copyright Act, instead disposing of them on the state‑law merits.

Why the court’s analysis on the state‑law claims is important
Two doctrinal points are worth emphasizing.

First, courts will not use platform boilerplate to manufacture a contractual remedy for nonparties unless the contract itself clearly contemplates such enforcement. The Illinois presumption against third‑party beneficiaries is a demanding one: when the contracting parties intend third‑party enforcement, “they said so expressly,” and the absence of such language dooms the claim. That principle protects platforms from creative attempts by content creators to treat posted Terms of Service as an open-ended source of third‑party rights. From a policy perspective, the Second Circuit’s application of the presumption prevents a flood of litigation in which every user whose content was reused in ways they dislike seeks to invoke platform terms that were never drafted with third‑party enforcement in mind.

Second, alleging an implied‑in‑fact contract with a person who solicited content through a platform (here, Kimmel) is not a free pass around formalities. New York law requires pleading the essential terms and a meeting of the minds; an allegation that a user requested a video and a creator complied, without more, does not plausibly establish the mutual assent necessary to form an enforceable agreement imposing the platform’s terms on the requester. The court’s approach preserves the distinction between transactional bargains (which can create enforceable duties) and the informal interactions that characterize much of platform commerce.

Analytical perspective: fair use remains the real gatekeeper
The court’s readiness to treat fair use as dispositive at the pleading stage is significant. Fair‑use defenses have been increasingly adjudicated early in litigation when the facts necessary to evaluate the statutory factors are supplied by the complaint and incorporated documents. Here, the videos were short, published immediately, and they were used in a clearly critical, contextual manner on national television. Those facts enabled the court to find transformative purpose and lack of market usurpation without discovery. For creators, the lesson is stark: if the defendant’s reuse is plainly commentary or criticism (and especially when it is unlikely the creator would have a licensing market for critical uses), copyright law may not offer a remedy, and state‑law contract theories face their own obstacles.

Where the decision leaves unresolved law: preemption and the limits of state law
Although the Second Circuit disposed of Santos’s state‑law claims on the merits, it expressly avoided resolving whether they were preempted by the Copyright Act. That question remains consequential. If state‑law claims that hinge on ownership and use of copyrighted material are consistently held preempted, creators could be shut out of state law avenues entirely, leaving them to rely solely on copyright — which, as this case shows, may be unusable in many commentary contexts. Conversely, an approach that allows carefully tailored state‑law claims to proceed could provide creators some options when fair use blocks copyright relief. Practitioners should expect future litigation to test the preemption boundary: claims that focus on contractual promises distinct from copyright interests (for example, confidentiality or express licensing promises made outside the scope of the Copyright Act) might fare differently than third‑party enforcement theories that essentially repackage infringement claims as contract disputes.

Practical takeaways for platforms and creators
For platforms that host paid-recorded messages or personalized content (Cameo and dozens of others), the opinion suggests three practical drafting moves.

1) If platforms want users to be able to confer enforceable rights on content recipients or other noncontracting third parties, they should include explicit third‑party beneficiary clauses identifying who may enforce what promises. Silence or general statements will not be enough under jurisdictions like Illinois; clarity encourages predictability and reduces litigation.

2) Where platforms intend to create licensing regimes or to preserve certain markets (for instance, to allow creators to control broadcast uses), they should draft clear licensing terms and provide mechanisms for creators to opt into or out of particular uses. If a platform’s business model involves facilitating dissemination to media outlets, platforms should consider explicit licensing provisions that spell out who can reuse content and under what conditions.

3) Platforms should also consider enabling express, bilateral agreements between creator and requester when the parties wish to limit downstream uses. The Santos decision highlights the weakness of implied bargains in the platform context; providing lightweight workflows that capture assent to specific terms (checkbox consents, toggleable restrictions, or explicit “do not broadcast” certificates) will create better evidence of mutual assent than the nebulous interactions the court rejected.

For creators, the practical lesson is similarly firm: do not assume that platform terms (or informal interactions) will provide a dependable legal remedy if your content is reused in unwelcome ways. Where possible, capture express agreements about permitted uses at the time you create content, and if preventing broadcast or reuse is important, consider contractual language that binds the requester directly — not just a claim against the platform.

Downstream litigation and policy effects
Expect litigants on both sides to draw from Santos. Media defendants will point to it as a model for early dismissal when their reuse of platform‑originated content is critical or parodic. Creators and their counsel, recognizing the limits of both copyright and informal contract claims, may push platforms to redesign user workflows so that enforceable licenses are created at the point of sale. Legislatures and regulators paying attention to creator economics might also see in this line of cases an argument for disclosure rules or default licensing regimes that protect small creators who cannot negotiate bespoke contracts.

A final note on temperament: the court did not reach for expansive pronouncements about the shape of online contracting generally, nor did it treat the platform as a safe harbor for bad actors. Instead, it applied traditional doctrines — third‑party beneficiary presumptions, meeting‑of‑the‑minds requirements, and pleading standards for fraud — to a contemporary fact pattern. That methodological restraint means the decision is a dependable guidepost rather than a doctrinal earthquake, but it leaves open important questions (notably preemption) that will surface again as platforms and creators continue to collide in new forms of expressive reuse.
