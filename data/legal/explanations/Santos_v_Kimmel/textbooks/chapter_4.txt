Chapter 4: # Factors Two and Three: Nature of the Work and Amount/Substantiality

In the fair use inquiry the second and third statutory factors — (2) the nature of the copyrighted work and (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used — are often treated as subsidiary to the first and fourth factors (purpose/character and market effect). Nevertheless, they are important lenses for understanding why some uses that reproduce expressive works will nonetheless qualify as fair. This chapter explains what those factors mean, how courts apply them (with attention to Second Circuit law), and how they operate in the contemporary world of short, personalized videos such as those at issue in Santos v. Kimmel. The goal is to equip a student to analyze new fact patterns where defendants reproduce creative works in service of commentary, criticism, parody, or reporting.

## Creative versus factual works and why factor two often carries limited weight

Factor two asks whether the original work is closer to the poles of expression or fact. The statutory text directs a court to consider “the nature of the copyrighted work” (17 U.S.C. § 107), and cases have long treated imaginative, highly expressive works (novels, songs, films, personal videos) as more deserving of protection under this factor than factual, informational works (news reports, scientific articles). The classic example is Harper & Row, where unpublished portions of a presidential memoir — a highly creative, expressive work — weighed against fair use. By contrast, the Second Circuit and Supreme Court have also recognized that the “creativity” of a work does not by itself doom fair use if the copying is sufficiently transformative or otherwise justified. In Campbell, the Supreme Court observed that factual works are “closer to the core of intended copyright protection” but did not make the factor dispositive.

There are two practical consequences of this doctrine. First, when a defendant’s use is strongly transformative — when the secondary work adds new expression, meaning, or message — factor two tends to carry limited weight. The Second Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that transformative purpose is the focal point of the fair use inquiry, and that the nature-of-work factor rarely outweighs a finding of transformation. Recent decisions (including Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive) stress that transformation and market effect are usually the decisive considerations. In Santos, the Second Circuit described the videos at issue as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity” — clearly expressive — but held that the defendants’ use was transformative because they recontextualized the videos to comment and mock the speaker’s willingness to say absurd things. That recontextualization undercut Santos’s argument that factor two should block fair use.

Second, unpublished status amplifies factor two but does not control. The Supreme Court has treated the use of unpublished works as a special concern because authors have a stronger right to control first publication. Harper & Row is the paradigmatic case: unauthorized quotation of unpublished memoirs weighed heavily against fair use. But later opinions (and the Court’s more recent skepticism about bad-faith considerations) caution that unpublished status is only one ingredient, not a trump card. The courts will weigh unpublished status against whether the use is transformative and whether it usurps any realistic licensing market.

For law students, the practical rule is: identify whether the original work is expressive or factual and whether it is published. Treat that assessment as a contextual input to the larger inquiry, not as dispositive. If the defendant has added new expression, meaning, or purpose, factor two will usually be a weak argument for the plaintiff.

## Publication status and its significance; statutory definition of publication

Publication plays a distinct role in the Second Circuit’s fair use jurisprudence. The Copyright Act defines “publication” in § 101: publication is the distribution of “copies or phonorecords of a work to the public by sale or other transfer of ownership, or by rental, lease, or lending,” or an “offering to distribute copies or phonorecords to a group of persons for purposes of further distribution, public performance, or public display.” Crucially, a public performance or display that does not distribute copies does not itself constitute “publication” under the statute.

Why does publication status matter? Two reasons. First, unpublished works traditionally enjoy greater protection under factor two because the right of first publication is treated as a core interest of authorship; courts ask whether the secondary use infringes the author’s control over the initial dissemination of expressive content. Second, publication affects the market-harm inquiry: if a work is unpublished, there is often no established market for licensing excerpts or derivative uses; yet courts still consider whether the secondary use usurps an actual or potential market.

Santos illustrates the interplay. The Second Circuit took judicial notice of Santos’s copyright registrations, which showed the Cameo videos were published the same day they were created. That fact undercut any argument that defendants had appropriated unpublished material whose publication the author wished to control: the videos were already in the public (or at least distributable) sphere contemporaneous with their creation. The Court therefore treated publication as a reason why factor two should not weigh strongly against fair use in this case. But the Court also made clear that publication does not automatically mandate fair use; rather, publication status is one factor among many.

When analyzing a new fact pattern, ask: was the original work unpublished at the time of the secondary use? If so, be attentive to the plaintiff’s right of first publication. But also ask whether the defendant’s use is transformative or substitutes for the original in a way that would cause market harm. Those latter considerations often eclipse publication status.

## Short, personalized videos: expressive content, personalization, and implications for factor two

Short-form personalized videos present a distinctive context for factor two. They are typically expressive: authors often intend to convey personality, encouragement, or an individualized message for a particular recipient. At the same time, their brevity and platform-driven distribution (for example, via a marketplace like Cameo) influence the expectations of authorial control and the viability of licensing markets.

From the author’s perspective, a Cameo video is both creative (it is a crafted performance) and transactional (it is produced pursuant to a paid request). Courts must decide whether the personalized nature of such content should increase the weight of factor two. The answer developed in modern case law is nuanced. Personalization underscores expressivity, which a plaintiff will press to make factor two unfavorable. But personalization does not substantially differentiate short videos from other expressive works in the fair use calculus: a short film, a song clip, and a short cameo video are all creative products. The critical question remains whether the defendant’s use introduces new meaning or message.

In Santos, the videos were personalized messages that a reasonable observer would understand as conveying positivity. Yet the defendants’ use of those clips as part of a mocking commentary series “Will Santos Say It?” reoriented the videos’ meaning. That reorientation was dispositive. For short videos, the Court’s approach confirms that personal expression does not immunize a work from fair use when the secondary use transforms the message and does not substitute for the original work in a licensing market.

Two practical points for short-form creators and critics. Creators who wish to maintain exclusive control should consider contractual limitations on downstream use and clear license terms; absent such protection, courts will treat short personalized videos like other expressive works. Critics or commentators who use short videos should be mindful that the short length makes the admission of whole works more likely to be necessary and hence more easily defensible (as discussed next), but they should still aim to add distinct context or commentary to show transformation.

## Using the heart of the work or the whole work: necessity for commentary, criticism, or reporting

Factor three requires courts to consider both the quantitative portion taken (how much was copied) and the qualitative importance of what was copied (whether the copied material is the “heart” of the work). The Supreme Court in Campbell and Harper & Row emphasized that taking the “heart” of a work — even if a small portion — can weigh heavily against fair use because it captures what is most valuable about the original. Yet the same doctrines also recognize that some critical uses require quotation of the most salient parts to make the commentary intelligible.

Two guiding principles emerge:

- Necessity relative to the purpose: A copying that reproduces the portion necessary to the secondary purpose is more likely to be fair. Parodies are the classic example: a parody must conjure up the original to succeed as commentary and therefore may lawfully take enough of the original — possibly including its “heart” — as required to “conjure” that original. The Court in Campbell recognized that parodic appropriation of the heart may be acceptable when the purpose is comment.

- Short works and proportion: When the original is itself very short, using the entire work is more likely to be proportionate. A 30-second video’s entire content may be necessary to the secondary work’s point; copying that whole work may not supplant the market because there is little or no market for licensing the same exact clip for the same use (commentary/parody). In Santos, the court accepted that the defendants’ airing of entire brief Cameo videos could be justified by the defendants’ need to show what Santos actually said and how absurd it sounded in context. The Court emphasized that courts must ask whether the secondary use competes with the original work as a market substitute, not simply whether it reproduces the whole.

Applying factor three therefore requires granular inquiry: could the defendant have achieved its purpose with less? If the answer is no — if brevity makes the entire clip necessary to illustrate the commentary — courts are more willing to find the amount used reasonable. Conversely, copying extensive portions of a long work where only minimal quotation is needed will cut against fair use.

A caution for litigants: plaintiffs should allege with specificity both the qualitative importance of the copied material and why its full reproduction is not necessary for the defendant’s purported purpose. Defendants should plead or prove why the particular amount copied was necessary to achieve the secondary, transformative purpose.

## Editing, montages, and contextual framing to establish new meaning or purpose

Transformation is not only a matter of adding text or voiceover; it can be accomplished by editing, montage, juxtaposition, and contextual framing. Courts look at whether the secondary work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” — new meaning, different message, or critical commentary — and editing techniques play a central role in creating that new meaning.

Typologies of transformative editing include:

- Juxtaposition: Placing a clip next to contradictory footage or captions to create irony or commentary. For example, airing a cheerful personalized message next to a title card or voiceover that calls attention to the speaker’s hypocrisy transforms the viewer’s perception of the original.

- Montage and sampling: Combining multiple works or excerpts into a composite to make a larger point about a subject often renders the pieces transformative because they are consumed as components of a new whole.

- Captioning and recontextualizing: Adding explanatory captions, graphic overlays, or surrounding commentary that re-frames the original work’s intent.

Cariou v. Prince and Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith illustrate the point across visual arts contexts: altering photographs or painting over them may create a new meaning, and the court’s focus is the net change in expressive purpose. The Second Circuit’s reasoning in Santos applied that same logic to short video clips: Kimmel’s segments did not merely rerun the Cameo videos; they reframed them as examples of Santos’s alleged willingness to say anything for money, which is a different communicative purpose.

Practically, when editing or montage is used to make a new point, courts will ask whether the additional elements are substantial enough to create a new expression, meaning, or message. Superficial changes (minor cropping, captioning that only repeats the original message) are less likely to be transformative. Robust framing that alters the viewer’s take-away tends to support fair use.

## Applying factors two and three together — analytical checklist and litigating tips

When confronting a fact pattern, work through factors two and three as part of the full fair use mosaic. The following checklist encapsulates the principal inquiries courts undertake:

1. Nature (Factor Two)
   - Is the original work primarily factual or primarily creative/expressive?
   - Was the work unpublished at the time of the challenged use?
   - Does the plaintiff allege special value in controlling first publication?
   - How much weight should be accorded to nature in light of the defendant’s claimed transformative purpose?

2. Amount/Substantiality (Factor Three)
   - How much of the original was taken in quantitative terms?
   - Did the defendant use the “heart” or a qualitatively significant part?
   - Was the amount taken reasonably necessary for the defendant’s purpose (commentary, parody, reporting)?
   - Would a smaller excerpt have sufficed?
   - Does the short length of the original make full copying proportionate?

3. Interaction with other factors
   - Does the use transform the original by adding new meaning, message, or purpose? If so, factor two’s emphasis on protectiveness is diminished.
   - Does the amount taken create a substitute for the original in any actual or likely market? If not, copying of the heart or the whole may be justified.

Litigation advice derived from Santos and related precedents:

- For defendants relying on fair use, early emphasis on transformative purpose is crucial and may allow dismissal at the pleadings stage if the complaint’s allegations themselves show transformation and lack of market harm. Demonstrate how editing, juxtaposition, or commentary changes the original meaning.

- For plaintiffs, plead the nature of the work and the qualitative centrality of the copied portion with specificity. If the work was unpublished, make clear why first-publication interests are implicated. Allegations about market harm should address realistic licensing markets, including whether there existed (or could exist) a market for the exact use the defendant made (e.g., licensing for commentary or parody).

- For both sides in cases involving short personalized videos, remember that shortness can cut both ways: it makes copying the whole work more likely to be necessary, but shortness also means there may be little or no market for the precise use, which supports fair use.

## Conclusion: doctrinal balance in the age of short-form content

Factors two and three are conceptually distinct but practically interrelated. The nature of the work (creative v. factual, published v. unpublished) informs how strongly courts protect the original, while the amount/substantiality question tests whether the secondary user took no more than was necessary and whether the portion taken captures the work’s essence. Modern Second Circuit doctrine — as applied in Santos v. Kimmel — treats these factors as important but subsidiary to whether the secondary use is transformative and whether it usurps the copyright owner’s market. For short personalized videos, courts will pay attention to both the necessity of reproducing short works in their entirety and to the transforming effect of editing, montage, and explicit commentary. The upshot is pragmatic: transformation and market effect remain the decisive elements, and a defendant who recontextualizes short creative clips for commentary or parody will often, though not always, succeed on fair use even when reproducing substantial or qualitatively important parts of the original.
