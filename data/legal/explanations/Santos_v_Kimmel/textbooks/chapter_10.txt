Chapter 10: # Implied-in-Fact Contracts in Entertainment and Media Deals

Implied-in-fact contracts occupy a recurring place in entertainment and media litigation. Creators, performers, and studios often deal informally, through emails, platform-mediated exchanges, and industry customs. Yet when a dispute reaches court, plaintiffs who assert that an enforceable promise arose from conduct confront distinct and exacting requirements. This chapter explains how New York law treats implied-in-fact contracts, what a plaintiff must plead to survive a motion to dismiss, how courts identify evidence of a “meeting of the minds,” and how courts distinguish ordinary implied contractual liability from specialized idea‑submission doctrines (the Desny line). We use Santos v. Kimmel as the vehicle for applying these doctrinal rules to a media‑era fact pattern in which a late‑night host obtained and broadcasted personalized videos purchased through a third‑party platform.

## New York standards: intent, surrounding circumstances, and mutual assent

New York treats an implied‑in‑fact contract as a true contract: it is formed by the parties’ conduct rather than their explicit words, and it is governed by the same requirement of mutual assent that underlies express contracts. The courts therefore ask whether the conduct and surrounding circumstances demonstrate that both parties intended to be bound and had a meeting of the minds as to the essential terms. As the Second Circuit summarized in Santos, “[u]nder New York law, we consider ‘the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances’ to determine whether an implied‑in‑fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation omitted).

That inquiry is objective. Courts look to outward manifestations — communications, course of dealing, and conduct — rather than to the parties’ secret subjective intentions. The plaintiff therefore must point to specific factual circumstances that would permit a reasonable finder of fact to conclude that both sides understood and consented to the same bargain. Factors courts consider include: (1) explicit or implicit communications that operate as offers and acceptances; (2) a course of dealing or prior relationship showing habitual practices; (3) industry custom and usage that fill in missing details consistent with reasonable expectations; and (4) the contemporaneous conduct of the parties (for example, performance that only makes sense if a contract existed). But the more essential the omitted term, the more persuasive the evidence of mutual assent must be.

New York also draws a clear line between an implied contract (a consensual agreement inferred from conduct) and a quasi‑contract or restitutionary claim (an equitable remedy imposed to prevent unjust enrichment). An implied‑in‑fact claim requires proof of an objective agreement; restitution does not. Plaintiffs who conflate the two may fail to state a cognizable contract claim if they do not plead the agreement itself.

## Pleading essential terms: Caniglia and the requirement of specificity

The New York authorities emphasize that a complaint alleging an implied‑in‑fact contract must do more than assert, in conclusory fashion, that “the parties had a contract.” A plaintiff must plead the essential terms of the putative agreement with sufficient specificity to allow a court to evaluate both whether a contract could exist and what obligations it would impose. The controlling point is illustrated by Caniglia v. Chicago Trib.‑N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146 (1st Dep’t 1994), relied upon in Santos. Under Caniglia and related decisions, to survive dismissal plaintiffs must identify the essential elements — who promised what, in exchange for what, and on what terms — and allege them in nonconclusory language.

What are “essential terms”? While there is no rigid universal checklist, courts expect allegations adequate to define the parties, the subject matter, performance obligations, consideration or price (if applicable), and timing or scope of performance so that the court can enforce the bargain. In employment or services contracts, for example, courts look for the scope of services, compensation, and duration. In distribution or license contexts, courts look for grant language (exclusive or nonexclusive), territory, duration, and financial terms. A pleading that merely alleges that “defendant agreed to pay plaintiff” without stating the amount, the basis for payment, or when payment was due will typically be insufficient.

Two practical consequences follow. First, plaintiffs who rely on implied agreements should plead the communications and events that supply the missing terms — for example, emails proposing a particular fee, testimony that a customary one‑time license fee applies in the industry, or an invoice reflecting a negotiated price. Second, at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage, courts will not invent contractual terms from generalized allegations of unfairness. When the alleged agreement is too indefinite or the claimed obligations are amorphous, dismissal is appropriate.

Caniglia and its progeny therefore function as gatekeepers: they prevent vague claims of “we had a deal” from proceeding without concrete allegations that can be litigated. In the entertainment context, where informal exchanges are common, plaintiffs must take care to plead with the precision that courts require.

## Evidence of a meeting of the minds: DG & A and similar authorities

Proving a meeting of the minds can be difficult when much of the exchange is nonverbal or mediated through a platform. New York courts have developed several categories of evidence that can support a finding of mutual assent, and a number of decisions illustrate both what works and what does not.

DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305 (3d Dep’t 2008), exemplifies a challenge to an implied‑in‑fact contract where the plaintiff’s allegations did not demonstrate a meeting of the minds. Courts look for objective indicia: (1) offer and acceptance — whether a party made a concrete proposal and the other party accepted it; (2) performative conduct consistent only with acceptance — for example, immediate performance in reliance on a specific promise; (3) contemporaneous statements or writing reflecting agreement; (4) course of dealing or a preexisting relationship that created mutual expectations; and (5) industry custom and usage capable of being proved at trial.

Consider examples that courts find persuasive. An email exchange in which one party proposes to license a short segment for broadcast at a specified fee and the other party replies “agreed” or pays the fee supports a finding of mutual assent. A producer’s invitation soliciting submissions with an express promise of compensation upon selection, coupled with the producer’s selection and public use, can create an implied promise to pay. Conversely, a mere unilateral performance (e.g., a creator uploads a video to a public platform and a broadcaster later uses it) will rarely suffice absent additional communications indicating assent to particular terms.

In practice, courts require more than a plaintiff’s belief that a recipient “should have known” the terms or that the creator expected payment. The complaint must allege tangible facts showing that the recipient knew the terms and intended to be bound by them. Where alleged communications are ambiguous, courts will dismiss. Where the parties’ interactions are mediated by a third‑party platform, moreover, plaintiffs must allege how the platform’s structure, the parties’ use of it, or explicit messages created mutual expectations as to use and payment.

DG & A and similar cases thus set a firm evidentiary floor for implied‑in‑fact claims: proof of an objective agreement, not mere expectation or unilateral conduct.

## Rocky Point and scope of implied terms drawn from conduct and customs

When an implied contract is found to exist, courts often must determine its terms. Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Group, Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269 (4th Dep’t 2002), illustrates the principle that courts may infer or fill in some contractual terms from the parties’ conduct, surrounding circumstances, and established industry practice. But there are important limits. Implied terms can be used to give effect to what the parties manifestly agreed, or to supply reasonable details consistent with their conduct; they cannot be used to fabricate an agreement where no objective assent existed or to create complex economic bargains where the parties left essential terms to future negotiation.

Rocky Point is useful as a guide to when courts will accept implied terms. Where conduct is consistent and continuous — for example, repeated licensing transactions between the same parties, with patterns of payment, scope, and timing — courts are comfortable reading that history into an implied agreement covering subsequent transactions. Where a particular industry has uniform customs that parties reasonably expect to control absent express contrary provision, those customs can inform the content of an implied contract. But the key is that the customs and conduct must be objectively ascertainable and reasonably certain.

This approach recognizes commercial realities: many media and entertainment deals are accomplished through standard forms, oral promises, or customary practices, and the law will enforce those bargains where the evidence shows what was agreed. Yet it also protects parties from having courts impose unbargained‑for obligations based on vague allegations. Therefore, a plaintiff alleging an implied license or implied procedural duty should plead facts showing the consistent practices, prior dealings, or industry norms that supply the missing terms.

## Comparative note: Desny v. Wilder and idea‑submission implied contracts

The idea‑submission line of cases — beginning with Desny v. Wilder, 46 Cal.2d 715 (1956) — addresses a recurring entertainment controversy: when a creator brings an idea to a studio or producer and the producer later exploits that idea, does the submitter have an enforceable right to compensation even without a signed contract? Desny holds that when a defendant solicits ideas or accepts submissions under circumstances that reasonably justify an expectation of payment, an implied promise to pay can be inferred.

Desny has been influential, particularly in California, and courts have distilled elements for an idea‑submission claim: (1) plaintiff disclosed the idea to the defendant under circumstances that create a reasonable expectation of compensation; (2) the idea was novel or of sufficient value; (3) the defendant used the idea and derived benefit; and (4) the defendant’s use was wrongful in light of the expectation of payment. Many courts treat such claims as falling within the realm of implied‑in‑fact contracts because the plaintiff alleges an implied promise to pay arising from the submission context.

But New York courts are more cautious and formalistic. While New York recognizes recovery where a studio solicits materials under an implied promise to pay, courts generally require the same specificity and objective evidence of mutual assent discussed above. New York decisions often scrutinize whether the plaintiff has pleaded facts showing that the recipient in fact solicited or encouraged submissions with an expectation of payment, and whether the parties’ conduct would lead a reasonable person to expect compensation. In short, New York does not reject the idea‑submission theory outright, but it applies its contractual pleading strictures to prevent speculative claims.

A practical upshot for students: when advancing a Desny‑style theory in New York, allege the precise solicitations, the context in which materials were solicited (for example, an open call promising “selected submissions will be paid”), any communications acknowledging receipt and promising compensation, and facts showing the defendant’s actual use and resultant benefit. Without such details, New York courts will treat the claim as an unmoored expectation rather than an enforceable promise.

## Application to Santos: failure to plead specific terms or assent to abide by platform TOS

Santos v. Kimmel provides a contemporary example of the application of these principles to platform‑mediated transactions. The Second Circuit rejected Santos’s claim that an implied‑in‑fact contract arose between him and Kimmel (or the broadcast defendants) that would prevent broadcast of the Cameo videos absent permission or payment. The decision highlights three recurring problems plaintiffs face in this context.

First, the plaintiff was not a party to the Cameo platform’s Terms of Service, and he did not plausibly allege that the parties to the transaction intended to confer enforceable benefits on third parties. When contracts are negotiated by and among platform users and the platform itself, the law presumes that incidental non‑parties are not intended to be third‑party beneficiaries unless the agreement manifests an express declaration to that effect. The Santos opinion notes that the applicable Terms contained explicit third‑party beneficiary clauses in some places and omitted them here — a telling absence under Illinois law for the Terms. The lesson is straightforward: a creator relying on a platform’s terms should plead why those terms, as drafted, were meant to protect him and why the user who uploaded or received content manifested assent to those terms as enforceable against third parties.

Second, Santos’s complaint failed to plead the essential terms of any implied contract with the defendants. The complaint did not identify what specific promises Kimmel made or accepted — there were no allegations that Kimmel agreed to a restricted license, a non‑broadcast covenant, a payment to Santos for further use, or any other concrete obligation. Santos alleged only that he “performed” by making and posting the videos and that Kimmel later aired them. Under Caniglia and New York precedent, that sort of conclusory allegation is insufficient. A court cannot infer from the mere fact of unilateral performance that the recipient agreed to the plaintiff’s preferred terms — especially where the communications occurred through an interaction with a third‑party platform and where the complaint lacks any contemporaneous message or exchange indicating an agreement.

Third, the complaint did not establish a meeting of the minds. Santos argued that the very act of requesting a personalized video through Cameo indicated an understanding about how the video could be used. But the pleadings did not show that Kimmel (or his agents) ever manifested assent to Santos’s expectations. There were no allegations that Kimmel communicated any licensing terms, offered payment beyond the Cameo fee, made promises about limitation of use, or otherwise acted in a manner that could only be rationally explained by acceptance of Santos’s asserted bargain. Performance by Santos — delivering a video — could as easily be interpreted as compliance with the platform’s ordinary purchase flow (a one‑to‑one personalized message for a fee), not as acceptance of a broader permission to restrict dissemination.

Santos also attempted to rely on the notion that a defendant who “instigates” creation cannot later claim fair use or lack of contractual obligation. But even where a defendant solicits content, courts ask whether the solicitation carried an objective promise — for payment or for restricted use — and whether the parties communicated specific terms. A mere anonymous request via a platform, absent messages establishing the requester’s intent to be bound by particular conditions, will not suffice.

Finally, Santos’s pleading problems underscore the practical realities of platform transactions. Many creators using services like Cameo have not bargained for downstream rights or broadcast licenses. Indeed, platform terms often contemplate only a buyer‑recipient relationship, and creators who wish to preserve more restrictive rights must make them explicit in messages or contracts. For a claim that a broadcaster breached a contract by airing platform‑sold content, a plaintiff should allege facts showing (1) that the broadcaster agreed, directly or indirectly, to particular restrictions; (2) what those restrictions were (e.g., no public broadcast, limited noncommercial use only); and (3) how the broadcaster’s conduct violated those specific restrictions.

Practical pleading checklist drawn from Santos and the authorities discussed above
- Identify the parties who actually made the alleged agreement; do not rely on a third‑party platform’s TOS unless you can show the relevant party assented to being bound and the TOS manifest intent to benefit you.
- Plead the essential terms with specificity: scope of the grant or restriction, duration, territory, consideration or payment mechanism, and any relevant conditions or notice requirements.
- Allege objective communications or conduct showing offer and acceptance (emails, text messages, messages on the platform, recorded conversations, or contemporaneous acknowledgments).
- Plead industry custom or prior dealings only where they are concrete and objectively ascertainable; explain how those customs supplied the missing terms.
- If claiming an idea‑submission implied promise (Desny style), plead the solicitation context, any express or implied promise to pay, the novelty and value of the idea, and the defendant’s use and benefit.
- Be prepared to show how unilateral performance (e.g., uploading a video) is not merely compliance with a platform’s ordinary terms but is consistent only with the existence of an enforceable promise.

Conclusion

New York law enforces implied‑in‑fact contracts where the parties’ conduct and the surrounding circumstances objectively demonstrate a meeting of the minds and the complaint alleges the essential terms with specificity. Santos v. Kimmel illustrates how courts apply these principles in the entertainment‑platform context: absent clear allegations of offer, acceptance, specific obligations, or express third‑party beneficiary language, claims that a broadcaster breached an implied agreement by airing platform‑sold content will usually fail at the pleading stage. For creators and practitioners, the case reinforces the importance of creating and preserving objective evidence of the agreement they intend — whether through explicit contractual language, contemporaneous communications, or an industry practice so well established that it can supply missing terms.

# Fraudulent Inducement and the Out‑of‑Pocket Rule

This chapter examines fraudulent inducement under New York law and the distinctive damages rule that often defeats fraud claims where the plaintiff received the agreed consideration. We explain the common‑law tort of fraudulent inducement, develop how New York measures damages for that tort (the “out‑of‑pocket” rule), and then apply these principles to contemporary fact patterns that involve deception by ruse—stings, pseudonymous solicitations, and prank or “gotcha” newsgathering or comedy. The recent Second Circuit decision in Santos v. Kimmel provides a compact illustration: a celebrity alleges he was tricked into producing paid personalized videos, but the court affirmed dismissal because the complaint did not plead any cognizable pecuniary loss.

## Elements of fraudulent inducement under New York law

Under New York law, a claim of fraudulent inducement (commonly called “fraud” or “fraudulent misrepresentation”) has well‑established elements. To state a claim a plaintiff must allege, with the particularity required for fraud claims, the following components:

- a material misrepresentation or omission of fact by the defendant;
- knowledge of the falsity (or reckless disregard for the truth) on the part of the defendant (scienter);
- the defendant’s intent that the plaintiff rely on the misrepresentation;
- reasonable and justifiable reliance by the plaintiff on the misrepresentation; and
- damages proximately caused by that reliance.

These elements track appellate decisions and controlling New York precedent. The “misrepresentation” element ordinarily requires a false statement concerning an existing fact; mere statements of opinion or laudatory puffery will not suffice. Promises about future conduct can support a fraud claim if the plaintiff alleges that the defendant had no present intent to perform (i.e., promissory fraud)—but courts require particularized facts supporting the claim that the promisor intended not to perform at the time of the promise.

Reliance must be reasonable or justifiable under the circumstances. New York applies an objective standard: a plaintiffs’ reliance is not reasonable if the plaintiff knew the truth or if the falsehood was so obviously false that a reasonably prudent person would have investigated further. Finally, damages are not an afterthought in New York fraud claims; a plaintiff must allege actual, pecuniary harm resulting from the reliance. The measure and proof of that pecuniary harm are the focus of the out‑of‑pocket rule discussed next.

Practically speaking for pleadings: because fraud is subject to Rule 9(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, plaintiffs must plead the “who, what, when, where and how” with respect to the alleged misrepresentations, including dates, content, speaker, and the manner in which the misrepresentation induced the plaintiff to act. They must also plead facts supporting scienter and the causal link to damages.

## Lama Holding and Connaughton: out‑of‑pocket loss versus benefit‑of‑the‑bargain

A crucial doctrinal point in New York fraudulent inducement law is the rule that damages for common‑law fraud are measured by the plaintiff’s out‑of‑pocket losses rather than the “benefit‑of‑the‑bargain” or expectation measure.

In Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996), the New York Court of Appeals held that plaintiffs asserting common‑law fraud may recover only their “out‑of‑pocket” losses—i.e., the difference between what they gave and what they received—rather than the difference between the value they were promised and the value actually delivered (the expectation or benefit‑of‑the‑bargain measure). Lama thus narrowed the recoverable damages in fraud actions: a plaintiff who was induced to enter a transaction and actually received what was bargained for generally cannot recover the hypothetical value of what was promised.

Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017), clarified how the out‑of‑pocket rule operates in pleading and proof. Connaughton emphasized that to sustain a fraud claim, a plaintiff must allege a cognizable pecuniary loss—concrete, economic injury caused by the defendant’s misrepresentations. Claims that assert only non‑pecuniary injury (e.g., damage to pride, embarrassment, or reputational harms absent a link to a monetary loss) or claims which allege that the plaintiff “would not have entered the transaction had he known the truth” but nonetheless received the promised consideration, fail to plead the required economic harm. In short, New York requires a tangible, monetary injury to accompany the other elements of fraud.

Two points follow from this doctrine. First, rescission and restitution operate differently from tort damages: a plaintiff who timely elects rescission may be able to recover the return of consideration (a form of restitution). But absent rescission, the tort claim for fraud seeks out‑of‑pocket losses. Second, New York’s limitation means that many complaints asserting deceit in contexts where the plaintiff actually received the contracted good or service are vulnerable to dismissal unless the complaint identifies separate, compensable economic losses (out‑of‑pocket expenditures, consequential losses, lost business opportunities, diminution in value, etc.).

## Stings, pseudonyms, and deception in newsgathering and comedy: injury analysis

Modern media, comedy, and investigative “stings” often involve deception: a reporter or entertainer induces a subject to speak or act under false pretenses. These contexts raise two distinct legal questions: (1) whether the deception establishes the elements of fraud (misrepresentation, scienter, reasonable reliance, etc.), and (2) whether the deception produced a cognizable, pecuniary injury under the out‑of‑pocket rule.

On the first question, courts recognize that deliberate misrepresentations of identity or purpose can satisfy the misrepresentation and scienter elements of fraud. If a defendant uses a pseudonym and knowingly misstates their identity or purpose to induce the plaintiff to act (for example, to secure a paid personalized video, an interview, or access), those facts may satisfy the “who/what/when” requirements of a fraud pleading—so long as the complaint alleges that the misrepresentation was material and intended to deceive.

But the second question is decisive in many modern cases. Courts routinely distinguish between economic and non‑economic harms. Deceptions that lead only to embarrassment, reputational damage, or moral injury, without proximate, pecuniary consequences, do not satisfy New York’s damages requirement for common‑law fraud. Frequently in “sting” or prank contexts the alleged injury is humiliation when the recorded content is later broadcast or mocked. That humiliation may support other claims in narrow circumstances (invasion of privacy, intentional infliction of emotional distress, or state privacy statutes), but it is not, by itself, the pecuniary loss New York requires for a fraud claim.

A related point concerns transactions in which the subject is paid for the requested performance. If a subject is paid the full agreed fee for their performance (for example, a paid Cameo request), the out‑of‑pocket rule generally forecloses fraud damages unless the plaintiff pleads additional, monetary harm caused by the deception—expenses undertaken because of the deception, loss of business due to the misuse, lost endorsement opportunities, diminution in the value of the plaintiff’s intellectual property, or similar measurable losses. These consequential losses must be pleaded with specificity and a clear causal link to the defendant’s misrepresentation.

First Amendment considerations also often arise in these contexts (especially when the defendant is a media organization or entertainer). While the First Amendment does not change the elements of fraud or the out‑of‑pocket requirement, it colors judicial fact‑finding about motive, public‑interest defenses, and whether a defendant’s use of the plaintiff’s content is privileged (e.g., parody, commentary, or fair use in a copyright case). But even if First Amendment doctrines justify the publication, they do not eliminate the statutory pleading requirements for a fraud tort, nor do they alter the need for a pecuniary injury for fraud damages in New York.

## What qualifies as cognizable pecuniary loss and how to plead it

Because the availability of damages frequently decides the viability of a fraud claim, plaintiffs must understand what counts as a cognizable pecuniary loss in New York and how to plead those losses.

1. Typical forms of cognizable pecuniary loss
- Out‑of‑pocket monetary expenditures made in reliance on the misrepresentation (for example, payments made to the defendant, costs incurred to prepare and perform services, travel expenses, fees paid to agents, and replacement costs).
- Consequential economic losses proximately caused by the misrepresentation, such as lost profits, lost business opportunities, or diminution in the value of the plaintiff’s property or goodwill—if those are alleged with particularity and causation is plausibly pleaded.
- Restitutionary recovery (return of consideration) where rescission is sought and permitted; restitution is a remedy distinct from tort damages and may entitle a plaintiff to the return of monies paid or the value of services rendered in some circumstances.
- In limited cases, a decline in market value (e.g., loss of licensing opportunities, reduced fees for future performances) that is foreseeable and causally connected to the deception.

2. What does not ordinarily qualify
- Mere embarrassment, humiliation, mental anguish, or reputational harm unconnected to an identifiable monetary loss are not recoverable as damages for common‑law fraud in New York.
- The “benefit‑of‑the‑bargain” expectation loss (the subjective value of what the plaintiff hoped to obtain) is not recoverable in a tort fraud action absent an alternative basis (e.g., contract damages or a statutory scheme that provides a different measure).

3. Pleading practice and particularity (what plaintiffs must allege)
- Specific dollar amounts: pleaders should specify amounts paid, expenses incurred, or lost profits claimed. General assertions of “millions lost” are insufficient without factual underpinning.
- Causation and timing: describe the chain of causation showing how the defendant’s misrepresentation directly produced the monetary loss (for example, “as a direct result of D’s misrepresentation, P incurred X in travel expenses and forfeited Y in scheduled paid appearances”).
- Reasonableness of reliance and foreseeability: allege facts supporting the reasonableness of the plaintiff’s reliance and the foreseeability of the claimed losses.
- Particularity as to fraud: under Rule 9(b), allege with particularity the content of the misrepresentation, who made it, when and where it was made, and the means by which it induced the plaintiff to act.

4. Examples of successful pleadings (illustrative)
- A plaintiff induced to sell stock on the basis of false financial statements who alleges specific dollar losses (price at which sold versus true value) and documents showing reliance.
- A performer who alleges that because of a defendant’s fraudulent representations they performed free of charge and incurred quantifiable expenses (travel, time‑based fees) and lost specific subsequent bookings directly traceable to the broadcast use.
- A business that alleges, with invoices and contractual documentation, that because of a defendant’s false statements it made specific expenditures it would not otherwise have made, and those expenditures were wasted.

In short, to survive a motion to dismiss a fraudulent inducement claim in New York, plaintiffs must do more than allege that they were tricked: they must allege concrete, pecuniary harm with specificity and show it was proximately caused by the defendant’s deception.

## Application to Santos: no out‑of‑pocket loss where plaintiff was paid for requested work

Santos v. Kimmel applies these principles in a contemporary media setting. The operative facts, as alleged, are straightforward: Jimmy Kimmel (using pseudonyms) submitted requests through the Cameo platform that solicited personalized videos from George Santos; Santos produced the videos and was paid for them; Kimmel then broadcast some of the videos on his late‑night program as part of a series mocking Santos. Santos alleged that Kimmel’s false representations induced him to create the videos under false pretenses, and he asserted a common‑law fraudulent inducement claim under New York law.

The Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege a cognizable out‑of‑pocket loss. Applying Lama and Connaughton, the court explained that New York requires the pleading of an actual pecuniary loss and that Sanchez’s complaint did not allege any such loss. Critically, Santos was paid for each video—the core transaction was completed, and the complaint did not identify any out‑of‑pocket expenditures, consequential monetary loss, or diminution in value that could be ascribed to Kimmel’s deception. Although the complaint alleged deception and a resulting humiliation when the videos were aired and mocked, humiliation and reputational injury are non‑pecuniary harms and, without an accompanying monetary loss, do not sustain a common‑law fraud claim in New York.

The opinion illustrates several practical points for plaintiffs and practitioners:

- If a defendant obtains work or performance from the plaintiff but pays the agreed fee, the plaintiff cannot rely on a bare claim of deception and embarrassment to recover fraud damages. They must instead allege specific monetary losses caused by the deception (for example, if the broadcast caused cancellation of future paid engagements, with invoices or booking records showing such losses).
- Where plaintiffs seek restitution (return of the payment), they must plead and timely pursue relief in that form (rescission or restitutionary equitable relief), which differs from compensatory tort damages. Santos did not plead an effective rescission claim that would have justified a restitution remedy.
- Rule 9(b) imposes particularity requirements for fraud allegations; in contexts like stings where covert recordings and pseudonymous communications are used, a well‑pleaded complaint should attach or describe the communications and specify dates, methods of contact, and amounts paid to demonstrate both the misrepresentation and any consequent out‑of‑pocket loss.

Finally, the Santos decision underscores the policy behind New York’s out‑of‑the‑pocket rule: tort damages are intended to redress actual economic losses caused by deceit, not to provide a windfall or to substitute tort recovery for disappointments unaccompanied by economic harm. In a media age in which people are often induced to participate in recorded performances that may later be used for satire or criticism, the out‑of‑pocket rule limits recovery in fraud to genuine pecuniary injuries—unless the plaintiff can plead (and ultimately prove) a monetary nexus between the deception and concrete economic harm.

Conclusion — doctrinal takeaway for practice and litigation strategy

When evaluating or drafting potential fraudulent inducement claims under New York law, remember the following checklist:

- Allegations: plead the false statement(s) of fact, identify the speaker(s), and state when and where the misrepresentations were made. Show scienter with particular facts.
- Reliance: plead facts demonstrating that reliance was reasonable and that the plaintiff actually relied on the misrepresentation.
- Damages: plead an identifiable, concrete pecuniary loss—amounts paid, expenses incurred, lost profits, lost opportunities, diminution in value, or restitution sought via rescission. Specify amounts and causal links.
- Rule 9(b): plead with the particularity Rule 9(b) requires for fraud claims.
- Remedies choice: consider whether rescission/restitution is more appropriate than tort damages where the plaintiff seeks return of consideration.
- Alternative claims: when pecuniary loss is absent or hard to allege, consider other causes of action (privacy torts, defamation, contractual claims where a party has enforceable contractual rights, or statutory claims) that may address non‑pecuniary injuries or provide different remedies.

Santos v. Kimmel shows how the out‑of‑pocket rule operates in media and entertainment contexts: deception alone is insufficient in New York to convert non‑pecuniary injury into recoverable tort damages; without tangible economic loss, the fraudulent inducement claim will likely be dismissed.
