Chapter 2: # Doctrinal Foundations of Fair Use Under 17 U.S.C. § 107

The fair use doctrine under 17 U.S.C. § 107 occupies a central position in American copyright law. It functions as a built-in limitation on the exclusive rights granted by the Copyright Act, permitting uses of copyrighted works that would otherwise be infringing when those uses serve purposes the Act was not meant to suppress — criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research. This chapter explains the statutory framework and the four § 107 factors, traces the modern development of the critical “transformativeness” inquiry (including the role of the “reasonable observer” standard), addresses the place of commerciality and of alleged bad faith, and shows how courts apply these principles in practice. The recent Second Circuit decision in Santos v. Kimmel (2025) is used throughout to illustrate how the doctrine operates at the pleading stage and how courts balance the statutory factors in contexts where a secondary user repurposes material for commentary and ridicule.

## Statutory text and purposes of § 107: preservation of creativity and protection of commentary, criticism, and news reporting

Section 107 lays out a nonexclusive list of fair use purposes — criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research — and directs courts to weigh four enumerated factors in determining whether a particular use is fair. That statutory text reflects Congress’s judgment that copyright should promote creative progress while simultaneously protecting the public’s interest in discourse about works and in further creative activity. The statute therefore rejects an absolutist approach to exclusive rights: not every copying is actionable; some uses are socially valuable precisely because they reuse, comment upon, or build on earlier expression.

Courts have interpreted § 107 against this background. The canonical statement in Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), emphasized that fair use is an “equitable rule of reason” aimed at balancing the interests of copyright owners against the public interest in the free exchange of ideas. The Second Circuit’s phrasing in cases such as Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive stresses that fair use “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Santos v. Kimmel applied the same principle: the district court and the court of appeals viewed the defendants’ use through the lens of § 107’s underlying purposes, concluding that transforming the videos into critical commentary was precisely the type of socially valuable reuse the doctrine protects.

Understanding § 107 therefore begins with its dual purposes. First, the statute preserves a fertile public domain for further expression: later creators may appropriate earlier works for new expressive or informational uses. Second, it protects socially valuable uses — commentary, criticism, news reporting — that serve public discourse even though they rely on preexisting works. Those purposes inform every aspect of the factor-based analysis that follows.

## The four nonexclusive factors and flexible, context-specific application

Section 107 prescribes four nonexclusive factors:

1. The purpose and character of the use, including whether it is of a commercial nature or for nonprofit educational purposes;
2. The nature of the copyrighted work;
3. The amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole; and
4. The effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

These factors are meant to be flexible and context-sensitive; no single factor is dispositive and the relative weight of each depends on the facts. Courts do not mechanically add up scores; rather they ask whether, taken together, the factors support a finding that the secondary use is consistent with the objectives of the Copyright Act.

Factor one — purpose and character of the use — has become the focal point of fair use analysis because of its emphasis on transformation: whether the secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or message, or instead merely supersedes the original. If a use is “transformative,” it is more likely to be fair, even if it is commercial or involves copying large portions of the original. Santos v. Kimmel illustrates the point: the court accepted the district court’s determination that the late-night segments were an attempt to comment on and mock Santos’s propensity to say outrageous things for money. That different communicative purpose — turning earnest, positive-styled videos into a vehicle for critique and ridicule — weighed strongly in favor of fair use under the first factor.

Factor two, the nature of the copyrighted work, asks whether the original is creative or factual and whether it is published or unpublished. Creative, unpublished works receive stronger protection; factual or published materials are more amenable to fair use. But the weight of this factor is often modest relative to the first and fourth factors, and in many modern cases it does not determine the outcome alone.

Factor three, amount and substantiality, considers both quantitative and qualitative aspects of the copying. Courts ask how much was taken and whether the “heart” of the work was copied. But substantial copying may be justified if it is necessary to accomplish a transformative purpose. In Santos the defendants used short, self‑contained video clips; the court found the copying appropriate in light of the use’s commentary function.

Factor four, market effect, focuses on whether the secondary use usurps the market for the original or its derivatives — the so‑called “usurpation” test adopted in Campbell and reiterated in later cases. Courts distinguish between actual market harm and speculative devaluation of the original; they ask whether the secondary work serves as a realistic substitute for the originals in the relevant markets that the copyright owner would otherwise exploit. In Santos the court accepted the complaint’s own allegations (and the context of late-night TV) as showing no plausible market usurpation: viewers were unlikely to substitute a satirical TV segment for authenticated personalized videos the plaintiff sold via an online platform.

Because the factors are nonexclusive, courts may take into account context-specific considerations; for example, whether the secondary use is part of an academic project, a news report, or a satirical routine. The flexible approach prevents doctrinal rigidity and allows fair use to adapt to changing technologies and cultural practices.

## Burden of proof and the nature of the affirmative defense

Fair use is an affirmative defense; the defendant bears the burden of proving it. Practically, however, that burden interacts with pleading-stage doctrine in important ways. A defendant may invoke fair use in a pre-answer motion to dismiss, and courts may resolve fair use questions at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage when the complaint and incorporated materials plainly show that the challenged use is fair as a matter of law. The Second Circuit has repeatedly recognized that fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” (see TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168 (2d Cir. 2016), quoted in Santos).

Santos v. Kimmel is a paradigmatic dismissal-at-the-pleadings case. The court considered the complaint, the registered copyrights (which courts may judicially notice), and the segments aired on television and concluded that the defendants’ use fell within the ambit of fair use even accepting Santos’s factual allegations. Two implications flow from this posture. First, plaintiffs cannot rely on bare allegations of exclusive rights when the publicly observable use obviously presents a transformative, non‑usurping reuse. Second, defendants who reasonably assert a fair use defense should consider early dispositive motions when documentary evidence and the face of the complaint tend to resolve the statutory factors in their favor.

Nonetheless, courts are not free to treat fair use as uniformly susceptible to resolution on paper. If disputed factual issues—such as the extent of market harm, subjective intent in some contexts, or the presence of a licensing market—are material, then summary judgment or trial may be necessary. The key is whether the record attached to the complaint (and appropriate judicially noticed materials) allows the court to weigh the § 107 factors as a matter of law.

## Transformativeness after Warhol v. Goldsmith: the reasonable observer, use‑context, and limits of new meaning alone

Transformativeness has been the most litigated (and litigiously productive) element of factor one. The Supreme Court’s decision in Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), refined the inquiry in important respects. Goldsmith emphasized that the proper focus is how a “reasonable observer” would perceive the derivative work relative to the original, rejecting an analysis that depended on a user’s subjective motives or on an internal judicial view divorced from how the public would interpret the works. In other words, transformativeness is an objective question of whether the secondary use communicates something new or different from the original in the perception of a reasonable observer.

Santos adopts and applies the reasonable-observer framework. The court explained that the question is not what the plaintiff or defendant subjectively intended but what a reasonable observer would think the videos communicated. Santos’s complaint alleged that the videos conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” A reasonable observer, however, would perceive Kimmel’s broadcast use as converting those earnest messages into the subject of ridicule — a sharply different communicative function. That change in expressive purpose supported a finding of transformativeness.

Goldsmith also warned against equating novelty of meaning with automatic fairness. Adding a new message is important, but not dispositive. The secondary work must add “new expression, meaning, or message” in a way that does not simply supplant the original’s market. Goldsmith is most instructive where a secondary work preserves the original’s central expressive content and merely redeploys it; in such cases Goldsmith tends to treat transformativeness with caution. The line is context-dependent and fact-sensitive: visually based appropriations, for example, may be judged more narrowly than textual snippets used for commentary.

Practical application requires the analyst to ask both qualitative and audience-centered questions: What new communicative purpose does the secondary work serve? Does the audience encounter the secondary work as communicating that different purpose? Does the secondary use rely on the original to express the new message in a way that could not have been achieved without copying? Santos illustrates these questions: the defendants used the original videos as raw material to make a point about Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things; the audience would perceive that repurposing as commentary rather than as the original message.

## Parody versus satire and commentary: Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose and beyond

An enduring doctrinal axis in fair use is the distinction between parody and satire. In Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), the Supreme Court explained that a parody — a work that imitates an original to make fun of or comment on that original — is a paradigmatic fair use because it needs to conjure elements of the original in order to comment on it. Satire, by contrast, uses the original to comment on something else; because it does not necessarily target the original, it may weigh less strongly in favor of fair use under factor one.

This distinction matters because parody’s need to “conjure” the original may justify copying more of the original work than would otherwise be tolerable. The Supreme Court in Campbell found that a commercial parody could still be fair where it transformed the original’s message into something new and critical. The distinction is nuanced in practice: courts ask whether the secondary work’s objective is to target the original (parody) or to use the original as a vehicle to critique some other subject (satire).

Santos sits comfortably within this framework. Kimmel’s segments targeted Santos himself — his conduct and statements — rather than using the videos to criticize some unrelated subject. The segments’ purpose was to expose and mock Santos’s willingness to say outlandish things for money; they depended on showing the original videos as the target of the criticism. That relationship to the original is more closely akin to parody or direct commentary, so under Campbell it is strongly compatible with a finding of transformative fair use.

Beyond Campbell, courts have recognized that the parody-satire distinction is not a rigid categorical rule. Rather it is an analytic tool that helps assess the necessity of copying and the degree to which the secondary use adds new expressive content. Courts will probe the secondary work’s communicative target and whether the copy was necessary to accomplish the critical purpose.

## Commerciality within factor one and its interplay with transformativeness

The commercial nature of a use is explicitly part of factor one, and the Supreme Court has admonished that commercialization weighs against fair use. But in practice commerciality is only one element of the first-factor inquiry and can be outweighed by significant transformativeness. Campbell itself held that a “commercial” parody could still be fair because the transformative character of the use was strong.

Santos demonstrates this interplay. Jimmy Kimmel Live! is a commercial television program; airing the videos plainly had a commercial aspect. Yet the Second Circuit treated the commerciality point as subordinate to the more important question of whether the use was transformative. Because the late-night segments added a new critical message — lampooning Santos’s statements and willingness to be paid for them — the court found that the purpose-and-character factor favored fair use despite the commercial setting.

Students should therefore think of commerciality as a factor that matters but rarely dispositive on its own. A highly transformative use may neutralize or overcome the weight of commerciality. Conversely, purely commercial copying that is not transformative will be less likely to qualify as fair use. When analyzing a new fact pattern, evaluate whether the commercial context is incidental to an expressive purpose or whether it is the primary aim; if the latter and there is no substantial transformation, factor one will likely weigh against fair use.

## Good faith and bad faith: Harper & Row’s dictum and subsequent skepticism

Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539 (1985), included language suggesting that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” and for a time lower courts treated subjective bad faith as a potentially relevant consideration. But the Supreme Court’s later opinions, most notably Campbell and Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., 593 U.S. 1 (2021), signaled skepticism about imbuing bad faith with significant doctrinal importance. The concern is that linking fair use to the actor’s moral blameworthiness risks conflating copyright policy with tort-style moral judgments and undermines the objective balancing § 107 requires.

Santos offers a modern application of that skepticism. Santos argued that Kimmel’s alleged deception (using fake names to obtain personalized videos) showed bad faith that should preclude a fair use defense. The court declined to treat bad faith as dispositive. It noted that the complaint itself contradicted any claim that the defendants intended to usurp Santos’s market in the videos, and emphasized that the complaint alleged an expressive purpose — commentary and ridicule — that is squarely within the scope of fair use. The Second Circuit relied on Campbell and Google for the proposition that good or bad faith is not controlling; fair use focuses on objective questions of purpose, transformation, and market impact rather than on a subjective morality test.

That being said, bad faith can sometimes be relevant to subsidiary factual questions — for example, when it bears on whether the secondary user intended to supplant the original or whether the defendant sought to hide the use to evade licensing markets. But courts will not treat bad faith as a per se override of the statutory balancing. Students should therefore treat allegations of deception or bad motives as potentially colorable facts that inform but do not determine the fair use calculus.

## Practical application: a checklist for analyzing fair use in new fact patterns

When confronted with a new fact pattern, a systematic approach will produce a reliable analysis. The following checklist reflects the doctrinal priorities courts apply:

1. Identify the asserted purposes of the secondary use. Ask whether the secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or message and whether the audience would perceive a different communicative purpose (the “reasonable observer” test emphasized in Goldsmith and applied in Santos).
2. Evaluate commerciality as a component of factor one. Is the commercial nature incidental to a transformative purpose, or is the use primarily commercial without added expression?
3. Examine the nature of the original work (factor two): is it creative or factual, published or unpublished? Give appropriate but generally modest weight to this factor.
4. Consider amount and substantiality (factor three): how much was copied and was the copying necessary for the transformative purpose? Was the “heart” of the work taken and if so, was that taking reasonably necessary?
5. Analyze market effect (factor four) with special attention to the usurpation test: does the secondary use act as a realistic substitute for the original or its derivative markets? Distinguish mere critical devaluation from actual substitution.
6. Consider whether parody or direct commentary is at issue: if the secondary work targets the original, that supports the necessity of copying and favors fair use; if the work is satire, the justification may be weaker.
7. Consider any allegations of bad faith as contextual evidence; do not allow those allegations to override the objective statutory balancing but do account for them where they bear on market impact or intent to usurp the market.
8. If a motion to dismiss is contemplated, assemble documentary evidence (the works, registrations, public broadcasts) that may permit the court to decide fair use as a matter of law; if factual disputes about market harm or intent exist, anticipate the need for discovery.

Santos v. Kimmel exemplifies this approach: the court examined the works, read the complaint in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, and concluded, on the face of the materials, that the defendants’ use was commentary-directed and non‑usurping. The defendants met their burden at the pleadings stage because the necessary factual predicates for fair use were either undisputed or apparent from the materials before the court.

## Conclusion — the balancing act at the heart of § 107

Fair use is an exercise in balance. Section 107 gives courts a statutory framework, but the application is inevitably contextual and fact-driven. The modern focus on transformativeness — refined by the Supreme Court’s reasonable-observer test in Goldsmith and applied in the Second Circuit’s Santos decision — requires a careful, audience-centered inquiry into whether a secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or message. Commerciality, the nature of the original work, the amount taken, and market effect are all important, but none operates in isolation.

For students and practitioners, the key lessons are these: (1) begin with the purpose and character of the use; (2) ask how a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary work relative to the original; (3) assess whether the secondary use realistically substitutes for the original in relevant markets; and (4) remember that bad faith allegations are contextually relevant but not dispositive. Santos v. Kimmel confirms that when a secondary use recasts expressive material into an overtly critical or satirical context and does not supplant the original market, fair use can be resolved in favor of the defendant even at the pleading stage. The doctrine remains flexible precisely to accommodate those kinds of socially valuable reuses that the Copyright Act was not designed to suppress.
