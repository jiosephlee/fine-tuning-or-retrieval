Chapter 13: # Significance, Strategy, and Future Litigation

This chapter draws out the doctrinal lessons and practical consequences of Santos v. Kimmel, situating the Second Circuit’s decision within the larger fair use landscape and considering how creators, platforms, and media companies should respond. The opinion is notable for the court’s early (12(b)(6)) resolution of a copyright dispute on fair use grounds, its emphasis on an objective “reasonable observer” test for transformativeness, its reiteration that market-usurpation—rather than mere reputational or expressive harm—is the critical inquiry for the fourth factor, and its refusal to give dispositive weight to subjective bad faith. Each of those positions carries consequences for litigation strategy, platform drafting, and the emerging market for personalized creator content. Below we unpack those doctrinal takeaways, explain tactical choices for litigants, propose contract-drafting best practices for platforms, analyze implications for the creator economy, and identify unresolved questions likely to surface in future cases.

## Doctrinal takeaways: objective transformativeness, market-usurpation focus, and limited role of bad faith

Santos v. Kimmel is best read as a practical application of the modern fair use framework with three reaffirmed themes.

First, transformativeness is judged objectively from the perspective of a “reasonable observer,” not by the subjective intent of either the original creator or the secondary user. The court accepted the district court’s characterization that the JKL segments’ purpose was to lampoon Santos’ willingness to utter absurdities for payment, and it rejected Santos’s argument that the videos were created for the same purpose. The Second Circuit emphasized that what matters is how a reasonable observer would understand the videos: if the original videos communicated “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” repurposing them into a sarcasm-laden segment that highlights performative willing-to-say absurd-things-for-money alters the communicative purpose sufficiently to be transformative. For law students, the lesson is twofold. One must distinguish (a) the creator’s stated intent and (b) the reasonable perception of the work in context; fair use evaluation privileges the latter. In practice, courts will compare the original work’s communicative content with the secondary use’s message and purpose to determine whether something “new” and different has been added.

Second, the opinion underscores the centrality of market-usurpation in analyzing the fourth fair use factor. The court reiterated that not all deleterious effects on value qualify as cognizable markets to be protected—copyright does not give creators a monopoly on criticism, parody, or critical commentary that would otherwise be outside the relevant licensing market. The inquiry asks whether the secondary use functions as a market substitute for licensing the original work, not whether the secondary use damages the original’s reputational or expressive value. That distinction matters at both pleading and trial stages: a complaint that alleges reputational devaluation or general “harm” will not survive if the complaint lacks plausible allegations that the defendant’s use substituted for a likely licensing market.

Third, Santos illustrates that evidence of bad faith or deception (Kimmel used pseudonyms on Cameo) is not automatically fatal to a fair use defense. The court noted Harper & Row’s statement that fair use presupposes good faith but observed that subsequent Supreme Court decisions have been skeptical about elevating good faith to a determinative factor. The Second Circuit applied that skepticism: even if Kimmel’s tactics were deceptive, the complaint itself portrayed defendants’ conduct as motivated by critical commentary rather than a desire to supplant Santos’s market. Thus, misrepresentations by a secondary user do not necessarily defeat fair use where the other factors—including transformativeness and lack of market usurpation—favor the defendant.

Taken together, these points push doctrinal analysis toward an objective, context-driven assessment of what meaning a secondary use conveys and whether the secondary use competes with a cognizable licensing market for the original work. Fair use is flexible, but courts will not accord decisive weight to a creator’s subjective intent nor to vague allegations of reputational harm.

## Litigation strategies: positioning for or against early fair use adjudication

Santos was resolved at the pleading stage on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion because the materials necessary to decide fair use were before the court and the complaint itself established the facts the court needed to resolve the defense. That outcome provides a strategic roadmap for both plaintiffs and defendants.

For defendants seeking early resolution:
- Assemble the judicially cognizable record. The Second Circuit relied on materials properly before the court: the complaint’s allegations, the alleged works themselves, and Santos’ registrations (which courts may take judicial notice of). Attach the contested materials and any registrations to the motion to dismiss or make them part of the complaint by incorporation. Where the fair use analysis depends on a side-by-side comparison of the works, provide those comparisons to show transformativeness and limited market impact.
- Emphasize an objective, reasonable-observer account of meaning. Build the argument that the secondary use materially alters the original’s message or purpose. Use visual or transcript comparisons to show how the secondary use recontextualizes the original content.
- Address market effects head-on. Show why the secondary use cannot serve as a substitute in any existing licensing market: is the work already published? Would licensees ever pay for a critical or parodic use? If the challenged use appears in short clips excerpted for commentary rather than for entertainment or replacement, stress those facts.
- Anticipate and deflect bad-faith allegations. Acknowledge misconduct where necessary but frame conduct as tactical or theatrical and emphasize that transformativeness and lack of market substitution mitigate any misconduct’s significance.

For plaintiffs trying to avoid early dismissal:
- Avoid conclusory allegations. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal in part because Santos’s complaint lacked nonconclusory allegations about market harm and failed to plead essential terms for the contract claims. Plaintiffs must plead concrete facts—specific markets that defendants’ use has or will usurp, particular licensing opportunities lost, or how the secondary use competes commercially with the original.
- Preserve the record about intent and use. If the defendant’s conduct involves deception or targeted inducement to create content for a broadcast, plead those facts in detail—who used what account, the timing of requests, communications showing intent to broadcast, and the creator’s expectations about permissible use. Those facts may undercut a defendant’s transformativeness argument or support a claim of bad-faith inducement in narrow circumstances.
- Distinguish your work and its markets. A plaintiff who can plausibly allege a preexisting or realistic licensing market for the specific kind of work (e.g., individualized paid videos licensed for broadcast) will be in a stronger position. Allegations that the creator explicitly intended to license the works for certain uses and had been negotiating such licenses will help resist dismissal.

Both sides should remember that fair use is fact-intensive; early resolution is possible where the record is complete enough for an objective comparison, but when contested facts (about meaning, market, or intent) are plausibly in dispute, defendants may face a harder path on a motion to dismiss and might prefer summary judgment once discovery establishes the context.

## Platform drafting: making (or avoiding) third-party beneficiary rights explicit

Santos also illustrates the risks and limits of relying on platform Terms of Service (ToS) as a vehicle to confer or avoid third-party rights. Santos attempted to assert direct breach claims against the Cameo ToS and an implied-in-fact contract; the court rejected both theories because Santos was not a party to the ToS and did not plausibly allege an implied contract.

For platforms and creators, the key drafting and business-design lessons are practical, not merely academic.

For platforms and purchasers who want to ensure that noncontracting creators can sue third-party abusers:
- Include express third-party beneficiary language. If a platform wishes to enable creators to enforce usage restrictions against platform users or platform licensees, the ToS should say so explicitly: name classes of intended beneficiaries, state the specific rights conferred to them, and provide a clear mechanism for enforcement (e.g., notice-and-cure, indemnities).
- Draft clear licensing grants and restrictions. If user-generated content may be used by purchasers for broadcast or other public-facing uses, define the permitted and prohibited uses clearly. If a creator sells a personalized video only for private, non-broadcast use, the sales interface and purchase confirmation should limit broad downstream uses. Conversely, if purchasers are to be permitted broad redistribution, the platform should require affirmative checkbox consent from the creator.
- Design a separate, maker-facing contract. Platforms can offer creators a distinct creator agreement that sets out resale, redistribution, and enforcement rights. Making creators affirmatively opt into these terms reduces ambiguity about expectations.
- Preserve audit trails and consent records. When disputes arise, the identity of buyers, timestamps, IP addresses, consent checkboxes, and message logs often determine whether a purchaser intended to broadcast a video. Maintain logs that can be produced in litigation.

For platforms and media companies who prefer to avoid creating third-party enforcement rights:
- Use explicit disclaimers and narrowly tailored licenses. If the platform’s ToS are not intended to create enforceable rights in nonparties, the drafting should avoid broad beneficiary language and explicitly state that no third-party has enforcement rights absent an express written agreement.
- Provide transparent notice to creators about possible downstream uses. Platforms that obscure likely downstream uses increase litigation risk; transparent notices help manage creator expectations and reduce claims of reliance or inducement.

Two additional points follow from Santos’s analysis. First, governing law and forum-selection clauses matter. Cameo’s ToS were governed by Illinois law, and the court applied Illinois contract doctrines (strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries) to reject Santos’s claim. Drafters should choose governing law with an eye toward third-party-beneficiary doctrines. Second, an implied-in-fact contract claim requires specific pleading about the essential terms and a meeting of the minds. Platforms should avoid relying on ambiguity to preserve flexibility; clarity reduces litigation risk.

## Creator economy implications: personalized content, licensing expectations, and public-figure commentary

Santos arose in the new economy of micro-payments for personalized content. Cameo and similar services allow fans or media to commission bespoke videos. The case illuminates how courts will treat such content when it migrates from a private, paid interaction to public commentary.

Expectations and default rules. Many creators who produce paid personalized content do not contemplate or are not told that purchasers can use the content in broadcast contexts. Santos’s dispute underscores the need for creators to set expectations explicitly. Without explicit licensing terms, courts may view broadcast or critical reuse as transformative commentary protected by fair use, especially when the secondary use recontextualizes the content into satire or criticism. Creators who want to preserve broad exclusive rights must contract for them.

Pricing and licensing markets. Santos failed on the market-harm theory because the complaint did not plausibly allege a realistic licensing market that the JKL segments could usurp. But the creator economy is evolving: as platforms mature, a specialized licensing market for personalized videos might develop (e.g., news programs pay for permission to show user-created content, or aggregators license clips for compilations). Creators who believe that a licensing market could exist should plan for licensing-pricing mechanisms and express licensing opt-ins/opt-outs. Conversely, broadcasters relying on fair use should monitor market developments; if creators successfully establish a licensing market for these sorts of clips, the fourth-factor analysis could shift.

Public figures and commentary. The Santos fact pattern is particularly favorable to defendants because the secondary use was commentary about a public figure’s behavior—one of the classic contexts courts have treated as protected by fair use. In practical terms, producers of news, comedy, and commentary have more latitude to repurpose creator-produced content when the repurposing is itself expressive criticism or satire aimed at a public figure. That latitude shrinks if the broadcast is merely a straight reproduction that commodifies the content without changing its message.

Monetization choices. Creators should consider tiered offerings: private/personal-only deliverables at one price point, and broad-license versions at higher rates. Platforms should provide easy paths for creators to elect licensing terms and for purchasers to purchase broader rights when needed.

## Open questions: boundaries of satire/commentary, platform-induced reliance, and evolving markets for critical uses

Santos resolves certain issues but leaves others open—many of which will shape future litigation and industry practice.

Where is the boundary between satire/commentary and nontransformative reuse? Santos treats recontextualization for satirical commentary as transformative. But how far must recontextualization go? Two arrays of fact patterns will test the line:
- Close derivative reuse: Suppose a broadcaster shows a complete personalized video verbatim, with a narrator saying, “see how absurd this is?” but without editing, juxtaposition, or new meaning. Is mere juxtaposition sufficient? Courts will likely analyze whether the audience reads the original work differently in the new context or whether the new use merely repackages the original for entertainment. The reasonable-observer test gives courts a tool, but its application will turn on nuanced fact-finding about audience perception.
- Entertainment vs. commentary: A late-night show that uses clips to mock a figure is classic commentary. But a compilation program that uses similar clips for entertainment without any explicit critical framing poses a closer question. The higher the secondary use’s commercial character and the closer its function is to replacing the original work’s market, the less likely a finding of fair use.

What is the role of platform-induced reliance or instigation? Santos touched on the notion that Kimmel “instigated” the original works by requesting them through Cameo. Santos argued that this makes the secondary use nontransformative because Kimmel designed the videos for his later purpose. The court rejected that line because the relevant question remains what the reasonable observer would perceive. But future cases may arise where secondary users’ conduct goes beyond mere solicitation and into directed production—e.g., a broadcaster communicates precise scripting, phrases, or staging to induce creators to perform, with the explicit intention of later broadcast. When the secondary user clearly directs the creative choices, courts may be less willing to treat the secondary use as truly transformative since the original work was created at the instigation of the secondary user for the secondary user’s purpose. To preserve defenses, platforms and creators should document who set the creative parameters.

How will evolving licensing markets change the fourth-factor analysis? The Second Circuit stressed that critical or satirical uses are unlikely to be the kind of uses creators would license. But if a robust licensing market arises—platforms that broker broadcast rights for personalized videos, or subscription services that compile creator content—then an argument that broadcasters are usurping a cognizable market becomes stronger. Fair use is fact-sensitive and partly market-driven; courts will revisit the fourth factor if and when transactional evidence shows a realistic market for such licenses.

Will courts ever elevate bad-faith or deception to a decisive role? Santos reiterates the contemporary judicial skepticism about treating bad faith as determinative. Still, egregious conduct—targeted deception to obtain copyrighted works and then reproduce them verbatim for profit—could sway a court’s fair use calculus in close cases. Litigation exploiting egregious tactics (e.g., fabricated identities, explicit pre-broadcast promises that are broken) might produce a different result than the Second Circuit reached in Santos.

What are the preemption and contract-law boundaries? The opinion avoided deciding whether state contract claims are preempted by the Copyright Act, instead dismissing them on their merits. Future cases might squarely present whether state-law claims that enforce contractual restrictions on use of copyrighted material (distinct from copyright statutory claims) are preempted. Thoughtful drafting—express third-party beneficiary clauses, explicit waivers, or separate licensing agreements—can reduce litigation risk, but the interplay between federal copyright and state contract law remains a fertile question for appellate courts.

## Practical checklists and closing guidance

For creators:
- Do not rely on a platform’s default settings; negotiate or select licensing options when you want to preserve broadcast rights.
- Keep contemporaneous records of the creator’s expectations, communications with purchasers, and any license sales.
- Consider tiered offerings (private-only vs. broad-license) and price accordingly.

For platforms:
- Draft ToS with clear, explicit language about third-party enforcement rights, permissible uses, and licensing pathways.
- Provide creator-facing agreement options and affirmative consent mechanisms for broad redistribution.
- Preserve logs and evidence of buyer identities and purchase confirmations.

For media companies:
- Before broadcasting user-created content, assemble the record: the original clip, registration status, evidence of recontextualization, and a clear statement of how the broadcast transforms the original work’s meaning.
- Where possible, secure express licenses for uses that might be nontransformative or that could operate in a developing licensing market.
- If relying on fair use, prepare to show how the use changes the message and why it does not substitute for a licensing market.

For litigators:
- Defendants: move early when the record is complete; attach contested works and registrations; craft a strong reasonable-observer argument.
- Plaintiffs: plead market-harm facts and detailed allegations of inducement, direction, and reliance; preserve discovery to probe intent and market effects.

Santos v. Kimmel reiterates that fair use is both flexible and capacious; in the era of personalized creator content, courts will continue to balance the competing objectives of protecting creators’ markets and allowing robust public discourse and criticism. The case guides litigants and industry actors toward clarity—both in factual presentation in court and in contractual clarity in the marketplace—while leaving open important line-drawing questions that future litigation will have to resolve.
