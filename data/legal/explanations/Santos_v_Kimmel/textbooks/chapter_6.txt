Chapter 6: # Early Adjudication of Fair Use: Motions to Dismiss and Judicial Notice

One of the most consequential practical questions in modern copyright litigation is whether a court may resolve the affirmative defense of fair use at the earliest stages of a case — most commonly on a motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). This chapter explains the doctrinal framework governing early resolution of fair use, describes what materials a court may properly consider at the pleading stage, surveys Second Circuit guidance and its articulation in Santos v. Kimmel, and weighs the competing policy considerations that counsel for and against early disposition. Finally, the chapter offers practical drafting and litigation strategies for lawyers who must plead or defend copyright claims when fair use is in play.

Fair use is an affirmative defense that, by statute and Supreme Court precedent, is highly fact-sensitive. Nonetheless, the Second Circuit has long recognized circumstances in which the defense is so plainly established by the allegations of the complaint and by materials properly considered on a motion to dismiss that dismissal is appropriate without discovery. Santos v. Kimmel (24‑2196‑cv) is a recent example of that line of authority: the court affirmed dismissal where the complaint and publicly available materials — primarily copyright registrations and the videos themselves — made clear that defendants’ televised uses were transformative commentary and did not usurp any plausible licensing market for the original works. Understanding why the court reached that result requires walking through the 12(b)(6) standard, the incorporation‑by‑reference and judicial‑notice doctrines, and the fair use factors as applied in the Second Circuit.

## Rule 12(b)(6) standards and the role of incorporation by reference

A complaint survives a Rule 12(b)(6) motion if it states a claim that is plausible on its face. In evaluating plausibility, courts accept the complaint’s well‑pleaded factual allegations as true and draw all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. But the plaintiff does not get to rest on conclusory allegations, and courts may consider certain documents outside the four corners of the complaint without converting the motion into one for summary judgment.

Two distinct doctrines govern what extra‑pleading materials are appropriately considered on a motion to dismiss. The first is the incorporation‑by‑reference doctrine: if a plaintiff’s complaint “relies upon” a document and the document is either attached to the complaint or its terms are relied upon in the complaint, a court may treat the document as part of the pleadings. The classic example is a contract that forms the basis of a breach claim. Treating such documents as part of the pleadings prevents a plaintiff from misstating or omitting important terms while shielding the court from factual disputes that are properly resolved at a later stage.

The second doctrine is judicial notice of adjudicative facts. Federal Rule of Evidence 201 permits a court to take judicial notice of facts not subject to reasonable dispute because they are either generally known within the trial court’s jurisdiction or capable of accurate and ready determination from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned. In the copyright context, the Second Circuit has repeatedly allowed district courts to take judicial notice of registrations and recorded information in the Copyright Office’s public database. It has also been willing, in appropriate cases, to consider the content of the works themselves when those works are central to the complaint and are either attached to the complaint or readily accessible to the court.

But these doctrines have limits. Courts must be wary of resolving disputed facts at the pleading stage. If a complaint’s allegations give rise to a plausible dispute about material facts that bear on fair use — for example, whether the secondary work truly has a different communicative purpose or whether the defendant’s use supplants a recognized market — then dismissal is premature. The incorporation‑by‑reference doctrine does not authorize a court to accept as true a defendant’s version of disputed extrinsic facts that undercut the plaintiff’s nonconclusory allegations. Likewise, judicial notice is confined to facts not subject to reasonable dispute; a court may not judicially notice contested factual inferences from a work’s content where interpretation requires further factual development. Santos v. Kimmel illustrates how these doctrines operate together: the court relied on the complaint and on judicially noticeable materials — the Copyright Office registrations and the videos themselves — to conclude that fair use was apparent on the face of the pleadings.

## Judicial notice of copyright registrations and of the allegedly infringing and original works

The Second Circuit has been clear that copyright registrations recorded in the Copyright Office are proper subjects of judicial notice. Island Software & Computer Services, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp. established that courts may take judicial notice of registrations published in the Copyright Office registry, and subsequent decisions have reiterated that such records are admissible on a motion to dismiss. The significance of that authority is twofold. First, a registration and its effective date can establish a predicate fact (e.g., whether a work was published, and when) that bears on fair use factors such as the “nature of the copyrighted work” and market harm. Second, a registration is a public record whose contents are considered reliably ascertainable without discovery.

Even where a registration is not dispositive, the registration’s content — including deposit materials that are publicly accessible — can inform a court’s view of the original work. Where a defendant attaches or where the court judicially notices the original and the allegedly infringing work, the parties and the court can directly compare the two without needing further factual discovery. The Second Circuit has repeatedly held that when the question of fair use can be decided by comparing the two works themselves, a court need not await summary judgment or trial. Cariou v. Prince is instructive in this respect: the court held that a proffered fair‑use defense could be assessed by direct comparison of Prince’s altered photographs with Cariou’s originals, and it remanded for further factual development where such development was necessary. Conversely, when the comparison plainly establishes transformation or absence of market harm, dismissal may follow.

Judicial notice of the works, however, is not an automatic license to resolve all factual disputes. Courts must distinguish between noticing the existence and contents of a work (which is usually permissible) and taking notice of contested inferences about the way a reasonable viewer would perceive the work or about the existence of a licensing market (which is often not). In Santos, the court took judicial notice of the registration records and the videos and concluded as a matter of law that a reasonable observer would understand the shows’ segments to be satirical commentary distinct from the original purpose of Santos's videos. That conclusion was supported by unambiguous contextual indicators in the publicly viewable materials (e.g., the segment titles and the host’s editorial framing) and therefore did not require further factual development.

A final practical point about exhibits: when a defendant attaches the allegedly infringing and original works to a motion to dismiss, the court should be mindful of the potential need to convert the motion to one for summary judgment if those materials give rise to factual disputes outside the pleadings. The safer course for defendants seeking dismissal is to ensure that the works are properly the subject of judicial notice or are relied on in the complaint; otherwise, the remedy may convert to summary judgment procedures, with the attendant burdens of Rule 56.

## Second Circuit guidance: TCA Television, Cariou v. Prince, and related precedents

The Second Circuit has developed a body of case law that explains when fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage and the standards guiding that determination. A few cases merit special attention.

TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum affirmed dismissal of a copyright claim on fair use grounds at the pleading stage. The court recognized that fair use is an affirmative defense but held that when the complaint and attached materials make clear that the defendant’s use was transformative and no plausible market harm existed, dismissal is appropriate. TCA sets an important precedent for the proposition that fair use may be decided early where the record presented to the court is sufficiently complete.

Cariou v. Prince is sometimes viewed as the canonical Second Circuit fair‑use decision. There, the court explained that the inquiry into whether a work is transformative centers on whether the secondary work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, altering the first with new expression, meaning, or message” (internal quotation and paraphrase). Cariou also made clear that courts should not apply a single formula but must evaluate the transformative character in light of the works themselves. Importantly, Cariou remanded certain issues for further proceedings where the court could not conclude, on the record before it, that the alleged transformations were sufficient to constitute fair use. Cariou thus points to both sides of the coin: sometimes the comparison of works resolves the question; sometimes further factual development is necessary.

DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., though not a fair‑use case per se, supplies controlling law about motion‑to‑dismiss practice in the Second Circuit: courts may consider documents attached to, incorporated by reference in, or integral to the complaint. Island Software repeats the proposition that courts may take judicial notice of the Copyright Office’s records. Later decisions such as Authors Guild v. Google, Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive, and the Supreme Court’s decisions in Campbell, Harper & Row, and the more recent Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts v. Goldsmith and Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., supply substantive guideposts for how to analyze transformative purpose and market effect. The Supreme Court’s recent decisions have narrowed the role of subjective intent and have required courts to focus on how an ordinary, reasonable observer would construe the secondary use, rather than on the defendant’s purported motives.

Santos v. Kimmel sits comfortably within this line. The Second Circuit applied the Cariou/TCA approach: it carefully inspected the complaint, the registrations, and the publicly available videos; it framed the first fair‑use factor around transformativeness as judged by a reasonable observer; it rejected reliance on subjective motives or manufactured bad faith as dispositive; and it concluded that the complaint and materials made fair use “so clearly established” that dismissal was proper. The opinion illustrates the point made in prior cases: the defenses of fair use and free expression may justify early resolution when the documentary record is unambiguous.

## Policy considerations: chilling effects versus early disposition of speech‑related cases

Resolving fair use on the pleadings has important policy implications. Two competing values dominate the analysis.

On the one hand, early dismissal can be an important protection for freedom of expression, the efficient administration of justice, and judicial economy. Copyright litigation is expensive. If a defendant’s use is plainly a protected commentary, parody, or other noninfringing exercise, the ability to test the claim early protects defendants (and would‑be speakers) from the chilling effects of protracted litigation. The Second Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that fair use is meant, in part, to prevent the Copyright Act from silencing criticism and commentary; where the record unmistakably shows that the use was for commentary and not a licensing substitute, dismissing the complaint is consonant with the statute’s policy goals.

On the other hand, permitting too broad an early‑dismissal practice runs the risk of improperly short‑circuiting plaintiffs’ rights where factual disputes exist — particularly facts relating to market harm, the extent of copying, or whether purported “commentary” actually serves as an unlicensed substitute for the original. A plaintiff should be able to take limited discovery when the complaint alleges plausible facts that, if proved, would defeat a fair‑use defense. Courts must therefore calibrate carefully: early dismissal is appropriate only where the complaint and materials properly considered demonstrate fair use without the need for contested factual findings.

Santos illustrates the tension. The court emphasized the public‑facing nature of the materials and the unambiguous editorial framing on the television program. Those facts made the second use plainly transformative as a matter of law and eliminated a plausible market‑replacement argument. But Santos also shows the limits to early resolution: the opinion distinguishes between interpreting the communicative purpose of a work — often amenable to resolution from the works themselves — and resolving contested, nonpublic facts about licensing markets or behind‑the‑scenes conduct that might bear on bad faith or market substitution. The Second Circuit further noted the diminishing role of moral‑character in fair‑use analysis: the Supreme Court has questioned whether a defendant’s bad faith should negate fair use, and Santos declined to allow Santos’s allegations of deception to govern the analysis where the complaint otherwise established commentary as the purpose of the use.

A final policy point concerns forum and motion practice: defendants who wish to obtain early dismissal must take care to assemble a clean record for the court’s inspection. Publicly accessible registrations, program transcripts, video clips, and contemporaneous public framing all help the court see the secondary work in its proper communicative context. Plaintiffs wishing to avoid early dismissal should plead nonconclusory facts showing either that the secondary use is not meaningfully transformative, that a plausible licensing market exists for the specific kind of second use at issue, or that disputed factual issues make resolution at the pleading stage inappropriate.

## Practical tips for drafting complaints and motions with the fair use issue in mind

For plaintiffs. A copyright plaintiff seeking to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion must anticipate the defenses that a defendant will present on the face of the complaint and through publicly available records. First, plead with particularity the factual bases for your claim: describe the original work’s purpose, the circumstances under which it was created and registered (attach the registration), and the exact portions alleged to be infringed. Second, allege facts that make fair use less likely, not merely conclusory assertions. Specifically, identify any actual or plausible markets for licensing the type of use at issue and plead facts showing that the defendant’s use could usurp those markets. If the work was unpublished at the time of the secondary use, make that clear, because unpublished status can affect the second fair‑use factor. Third, if the defendant has presented the works or registrations publicly, address those materials directly in the complaint and explain why they do not establish fair use; courts are less likely to treat a defendant’s public record as exculpatory if the plaintiff has already explained contrary plausible interpretations.

For defendants. When moving to dismiss on fair‑use grounds, assemble a concise, self‑contained record that the court may properly consider without conversion to summary judgment. Attach or incorporate by reference the allegedly infringing and original works, the relevant Copyright Office registrations (and their dates), and any contemporaneous public framing (e.g., program episode titles, host statements, or headlines) that reveal the communicative context. On the substantive side, focus on the first and fourth fair‑use factors: show transformation by explaining the different communicative purpose and audience reception of the secondary work, and negate plausible market harm by demonstrating the absence of any realistic licensing market for the particular secondary use. Be prepared to argue that any allegations of bad faith, deception, or behind‑the‑scenes conduct either are not material to fair use or are contradicted by the complaint itself.

For both sides, consider jurisdictional and choice‑of‑law questions at the outset. As the Santos opinion demonstrates, the governing law for non‑copyright claims (for example, contract or fraud) may differ from the law governing the Terms of Service or other agreements presented as support for a copyright claim defense. Those choice‑of‑law determinations can affect the viability of ancillary state‑law claims and should be addressed early.

A procedural tip for plaintiffs is to limit reference to public materials that could be used by a defendant to show fair use. If the defendant’s public materials — for example, a televised segment title that advertises satire of the plaintiff — are integral to the dispute, plaintiff should preempt the defendant’s narrative by pleading facts that mitigate the inference the court might draw from those materials. Conversely, defendants should avoid presenting spurious or disputed materials as if they were uncontested. If a document attached to a motion is likely to generate factual controversy, be prepared to accept conversion to summary judgment or to show that the document is the proper subject of judicial notice.

Concluding guidance: fair use is a flexible, context‑dependent doctrine. Early adjudication is a powerful tool when the public record plainly shows transformation and absence of market harm; but courts must carefully preserve plaintiffs’ ability to develop proof where factual disputes exist. The lawyer’s job in drafting either a complaint or a dismissal motion is to anticipate how the court will view the works themselves and to build a record — through pleading, exhibits, or public records — that supports the party’s preferred legal inference from that record. Santos v. Kimmel exemplifies the circumstances under which a court will accept the defendant’s fair‑use defense at the pleading stage: when the alleged secondary use is publicly contextualized as commentary and when the complaint and publicly available registration data foreclose a plausible market‑replacement theory. When those elements are present, the Second Circuit will not hesitate to dispose of the case early; when they are not, the litigation must proceed to discovery and further factual development.
