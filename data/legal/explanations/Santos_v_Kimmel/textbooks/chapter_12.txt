Chapter 12: # Comparative Case Law and Adjacent Doctrines

The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel sits at the crossroads of several strands of fair use and related doctrine: appropriation art and transformative use, news reporting and full‑work copying, searchability and snippets, and the special problems posed by functional broadcast‑monitoring services. This chapter situates Santos within that case law, explains how courts weigh the statutory fair‑use factors in these contexts, and sketches the ways in which non‑copyright claims (contract, fraud, and the right of publicity) interact with—and sometimes are displaced by—the Copyright Act. Throughout, the goal is doctrinal clarity: to give students a usable map for applying these precedents to new fact patterns.

## Swatch v. Bloomberg: news reporting, complete copying, and market effects

Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P. is frequently cited for the proposition that complete copying is not automatically fatal to a fair‑use defense when the secondary use serves a news‑reporting function and does not usurp a licensing market. In Swatch, Bloomberg ran an article about Swatch that included photographs of watches. Bloomberg’s republication of the images in full was found to be fair use in light of the purpose of the use and the lack of a plausible licensing market for the precise use Bloomberg made.

What Swatch teaches about the first and fourth fair‑use factors is especially relevant to Santos. The first factor—purpose and character—puts a heavy thumb on the scale when the secondary use is commentary, criticism, or news reporting. The court evaluates whether the secondary use is “transformative,” meaning it adds a new purpose, message, or context that differs from the original. Swatch recognized that news reporting often repurposes copyrighted material to inform the public and that such uses can be transformative even when they incorporate entire works.

But Swatch also illustrates the limits of a form‑over‑substance approach. The fourth factor—market effect—asks whether the secondary use usurps a likely market for licensing the copyrighted work. Swatch found no plausible market harm because news reporting of the sort Bloomberg did was not a market for which copyright holders typically license images; there was little risk Bloomberg’s use would supplant an existing or potential market for Swatch’s images. Courts thus distinguish “devaluation” (where the secondary work diminishes the value of the original by disparaging it) from “usurpation” (where the secondary work is a close market substitute). Swatch was a classic example where complete copying did not translate into market usurpation.

Santos parallels Swatch: Kimmel aired Santos’s cameo videos in full as part of a televised segment that mocked their content and the circumstances in which they were created. The Second Circuit relied on the same structural reasoning as Swatch—transformative commentary and the absence of a plausible licensing market for the exact expressive use Kimmel made—to conclude that complete copying of the videos could be fair use. The takeaway for students is that wholesale copying in a media context can be fair when the secondary user’s purpose (reportage or commentary) is different in kind from the original and when no competing derivative market is plausibly being replaced by the secondary use.

## Authors Guild v. Google Books: searchability, snippet view, and transformativeness

Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., decided by the Second Circuit in 2015, is the paradigmatic case for a very different category of reuse: indexing and search. Google’s Library Project scanned millions of books and made “snippets”—small excerpts—available through search results. The court held that Google’s use was a transformative one because it created a new, useful function (searchability and access) that did not exploit the expressive content of the books in the way that the market for books normally does.

Google Books sharpens three lessons about fair use:

- Transformative purpose may derive from providing information services rather than commentary. Courts will recognize a transformation when the secondary use performs a new informational or functional role, such as enabling discovery or search, even if the underlying copyrighted text is not itself altered.

- The amount and substantiality inquiry is contextual. Small snippets reviewed in search results weigh in favor of fair use because they are proportionate to the new function (discoverability) and because they do not reveal the “heart” of a work in a way that substitutes for reading the book.

- Market effect analysis focuses on whether the secondary use is a substitute for the original or for a market for derivatives. Google Books’ snippet view was not a substitute for buying or licensing full books; indeed, it arguably enhanced book sales and scholarship by making works discoverable.

Compare Google Books to news programs and entertainment shows. The Google Books paradigm is useful where the secondary use is functional, informational, or infrastructural—search, indexing, or archiving. But the court in Santos did not rely on a Google‑Books style analysis because Kimmel’s use was expressive commentary, not an indexing function. Nonetheless, the Google case is instructive as a reminder that “transformative” covers more than parody and criticism; it also embraces new utilitarian functions.

## TCA Television v. McCollum and Cariou v. Prince: early‑stage resolution and visual‑art transformations

Two strands of Second Circuit authority are helpful when litigation touches appropriation art and when courts face the question whether fair use can be resolved before discovery.

TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum and related cases demonstrate that fair use can be decided at an early stage—sometimes even on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion or on summary judgment—when the complaint and materials subject to judicial notice make the necessary facts plain. The key is whether, viewing the complaint and incorporated materials in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, the use is so clearly transformative and the market‑effect considerations so weak that no reasonable jury could find infringement. Santos follows that approach: because Santos’s complaint itself described the original videos’ expressive content and the defendants’ contrasting purpose (satirical commentary), and because Santos’s own copyright registrations were before the court, the Second Circuit concluded that fair use was resolvable on the pleadings.

Cariou v. Prince offers a model for handling visual‑arts appropriation. There, a photographer sued an artist who had used and altered photographs. The Second Circuit announced a pragmatic, picture‑by‑picture assessment of transformativeness: a work is transformative when it “add[s] new expression, meaning, or message” to the original. Cariou reversed the district court’s finding for many of the appropriation pieces, remanding for a more nuanced, context‑sensitive analysis. Cariou showed that courts must compare the original and the secondary artwork to determine whether the latter has a different aesthetic or communicative purpose.

Santos’ reliance on these precedents is twofold. First, the case exemplifies the propriety of early dismissal when the pleadings and publicly available registrations make the outcome inevitable. Second, like Cariou, Santos confirms the visual‑comparative inquiry: a reasonable observer’s perception of what the original work communicated compared to the new context created by the secondary use is central to the transformativeness inquiry. In Santos the court emphasized that transformation is assessed from the reasonable observer’s perspective—not on the subjective intent of the copyright holder or of the secondary user—a point sharpened by recent Supreme Court decisions.

## TVEyes and broadcast monitoring cases: functional uses and limits

Broadcast monitoring and clipping services present a distinct challenge. These services typically provide subscribers with recorded broadcasts, searchable clips, and alerts; the use is often commercial and may substitute for obtaining licensed clips from a broadcaster. Courts have been wary of treating such functional, market‑serving services as fair uses when the secondary use effectively fills the market role of licensing or distribution—especially when the defendant operates a subscription business.

The central doctrinal pivot is functionality. If the secondary use is essentially utilitarian—indexing broadcasts, delivering verbatim clips to customers for their monitoring or competitive needs—courts ask whether the service supplants a licensing market or otherwise harms the owners’ revenue alternatives. The more a use is a mere vehicle for duplication and sale or subscription income, the less likely it is to be transformative in the expressive sense that favors fair use.

This functional/market analysis helps explain why courts treat entertainment‑oriented reuses (parody, critique, news commentary) differently from monitoring services. Santos falls on the expressive side of this divide: Kimmel’s program used the clips as material for commentary in a mass‑audience entertainment format, not as a subscription service that simply furnished raw clips to paying customers. Thus the broadcast‑monitoring cases operate as cautionary precedents—warning courts not to conflate all broadcast copying with fair use—rather than as direct impediments to expressive repurposing.

When confronting a fact pattern involving broadcast clips, ask first: is the secondary use primarily expressive/commentary or primarily functional/commercial? If the latter, market‑substitution concerns will loom larger. If the former, transformative purpose and a lack of a licensing market for the expressive use will be likely to tip the balance in favor of fair use.

## Parody lineage: Campbell and its progeny; satire and commentary in modern media

Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc. is the lodestar of parody‑related fair use doctrine. The Supreme Court held that a commercial parody—2 Live Crew’s version of “Oh, Pretty Woman”—could be fair use. Campbell instructs courts to focus first on transformativeness: does the secondary work use the original to comment on or criticize that original (parody), or does it merely use the original as a vehicle to comment on something else (satire)? The Court noted that parodies that mimic an original to make a critical point about it are more likely to be transformative, and it also observed that market harm from parodies is less likely because license markets do not typically cover critical distortions of originals.

Two connected principles run through the lineage from Campbell to the present:

- The reasonable‑observer test for purpose: courts evaluate whether the secondary use conveys a different message or purpose to viewers or readers. The test is objective; subjective intent matters less than public perception.

- The reduced weight of market‑harm concerns for critical works: where the secondary use is criticism or parody, the market‑harm factor often counts for less because licensing markets do not customarily include rights to lampoon an original.

Santos’ “Will Santos Say It?” segments fall within Campbell’s orbit but track modern nuances. Kimmel used Santos’s cameo videos to ridicule and comment on Santos’s public persona and the peculiar commercial arrangement (paid cameos). Santos argued he created the videos for inspirational messaging; Kimmel repurposed them for satire. Under Campbell and later cases, courts will find a transformative purpose where the secondary user’s new message is readily discernible to a reasonable observer—which is precisely the Second Circuit’s reasoning in Santos.

Important doctrinal distinctions remain. Satire directed at society, not the original, is less likely to be transformative in the Campbell sense because it does not rely on the original to make its critical point; instead it uses the original instrumentally. Courts view such uses with more skepticism as to fair use. In practice, modern media often blurs the line: late‑night segments that lampoon public figures generally use originals in service of social commentary about the figures themselves—and so have been treated as transformative.

Finally, Harper & Row’s old language about “good faith” has been deemphasized by later decisions. Although bad faith can in some contexts be relevant to the first factor, the Supreme Court and the Second Circuit have cast doubt on giving good or bad faith dispositive weight in the fair use calculus. Santos invoked Harper & Row’s formulation, but the Second Circuit treated the defendants’ deceptive procurement of the videos as immaterial to the fundamental transformativeness and market‑effect inquiries.

## Right of publicity overview and preemption intersections (brief note)

Copyright is not the only legal regime that protects certain exploitations of a person’s likeness or creative work. The right of publicity—an aspect of state law—permits a person to control commercial exploitation of her name, image, or persona. But when a plaintiff asserts state law claims that depend on copying expressive works, § 301 of the Copyright Act may preempt those claims if they are “equivalent” to the exclusive rights within copyright and do not contain extra elements that make them qualitatively different.

Two basic preemption principles are worth stressing:

- If a state‑law claim protects rights equivalent to copyright—i.e., it seeks to prevent unauthorized reproduction, distribution, or display of a work fixed in a tangible medium—it is likely preempted unless it contains an element that is qualitatively beyond mere copyright protection.

- Right‑of‑publicity claims often survive preemption because they require an extra element—use of identity for commercial exploitation—that is distinct from copyright’s bundle of exclusive rights. Nonetheless, survival in the preemption sense does not guarantee success on the merits: First Amendment defenses, newsworthiness exceptions, and other limitations may bar liability.

Santos did not prevail on his state law breach and fraud claims, and the Second Circuit disposed of them on ordinary contract and tort pleading grounds without resolving the preemption question. But the opinion underscores the practical point that plaintiffs who seek to litigate identity‑based harms arising from expressive use must craft causes of action that contain elements beyond simple copying and must be prepared to confront First Amendment defenses and newsworthiness exceptions. In the television‑broadcast context, courts often balance the publicity interests of speakers and public figures against a plaintiff’s right to control commercial exploitation of identity.

## Practical synthesis and applying the precedents

Putting the doctrinal pieces together yields a practical approach when faced with secondary use of expressive works—particularly in media contexts:

1. Identify the nature of the secondary use. Is it expressive commentary/parody/news reporting, or is it functional/indexing/commercial redistribution? Expressive uses favor the first factor; functional uses raise market‑substitution concerns.

2. Ask what a reasonable observer would perceive as the new work’s purpose. The inquiry is objective and focuses on the new message, context, or function—not merely the defendant’s subjective intent. Cases from Cariou through Andy Warhol and Santos emphasize this point.

3. Compare the works. For visual or audiovisual materials, courts perform a side‑by‑side comparison to assess whether the secondary work adds new expression, meaning, or message. Partial or full copying does not decide the matter in isolation—context and purpose do.

4. Consider the amount used in context. Even wholesale copying can be fair if it is appropriate to the secondary purpose and does not reveal or substitute for the heart of the original in a way that harms legitimate markets.

5. Evaluate market effect narrowly: ask whether the secondary use is a likely substitute for the original or for a derivative market that the copyright holder would reasonably enter. News reporting and parody often will not be such substitutes. Functional monitoring services, however, are more likely to be seen as market substitutes.

6. Remember that fair use can be resolved at an early stage when the complaint and incorporated materials make the analysis straightforward. TCA, Cariou, and Santos illustrate that judges need not wait for discovery where the operative facts relevant to fair use are apparent.

7. If a plaintiff brings state‑law claims alongside copyright, parse those claims for extra elements that could avoid preemption; but be ready for First Amendment and newsworthiness defenses. Contract and fraud claims will often founder if they are not pleaded with precise factual and legal elements beyond mere copying.

Santos v. Kimmel thus fits squarely into a developing jurisprudence that treats expressive transformation—whether in the form of parody, satire, news commentary, or informative repurposing—as a powerful defense to copyright claims, provided the secondary use does not simply substitute for the original in a relevant market. At the same time, the case reminds litigants and courts to respect the line between expressive reuse and functional commercial replication: when the secondary use is primarily a marketplace substitute (indexing, clipping for customers, or raw redistribution), fair use is a harder sell.
