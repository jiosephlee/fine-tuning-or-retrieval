Chapter 5: # Factor Four: Market Harm and the Usurpation Versus Criticism Distinction

The fourth statutory fair use factor—the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work (17 U.S.C. § 107(4))—is often decisive. It asks whether the secondary use usurps the market that the copyright owner would normally exploit, or in practical terms whether the secondary use acts as a competing substitute. Over the last several decades the courts have refined the inquiry away from a generalized concern about reputation or devaluation toward a focused analysis of substitution and licensing markets. Santos v. Kimmel, decided by the Second Circuit in 2025, furnishes a clear example of the modern approach: the court affirmed dismissal on the pleadings because the allegedly infringing television segments did not and could not plausibly be shown to usurp Santos’s markets for the underlying personalized videos. This chapter explains the doctrinal underpinnings of factor four, shows how Santos applies them, and draws lessons for litigants and students about what courts will and will not treat as cognizable market harm in the era of platforms, clips, remixes, and short-form content.

## The statutory text and Campbell’s usurpation test

Section 107(4) directs courts to consider “the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” The Supreme Court in Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), distilled the relevant inquiry: courts should look not generally to whether the secondary use “damages” the value of the original through criticism, parody, or devaluation, but whether it “usurps” the traditional or likely markets for licensing the original work. The critical insight from Campbell is two-fold: (1) not all adverse effects on value are cognizable under factor four—an effect that merely “devalues” a work through criticism or parody is often not the type of market injury the Copyright Act protects; and (2) the proper focus is on whether the secondary use functions as a market substitute that deprives the copyright holder of actual or likely licensing opportunities.

This substitutional or usurpation test has practical consequences. A court will scrutinize whether the defendant’s work serves the same functional purpose as the original in relevant markets—e.g., if the defendant’s work is a plausible commercial alternative to the original (for example, a competing background-music track licensed for films), factor four will weigh against fair use. By contrast, if the defendant’s work repurposes the original for a distinct communicative function—commentary, criticism, parody, news reporting—it is less likely to be viewed as a substitute for the original’s market and more likely to be protected as fair use.

Santos v. Kimmel exemplifies this approach. The Santos videos were short, personalized messages that, according to Santos’s complaint, conveyed “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Kimmel used those same clips in television segments that mocked Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for money. The Second Circuit, applying the Campbell/usurpation framework, emphasized that courts “ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Because the television segments served a radically different purpose—satire and criticism—they were not competing commercial substitutes for Santos’s personalized videos, and factor four therefore did not weigh against fair use.

## Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive: substitution over devaluation

Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive (2d Cir. 2024) is a recent Second Circuit restatement of the substitution focus. The dispute involved the Internet Archive’s library-scanning program, which allowed controlled digital lending of ebooks. The plaintiffs argued the Archive’s program harmed their market; the Archive argued for fair use. The Second Circuit emphasized that factor four is concerned with whether the secondary use “usurps” the copyright owner’s market by serving as a substitute—mere devaluation (e.g., critical commentary that might reduce demand) is not the issue. Hachette thus reinforced that the operative question is whether the defendant’s use functions as a commercially reasonable or likely alternative that the copyright holder would otherwise exploit or license.

Hachette also illustrates the interplay between the other fair use factors and the market-harm inquiry. A use that is strongly transformative in purpose and character (factor one) is less likely to be treated as a market substitute because, by definition, it performs a different communicative task. But Hachette also makes clear that evidence about the structure and operation of real markets—what purchasers actually seek and what licensing practices exist—matters. A defendant cannot avoid factor four merely by claiming noncommercial intent; a court will examine whether consumers might use the secondary work instead of acquiring the original, and whether there is a realistic market in which the secondary work displaces licensing of the original.

Santos similarly relied on Hachette’s emphasis: the television use was transformative criticism and therefore unlikely to operate as a substitute for the original personalized videos or for any reasonable licensing market for them.

## Presumptions of harm and their erosion in transformative contexts

Older decisions sometimes treated certain categories of works or uses as raising presumptions of market harm. Two classical examples are (1) the treatment of unpublished works, where the Supreme Court in Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises (1985) found a presumption of market harm from unauthorized publication of unpublished material, and (2) cases where the secondary use is a near replica of a commercially available original.

Harper & Row remains good law to the limited extent that the unauthorized publication of unpublished material can pose special concerns: a copyright owner’s right to control first publication is a significant market right. But courts have been reluctant to allow such special rules to swallow the flexibility of fair use. The Supreme Court and the federal appellate courts have increasingly scrutinized rigid presumptions—especially when the secondary work is transformative. Google v. Oracle (2021), while not a straightforward fair use holding, signaled judicial skepticism about using categorical inferences of bad faith or harm to displace the holistic fair use inquiry. The Second Circuit in Santos explicitly noted that the Supreme Court has expressed “some skepticism about whether bad faith has any role in a fair use analysis” and declined to treat Kimmel’s false identities as dispositive of market-harm concerns, particularly where the complaint itself alleged an explanatory purpose—satirical criticism—that undermined the notion of substitution.

In short, presumptions of harm have been attenuated where the secondary use is transformative: when the new work serves a different function, a court will be less willing to infer that the public would choose the new work instead of the original and thus less willing to find cognizable market harm. Santos demonstrates that tendency: not only did Kimmel’s use perform a different communicative function, but the complaint alleged no plausible licensing market that Kimmel’s broadcasts could have replaced.

## Parody, critical uses, and the unlikelihood of a viable licensing market

Campbell and succeeding cases teach a special principle about parody and critical uses: creators of expressive works ordinarily will not and do not license their works for use as the object of critique, parody, or lampoon. The Supreme Court explained in Campbell that the unlikelihood that authors of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons removes such uses “from the very notion of a potential licensing market.” Consequently, when the secondary work is a critical or parodic use of the original, factor four often weighs in favor of fair use because there is no meaningful licensing market for the use the defendant has made.

This is not an absolute rule. A defendant cannot claim free rein simply because the secondary use includes criticism. The court will ask whether the secondary work is plausibly a substitute for markets the copyright owner actually exploits or could reasonably exploit. A parody that reproduces the heart of the original and functions as a direct replacement for commercially exploited derivatives might still be regarded as substitutive. But in many cases—news reports, commentary, lampoons, reaction videos—those secondary purposes are qualitatively distinct from the markets a creator normally develops.

Santos fits the paradigm: Kimmel’s television segments were (as the complaint itself acknowledged) designed to mock Santos and thereby to comment on his behavior. The Second Circuit followed Campbell in concluding that a television segment that satirizes a person’s paid Cameo messages is not a product for which the copyright owner has a sensible licensing market. The court reiterated Campbell’s instructive sentence: licensing markets for lampoons are unlikely to exist, and an absence of such a market undercuts claims of market usurpation.

## Assessing cognizable derivative and traditional markets in the platform age

Although the core test—would the secondary work act as a substitute for the copyright holder’s market—remains constant, the kinds of markets courts must examine have proliferated in the platform age. Traditional markets once focused on print, film, broadcast, and record sales. Today, new markets and licensing practices matter: short-form clips (TikTok, Instagram Reels), highlight reels, user-generated remixes and mashups, video hosting, ephemeral messaging, and personalized video marketplaces (like Cameo) have created potential licensing opportunities that did not exist a decade ago.

When applying factor four today, courts should (and do) inquire about both (a) traditional derivative markets that the copyright owner actually exploits—e.g., sequels, movie adaptations, soundtrack licensing—and (b) reasonable, non-speculative markets that authors could be expected to develop in light of technology—e.g., licensing short-form excerpts for commercial advertising, compilations, or subscription clip services. The relevant question is not whether any market exists for any conceivable use, but whether the secondary use occupies a market that is “traditional, reasonable, or likely to be developed” by the copyright owner.

This inquiry requires attention to evidence: How does the copyright owner currently monetize the work? Are there established licensing practices for the kind of use at issue? Would consumers accept the defendant’s work as an alternative to acquiring rights in the original (for background use, for commentary, etc.)? Courts are wary of imaginative hypothetical markets conjured merely to defeat fair use. The Second Circuit in Santos relied on exactly this scrutiny: Santos alleged no plausible market that Kimmel’s broadcast could have supplanted, and the complaint’s own allegations (publication on the date of creation; the intrinsic personal nature of the videos) cut against the existence of a substantial licensing market for the particular use.

Specific platform-age examples show how the analysis plays out:

- Reaction and commentary videos. Creators of reaction videos typically use short excerpts for commentary or critique. Courts increasingly see these uses as transformative and unlikely to be substitutes for the originals, so factor four often favors fair use absent evidence of a well-developed licensing market for such clips.

- Highlight reels and sports clips. Sports leagues maintain active licensing regimes for highlights and clips; a commercial service that repackages game footage for mass consumption could usurp those markets. Factor four may therefore weigh against fair use in that context.

- Short-form remixes and musical sampling. If the secondary work functions as a new expressive work and uses limited portions, factor four might favor fair use. But if the secondary work supplants the market for licensing micro-samples—for ringtones, background loops, or other commercial uses—factor four may weigh against fair use.

- Personalized marketplaces (Cameo-style). These marketplaces produce content tailored to individual purchasers. The owner’s conventional markets may be narrow (personalized messages for a fee) and unlikely to be supplanted by broadcast or commentary uses. As Santos shows, when the original content is meant for individualized personal consumption, broadcast of those same clips for a different purpose is not a plausible substitute licensing market.

The platform age thus requires courts to identify not merely whether a secondary work is “market-harmful” in the abstract, but whether it occupies an identifiable licensing market that the copyright holder actually utilizes or reasonably could develop.

## Empirical and pleading-stage considerations

Santos also illustrates how courts handle market-harm allegations at the pleading stage. A plaintiff invoking market harm must allege facts showing plausibly that a cognizable market or class of potential markets exists—mere conclusions that the defendant’s use “damaged” the plaintiff’s market are insufficient. Under Twombly/Iqbal, plaintiffs must offer nonconclusory factual allegations about how the defendant’s use usurped or threatened actual or potential markets.

Several practical evidentiary observations follow:

- Publication timing matters. In Santos the registered videos were published the same day they were created. Publication timing can defeat arguments that the secondary use usurped a first-publication market or that the plaintiff retained exclusive first-publication exploitation rights. When a work is already widely published or publicly available, it is harder to establish that a defendant’s republishing supplanted a necessary market.

- Pleading the existence of licensing markets requires specifics. A plaintiff should plead—realistically and with particularity—the sorts of licenses sought or refused, licensing fees, identified potential licensees, or industry norms that show a market for the use at issue. Vague references to “loss of value” will not survive dismissal.

- Judicial notice and documents matter. Courts may take judicial notice of copyright registrations and published records that contradict a plaintiff’s market-harm theory. In Santos the court considered the registrations and the plaintiff’s own allegations when concluding that no plausible market-harm claim had been pleaded.

- Empirical proof at summary or trial. Where the claim survives pleading, empirical evidence may be decisive. Market analyses, consumer surveys, licensing histories, and expert testimony about consumer behavior and substitution effects can show whether a secondary use reduced demand or substituted for the original. Conversely, market data showing that the secondary use increased interest in the original—or had no impact—can favor fair use.

- Stage-sensitive inferences. Fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage when the complaint and any incorporated documents make clear that the secondary use cannot plausibly have usurped the original’s markets. The Second Circuit in cases such as TCA Television and Santos affirmed that fair use may be a basis for dismissal when the relevant facts are evident from the complaint and judicially noticeable materials.

## Applying the doctrine: lessons from Santos and hypotheticals

Santos v. Kimmel provides a compact lesson:

- Identify the purpose and character of the secondary use. If the use is critical, satirical, or otherwise transformative, it is less likely to be a market substitute.

- Define the relevant markets concretely. Ask: what markets does the plaintiff exploit now, and what reasonable markets could the plaintiff develop? Do the defendants’ uses compete in those same markets?

- Pay attention to publication and licensing facts. If the plaintiff’s work was published and made available in the same channels the defendant used, or if the plaintiff has no licensing history or viable licensing market for the defendant’s type of use, factor four will likely favor fair use.

- At the pleading stage, plead specific facts about licensing and markets. Unsupported assertions of harm are insufficient.

Practical hypotheticals clarify application:

- Parody of a pop song posted on a late-night show. If the show’s parody addresses public conduct and is not a satisfactory substitute for purchasing the original song for listening, factor four likely favors fair use (Campbell).

- Compilation service that packages short-form user clips of a musician’s songs and sells subscriptions. If the service offers a direct market alternative to purchasing licensed song snippets or streaming the artist’s works, factor four may weigh against fair use.

- News program that uses short excerpts of a politician’s personalized paid videos to criticize his public statements. As in Santos, the use is critical, the function differs from the original (public commentary versus personalized messages), and there is no plausible licensing market for criticism; factor four will likely favor fair use.

- Sports highlight app that aggregates entire game clips and sells access without obtaining league licenses. Because leagues have active markets for highlight licensing, factor four may weigh against fair use.

## Conclusion

Factor four anchors the fair use inquiry in market realities. The modern tendency in the courts—exemplified by Hachette, by the Second Circuit’s decision in Santos, and by Campbell’s original formulation—is to scrutinize substitutional effect rather than generalized reputational damage. Transformative critical or satirical uses are less likely to be substitutes for the original’s markets and thus less likely to produce cognizable market harm. In the platform era, courts must carefully identify the specific markets at stake, evaluate plausible licensing practices (not fanciful hypotheticals), and require factual allegations or proof that the secondary use actually occupies or threatens those markets. Santos demonstrates that where the secondary use performs a different function and no viable licensing market for the secondary use exists, factor four will not bar a fair use defense even at the pleading stage.
