Chapter 8: # Copyright Preemption and State-Law Workarounds

This chapter examines how federal copyright law interacts with state-law causes of action that arise from conduct involving copyrighted works. The litigation between George Santos and Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney provides a compact modern example of how courts apply the Section 301 preemption framework in the Second Circuit, and how litigants attempt — sometimes successfully, sometimes not — to escape federal preemption by pleading state-law “extra elements.” We begin with the doctrinal framework the courts use to decide when the Copyright Act displaces state law, then turn to the line of Second Circuit decisions that give content and limits to the “extra element” test (including Melendez v. Sirius XM). We then catalog the common state-law claims that arise at the margins of copyright disputes — contract, unjust enrichment, fraud, and right of publicity — and explain the typical arguments used to plead them around preemption. Finally, we analyze how the courts in Santos approached preemption and why the Second Circuit nonetheless disposed of Santos’s state-law claims on ordinary pleading grounds, and conclude with practical drafting strategies and common pitfalls.

## Section 301 preemption test in the Second Circuit: subject-matter and general equivalence

Section 301(a) of the Copyright Act contains the principal preemption provision: it withdraws from state law any cause of action that is equivalent to one of the exclusive rights enumerated in the federal statute and that falls within the subject matter of copyright. The Second Circuit applies this two-step inquiry each time a plaintiff advances a state-law claim grounded in conduct that also implicates copyrighted material.

The first step — the “subject-matter” inquiry — asks whether the works at issue are of a type protected by copyright law. This is a relatively straightforward threshold: the pieces must be original works fixed in a tangible medium of expression, falling within the categories listed in Sections 102 and 103 of the Copyright Act. If a plaintiff’s claim relies on material that is not the kind of work the Copyright Act protects (for example, uncopyrightable ideas, facts, or processes), then Section 301 does not provide preemption.

The second step — the “general equivalence” or “extra element” inquiry — asks whether the state-law cause of action asserts rights that are qualitatively different from the bundle of exclusive rights that copyright grants (reproduction, distribution, public performance, derivative works, etc.). The classic phrasing is that a state-law claim is preempted if it is “equivalent to” an exclusive federal right — meaning that it attempts to vindicate the same interests and would be established by proving the same facts as a copyright claim. If, instead, the state-law claim contains an “extra element” that changes the nature of the claim so that it is not merely a copyright substitute, preemption does not apply.

How this plays out in practice: an ordinary claim for copying a protected work — alleging unauthorized reproduction or distribution — will be preempted because it plainly falls within the federal rights and can be redressed under the Copyright Act. By contrast, a claim that depends on some additional factual element not required to prove copyright infringement — for example, a state-law fraud claim that requires proof of a misrepresentation, reliance, and pecuniary loss — may survive preemption so long as the extra element is both necessary to the cause of action and “qualitatively distinct” from rights under copyright.

The line between “equivalent” and “qualitatively different” is often the contested ground of litigation. The Second Circuit emphasizes that the extra-element test is not a formalistic exercise: courts ask whether the extra element alters the nature of the remedy to be provided or requires proof of facts beyond those necessary to establish the copyright claim. If it does, the state-law claim may proceed. If it does not — if the only wrong alleged is the unauthorized use of the copyrighted work and the state-law claim simply repackages that wrong — preemption applies.

## Melendez v. Sirius XM and the “extra element” analysis

Melendez v. Sirius XM, 50 F.4th 294 (2d Cir. 2022), is the Second Circuit’s leading recent exposition of how the extra-element test works in practice. Melendez involved claims by a plaintiff whose recording had been rebroadcast; the plaintiff advanced state-law claims that the court found “qualitatively different” from copyright infringement because they required proof of extra elements beyond what federal law demands.

Melendez is useful for two related propositions. First, it underscores that not every state-law claim that happens to involve copyrighted material is preempted; the presence of an additional, non-copyright element that changes the nature of the duty or the required proof can preserve the claim. Second, Melendez warns courts and litigants not to treat “extra element” analysis as merely a label; the courts examine the substance of the state-law cause and the relief sought to determine whether federal law truly subsumes the state claim.

Two features make an “extra element” persuasive under Melendez: (1) the extra element must be an element of the cause of action itself, not merely an allegation about how the defendant infringed (for example, alleging intent or bad faith does not always create a non-equivalent claim if copyright infringement can be established without that element); and (2) the extra element must be “qualitatively different” from the exclusive federal rights — that is, it must impose an obligation or confer a remedy that copyright law does not encompass.

Melendez leaves open important contours. For example, what counts as “qualitatively different” depends on the context; courts will examine the specific elements required by state law and the relief the plaintiff seeks. If, as in some fraud claims, the extra elements require proof of misrepresentation and reliance, courts often find that those elements create a distinct claim; but per Campbell and later authorities, the relevance of a defendant’s subjective intent or bad faith to fair use is limited, and courts will not automatically treat allegations of bad faith as sufficient to avoid preemption.

Melendez therefore occupies a middle ground. It invites careful pleading by plaintiffs who wish to preserve state-law claims in the shadow of federal copyright, but it also prevents a rote invocation of “extra elements” to avoid preemption when the substance of the claim is simply repackaged infringement.

## Common state-law claims adjacent to copyright: contract, unjust enrichment, fraud, right of publicity

State-law claims that commonly appear alongside or instead of federal copyright claims include breach of contract (express and implied), unjust enrichment, promissory estoppel, fraud (or fraudulent inducement), and the right of publicity. Each has its own doctrinal elements and its own risks of preemption.

Breach of contract. A contract claim will escape preemption if it depends on contract terms and obligations that are independent of the exclusive rights of copyright. A simple example that courts accept is when a licensing agreement contains covenants or conditions that go beyond the mere grant of reproduction or distribution rights — for instance, confidentiality provisions, quality control clauses, or express prohibitions on certain types of subsequent uses. An express breach claim rests on the bargained-for terms; its elements (existence of a contract, specific contractual term, breach of that term, damages) are distinct from a claim that someone infringed an exclusive copyright right.

But contract claims fail when plaintiffs lack privity (they are not parties to the contract) and when the contract does not plainly create enforcement rights in third parties. Courts in the Second Circuit (and elsewhere) apply ordinary state contract doctrines: third-party beneficiary status requires a clear intent in the contract to benefit the third party; implied-in-fact contracts require a meeting of the minds and the essential terms; and unilateral conditions or platform terms of service (TOS) often do not create enforceable duties to non-contracting parties. The Santos case illustrates both pitfalls: Santos attempted to rely on Cameo’s Terms of Service to hold Kimmel and the broadcasters liable, but the TOS were governed by Illinois law and contained no express third-party beneficiary language for non-users; under Illinois law a strong presumption disfavors conferring contractual benefits on noncontracting third parties. Similarly, Santos’s attempt to plead an implied-in-fact contract under New York law failed because he did not plead the essential contractual terms, meeting of the minds, or how consideration was exchanged.

Unjust enrichment. Unjust enrichment is a typical fallback allegation when plaintiffs want relief but lack a written contract. To avoid preemption, unjust enrichment claims must assert a theory that is not equivalent to copyright. Courts are receptive when the unjust enrichment claim rests on an unjust retention of profits for reasons distinct from a copyright violation — for example, where the enrichment arose from an independent wrongful act (misappropriation of confidential information, fraud, or conversion), or where the defendant’s conduct violated duties independent of the exclusive federal rights. But many unjust enrichment claims are preempted because a request for restitution for the value of unauthorized copies simply duplicates the remedy available under federal law.

Fraud and fraudulent inducement. Fraud claims can escape preemption when they require proof of additional elements — a knowingly false representation of material fact, reasonable reliance, and resulting pecuniary loss. Courts often find that these elements make a fraud claim “qualitatively different” from copyright infringement. Nonetheless, the fraud claim will still be preempted if the alleged misrepresentation merely echoes a claim of unauthorized use of the copyrighted material and the damages are coextensive with the value of the copyrighted work. The Santos opinion is instructive: Santos alleged fraudulent inducement against the Cameo requester(s), but under New York law he failed to plead the requisite out-of-pocket loss; without that economic injury, the fraud claim could not survive regardless of preemption.

Right of publicity. The right of publicity — the state-law protection against unauthorized commercial exploitation of an individual’s persona — sits at a complicated intersection with copyright. If an alleged wrong is grounded in the misappropriation of a person’s likeness or identity for commercial benefit, the claim may be separate from copyright and avoid preemption because it protects a different interest (personality/identity rather than creative expression). But if the claim is essentially a complaint about re-use of a copyrighted recording or photograph, courts may find the claim preempted. The survival of a publicity claim often depends on whether the conduct is primarily expressive (invoking First Amendment considerations) or commercial, and on whether the remedy sought targets appropriation of identity rather than copying of expressive content.

Summary. The key lesson across these claim types is consistency: if the state-law claim requires proof of an extra element — an element that changes both the nature of the duty and the proof required — the claim has a chance of surviving preemption. If the state-law cause boils down to “you used my copyrighted work without permission,” it is unlikely to survive Section 301.

## District court’s preemption ruling versus the Second Circuit’s decision to reach the merits

A notable structural feature of the Santos litigation is how the courts handled preemption. The district court dismissed Santos’s contract and implied-contract claims on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. The Second Circuit, however, avoided deciding the preemption question on the merits; instead, the panel concluded that Santos’s state-law claims failed for independent reasons under governing state law: he was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and did not plausibly allege the elements of an implied contract or the pecuniary loss element of fraudulent inducement.

This sequencing matters for two reasons.

First, reaching the merits on ordinary state-law grounds is often a more straightforward and narrow disposition than deciding a federal preemption issue. Courts will sometimes dispose of a case on state-law pleading deficiencies where those defects are clear and do not require a sweeping interpretation of the Copyright Act. That was the Second Circuit’s approach here: because Santos’s pleading failed to establish essential elements of the contract and fraud claims, the court did not need to resolve whether the Copyright Act would have displaced those claims.

Second, the district court’s preemption ruling (though not dispositive on appeal) highlights how defendants often seek to invoke Section 301 as early, broad relief. Preemption can be an attractive defense because, if it applies, it can eliminate state-law claims wholesale. But plaintiffs can thwart preemption by pleading state-law elements that are independent, specific, and well-pleaded. Conversely, plaintiffs who seek to preserve noncopyright causes of action should anticipate preemption challenges and draft their complaints to affirmatively plead the extra elements that make their claims qualitatively different.

One practical corollary is that an appellate court’s choice to decide or avoid a preemption question does not change the underlying doctrine; it simply reflects judicial economy and a preference to resolve cases on narrower, settled grounds when available. Nevertheless, litigants should be mindful that a district court’s preemption ruling may shape discovery and strategy even if the appellate court ultimately resolves the case on state-law pleading grounds.

## Drafting strategies and pitfalls when pleading state claims in copyright-adjacent disputes

For practitioners and litigants working at the interface of copyright and state law, the Santos litigation supplies a set of practical dos and don’ts. Below are core drafting strategies to minimize the risk of preemption and to survive early Rule 12(b)(6) scrutiny.

1. Plead the “extra element” explicitly and with particularity. If invoking fraud, identify the misrepresentation or omission, the speaker, the audience, the time and place, and the specific statements relied upon. Allege reasonable reliance and the causal link to the claimed pecuniary injury. If reliance cannot be plausibly pleaded, the fraud claim will likely fail or be treated as preempted. The Second Circuit in Santos affirmed dismissal of Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim because he failed to allege an actual out-of-pocket loss under New York law.

2. In contract-based theories, plead privity or third-party beneficiary status plainly. If you rely on a Terms of Service or other contract to create a right in a non-signatory, include the precise contract language showing an intent to confer enforceable rights on third parties. If you allege an implied-in-fact contract, plead the essential terms: offer, acceptance, consideration, and a meeting of the minds, describing how those elements were met by the parties’ conduct. The Santos court rejected Santos’s direct and implied contract claims because he neither showed contractual privity nor pled the specific essential terms required under New York law.

3. Quantify and connect damages to the pleaded theory. A state-law cause that survives preemption still requires damages that flow from the extra element, not merely the value of the copyrighted work. For example, if you allege reliance-based damages for fraud, explain how the misrepresentation caused economic loss beyond the lost licensing value of the copyrighted work. If only statutory or discretionary remedies are sought, explain why the state claim provides relief that copyright does not.

4. Avoid pleading purely duplicative legal theories. Do not draft state-law counts that merely restate the copyright infringement allegations in different clothes. Courts look at the substance, not the label. If the gravamen of the plaintiff’s complaint is that the defendant reproduced, distributed, or publicly performed the plaintiff’s copyrighted work, a state-law claim that merely seeks the same relief will be vulnerable to preemption.

5. Use alternative theories carefully and coherently. It is common to plead copyright alongside contract, fraud, and unjust enrichment. But ensure that each count stands on its own by alleging distinct elements and facts. If the fraud count depends entirely on the same proof as the copyright claim and lacks a separate reliance/damage theory, a court may treat it as preempted or dismiss it on pleading grounds.

6. Consider choice-of-law implications in advance. Platform terms of service are often governed by location-specific law and forum-selection clauses; those choices matter for issues like third-party beneficiaries and the existence of reliance-based damages. In Santos, Illinois law governed the Cameo Terms and supplied a strong presumption against conferring third-party benefits; New York law governed Santos’s implied contract and fraud claims and required particular elements that Santos did not plead.

7. Preserve factual evidence and communications that show independent duties. If you want to prove an implied contract, reliance, or misrepresentation, preserve messages, emails, invoices, or testimony that support the alleged elements. Vague assertions about “the parties’ understanding” or “industry custom” seldom withstand Rule 12 scrutiny.

8. Anticipate affirmative defenses at the pleading stage. Fair use is an affirmative defense to copyright claims, and the courts will sometimes decide fair use at the pleading stage when the necessary facts are apparent from the complaint and documents integral to it. If a state-law claim depends on the same materials and would be affected by a fair-use finding, prepare to explain why the state claim asserts an independent obligation.

Pitfalls to avoid

- Do not assume that alleging subjective bad faith automatically creates an “extra element.” Courts have been skeptical about treating bad faith alone as a means to avoid preemption, especially where the gravamen of the complaint remains copying. Harper & Row’s language about good faith has been narrowed by later decisions (Campbell, Google v. Oracle) and the Supreme Court’s skepticism about excluding bad actors from fair use.

- Do not plead damages as conclusory. Courts will dismiss claims that assert generic “lost licensing revenue” or “harm to market value” without explaining how the state claim’s damages differ from what would be recoverable under the Copyright Act.

- Do not neglect to plead causation. Even if you identify an extra element like reliance, you must connect the defendant’s conduct to the specific losses alleged.

- Beware platform terms of service. Plaintiffs often try to use a platform’s TOS to sue downstream users; but many platforms disclaim third-party beneficiaries and limit remedies. Read and quote the relevant provisions, and plead why the TOS were intended to benefit non-users.

Checklist for pleading defensible state-law claims alongside copyright claims

- Identify the specific state cause of action and cite its elements.
- For each element, plead concrete facts showing how the element is satisfied.
- If relying on a contract, attach the contract and quote the precise provisions that create a right in the plaintiff (e.g., third-party beneficiary language).
- If alleging fraud, plead the who, what, when, where, and how, and quantify reliance and damages.
- If alleging unjust enrichment, explain why common-law restitution is justified for reasons independent of copyright (e.g., wrongful retention after fraud).
- Explain why the prospective remedies are not duplicative of copyright remedies (e.g., punitive damages for fraud, disgorgement for unjust enrichment for wrongful behavior unrelated to reproduction rights).
- Anticipate and brief likely preemption and fair-use defenses in initial papers.

## Applying the doctrine to Santos: what the courts did and why the outcome matters

Santos’s complaint illustrates how the rules above operate in a real dispute. Santos alleged that Kimmel had used the Cameo platform to procure videos, which Santos created and published on the same day, and then broadcast those clips on a national television program to mock Santos in a recurring segment. Santos asserted federal copyright infringement claims and state-law claims for breach of contract, breach of an implied-in-fact contract, and fraudulent inducement.

The district court dismissed the copyright claims at the pleading stage, finding on the face of the complaint and the registered copyrights that Kimmel’s broadcast segments were a paradigmatic example of a transformative use — satire or commentary — and therefore were protected by fair use. The Second Circuit affirmed that disposition because fair use is flexible and can be resolved where the relevant facts and registrations are on the record. That disposition shows two things: (1) fair use can be raised and disposed of on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint and attached materials make the relevant facts plain; and (2) if fair use applies, the federal copyright claim itself will fail, which affects plaintiffs’ strategic posture vis-à-vis state claims.

As to Santos’s state claims, the district court concluded that the contract and implied-contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act and that the fraud claim failed to allege the required elements. The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal, but not on the preemption ground; rather, the court held that (1) under Illinois law the Cameo Terms of Service did not confer enforceable rights on Santos as a non-signatory; (2) under New York law Santos had not pled the essential terms or a meeting of minds for an implied-in-fact contract; and (3) Santos had not alleged any actual out-of-pocket loss needed to sustain a fraudulent inducement claim under New York law.

This disposition is instructive for two reasons. First, it illustrates how courts will apply ordinary state-law principles to claims that plaintiffs may try to validate as copyright workarounds. Absent clear contractual privity, explicit third-party beneficiary language, or the essential elements of an implied promise, a contract-based route around copyright is fragile. Second, it shows that plaintiffs cannot rely on pleading deficiencies as a fallback; even if preemption could be avoided doctrinally, plaintiffs must still satisfy ordinary elements of the state law causes they invoke.

Finally, Santos underscores a practical device for defendants: in copyright-adjacent disputes, defendants have multiple early-bandwidth defenses (fair use, preemption, failure to plead contract elements, failure to plead damages for fraud). A defendant who can negate one or more elements at the pleading stage can often resolve the case without broad preemption rulings.

Conclusion

Section 301 preemption establishes a broad federal baseline, but courts and litigants operate in the margins where state law can and does remain available. The “extra element” test (as explicated in Melendez and applied in subsequent decisions) is the principal doctrinal mechanism that allows state-law claims to survive federal preemption. Success in preserving state-law claims turns on careful fact-pleading: identify and plead the extra element with specificity, tie damages to non-copyright injuries, and plead contractual or reliance elements in detail. The Santos litigation is a cautionary tale: trying to convert a copyright dispute into a contract or fraud claim without pleading the independent elements will not typically defeat federal preemption or survive the ordinary pleading requirements.
