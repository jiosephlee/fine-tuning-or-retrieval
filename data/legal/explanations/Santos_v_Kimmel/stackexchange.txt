Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### If the defendant instigated the creation of the videos, how can his later use still be "transformative" fair use?
Question:
The opinion rejects Santos’s argument that Kimmel can’t claim transformation because he "instigated the creation" of the Cameo videos by submitting the prompts. The panel instead applies a reasonable observer standard (citing Warhol) and says the JKL segments had a different purpose—commenting on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—than the videos’ original purpose of encouragement/positivity. I’m confused how the instigator point fits: If Kimmel designed the prompts to elicit specific statements, doesn’t that make the original purpose the same as his later use? Does it matter that Santos contends his original purpose aligned with Kimmel’s (i.e., to say what the buyer asked for, for money)? How do courts cabin manipulation of the "original" purpose by the secondary user when assessing transformation?
Answer:
Short answer: Because fair-use transformation looks to what a reasonable observer would perceive the original work’s purpose to be, not to who secretly prompted its creation. The Second Circuit (quoting Warhol/Goldsmith) rejected Santos’s “instigator” argument for that reason: even if Kimmel solicited the lines, the published Cameo videos objectively conveyed messages of encouragement/positivity, while the JKL segments re‑purposed those clips for satire/criticism. That difference in purpose/character — judged objectively — supports a finding of transformation.

Why that is so (doctrine and how the opinion applies it)
- The key fair‑use inquiry under the first factor is whether the new work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character,” i.e., is transformative. The court relies on the objective, reasonable‑observer test articulated in Warhol/Goldsmith: subjective intent of the original author or the secondary user does not control the purpose-of-use inquiry. (Opinion at ¶ I, citing Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023).)
- The Santos opinion explains the consequence: even if Kimmel “instigated” or “designed” prompts, what matters is how the videos would be understood by a reasonable observer. The complaint itself alleged that the published Cameo videos conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” A reasonable observer would therefore see the original purpose as encouragement — not as an invitation to say absurd things for money. The JKL segments, by contrast, had the clear purpose of sarcastic criticism (mocking Santos’s willingness to say odd things for money). That objective difference supports transformation. (Opinion at ¶¶ I–II, pp. discussing reasonable‑observer standard and App’x citations.)
- The opinion also notes that the Harper & Row language about good faith is not decisive here: even if bad faith were argued from Kimmel’s misrepresentations, the complaint does not plausibly allege an intent to supplant Santos’s market; the complaint itself paints the defendants’ purpose as criticism/satire, which aligns with fair use. (Opinion at ¶ I, discussing Harper & Row.)

How courts cabin “manipulation” of the original purpose
- By using an objective test: courts look at the content and context of the original, as published, and ask what a reasonable observer would take its purpose to be. That prevents a secondary user from defeating transformation simply by showing they elicited particular wording, and it prevents a copyright holder from insisting on a subjective intent that conflicts with the work’s objective effect.
- That does not mean instigation is irrelevant in every case. If the published original, as reasonably perceived, genuinely served the same expressive purpose as the secondary use, then transformation is less likely. Put another way: instigation won’t convert nontransformative reuse into transformative reuse where the objective purposes are the same; but instigation alone cannot change the objective meaning of the published work if that meaning is plainly different from the secondary use’s purpose.
- The opinion illustrates both points: Santos’s argument that Kimmel “designed” the originals fails because the objective content of the videos (as alleged) is encouraging, while JKL’s use is critical/satirical — an objectively different purpose. The panel therefore treated instigation as immaterial to the first‑factor analysis under the reasonable‑observer standard. (Opinion at ¶ I.)

Bottom line for your hypothetical questions
- Kimmel prompting Santos doesn’t automatically make the original purpose identical to the later use. What matters is how the original came across to a reasonable observer once it was published. If that objective perception differs from the secondary use’s purpose (as the court found here), the secondary use can be transformative despite the instigation.
- If the published original really did communicate the same purpose as the reuse (objectively), then a court could — and should — find less (or no) transformation even if the secondary user instigated the content. The reasonable‑observer test is the guardrail that cabins manipulation.

### Does deception in obtaining a work (fake names, misrepresentations) negate fair use after Harper & Row?
Question:
Santos argued that Kimmel’s fake-name Cameo requests show bad faith that should nullify fair use. The panel says Harper & Row’s "fair use presupposes good faith" language has been distanced from, and emphasizes defendants’ purpose as commentary/criticism rather than market substitution. What is the current Second Circuit (and Supreme Court) view on whether bad faith or deception can weigh against, or defeat, fair use? Is there a meaningful distinction between deception used to scoop or supplant a market (e.g., unpublished manuscript in Harper & Row) and deception used to facilitate commentary? How do courts weigh bad faith under factor one versus treating it as irrelevant?
Answer:
Short answer: No — after Harper & Row, deception or bad faith is not an automatic, dispositive bar to fair use. Courts in the Second Circuit (as the Santos panel explains) treat deceptive conduct as one piece of evidence about the “purpose and character” of the use (and as relevant to market-substitution concerns), not as a standalone trump to the statutory four‑factor inquiry. Santos v. Kimmel illustrates the point: the panel rejected Santos’s contention that Kimmel’s fake‑name requests “nullify” fair use, holding that the Harper & Row language that “fair use presupposes good faith” has been narrowed and that the complaint itself showed a clearly critical, transformative purpose and no market usurpation.

Why — doctrinally — that is so
- Fair use remains a four‑factor, flexible inquiry (17 U.S.C. § 107). The Second Circuit emphasizes that the first factor — “purpose and character” — focuses chiefly on whether the secondary use is transformative (i.e., whether it adds a new purpose or character rather than merely superseding the original). Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § I (citing Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith and other Second Circuit precedent).
- Harper & Row’s statement that “fair use presupposes good faith” is acknowledged but the Santos panel notes the Supreme Court has since “distanced itself from this statement.” Santos, slip op. § I. Thus bad faith is not treated as an independent, dispositive rule that automatically defeats fair use.
- Instead, courts use evidence of deception to inform the first factor (purpose/character) — does the deception reveal an intent to copy or to supplant the market for the original? — and to inform the fourth factor (market effect). The panel invokes the familiar line that fair use fails when the secondary work “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute,” and that criticism/parody typically do not occupy a licensing market (citing Campbell and Hachette lines quoted in the opinion). Santos, slip op. § I.

Is there a meaningful distinction between types of deception?
- Yes, practically and doctrinally. Santos draws the distinction the way courts do: deception that evidences an effort to scoop, steal, or commercially supplant an unpublished or marketable work is the kind of misconduct that historically weighed heavily (Harper & Row). By contrast, deception used to facilitate commentary or critical speech — where the secondary use is plainly transformative and does not act as a market substitute — is far less likely to defeat fair use. Santos rejected the plaintiff’s argument because the complaint itself portrayed defendants’ motive as sarcastic criticism, not market substitution. Santos, slip op. § I.

How courts actually weigh bad faith
- They do not treat bad faith as an automatic disqualifier. Rather:
  - Bad faith/deception can be evidence that the use was not transformative or that the defendant intended to supplant the copyright holder’s market — and so it can weigh against fair use under factor one (and relatedly factor four).
  - But if the use is genuinely transformative (criticism, commentary, parody, etc.) and there is no plausible market substitution, courts will credit fair use despite deceptive means. That is exactly what the Second Circuit did in Santos: it found factor one “strongly supports” fair use because the alleged purpose was commentary, and factor four showed no market harm; therefore the deceptive submission of requests did not nullify fair use. Santos, slip op. § I.

Practical bottom line for litigation and pleading
- On a motion to dismiss, if the complaint itself shows a transformative purpose and the absence of market substitution, allegations of deception will likely be insufficient to defeat a fair use defense (as in Santos).
- Conversely, if the plaintiff plausibly alleges that deception was undertaken to obtain unpublished material or to supplant a licensing market (the archetypal Harper & Row scenario), that misconduct will weigh heavily against fair use and may be fatal.

Key citations from the opinion
- “It is true that the Supreme Court stated in Harper & Row that ‘[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.’ … But the Court has since distanced itself from this statement.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § I.
- The court treated deception as relevant only insofar as it bore on purpose/market substitution and concluded the complaint showed a critical, transformative purpose and no market usurpation, so factor one “strongly supports” fair use. Santos, slip op. § I.

In short: bad faith is a relevant factor but not a per se bar — its effect depends on whether it shows market‑supplanting intent or undermines the transformative character of the secondary use. Santos is the recent Second Circuit application of that principle.

### How can fair use be decided on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion—what materials can the court consider at the pleadings stage?
Question:
The court affirms dismissal because fair use was clear from the complaint and the works themselves, citing TCA Television and Cariou. Procedurally, what has to be in or attached to the complaint (or incorporated by reference) to let a court consider the original Cameo videos and the JKL segments on a 12(b)(6) motion? Can the court take judicial notice of the videos if they are publicly available online, or must they be exhibits? The panel also took notice of copyright registrations—are there limits on using registration data (e.g., publication dates) at this stage to resolve factor-two issues?
Answer:
Short answer
A court can resolve fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion only when the complaint plus the works before the court make fair use “so clearly established” that dismissal is proper. Santos shows what that means procedurally: the judge may consider (1) the allegations in the complaint, (2) documents attached as exhibits or incorporated by reference, and (3) certain public records (most importantly, Copyright Office registrations). What the panel did not do in terms of broad judicial notice was expand those categories — it treated the original Cameo videos and the JKL segments as the works “before the court” (Cariou) and relied on judicial notice of registrations (Island Software/TCA). So to get a 12(b)(6) fair‑use dismissal, put the works or properly incorporate them and (if relevant) attach or identify the registrations.

Explanation and supporting authority from Santos

1) When fair use can be decided on the pleadings
- Santos reiterates the governing principle: fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal” at the pleading stage. The panel relied on TCA Television and Cariou for that proposition (opinion ¶ I, citing TCA Television, Cariou). That means the court must be able to decide fair use on the basis of the complaint and the works that the complaint places before the court.

2) What materials the court may consider at 12(b)(6)
- The opinion applies the standard list from DiFolco: the court may consider “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” (Santos, quoting DiFolco, ¶ I.)  
- The panel also said it needed only “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” to decide fair use (quoting Cariou), and it resolved fair use based on the complaint plus those works. In other words: the actual works must be before the court either by attachment or by incorporation by reference so the court can compare them.

Practical implication: to enable a 12(b)(6) fair‑use decision, the plaintiff’s complaint should put the defendant’s allegedly infringing work and the plaintiff’s original work before the court — typically by attaching the works (or accurate reproductions/transcripts) as exhibits or by pleading facts that incorporate them and make them integral to the complaint.

3) Judicial notice of online videos?
- Santos does not state that the panel took judicial notice of videos simply because they were publicly available online. Instead, the court proceeded on the ground that the original videos and the JKL segments were the works “before the court” (Cariou) and applied DiFolco’s exhibit/incorporation rule. The opinion expressly notes the court may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations… as published in the Copyright Office’s registry” (citing Island Software and TCA Television), but it does not adopt a general rule in this opinion that publicly available web videos may be judicially noticed without being attached or incorporated.  
- Bottom line from Santos: do not rely on automatic judicial notice of internet videos. If you want the court to compare works at the pleadings stage, the safer route — and the route the opinion contemplates — is to attach or incorporate the relevant video files, transcripts, or other faithful reproductions so the court actually has the works before it.

4) Use of copyright registrations at the pleading stage
- Santos expressly relied on the Copyright Office registrations and the court’s authority to take judicial notice of them: “we may also take judicial notice of ‘copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.’” (Opinion, citing Island Software & TCA Television.) The panel used the registrations to establish that the videos were published the same day they were created (opinion ¶ I).  
- That shows courts will accept registration information from the public registry at the pleadings stage to establish objective facts in the registry (e.g., registration and publication dates), consistent with Island Software/TCA. But Santos does not suggest that registration entries displace disputed factual issues that are not of public record; the opinion uses registration data to supply an uncontested, public‑record fact (publication date) to inform a fair‑use factor analysis.

Practical implication: include registration numbers or certificates (or plainly identify them in the complaint) so the court can take judicial notice of whatever the Copyright Office registry shows (e.g., registration/publication date). Courts will accept those registry facts at the pleadings stage, but they will not convert contested, non‑public factual disputes into judicially noticed facts.

Short checklist for litigants who want a 12(b)(6) fair‑use decision (based on Santos)
- Attach or incorporate the plaintiff’s original work(s) and the defendant’s allegedly infringing work(s) (videos, transcripts, screenshots, etc.) so the court actually has the works for side‑by‑side comparison (Cariou; DiFolco).  
- Plead factual allegations that make the works integral to the claim (so they are properly before the court).  
- Attach or identify Copyright Office registration certificates/numbers if you rely on publication or registration dates; the court can take judicial notice of those registry entries (Island Software/TCA as applied in Santos).  
- Don’t assume the court will judicially notice web videos merely because they are publicly available unless you have placed them before the court by exhibit or incorporation; Santos treats the works as the ones “before the court,” not as newly noticed internet evidence.

Conclusion
Santos confirms that fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage, but only when the complaint and the actual works before the court plainly show fair use. Procedurally, that means you must put the works before the court (attach them or incorporate them) and, if useful, supply registration information for judicial notice. The opinion authorizes judicial notice of Copyright Office registry entries but does not endorse taking judicial notice of online videos as a substitute for attaching or incorporating the works themselves.

### Why doesn’t broadcasting Cameo videos on TV create cognizable market harm under factor four?
Question:
On market effect, the panel quotes Hachette and Campbell and says the relevant question is usurpation of a substitutive market, not harm from criticism. But Santos sells personalized videos; couldn’t there be a licensing market for re-broadcasting those videos or for creating similar sponsored content? How do courts distinguish between (a) a genuine, substitutive licensing market for using the plaintiff’s videos on TV and (b) a non-cognizable market for licensing criticism or lampooning? Does it matter that Cameo or Santos might offer licenses for media use in non-critical contexts?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the fourth fair‑use factor asks whether the secondary use usurps a traditional or likely licensing market for the original work — not merely whether the secondary use harms or “devalues” the work by criticizing it. Courts (and the Santos panel) therefore require a plausible, substitutive licensing market that the defendant’s use would supplant. Critical, transformative uses like Kimmel’s are unlikely to fall into that market, so broadcasting Cameo videos as lampoons did not create cognizable market harm under factor four.

Why — doctrine and how courts distinguish the two kinds of markets
- Factor four is about usurpation of a market for the original work (or a derivative) — a market the copyright owner would reasonably exploit — not about loss of value caused by criticism. As the Santos opinion quotes, “we ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” (Santos opinion quoting Hachette and Campbell.)
- The key questions are (1) what is the nature of the defendant’s use (transformative criticism/commentary v. a substitute commercial exploitation), and (2) whether there is a cognizable licensing market for that specific kind of use. Campbell makes the point directly: “[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.” Santos applies that principle to these facts.

How courts apply that to distinguish (a) vs. (b)
- If the plaintiff can plausibly show an actual or likely market in which users would pay to license the exact kind of use the defendant made (e.g., licensing unaltered clips for rebroadcast as ads or program segments), factor four points against fair use. A court will look at the original purpose of the works, the nature of the license market pleaded, and whether the defendant’s use is functionally equivalent to the licensed exploitation.
- By contrast, if the defendant’s use is transformative criticism/parody (adding new meaning, message, or purpose), there is usually no realistic market in which owners license others to make those critical uses. In that situation courts treat the licensing market as non‑cognizable for factor four (Campbell), and the fourth factor does not weigh against fair use.

How that controlled the result in Santos
- The panel concluded Kimmel’s segments were transformative criticism (first factor) — a reasonable observer would view the videos as re‑used for mockery/commentary, not replaced commercial promotion — and the complaint itself acknowledged the defendants’ purpose. The court therefore held Santos failed to allege market harm under factor four: the TV segments did not usurp a plausible licensing market for Santos’s original Cameo videos. The opinion repeats the Campbell/Hachette formulation and affirms dismissal at the pleading stage because the complaint did not plausibly identify a substitutive market the defendants had taken.
- The opinion also notes you need only compare the original works and the secondary works to decide fair use (Cariou), and that Santos’s own allegations (e.g., the videos’ published purpose and timing) undermined any claim that the defendants supplanted a licensing market.

Does it matter that Santos or Cameo might license media use in non‑critical contexts?
- Yes — but only to the extent the pleaded licensing market matches the defendants’ use. A plaintiff who ordinarily licenses unaltered commercial rebroadcast rights could plausibly allege a cognizable market for exactly those non‑transformative reuses. That market would be relevant to factor four.
- However, a market for non‑critical/promotional uses does not automatically translate into a market for critical, satirical, or lampooning uses. Campbell and Santos treat licensing for criticism/parody as unlikely; therefore offering licenses for non‑critical contexts does not, without more, make a licensing market for the defendants’ critical TV segments cognizable.

Bottom line
- Factor four asks about usurpation of a substitutive licensing market for the particular use at issue. Transformative criticism (like the Kimmel segments) is not the kind of use creators generally license, so broadcasting the Cameo videos as part of a mocking TV segment did not plausibly usurp a cognizable market and therefore did not create the kind of market harm that defeats fair use in Santos.

### How did the "nature of the work" factor apply where the videos are creative but were "published the same day"?
Question:
The panel notes that the copyright registrations show the videos were published the same day they were created and cites 17 U.S.C. § 101. I’m trying to understand the factor-two analysis: Are these Cameo videos considered highly creative (which would weigh against fair use) but with the "published" status mitigating that? How exactly does "publication" under § 101 get determined from the registration record, and why does being published matter here if the works are otherwise expressive and personalized?
Answer:
Short answer
- The “nature of the work” factor (factor two) ordinarily disfavors fair use when the original is highly creative and especially when it is unpublished. The Santos opinion acknowledges the creative, expressive character of the Cameo videos but then points out that the copyright registrations show the videos were published the same day they were created. The court treated that publication status (established by the registration record) as undercutting the argument that the works were unpublished and so reduced the weight of factor two against fair use. Coupled with a strongly transformative use (factor one) and no cognizable market usurpation (factor four), publication helped tip the balance toward fair use.

Why publication matters under factor two
- Federal fair‑use doctrine treats creative, imaginative works as closer to the core of copyright and thus generally more protective (so factor two tends to weigh against fair use). Unpublished works receive extra protection because the author has a right to control the first public appearance of her expression (see Campbell, which the opinion cites for related principles).
- Publication removes that extra layer of protection: once a work has been published the “unpublished” concern is gone, so factor two is less powerful for the copyright holder.

How the court determined publication here
- The panel relied on the copyright registration entries in the record, stating explicitly that “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101.” The court may take judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations (see Island Software & Comput. Servs., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.; TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum), and the registrations themselves record a publication date. That is what the opinion used to treat the videos as published.

Putting it together for Santos v. Kimmel
- The opinion (1) found the JKL segments highly transformative (factor one strongly favored fair use); (2) recognized that the videos are expressive/creative but treated the registration evidence of same‑day publication as diminishing the usual force of factor two; and (3) found no market usurpation (factor four). The court therefore concluded that, even though the originals were creative, their published status (as shown by the registration entries) reduced factor two’s support for Santos and helped the court conclude fair use at the pleading stage.

Practical takeaways
- Creative work ≠ automatic win on factor two; unpublished status is what gives factor two its greatest bite. If a registration or other record shows publication, courts will treat the “unpublished” concern as absent and will give factor two less weight against fair use.

### Why couldn’t Santos enforce Cameo’s user Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary under Illinois law?
Question:
For the direct breach claim, the court applies Illinois law and says there’s a strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries unless the contract "practically" says so expressly. Santos argued Kimmel (a user) breached the TOS that users agree to, but the panel says there’s nothing close to an express declaration granting Santos enforcement rights, and notes other provisions do have explicit third-party language. Practically speaking, what sort of language would have been sufficient to let a Cameo creator like Santos enforce user TOS? Does the presence of some express third-party clauses elsewhere in the TOS create a negative inference against implied third-party status for creators?
Answer:
Short answer
Because Illinois law “strong[ly] presum[es] against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties” and requires the implication “to be so strong as to be practically an express declaration,” the panel found the Cameo user Terms lacked the near‑explicit language Illinois requires. The court stressed that other provisions of the same Terms do contain explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language, which shows how the drafters would have granted third‑party enforcement if they intended to do so. See Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (quoting Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur).

Why the court refused to treat Santos as a third‑party beneficiary
- Governing doctrine: Under Illinois law (which the court applied to the Terms), there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties,” and the contract must be “practically an express declaration” to overcome that presumption. The panel relied on that formulation in rejecting Santos’s direct breach claim. Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II (citing Barry).
- What the opinion found in the Terms: The court found “nothing close to an ‘express declaration’ that the relevant provisions . . . apply to [Santos] or other noncontracting parties.” The opinion also notes that the Terms elsewhere contain explicit third‑party beneficiary language, which “indicat[es] that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Id. (emphasis added).
- Practical effect: Because the Terms did not contain near‑explicit language saying creators like Santos were intended beneficiaries or had enforcement rights, the Illinois presumption against third‑party enforcement was not overcome.

What wording would have been sufficient (practical examples)
The court’s standard — requiring something “practically an express declaration” — means the Terms would need language plainly conveying that creators are intended beneficiaries with a right to enforce. Examples of the kind of wording that would meet that standard (and that the court said was missing) include clauses that:
- expressly name creators/performers as intended third‑party beneficiaries; or
- state that users’ promises are “for the benefit of” creators and that creators “may enforce” the Terms against users; or
- use unambiguous phrases such as “intended third‑party beneficiaries” and explicitly grant those beneficiaries enforcement rights.

(The opinion itself does not give a model clause; it articulates the standard and explains that nothing in the Cameo Terms came close to that “express declaration.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II.)

Does the presence of explicit third‑party clauses elsewhere create a negative inference?
Yes. The panel treated the presence of express third‑party‑beneficiary language in other parts of the Terms as evidence that the drafter knew how to confer enforcement rights when intended, and therefore the absence of such language in the provisions Santos relied on weighs against finding an implied third‑party beneficiary. The opinion states this explicitly: the existence of explicit third‑party language elsewhere “indicat[es] that when the parties . . . intended to allow provisions . . . to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II.

One related point the opinion makes (why Santos’s other contract theories also failed)
Even apart from the third‑party‑beneficiary issue under Illinois law, the court rejected Santos’s attempt to plead an implied‑in‑fact contract (governed by New York law here) because he failed to plead the essential terms or a meeting of the minds. See Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II (discussing Caniglia and DG & A citations).

Bottom line
Under the standard the court applied, mere benefits to creators from user agreements are not enough; the Terms must practically (i.e., nearly literally) say the nonparty is an intended and enforceable beneficiary. The Cameo Terms did not do that, and the fact that some provisions did use explicit third‑party language made the omission in the disputed provisions dispositive.

### Why did New York law govern the implied-in-fact contract claim, and what "essential terms" had to be pleaded?
Question:
The court analyzes the implied-in-fact contract claim under New York law (citing Rocky Point, Caniglia, DG & A), while applying Illinois law to the express TOS claim. What choice-of-law principles explain that split—was it because the TOS had an Illinois choice-of-law clause but the alleged implied agreement arose in New York? Substantively, what specific "essential terms" would Santos have needed to allege non-conclusorily to state an implied contract with Kimmel (e.g., no broadcast use, no commercial exploitation, price adjustments), and how would a "meeting of the minds" be shown if there was no explicit discussion beyond the standard Cameo transaction?
Answer:
Short answer

The panel treated the two contract claims under different governing law because they were legally different contracts: the express Terms of Service claim was analyzed under the TOS (which the court says is governed by Illinois law), while the separate implied‑in‑fact contract claim was analyzed under New York law (the court expressly applies New York precedents). The opinion does not walk through a choice‑of‑law analysis in detail; it simply applies Illinois law to the TOS claim and New York law to the implied‑in‑fact claim and cites New York cases for the standards that govern pleading an implied‑in‑fact contract.

What New York law required Santos to plead

The panel relied on New York authorities — Rocky Point, Caniglia, and DG & A — to explain what Santos needed to plead for an implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law. Two key requirements emerge from the opinion:

- Allegations of the essential terms in nonconclusory language. The court quotes Caniglia for the rule that a plaintiff must “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” App’x 22 ¶¶ 78–79 (opinion citing Caniglia).
- An indication of a meeting of the minds. The court cites DG & A and Tjoa for the separate requirement that the complaint plausibly allege “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement.” The panel held Santos’s complaint failed both prongs.

What “essential terms” Santos would have needed to plead (concrete examples)

Under the Caniglia/Rocky Point/DG & A framework the plaintiff must identify the deal’s core terms with factual specificity, not mere legal labels. For this case that would mean alleging, with concrete facts, each of the following elements as to the putative implied contract between Santos and the Kimmel/ABC defendants:

- The parties and the relationship: who made the promise and who accepted it (e.g., that Kimmel, or his agent, directly requested and agreed to limits on use).
- Subject matter / scope of the agreement: a clear statement of what use was permitted and what was forbidden — e.g., “personalized Cameo video for private distribution only; no broadcast, no inclusion in television segments, no commercial exploitation.”
- Duration/territory/exclusivity (if relevant): how long the restriction applied and whether it was exclusive.
- Consideration / price: who paid whom, what payment (or other consideration) was provided, and whether payment was tied to the limited use; if Santos contends the fee was contingent on restricted use, the complaint must allege that factually.
- Performance details / delivery and acceptance mechanism: how the videos were to be delivered and how the requester signaled acceptance of the terms (e.g., platform confirmation, emailed instruction or acknowledgement).
- The specific provision alleged to have been breached: identify the particular promise (for example, “Kimmel agreed not to broadcast or commercially exploit the videos”).
- Damages/remedy tied to that breach.

How to plead a “meeting of the minds” where no explicit bargaining occurred

When there is no express negotiation beyond a standard platform transaction (the factual posture here), New York law requires factual allegations showing mutual assent through words or conduct. The opinion holds Santos failed to do that. Sufficient allegations would include particularized facts such as:

- Direct communications: copies or quoted text of messages, emails, or Cameo request/response content in which the requester (or an agent) expressly limited use (e.g., “please do not broadcast this; it’s for private use”) and the requester or platform acknowledged or accepted those limits.
- Platform confirmations or representations: factual allegations that Cameo or the requester’s account gave affirmative assurances that only private use was intended/allowed, or that a particular request carried an explicit usage restriction visible to both parties.
- Course of dealing or repeated practice: allegations that the requester repeatedly requested videos with the same explicit limitation and the requester consistently accepted them, creating a mutual expectation (with dates, examples, and content).
- Post‑request conduct evidencing assent: conduct by the requester showing assent to limitations (e.g., paying a premium for restricted use; instructing the talent about intended private use and not objecting when talent relied on that instruction).
- Agent/identity facts: if Cameo’s standard process obscured the requester’s identity, allegations that defendants used aliases but the requester’s agents nonetheless were aware of and accepted the restriction, or that the person acting as requester was an agent of the broadcast employer and communicated acceptance on behalf of the employer.

Why Santos’s complaint failed under the opinion

The panel concluded Santos’s complaint contained only boilerplate or conclusory allegations about an implied contract and pointed to nothing like the examples above. The court emphasized that Santos “fails to plead the essential terms” and “does not plausibly allege an indication of a meeting of minds” — the complaint rested on a standard Cameo transaction and did not plead any specific promise by Kimmel (or factual communications evidencing assent) that would create an enforceable implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law.

Bottom line

- The opinion applies Illinois law to the express TOS claim (because that claim is analyzed under the TOS) and New York law to the separate implied‑in‑fact claim (the panel expressly applies New York cases for that claim). The opinion itself does not narrate a full choice‑of‑law exposition; it simply applies those governing bodies of law to the corresponding claims.
- To survive dismissal under New York law Santos needed to plead, in nonconclusory detail, the essential terms of the implied agreement (scope of permitted/forbidden uses, consideration, duration, etc.) and concrete facts showing mutual assent (messages, confirmations, course of dealing, or other conduct); pleading an implied contract based solely on the ordinary Cameo workflow without such factual detail was insufficient, which is why the court affirmed dismissal.

### What qualifies as "out-of-pocket" loss for fraudulent inducement in New York when the plaintiff was paid for the work?
Question:
For the fraud claim, the panel (citing Lama Holding and Connaughton) says Santos failed to allege any out-of-pocket loss. If Santos was paid Cameo’s fee but alleges he was tricked into making videos for a purpose he wouldn’t have agreed to (national broadcast mockery), what types of damages could count as out-of-pocket in New York? Could time and effort, foregone opportunities, or costs to remediate reputational harm ever qualify? If benefit-of-the-bargain is unavailable in fraud, are rescission, restitution, or unjust enrichment viable alternatives on similar facts, or do those fail for the same reasons?
Answer:
Short answer
Under the rule the panel applied (citing Lama Holding and Connaughton), a New York fraudulent‑inducement plaintiff must plead an actual pecuniary, out‑of‑pocket loss. Because Santos alleges he was paid Cameo’s fee and does not allege any net monetary loss, the panel held he failed to plead fraud. Time and effort, mere reputational injury, or conclusory allegations of “being tricked” will not suffice unless the plaintiff connects them to concrete, pecuniary harm (lost contracts, out‑of‑pocket expenses, costs incurred to remedy the injury, etc.). Benefit‑of‑the‑bargain damages are not the substitute for the out‑of‑pocket measure in a New York fraud claim. The opinion does not analyze rescission/restitution/unjust enrichment, so it does not decide whether equitable remedies would survive on these facts.

What the opinion says (doctrine and holding)
- The panel affirmed dismissal of Santos’s fraudulent‑inducement claim because he “failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud,” and it cites Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), and Connaughton v. Chipotle, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. at § II.  
- That is the controlling point the court applied: New York fraud requires pleading an actual pecuniary (out‑of‑pocket) loss; absence of such an allegation defeats the claim at the pleading stage.

What counts (and what usually does not) as “out‑of‑pocket” under that standard
- Likely examples that would satisfy the panel’s articulation (if alleged with specificity):  
  - Actual monetary expenditures you paid because of the inducement (e.g., third‑party production costs you personally advanced and were not reimbursed).  
  - Provable lost economic opportunities directly caused by the fraud (e.g., a contract you turned down because you relied on the misrepresentation, or demonstrable lost bookings/fees).  
  - Reasonable, documented costs incurred to remediate the harm when those costs are pecuniary (e.g., hiring a PR firm and paying invoices to repair a reputation, if the expenditures flowed from the fraud and are alleged concretely).  
- Items that ordinarily will not count unless converted into a pecuniary form:  
  - Time and effort as abstract loss (hours spent creating videos) without showing a monetary valuation or resulting pecuniary injury.  
  - Pure reputational injury standing alone, unless it produced a concrete economic loss (lost income, canceled deals, measurable diminution of market value).  
  - Mere emotional harm or wounded pride—not a substitute for pecuniary loss under the courts’ framing of “out‑of‑pocket.”

Benefit‑of‑the‑bargain, rescission, restitution, unjust enrichment
- Benefit‑of‑the‑bargain: The panel relied on Lama/Connaughton’s out‑of‑pocket requirement for fraud; that approach treats fraud damages as requiring actual pecuniary loss rather than an expectation (benefit‑of‑the‑bargain) measure. The opinion thus treats benefit‑of‑the‑bargain as not satisfying the fraud element the court applied. Santos v. Kimmel, § II.  
- Rescission/restitution/unjust enrichment: The panel did not analyze these equitable remedies in the opinion. The court did dismiss Santos’s contract claims for other reasons (preemption and pleading failures) and dismissed the fraud claim for lack of out‑of‑pocket loss, but it does not say whether an unjust‑enrichment or restitution claim would survive here. Whether those remedies could succeed would depend on pleading the separate elements of each equitable claim (for unjust enrichment: that the defendant received a benefit and it would be unjust to retain it; for rescission: appropriate equitable grounds and remedy). The panel’s ruling on fraud does not itself resolve those separate doctrines.

Practical takeaway for pleading
- If the plaintiff was paid and wants a fraud claim to survive in New York, plead a specific, concrete pecuniary loss flowing from the alleged misrepresentation (e.g., unreimbursed outlays, demonstrable lost fees/opportunities, or concrete remediation expenses). Allegations of having been “tricked” without a money loss are likely to be dismissed under the reasoning the panel applied in Santos v. Kimmel.
