Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Petitioners' Personal and Professional Background in Bogota, Colombia

The petitioners in this case are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, along with their two minor sons. The family resided in Bogota, Colombia, where they owned and worked in a furniture factory. Jimenez's professional life was notably intertwined with the Colombian legal system. In 2022, he was a law student holding a provisional law license, and he worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor's office in Colombia. In that capacity, he assisted victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. Jimenez's father also worked within the Colombian government's criminal justice system—a fact that becomes relevant later in the court's analysis of whether the family had meaningful avenues to seek government protection. The family relocated to the United States in December 2022, but understanding why they left requires examining the convergence of their political activities and Jimenez's legal work, each of which exposed them to distinct forms of danger.

## Petitioners' Political Activities with the Colombian Liberal Party

Since 2012, Jimenez and Duarte had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party. Their political engagement intensified during the 2022 Colombian presidential election when they supported Federico Gutierrez, a candidate running against the eventual winner, Gustavo Petro. Petitioners actively campaigned for Gutierrez, going door to door in local neighborhoods to solicit favorable votes. Beginning around the end of May 2022, Jimenez started receiving threatening phone calls once or twice a week related to his involvement with the Liberal Party. Other Liberal Party leaders and colleagues received similar threats. In early June 2022, a colleague was kidnapped and murdered despite having reported the threats he received. Petitioners were not deterred by these events and continued their campaign activities. This initial wave of politically connected threats is significant because it forms part of the background against which the later, more severe threats occurred—and because the court ultimately distinguishes between the two sets of threats when evaluating whether Petitioners' harm was connected to a protected ground.

## Jimenez's Legal Work and the Property Dispute Involving Don Rafa

A separate chain of events began in June 2022, when Jimenez's legal work brought him into contact with a dangerous figure. A woman from San Bernardo asked Jimenez, in his capacity as a provisional lawyer, for help with a property dispute. She had not received rent from her tenant for over a year and wanted Jimenez to speak with the tenant about restitution and vacating the property. Jimenez visited the property to negotiate an agreement, but the tenant told him not to "look for trouble" and refused to negotiate. The client declined to pursue a formal eviction process, hoping instead that the tenant would surrender the property upon learning that a lawyer was involved.

Through his investigation of the property title, Jimenez made several discoveries. First, the tenant was the client's ex-husband; the property had been transferred to the client as part of a divorce settlement. Second, the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as "Don Rafa," a leader of a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with the current president of Colombia, Gustavo Petro. Third, the third floor of the property had been sealed off by the health department because it was being used as a day-rental for migrants and as a base for narco-trafficking and prostitution, with affiliated reports of kidnapping and murder. Law enforcement had attempted several searches and seizures at the property, but those efforts were seemingly unsuccessful. A local police officer warned Jimenez to stay away from the property and not to seek problems.

This episode is central to the case because it identifies Don Rafa as the source of the subsequent threats against the family and because it provides an alternative, non-political explanation for the harm Petitioners suffered—a point the court relies on heavily in its nexus analysis.

## Escalation of Threats and Intimidation Against the Family

After Jimenez's encounter with Don Rafa's property, the threats against Petitioners escalated in both frequency and severity. Later in June 2022, Jimenez received a threatening brochure at his furniture factory stating, "death to snitches from Liberal Party." He disregarded and discarded it. The next month, while Jimenez was working at the factory at night, two men held him at gunpoint and told him to leave the neighborhood. One accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and held him responsible for the searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property. After the men left, Jimenez and his wife locked the factory and began closely monitoring their children's commutes to school.

Approximately two months later, while Petitioners were out of town, the same two men returned to the factory. They assaulted an employee, drew a gun, and demanded to know Petitioners' location. The men again characterized Petitioners as "snitches for the police" who were involved in politics. They threatened to kill Petitioners if they were seen again, stating that the order came from Don Rafa. The employee told the men that Petitioners were traveling, and the men left without harming anyone else.

Days later, the threats reached the family directly when Jimenez witnessed a man put his arm around his younger son's shoulder as the boy exited his school. The man covered the son's mouth as the boy tried to escape and told him to "run to your dad." Jimenez ran to his son, pushed the man, and the son escaped to the car. The family then moved to Jimenez's mother's home in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to leave for the United States.

Before departing, Jimenez reported these events to a police patrol officer. The officer responded that nothing could be done, that police officers were contributing to the problem, and that Jimenez and his family should leave. This report to a single officer constitutes the extent of Petitioners' efforts to seek government protection—a fact the court treats as significant in evaluating whether the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

## DHS Notices to Appear and Removal Proceedings Before the Immigration Judge

Three months after Petitioners arrived in the United States without being admitted or paroled by an immigration officer, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) served each of them with individual Notices to Appear. The Notices charged each petitioner as removable from the United States under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Notices to Appear are the charging documents that initiate formal removal proceedings against a noncitizen; they specify the legal basis for removal and the allegations of fact supporting those charges.

In August 2023, Petitioners admitted the truth of DHS's allegations. The Immigration Judge (IJ) found them inadmissible and ordered removal to Colombia. Faced with removal, Petitioners applied for three forms of relief: asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). The minor children proceeded as "rider respondents" and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez's asylum application, meaning their claims depended on the outcome of Jimenez's case.

## IJ's Denial of Asylum, Withholding of Removal, and CAT Claims

Based on the testimony of Jimenez and Duarte and other evidence in the record, the IJ denied all of Petitioners' applications. Notably, the IJ found Petitioners to be credible—a significant factual determination that means the IJ accepted their accounts of what happened in Colombia as truthful. However, the IJ concluded that Petitioners failed to establish four elements necessary for relief: (1) their harm amounted to past persecution; (2) a nexus between the harm and a statutorily protected ground; (3) internal relocation within Colombia was not reasonably available; and (4) the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them from their persecutors. The IJ also denied Petitioners' request for protection under the CAT.

Petitioners appealed the IJ's decision to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). The BIA affirmed the IJ's decision and dismissed the appeal. The BIA also rejected Petitioners' due process arguments that the IJ had violated their right to counsel. A single member of the BIA issued the affirmance, which is relevant to the standard of review on appeal because, as the Tenth Circuit explains, when a single BIA member affirms the IJ's decision, the court of appeals reviews the BIA's decision but may consult the IJ's more complete explanation of the same grounds.

## Scope of Appeal Before the Tenth Circuit

Petitioners sought review in the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, exercising jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. Importantly, Petitioners narrowed the scope of their appeal. They challenged only the BIA's denial of their applications for asylum and withholding of removal. They did not appeal the denial of protection under the CAT, nor did they pursue their due process claim regarding the right to counsel.

Within their asylum and withholding claims, Petitioners raised three specific arguments: (1) the BIA erred in finding that they did not establish past persecution; (2) the BIA erred in finding that their alleged persecution lacked a nexus to a statutorily protected ground; and (3) the BIA erred in finding that they did not have a well-founded fear of future persecution that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to address. The Tenth Circuit's review of these factual determinations proceeded under the substantial-evidence standard, under which the BIA's findings are conclusive unless the record compels a contrary conclusion—that is, unless any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude differently. This deferential standard of review frames the entire analytical discussion that follows, because Petitioners do not merely need to show that the BIA was wrong; they must show that the record compels a different result.

# Legal Framework for Asylum and Withholding of Removal

## Jurisdiction and Standard of Review

Federal courts of appeals have jurisdiction to review final orders of removal, including consolidated review of asylum claims, under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit exercised this jurisdiction to review the Board of Immigration Appeals' denial of the petitioners' applications for asylum and withholding of removal. Importantly, the court's review is confined to "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based" and does not permit consideration of evidence outside that record.

When a single member of the BIA affirms the immigration judge's decision, the court of appeals reviews the BIA's decision as the final agency action. However, the court is not precluded from consulting the IJ's more complete explanation of the same grounds that the BIA adopted. This principle, drawn from *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006), recognizes that the BIA may issue a streamlined or abbreviated opinion that incorporates or adopts the IJ's reasoning. In such cases, the IJ's opinion provides the fuller analytical framework, and the reviewing court may rely on it to understand the basis of the agency's decision. This is not a departure from reviewing the BIA's decision; rather, it is a practical accommodation that ensures the court evaluates the agency's actual reasoning rather than a truncated summary of it.

The standard of review applied to the BIA's factual findings is the substantial-evidence standard. Under this standard, the BIA's findings of fact are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." This is an exceedingly deferential standard. The court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment for that of the agency. Instead, the petitioner bears the heavy burden of showing that the evidence compels a contrary result. As the court noted in *Jimenez*, citing *Yuk v. Ashcroft*, 355 F.3d 1222, 1236 (10th Cir. 2004), "[i]t is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ's [or the BIA's] decision." This deferential posture reflects the institutional competence of the agency in making fact-bound determinations about asylum claims, which often turn on credibility assessments and evaluations of country conditions that the agency is uniquely positioned to assess.

## The Definition of "Refugee"

The threshold question in any asylum case is whether the applicant qualifies as a "refugee." The Immigration and Nationality Act defines a refugee as:

> any person who is outside any country of such person's nationality . . . who is unable or unwilling to return to, and is unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A). This definition, as quoted by the Tenth Circuit in *Niang v. Gonzales*, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194 (10th Cir. 2005), contains several critical components that structure the entire asylum analysis. First, the applicant must be outside the country of nationality. Second, the applicant must be unable or unwilling to return to that country. Third, the inability or unwillingness to return must be "because of" persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution. Fourth, the persecution must be "on account of" one of the five statutorily protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners claimed persecution on account of their political opinion—specifically, their membership in and campaigning for the Colombian Liberal Party. The protected-ground requirement is what distinguishes asylum from other forms of humanitarian relief: the harm an applicant fears must be connected to a characteristic that the law deems worthy of protection. Not all harm qualifies, and not all motives for harm suffice. The applicant must show that the persecutor targets the applicant because of who the applicant is or what the applicant believes, as embodied in one of the five protected grounds.

## Burden of Proof: Preponderance of the Evidence

Asylum is a discretionary form of relief. Even if an applicant establishes eligibility, the Attorney General retains discretion to grant or deny the application. But eligibility is the necessary first step, and the applicant bears the burden of proving eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. As the Tenth Circuit explained, citing *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 857 (10th Cir. 2025), and 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1), the applicant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she is a refugee.

The preponderance-of-the-evidence standard requires the applicant to show that the claimed facts are more likely true than not. This is a lower burden than the "clear and convincing" standard but still demands meaningful evidentiary support. The applicant must present credible testimony, documentary evidence, or other record evidence sufficient to persuade the adjudicator that the elements of refugee status are satisfied. In *Jimenez*, the IJ found the petitioners credible, but credibility alone was insufficient—the petitioners still needed to establish each element of their claim through the evidence in the record.

## The Three Elements of Past Persecution

One way an applicant may establish refugee status is through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. The Tenth Circuit has articulated three elements that an applicant must prove to establish past persecution, drawing from *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012), and *Niang*, 422 F.3d at 1194–95:

1. An incident, or incidents, that rise to the level of persecution;
2. That is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and
3. Is committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control.

These three elements correspond to three distinct analytical inquiries: the severity of the harm, the motive of the persecutor, and the government's capacity or willingness to protect the applicant. An applicant must satisfy all three; failure on any single element is fatal to the claim. In *Jimenez*, the court addressed each element in turn and found that the petitioners failed on all three.

### Element One: Harm Rising to the Level of Persecution

The INA does not define "persecution." The Tenth Circuit has described it as "the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive" and has emphasized that persecution "must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004). The court does not evaluate each incident in isolation but considers the cumulative effect of multiple incidents, because the aggregate impact of several mistreatments may constitute persecution even when no single incident would suffice. *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011).

In *Jimenez*, the court acknowledged the threats the petitioners received but held that threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution, citing *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013). The petitioners conceded they suffered no physical harm, and while physical harm is not strictly necessary, the harm must be offensive and more than "mere restrictions or sporadic threats." The court also rejected the petitioners' reliance on emotional and psychological harm, noting that it has rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.

### Element Two: Nexus to a Protected Ground

The second element requires the applicant to establish that the persecution was "on account of" a statutorily protected ground. For political opinion claims, the applicant must show that the political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i). This nexus standard requires more than a tangential or incidental connection between the protected characteristic and the harm. As the court explained in *Jimenez*, citing *Rivera-Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646, and *Dallakoti v. Holder*, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010), the protected characteristic "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm" or play "a minor role."

The nexus inquiry can accommodate mixed motives. A persecutor may have multiple reasons for targeting an applicant, and the applicant need not prove that the protected ground was the sole reason. But at least one central reason must be tied to a protected ground. In *Jimenez*, the court found that the predominant motive behind the threats was Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa's criminal enterprise—not the petitioners' political opinion. The political opinion was "merely tangential and subordinate" to the personal and professional dispute, so the nexus element was not satisfied.

### Element Three: Government Unable or Unwilling to Protect

The third element requires the applicant to show that the persecution was committed by the government or by forces the government is unable or unwilling to control. This requirement reflects the underlying premise of asylum: it is a remedy for when the home state has failed in its most fundamental obligation to protect its citizens. If the government can and will protect the applicant, the applicant does not need international protection.

The Tenth Circuit's analysis of this element focuses on the government's response to the applicant's past persecution. Two types of evidence weigh heavily: (1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody. *Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861. The analysis also requires evidence that the government had notice of the persecution and an opportunity to respond. A key fact is whether the applicant reported the persecution to the authorities. While failure to report is not necessarily fatal, the applicant may need to demonstrate that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. *Id.* at 861–62.

In *Jimenez*, the court found that the petitioners' efforts to seek government help were minimal. They spoke to two individual police officers, but the record did not show that these interactions reflected the Colombian government's position. The court cited *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023), for the proposition that "a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim." Moreover, the government had actually attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property, which countered the petitioners' argument that their persecutors were closely aligned with the government and that seeking help would have been futile.

## Presumption of Well-Founded Fear from Past Persecution

If an applicant successfully establishes past persecution, a rebuttable presumption arises that the applicant has a well-founded fear of future persecution. This presumption shifts certain burdens to the government, which may rebut it by showing, by a preponderance of the evidence, either: (1) that there has been a fundamental change in circumstances such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution, or (2) that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the country. 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i); *Rivera-Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646.

In *Jimenez*, the court never reached the presumption because the petitioners failed to establish past persecution in the first instance. Nevertheless, the court addressed the relocation issue as an alternative ground, noting that even if the petitioners had established a well-founded fear, the government could rebut the presumption. The record showed that the petitioners' family members continued to live in Colombia unharmed, that Jimenez's father still worked in the criminal justice system, and that the family had safely lived with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. The men who threatened the petitioners told them to leave the neighborhood, not the country. These facts supported the conclusion that safe internal relocation was reasonably available.

## The Relationship Between Asylum and Withholding of Removal

Asylum and withholding of removal are related forms of relief but operate under different legal standards. Asylum is a discretionary form of relief that requires the applicant to establish refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence. Withholding of removal, by contrast, is a mandatory form of relief—if the applicant meets the higher standard, the Attorney General must withhold removal—but the standard is more demanding.

The relationship between the two forms of relief is hierarchical: because the standard for withholding of removal is higher than the standard for asylum, an applicant who fails to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution for purposes of asylum necessarily fails to meet the standard for withholding of removal. In *Jimenez*, the court applied this principle directly: "because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." Citing *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, 860 F. App'x 121, 125 (10th Cir. 2021), and *Uanreroro*, 443 F.3d at 1202. This meant the court did not need to conduct a separate analysis for the withholding of removal claim; the failure of the asylum claim was dispositive of both.

## The BIA Decision and the IJ's More Complete Explanation

A distinctive feature of immigration appellate practice is the relationship between the IJ's decision and the BIA's decision on appeal. When the BIA reviews an IJ's decision, it may issue a single-member affirmance, a summary affirmance, or a full opinion. When the BIA adopts the IJ's reasoning, the BIA's opinion may be brief, incorporating the IJ's analysis by reference.

In *Jimenez*, a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ's decision. The Tenth Circuit, following *Uanreroro*, reviewed the BIA's decision but consulted the IJ's more complete explanation of the same grounds. This practice ensures that the reviewing court has access to the full reasoning underlying the agency's decision, even when the BIA's opinion itself is abbreviated. The court is not substituting the IJ's decision for the BIA's; rather, it is reading the IJ's opinion as an exposition of the grounds that the BIA affirmed. This is particularly important when the BIA adopts the IJ's factual findings and legal conclusions without extensive independent analysis, as occurred in *Jimenez*.

Understanding this procedural posture is essential for evaluating the court's reasoning throughout the opinion. When the court in *Jimenez* refers to findings by "the BIA and IJ," it is relying on the IJ's detailed findings that the BIA adopted. The substantial-evidence standard applies to the BIA's findings, which in this case incorporate the IJ's underlying analysis. The petitioner's burden on appeal is to show that the evidence compels a result contrary to the agency's combined decision, not merely that the evidence could support a different outcome.

# Past Persecution Analysis

## The INA's Lack of a Statutory Definition of Persecution and the Tenth Circuit's Interpretive Standard

A central challenge in asylum law is that the Immigration and Nationality Act does not define the term "persecution." The statute defines "refugee" by reference to persecution—a person who is unable or unwilling to return to their country "because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion"—but it does not specify what conduct qualifies as persecutory. This legislative silence has forced federal courts to develop interpretive standards through case law.

The Tenth Circuit has addressed this gap by articulating a working definition. In *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, the court held that "a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." This standard performs two functions. First, it establishes a qualitative threshold: the harm must be "offensive," meaning it must cross a certain line of severity. Second, it sets a floor: the harm must be "more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." The standard thus distinguishes between conduct that is merely threatening or restrictive—however frightening—and conduct that is truly persecutory.

## Requirement That Persecution Entail Suffering or Harm That Is Offensive and More Than Mere Restrictions or Threats

The Tenth Circuit's standard has an important doctrinal consequence: not all mistreatment qualifies as persecution, even when it is genuinely distressing. The "offensive" requirement ensures that only harm reaching a certain level of severity will satisfy the persecution element. The "more than restrictions or threats" requirement further narrows the category, making clear that harassment, intimidation, and limitations on freedom, standing alone, generally do not rise to the level of persecution.

This does not mean that physical harm is always required. The Tenth Circuit has acknowledged that persecution can occur without physical violence. But the harm must be of a quality and intensity that distinguishes it from the ordinary hardships, threats, and restrictions that many people face. The standard guards against diluting the concept of persecution to the point that any adverse treatment motivated by a protected ground would support an asylum claim.

## Cumulative Effects Analysis: Considering Multiple Incidents Collectively

A critical doctrinal tool in the persecution inquiry is the cumulative effects analysis. The Tenth Circuit has held that courts should not "look at each incident in isolation, but instead consider them collectively, because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." This principle, articulated in *Ritonga v. Holder*, recognizes that a series of incidents, none of which individually amounts to persecution, may collectively cross the threshold when considered together.

For asylum applicants, the cumulative effects analysis can be a significant advantage: it allows them to aggregate multiple forms of mistreatment—threats, harassment, intimidation, and other harms—to build a stronger case that the totality of their experience constitutes persecution. However, the doctrine has limits. The cumulative effects analysis aggregates incidents; it does not transform individual incidents into something they are not. If the individual incidents consist primarily of threats and emotional distress, aggregating them may still fall short of persecution, because the component parts lack the severity that the standard requires.

## Why Threats Alone Are Insufficient to Constitute Persecution Under Tenth Circuit Precedent

The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly held that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." This rule, stated in *Karki v. Holder*, is central to the court's analysis in *Jimenez*. The rule does not mean that threats are irrelevant; the court explicitly acknowledged that threats are considered as part of the cumulative effects analysis. But threats, by themselves, do not satisfy the persecution element.

The rationale is tied to the statutory standard. If persecution must be "more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty," then threats alone cannot constitute persecution as a matter of law. This is so even when the threats are serious and frightening, and even when they come from dangerous individuals. The court's approach reflects a judgment that the asylum system requires a showing of actual harm—or at least harm beyond the communicative act of threatening—before the presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution attaches.

## Petitioners' Concession That They Suffered No Physical Harm and Its Significance

A pivotal moment in the *Jimenez* analysis was Petitioners' concession that they suffered no physical harm. At their merits hearing, Petitioners conceded they were not physically harmed, and in their opening brief they noted they experienced "no lasting physical harm." This concession significantly shaped the court's analysis.

Physical harm is not strictly necessary to establish persecution—the Tenth Circuit has acknowledged this possibility. But the absence of physical harm places a heavier burden on the applicant to show that the non-physical harm was sufficiently severe, offensive, and cumulative to meet the persecution standard. When an applicant concedes no physical harm occurred, the court's inquiry focuses on whether the remaining harm—threats, emotional distress, and psychological suffering—can independently satisfy the threshold. As the court noted, citing *Karki*, while physical harm is not required, "the suffered harm must be offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats."

The concession also constrained Petitioners' ability to argue that the assault on their factory employee should be attributed to them as personal harm. The employee was assaulted, not Petitioners themselves. While this incident was part of the pattern of threats directed at Petitioners, it did not constitute direct physical harm to Petitioners.

## Insufficiency of Emotional and Psychological Harm Without Physical Harm

Petitioners argued that their lasting emotional and psychological harm should satisfy the persecution standard. Duarte testified that she is unable to sleep or rest, and that their son began suffering panic attacks and stress. The court acknowledged that Petitioners had "endured unfortunate circumstances" but concluded that such psychological harm, stemming from threats alone, was insufficient to establish persecution.

The court's reasoning drew on *Brandy v. Holder*, where the Tenth Circuit found that harm including a ten-hour arrest and detention, combined with threats, did not rise to the level of persecution even though the events were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling." The court stated that it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone." This language signals a strong doctrinal presumption: emotional and psychological harm, without accompanying physical harm or other severe mistreatment, generally will not satisfy the persecution element.

This is a demanding standard. The court did not question the sincerity of Petitioners' psychological suffering. Rather, it held that such suffering, when it flows from threats rather than from physical persecution or other severe harm, does not meet the legal threshold the INA requires.

## Comparison to Prior Tenth Circuit Cases Involving More Extreme Harm That Still Failed the Persecution Threshold

To contextualize its conclusion, the court compared Petitioners' harm to prior cases in which the Tenth Circuit denied asylum despite more extreme facts. In *Chhetri v. Rosen*, the court found that "four telephone threats and one vague, in-person threat made to [the petitioner's] father" did not constitute persecution. In *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, the court held that threats with machetes and being robbed six times was insufficient to constitute persecution because "the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand."

These comparisons serve two functions. First, they demonstrate that the Tenth Circuit has consistently applied a demanding persecution standard, rejecting claims that involved harm more severe than what Petitioners experienced. If threats with machetes and six robberies did not suffice, Petitioners' threats and emotional distress—without any physical harm—fall even further short. Second, the comparisons illustrate that the persecution inquiry is qualitative, not merely quantitative. Even multiple incidents of threatening or criminal conduct may not amount to persecution if the harm is contingent, non-physical, or otherwise lacks the severity the standard demands.

## Application of These Principles to Petitioners' Facts and the Court's Conclusion

Applying these principles, the court concluded that substantial evidence supported the BIA's finding that Petitioners' harm, even considered in the aggregate, did not amount to past persecution. The court's analysis proceeded through several steps.

First, the court considered the threats. Petitioners received threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, and in-person threats at gunpoint. While the court considered these threats as part of the cumulative effects analysis, it held that threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution under *Karki*.

Second, the court addressed the absence of physical harm. Petitioners conceded they were not physically harmed. Although physical harm is not strictly required, its absence meant Petitioners had to show that their non-physical harm was sufficiently severe to meet the standard—a burden they could not satisfy.

Third, the court evaluated Petitioners' emotional and psychological harm. Duarte's sleeplessness and her son's panic attacks, while genuine, stemmed from the threats Petitioners received. Under the Tenth Circuit's precedent, emotional and psychological harm flowing from threats alone does not constitute persecution.

Fourth, the court considered the assault on Petitioners' factory employee. While this incident was part of the pattern of threats directed at Petitioners, it did not involve direct physical harm to Petitioners themselves, and it remained part of a broader course of conduct centered on threats and intimidation.

Finally, the court compared Petitioners' harm to the more extreme facts in *Chhetri* and *Carias-Mejia*, both of which failed the persecution threshold. Given that Petitioners' harm was less severe than the harm in those cases, the court concluded that it could not satisfy the persecution standard.

The court therefore held that "substantial evidence supports the BIA's finding that Petitioners' harm, even in the aggregate, does not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA." This conclusion meant Petitioners could not establish the first element of the refugee definition through a showing of past persecution, which would have given rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. Without past persecution, Petitioners had to rely on their argument for a well-founded fear of future persecution—an argument the court would also reject on other grounds.

# Nexus to a Statutorily Protected Ground

## The Nexus Requirement: Harm Must Be "On Account Of" a Protected Ground

Establishing that an applicant suffered harm is only the first step in proving eligibility for asylum. The applicant must also show that the harm was connected to one of the statutorily protected grounds enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. This connection is known as the "nexus" requirement. Without it, even severe mistreatment cannot form the basis for asylum relief, because the INA protects only those who are targeted for reasons the statute recognizes. The nexus requirement ensures that asylum remains a remedy for persecution as defined by Congress, rather than a general humanitarian refuge for anyone who has suffered harm abroad.

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Petitioners—Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons—argued that the harm they suffered satisfied the nexus requirement because they were targeted on account of their political opinion. The Tenth Circuit's analysis of this claim provides a clear illustration of how courts evaluate whether an applicant has connected their persecutors' motives to a protected ground, particularly when the persecutor may have been driven by multiple motivations.

## Petitioners' Argument: Political Opinion as the Motive for Persecution

Petitioners grounded their nexus argument in the political landscape of Colombia. They had been members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012 and actively campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, a candidate in the 2022 Colombian presidential election who was a rival of the current president, Gustavo Petro. Their political activities included door-to-door canvassing in local neighborhoods. Petitioners contended that their persecutor, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros—known as "Don Rafa"—a leader of a group of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Petro, targeted them because of their support for a political rival. In other words, Petitioners argued that Don Rafa's association with the governing political faction made their opposition political opinion the reason for the threats and intimidation they experienced.

This argument required Petitioners to do more than simply show that they held a political opinion and that they were harmed. They needed to demonstrate that the harm occurred *because of* that opinion—that the persecutor's awareness of and reaction to their political views was what drove the targeting.

## The "At Least One Central Reason" Standard

The Tenth Circuit applied the statutory standard codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i), which requires an asylum applicant to establish that the protected ground was "at least one central reason" for the persecution. The court, drawing on its prior decision in *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012), explained that the political opinion "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm" or play "a minor role" in the applicant's past treatment or fears of future mistreatment.

This standard sets a meaningful threshold. A persecutor's awareness that the victim holds a particular political opinion is not enough; the opinion must be a central driver of the persecution. The "at least one central reason" formulation distinguishes between situations where a protected ground is the true gravitational center of the persecutor's motive and situations where it is merely a background fact or a secondary consideration. The standard recognizes that persecutors are complex actors who may be aware of many things about their targets, but asylum law demands that the protected characteristic be more than coincidental to the harm.

## Mixed-Motive Analysis

The court acknowledged, citing *O.C.V. v. Bondi*, No. 23-9609 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025), that a persecutor can have multiple motives for targeting someone. This is the mixed-motive framework: a persecutor might act out of personal animus, financial interest, and political hostility all at once. The law does not require the applicant to prove that the protected ground was the *only* reason for the persecution. However, in a mixed-motive case, the applicant must still demonstrate that at least one protected ground was a *central* reason for the inflicted harm. A protected ground that is present but peripheral—one among several minor factors—will not satisfy the standard.

The mixed-motive doctrine thus provides some flexibility for applicants, recognizing the reality that human motivation is rarely singular. But it also maintains analytical rigor: the court must assess the relative weight of each motive and determine whether the protected ground occupies a central rather than a subordinate position in the persecutor's calculus.

## Limiting Review to Political Opinion

Before reaching the merits of the nexus analysis, the court noted a procedural constraint on Petitioners' arguments. Although Petitioners referenced both political opinion and membership in a particular social group as protected grounds, they failed to brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group or to establish their membership in such a group. The court therefore limited its review to whether Petitioners had established a nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion alone.

This limitation illustrates an important principle of appellate practice in immigration cases: arguments not adequately briefed are forfeited. The court does not construct social group definitions or formulate nexus arguments on the applicant's behalf. By failing to develop their particular social group claim, Petitioners narrowed the basis on which the court could evaluate their nexus argument, leaving political opinion as the sole protected ground at issue.

## The Court's Finding: Don Rafa's Primary Motive Was Personal

The court concluded that substantial evidence supported the BIA and IJ's findings that Petitioners were targeted not because of their political opinion, but because Jimenez had threatened Don Rafa's revenue and security through his involvement in the property dispute. Jimenez had been retained by a client seeking to recover property occupied by Don Rafa, who was using it as a base for narco-trafficking, prostitution, and other criminal activity. Jimenez's investigation and inquiries into the property directly threatened Don Rafa's criminal enterprise. When the two men confronted Jimenez at gunpoint at his furniture factory, they accused him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and held him responsible for the searches and seizures at the property. When the same men later returned and assaulted a factory employee, they again referred to Petitioners as "snitches for the police" and stated that Don Rafa had sent them.

The court found that these accusations centered on Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa's criminal operations, not on the Petitioners' political views. The men referenced Jimenez's work for the prosecutor's office and his role in triggering law enforcement action against the property—personal and professional actions that directly threatened Don Rafa's interests. The court determined that the predominant motive behind the threats was Jimenez's professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa, rather than the Petitioners' political opinion.

## Evidence That Escalated Threats Began Only After the Property Dispute

A critical piece of the court's reasoning was the temporal sequence of events. Prior to Jimenez's involvement in the property dispute with Don Rafa, Petitioners had received threatening phone calls related to their Liberal Party activities. But these earlier threats did not escalate. Petitioners were undeterred by them and continued their political campaigning, even after a colleague was kidnapped and murdered. Critically, Petitioners pointed to no evidence in the record connecting Don Rafa to those initial, politically motivated threats.

The escalated threats—the gunpoint confrontation at the factory, the assault on the employee, the threatening approach to Jimenez's son—began only after Jimenez became involved in the property dispute with Don Rafa. This timeline undermined Petitioners' claim that political opinion was a central reason for their persecution. If political opinion had been the driving motive, the court reasoned, one would expect the escalation to have occurred during the period of active political campaigning, when Petitioners were most visibly engaged in political activity. Instead, the escalation coincided precisely with Jimenez's interference in Don Rafa's criminal enterprise, supporting the conclusion that the personal motive, not the political one, was central.

## The Court's Conclusion: Political Opinion Was Tangential and Subordinate

Weighing the evidence under the substantial-evidence standard, the court concluded that Petitioners' political opinion was "merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm." The court emphasized that it was not its prerogative to reweigh the evidence, citing *Yuk v. Ashcroft*, 355 F.3d 1222 (10th Cir. 2004), but only to determine whether substantial evidence supported the agency's decision. The court found that it did.

The court's analysis demonstrates how the "at least one central reason" standard operates in practice. Even though Petitioners held a political opinion opposed to the faction with which Don Rafa was aligned, and even though the earlier threats they received were politically motivated, the evidence showed that the specific persecution Petitioners experienced at Don Rafa's hands was driven by a personal dispute over property and criminal operations. The political opinion was present in the background—Petitioners were, in fact, political activists opposed to the current government—but it was not a central reason for Don Rafa's targeting. It was, in the court's words, incidental and subordinate to the primary motive of protecting his criminal enterprise from Jimenez's interference. This conclusion doomed the nexus prong of Petitioners' asylum claim, because without a connection between the harm and a protected ground, the statutory definition of refugee cannot be satisfied.

# Government Protection, Safe Relocation, and Disposition

## The Unable-or-Unwilling Analysis

To qualify for asylum, an applicant must show that the persecution they suffered or fear was committed by the government or by forces the government is "unable or unwilling to control." This requirement—often called the "unable-or-unwilling" analysis—asks the court to evaluate the government's response to the applicant's plight. The Tenth Circuit's decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates how courts apply this analysis when an applicant claims that the government is complicit in, or indifferent to, their persecution.

The court explained that the unable-or-unwilling analysis "considers evidence about a government's response to an applicant's past persecution." Citing *Singh v. Bondi*, the court identified two categories of evidence that "weigh heavily" in this inquiry: first, whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and second, whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing the applicant in protective custody. These factors help the court determine whether the government has the capacity and willingness to protect its citizens from private actors who threaten them.

## Notice and the Opportunity to Respond

Intertwined with these two factors is a critical preliminary question: whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and a meaningful opportunity to respond. As the court emphasized, "[w]ithout such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped." This notice requirement reflects a simple logic: a court cannot conclude that a government is unable or unwilling to protect someone if that government never had the chance to try.

Accordingly, "a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government." A failure to report can undercut an applicant's claim that the government was unable or unwilling to act, because it leaves the record devoid of evidence about how the government would have responded. However, the court was careful to note that a failure to report is not necessarily fatal to an applicant's claim. An applicant may still satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous. This exception acknowledges that in some contexts, reporting persecution to the very authorities who are complicit in it would be pointless or even life-threatening.

## Petitioners' Minimal Efforts to Seek Government Help

Applying this framework to the Petitioners' case, the court found substantial evidence supporting the BIA's and IJ's findings that Petitioners' efforts to seek government help were "minimal, if any." This conclusion was striking given Petitioners' own connections to law enforcement in Colombia. Jimenez had a provisional law license, had worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor's Office, and his father still worked for the criminal justice system. Despite these connections, neither Jimenez nor his father reported the wrongdoing or corruption they experienced to the appropriate authorities.

The record showed that Petitioners spoke to two individual police officers about what had happened. One officer—a local patrol officer—told Jimenez that nothing could be done, that police officers were contributing to the problem, and that the family should leave. Another officer had earlier warned Jimenez to stay away from Don Rafa's property. But the court found that these isolated conversations did not demonstrate that the Colombian government as an institution was unable or unwilling to protect Petitioners. Critically, Petitioners did not point to any evidence showing that they sought help outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez's own law enforcement contacts. The court noted that the record contained no evidence of formal complaints, reports to higher authorities, or attempts to engage the prosecutorial or protective resources of the state.

## Individual Police Apathy Versus Systemic Government Failure

A central principle in the unable-or-unwilling analysis is the distinction between individual police apathy and systemic government failure. The court drew on *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, a Seventh Circuit decision, to explain that courts "often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures to prove that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect its people." While police apathy can be relevant evidence of a government's unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a single conversation with an unhelpful officer—or even two such conversations—does not necessarily establish that the government as a whole would fail to respond.

This distinction matters because individual officers may be corrupt, indifferent, or sympathetic to the persecutors without that attitude reflecting the policy or capacity of the state. To show that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them, an applicant must generally present evidence that the problem is institutional—that the government's protective apparatus is systematically compromised, not merely that one or two officers on the street were unhelpful. In Petitioners' case, the court found that the two conversations with local officers were insufficient to establish systemic failure, particularly given that Petitioners never tested the government's response through formal channels.

## Evidence Undermining Petitioners' Argument

The court also found that the record contained evidence that actively contradicted Petitioners' claim that the government was aligned with their persecutors and would be unable or unwilling to help. Petitioners argued that Don Rafa was politically aligned with Colombian President Gustavo Petro and that this alignment rendered the government complicit in the persecution. But the court pointed to evidence that the government had itself attempted several searches and seizures at the property Don Rafa occupied—the very property whose criminal activities had triggered the conflict with Jimenez. These law enforcement efforts, although "seemingly unsuccessful," demonstrated that the government was aware of the criminal activity at the property and had taken affirmative steps to address it.

This evidence cut against Petitioners' argument in two ways. First, it showed that the government was not simply turning a blind eye to Don Rafa's operations; it had affirmatively tried to intervene. Second, it undermined the claim that Don Rafa's political alignment with the president meant that the government would protect him from law enforcement. As the court observed, the government's own attempts to search and seize the property suggested that Petitioners' persecutors were not so closely aligned with the government as to be immune from its reach.

## Failure to Demonstrate Futility or Danger

Because Petitioners had not meaningfully reported the threats to the government, they needed to show that doing so would have been futile or dangerous. The court found that Petitioners failed to carry this burden. Their argument rested primarily on the assertion that their persecutors were closely aligned with the government, but as discussed above, the record evidence—particularly the government's own attempts to search Don Rafa's property—contradicted that assertion. Without evidence that reporting the threats would have been pointless or would have exposed them to greater danger, Petitioners could not overcome the inference that the government had not been given a fair opportunity to protect them.

The court cited *Chhetri v. Rosen* as an analogous case, in which an applicant declined to contact the police because he believed his persecutors were "part of the government." The court there found insufficient evidence that the government was unable or unwilling to protect the applicant, and the Tenth Circuit reached the same conclusion here. Belief that persecutors have government ties, without more, does not excuse an applicant from reporting the persecution and does not, by itself, establish the unable-or-unwilling requirement.

## Safe Internal Relocation

Having concluded that Petitioners failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution, the court nonetheless addressed their argument regarding internal relocation. Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), the government may rebut an applicant's presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution by showing, by a preponderance of the evidence, either that there has been a fundamental change in circumstances such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear of persecution, or that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their country. This provision recognizes that if an applicant can simply move to another region of their home country and live safely there, they may not qualify for asylum even if they faced persecution in their original location.

Although Petitioners had not established a presumption of future persecution in the first place, the court found that the record independently supported the feasibility of internal relocation. Several pieces of evidence supported this conclusion. First, Petitioners' family members and relatives continued to live in Colombia unharmed. Second, Jimenez's father still worked for the criminal justice system in Colombia without apparent incident. Third, and perhaps most significantly, Petitioners had themselves safely lived with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to leave for the United States. This demonstrated that at least some parts of Colombia were safe for them. Finally, the men who threatened Petitioners had told them to leave the neighborhood—they did not threaten the family to leave the country. This geographic limitation on the threat suggested that relocation to another part of Colombia would be a viable means of avoiding future harm.

## The Court's Rejection of Petitioners' Counterarguments

Petitioners raised several counterarguments against the feasibility of internal relocation, but the court found them unpersuasive. Petitioners argued that relocation was not feasible because "the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety," noting that those relatives might also hold political opinions opposed to the government. The court rejected this argument as speculative, stating that the court "cannot grant asylum based on such hypothetical hardships." The safety of family members in Colombia was not dispositive proof that relocation would be safe, but it was evidence the court could properly weigh, and Petitioners' unsupported speculation did not undermine it.

Petitioners also asserted that relocation was unworkable because of administrative and economic burdens, the country's judicial infrastructure, and the persecutors' ties to the president. The court characterized these arguments as "haphazardly brief[ed]" and concluded that, given its prior findings, they did not pass muster. The court's treatment of these arguments reflects a broader principle: conclusory or undeveloped arguments, particularly those unsupported by the record, will not carry an applicant's burden on a contested element of their asylum claim.

## Final Disposition

Having concluded that Petitioners failed to establish past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, and the government's inability or unwillingness to protect them, the court denied the petition for review. The court also noted that Petitioners could not establish a well-founded fear of future persecution, and that internal relocation was a feasible alternative.

The court then addressed the relationship between asylum and withholding of removal. Withholding of removal is a form of relief that, unlike asylum, is not discretionary—if an applicant meets the standard, the government is prohibited from removing the applicant to the country where they would face persecution. However, the standard for withholding of removal is higher than the standard for asylum. As the court explained, "[b]ecause Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." This principle reflects the logical relationship between the two forms of relief: if an applicant cannot meet the lower asylum threshold, they cannot meet the more demanding withholding of removal threshold as a matter of course.

The court therefore denied the petition for review in its entirety, affirming the BIA's decision and ending Petitioners' bid for relief under asylum and withholding of removal.
