Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The "Instigation" Problem in Fair Use: Does Commissioning a Work Preclude Transformative Use?

When a late-night comedian tricks a disgraced former congressman into recording personalized videos—and then airs them for laughs—who owns the joke? The Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* answers that question with a clean affirmative: fair use protects the parody, and the copyright holder cannot sue his way out of being the butt of it. But the opinion does more than dispatch a somewhat amusing lawsuit. It confronts a genuinely novel question in fair use doctrine: whether a secondary user who *instigates* the creation of a copyrighted work can still claim that a later use of that work is transformative. The court's answer—yes, and the inquiry is objective, not subjective—has implications that extend well beyond the world of Cameo videos and late-night television.

## The Factual Setup and the "Instigation" Argument

The facts are by now familiar. Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names, submitted requests through the Cameo platform to then-Congressman George Santos for personalized videos. Santos fulfilled the requests, apparently under the impression that the videos would convey "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity" to the fictitious recipients. Kimmel then aired the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a series titled "Will Santos Say It?"—a comedic segment designed to mock Santos's apparent willingness to say absurd things for money. Santos sued for copyright infringement, among other claims, and the district court dismissed the case on fair use grounds at the pleading stage.

Santos's central argument on appeal was creative. He conceded that the purpose of Kimmel's use was to comment on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. But he argued that this was *also* his original purpose in making the videos—because Kimmel had "instigated" their creation through the fictitious Cameo requests. In other words, Santos contended that Kimmel was the "designer" of the videos' original purpose, and therefore the secondary use could not be transformative. The use, on this theory, merely superseded the object of the original creation rather than adding something new.

This is not a frivolous argument. If a secondary user commissions a work specifically so they can later use it in a transformative way, one might ask whether the "transformation" is real or merely theatrical. The secondary user, after all, controlled both sides of the transaction. There is something circular about commissioning a work for one purpose, then claiming your later use of it serves a *different* purpose. Santos was essentially arguing that Kimmel engineered the original purpose and therefore could not claim to have transformed it.

## The Objective Standard and the Rejection of "Instigation"

The Second Circuit rejected this argument, and its reasoning is doctrinally significant. The court held that "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user," citing the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*. This is the key move. By focusing on the perspective of a reasonable observer, the court made Santos's instigation theory irrelevant as a matter of doctrine—regardless of its factual force.

The court noted that Santos's own complaint described the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." A reasonable observer seeing the *JKL* segments, by contrast, would understand them as commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. The two purposes are different. That the secondary user may have orchestrated the original creation does not change what a reasonable observer would perceive. The transformative use inquiry, in other words, is not about the genealogy of the work but about the contrast between the original and secondary uses as they appear to the audience.

This is a clean application of *Warhol*, which held that the purpose of a use must be assessed objectively rather than by reference to the subjective intent of either party. But *Santos* pushes the principle into new territory. In *Warhol*, the question was whether Lynn Goldsmith's photograph of Prince, commissioned for a magazine profile, was transformed when Andy Warhol created a series of silkscreen prints based on it. The Court emphasized that the purpose of Goldsmith's photograph—depicting Prince as he really was—differed from the purpose of Warhol's prints, which commented on celebrity and consumption. *Santos* presents a more unusual scenario: the secondary user *caused* the original work to exist in the first place, and did so under false pretenses. The court's answer—that this factual backdrop does not alter the objective transformative use analysis—clarifies that *Warhol*'s objective standard is robust and resistant to contextual complications.

One might wonder whether this is the right place to draw the line. There are contexts in which the relationship between the parties and the circumstances of creation seem relevant to whether a use is "fair." A photographer who commissions a portrait and then uses it in a way the subject would never have agreed to is in a different position from a comedian who tricks a public figure into recording a video. But the court's approach has the virtue of administrative simplicity. If the transformative use inquiry turned on the subjective intentions of the parties or the circumstances of the work's commission, fair use would become an even more fact-intensive inquiry than it already is—and one less amenable to resolution at the pleading stage.

## Bad Faith, Fictitious Names, and the Retreat from *Harper & Row*

Santos also argued that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to submit the Cameo requests demonstrated bad faith, which should nullify the fair use defense. This argument relied on the Supreme Court's statement in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." The court's treatment of this argument is notable for what it reveals about the evolving status of the good faith requirement.

The Second Circuit observed that "the Court has since distanced itself from this statement" from *Harper & Row*. This is an understatement. The *Harper & Row* good faith language has been effectively moribund for years, and the Second Circuit has previously cabined its application to cases where the secondary user's conduct suggests an intent to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work. Here, the court found that Santos's own complaint portrayed the defendants as motivated by "sarcastic criticism and commentary"—not by a desire to usurp Santos's commercial market for the videos.

The court's framing is important. By framing the bad faith inquiry in terms of market supplantation rather than generic dishonesty, the court effectively reduces the good faith factor to a variant of the fourth fair use factor—market harm. Kimmel's deception in using fictitious names, however unseemly, did not suggest that he was trying to compete with Santos in the market for Cameo videos. He was trying to get material for a comedy segment. That the material was obtained through misrepresentation was, for fair use purposes, beside the point.

This is a sensible approach, but it leaves a gap. What if a secondary user obtains a copyrighted work through truly egregious deception—say, hacking into a server or bribing an employee—and then uses it in a way that is technically transformative? The court's reasoning suggests that even such conduct would not defeat fair use so long as the use does not supplant the copyright holder's market. Whether that is the right result is a question the Second Circuit did not have to answer, but it may come back.

## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage

Perhaps the most practically significant aspect of the decision is the court's willingness to affirm dismissal of the copyright claims on fair use grounds at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage. The court noted that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage, citing its earlier decision in *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*. This is not a new proposition in the Second Circuit, but *Santos* reinforces it in a context where the facts are colorful enough that one might have expected the court to allow discovery.

The court's approach makes sense here. The complaint itself established the purpose of both the original works and the secondary use, the works were published on the same day they were created, and Santos could not allege market harm because the secondary use was not a competing substitute. When the complaint itself supplies the facts necessary to resolve the fair use inquiry, there is no reason to force the defendant to litigate through discovery.

But the pleading-stage fair use doctrine cuts both ways. It benefits defendants like Kimmel who can point to complaint allegations that themselves establish the transformative nature of the use. But it also risks locking plaintiffs out of fair use determinations that might benefit from factual development. In cases where the transformative nature of the use is less obvious from the face of the complaint, courts should be cautious about resolving fair use too early. *Santos* is an easy case on this dimension—the complaint virtually conceded the relevant facts—but not all cases will be so cooperative.

## The State Law Claims: A Brief Postscript

The court also dispatched Santos's state law claims with efficient brevity. The breach of contract claim failed because Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and could not invoke them as a third-party beneficiary under Illinois law. The implied contract claim failed for failure to plead essential terms. And the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege any out-of-pocket loss, as New York law requires. None of these rulings breaks new doctrinal ground, but they collectively illustrate the difficulty of using state law claims to circumvent copyright doctrine—a point the court implicitly reinforced by declining to reach the preemption question.

## Implications and Downstream Effects

*Santos v. Kimmel* is, on one level, a case about a comedian and a congressman. But its doctrinal contributions are real. First, it confirms that *Warhol*'s objective standard for transformative use applies even in the unusual context where the secondary user commissioned the original work. The "instigation" theory, however clever, does not survive an objective inquiry. Second, it further marginalizes the *Harper & Row* good faith requirement, reorienting the inquiry toward market supplantation rather than moral character. Third, it reinforces the Second Circuit's willingness to resolve fair use at the pleading stage when the complaint supplies the necessary facts.

The instigation question is the most interesting of these, and it may arise again in less comedic contexts. Consider a documentary filmmaker who commissions a subject to record a series of interviews, then uses the footage in a film that is highly critical of the subject. Or a journalist who obtains access to a copyrighted work under false pretenses and then publishes a transformative analysis of it. In each case, the secondary user's role in bringing the work into existence might tempt a court to treat the use as non-transformative. *Santos* suggests that temptation should be resisted. The transformative use inquiry asks what a reasonable observer would see, not what the parties intended or how the work came to be. That is a principled position, and one that should provide useful guidance in future cases—whether or not they involve late-night television.
