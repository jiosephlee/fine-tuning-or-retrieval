Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the fourth fair use factor distinguish between "damaging" a market and "usurping" it?
Question:
The court quotes Hachette Book Group for the principle that the fourth fair use factor asks "not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." I'm struggling with this distinction. If Kimmel's segments made Santos's original videos less valuable by mocking them, why isn't that market harm? The court also cites Campbell v. Acuff-Rose for the idea that creators won't license "critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions"—does this mean parody and criticism can essentially never cause cognizable market harm under the fourth factor?
Answer:
The distinction turns on whether the secondary use serves as a **substitute** for the original in the marketplace, not merely whether it makes the original less attractive or valuable.

The fourth factor targets what economists would call market substitution. The court, quoting *Hachette Book Group*, frames the inquiry as whether the second work "usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." The concern is that a potential consumer of the original work would instead consume the secondary work, thereby depriving the copyright holder of a sale or license they would otherwise have obtained. That is market usurpation.

Market "damage," by contrast, covers a broader category of effects that the fourth factor does **not** recognize as cognizable harm. A parody, critical review, or lampoon may well reduce the public's appetite for the original—people may find the original less appealing, less credible, or simply less interesting after seeing it mocked. But that kind of devaluation flows from the critical or transformative character of the secondary use itself. It is not the same thing as the secondary work stepping into the commercial role the original occupies. As the court notes, Kimmel's segments on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* served the purpose of commentary and criticism on Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." Someone who watches those segments is not thereby satisfying a demand that Santos's original Cameo videos were meant to fulfill. The segments do not compete with the videos; they comment on them.

Your second question—whether *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* means parody and criticism can *never* cause cognizable market harm—gets at a common point of confusion. The answer is no, but the doctrine does narrow what counts. *Campbell* stands for the proposition that when a secondary use is critical or lampooning in character, the copyright holder would not ordinarily license that kind of use, and so the "potential licensing market" for such uses is not one the fourth factor will protect. The court quotes *Campbell* directly: "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." In other words, Santos cannot claim that Kimmel's segments deprived him of a licensing opportunity because Santos would never have licensed someone to mock him in the first place. That is not a market the law recognizes as his to lose.

This does not create an absolute immunity for parody or criticism under the fourth factor. The principle is narrower: a copyright holder cannot bootstrap criticism-induced devaluation into cognizable market harm by pointing to lost sales or lost licensing fees that were never realistically available. If, however, a work that purports to be parody or criticism actually functions in the marketplace as a substitute for the original—such that consumers treat it as a replacement rather than as commentary—that would still weigh against fair use. The fourth factor asks about the actual market relationship between the two works, not the label attached to the second one.

In *Santos*, the court's analysis is straightforward on this point: Kimmel's segments did not compete with Santos's Cameo videos. They did not offer personalized video messages to consumers. They offered televised comedy and commentary. Whatever reputational damage Santos may have suffered, the court concludes it was not market harm of the kind the fourth factor recognizes.
