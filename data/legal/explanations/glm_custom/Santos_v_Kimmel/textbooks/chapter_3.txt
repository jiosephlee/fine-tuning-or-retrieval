Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # State Law Claims: Contract, Implied Contract, and Fraudulent Inducement

## Overview of Santos's Three State Law Claims and the District Court's Preemption Ruling

In addition to his copyright claims, Santos brought three state law causes of action against Kimmel and the other defendants: breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. These claims arose from the same factual backdrop as the copyright claims—Kimmel's use of fictitious names to request personalized Cameo videos from Santos, which were then aired on JKL as part of the mocking "Will Santos Say It?" segments. The District Court dismissed the breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act, and it dismissed the fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege out-of-pocket loss. On appeal, Santos challenged each of these rulings, arguing that his contract claims were "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and thus not preempted, and that his fraudulent inducement claim was improperly dismissed.

## The Copyright Act Preemption Question and Why the Court Declined to Resolve It

The Copyright Act contains an express preemption provision that governs when state law claims are displaced by federal copyright law. The District Court concluded that Santos's two contract claims were preempted because they were equivalent to copyright infringement claims—the rights they sought to protect fell within the scope of copyright's exclusive rights. On appeal, Santos contested this conclusion, invoking the Second Circuit's standard that state law claims must be "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" to avoid preemption.

The Second Circuit, however, chose not to resolve the preemption question. This is a significant procedural and doctrinal choice. The court explained that it "need not resolve this issue" because Santos's state law contract claims "fail to state a claim for relief under state law." This reflects an important principle of judicial economy: when a claim fails on an independent ground, courts need not address every argument raised by the parties. By proceeding directly to the merits of the state law claims, the court avoided a potentially complex preemption analysis and provided a cleaner, more straightforward basis for affirmance. The practical effect was that even if Santos were correct about preemption, his claims would still fail because they did not satisfy the pleading requirements of the underlying state law.

## Direct Breach of Contract Claim: Santos as a Non-Party to the Cameo Terms of Service

Santos's first state law claim was for direct breach of contract, based on the Terms of Service that govern the Cameo platform. Santos alleged that Kimmel, by using fictitious names and misrepresenting his identity when submitting video requests, breached provisions of the Cameo Terms of Service. A critical threshold problem with this claim, however, was that Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service. The Terms of Service constituted an agreement between Cameo and its users—people like Kimmel who registered on the platform and submitted requests. Santos, as the talent fulfilling those requests, did not himself agree to the Terms of Service. He therefore sought to enforce a contract to which he was a stranger, raising the question whether he could do so as a third-party beneficiary.

## Illinois Law on Third-Party Beneficiaries and the Presumption Against Conferring Benefits to Noncontracting Parties

The court applied Illinois law to the Terms of Service, apparently because the agreement specified Illinois as the governing law. Under Illinois law, third-party beneficiary claims face a substantial barrier: there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." This presumption means that courts will not lightly infer that a contract was intended to benefit someone who is not a party to it. To overcome this presumption, the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration." This is a demanding standard—it requires the contracting parties' intent to benefit the third party to be so clear and unmistakable that it is essentially explicit in the contract.

The court found that Santos identified nothing close to such an express declaration in the Terms of Service. He could point to no language in the relevant provisions indicating that they were intended to be enforceable by him or other noncontracting parties. Without such language, the strong Illinois presumption held, and Santos could not enforce the Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary.

## The Significance of Third-Party Beneficiary Language in Other Provisions of the Terms of Service

The court's analysis was bolstered by an important textual observation about the Terms of Service as a whole. While the provisions Santos sought to enforce did not contain third-party beneficiary language, other provisions of the same agreement did. This contrast was legally significant because it demonstrated that the parties to the Terms of Service knew how to draft provisions that allowed enforcement by third parties when they so intended. The presence of third-party beneficiary language in some provisions, and its absence in the provisions Santos relied on, supported a strong inference that the parties did not intend the provisions Santos invoked to be enforceable by nonparties. This interpretive principle—sometimes called the *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* canon, though the court did not label it as such—allowed the court to conclude that the omission of third-party beneficiary language from the relevant provisions was deliberate rather than accidental.

## Breach of Implied Contract Under New York Law: Intent of the Parties and Surrounding Circumstances

Santos's second state law claim was for breach of an implied-in-fact contract. Unlike an express contract, which is defined by its written or spoken terms, an implied-in-fact contract is inferred from the conduct and circumstances of the parties. The court applied New York law to this claim, drawing on the principle that the determination of whether an implied-in-fact contract was formed depends on "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances." Under New York law, courts look to the parties' behavior and the context of their interactions to determine whether, despite the absence of an explicit agreement, the parties in fact understood themselves to be bound by contractual obligations.

## Failure to Plead Essential Terms of the Implied Contract in Nonconclusory Language

Even under this more flexible standard, Santos's claim failed. The court held that Santos's complaint did not "allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties' . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." This requirement—that a plaintiff plead essential contract terms in nonconclusory language—serves an important pleading function. It ensures that the defendant has adequate notice of the contractual obligations it allegedly breached and that the court can assess whether a legally enforceable agreement existed. Conclusory allegations—those that merely assert legal conclusions without supporting factual detail—are insufficient under the pleading standards applied here. Santos's complaint, the court noted, failed to plead the essential terms of the putative implied contract that the defendants allegedly breached, leaving the court without enough to conclude that an enforceable implied contract existed.

## Failure to Allege a Meeting of the Minds That Kimmel Would Adhere to the Terms of Service

In addition to the failure to plead essential terms, the court identified a separate and equally fatal defect in the implied contract claim: Santos did not plausibly allege "an indication of a meeting of the minds of the parties constituting an agreement" that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service. A "meeting of the minds"—a shared understanding of the essential terms of the agreement—is a fundamental element of contract formation under New York law. Without it, there is no enforceable contract, express or implied. The court found nothing in Santos's complaint that would allow a reasonable inference that Kimmel and Santos had reached a mutual understanding about adherence to the Terms of Service. This deficiency was particularly significant given that the Terms of Service were an agreement between Kimmel and Cameo, not between Kimmel and Santos, and Santos pointed to no conduct or circumstance suggesting that Kimmel had separately agreed with Santos to follow those terms.

## Fraudulent Inducement Claim Under New York Law and the Out-of-Pocket Loss Requirement

Santos's third and final state law claim was for fraudulent inducement. This claim was based on Kimmel's use of fictitious names and false representations when submitting the Cameo video requests—the deception that led Santos to create the videos in the first place. Under New York law, a fraudulent inducement claim requires the plaintiff to allege, among other elements, that he suffered an out-of-pocket loss as a result of the fraud. This out-of-pocket loss requirement is a distinctive feature of New York fraud law. It means that the plaintiff must demonstrate actual monetary loss—the difference between what he paid and what he received—rather than relying on other measures of damage such as benefit-of-the-bargain or expectation damages. The requirement serves as a gatekeeping function, ensuring that fraud claims rest on concrete financial harm rather than speculative injury.

## Santos's Failure to Allege Actual Out-of-Pocket Loss as Required by Lama Holding Co. and Connaughton

The court agreed with the District Court that Santos failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket loss. In *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, the New York Court of Appeals established that out-of-pocket loss is the proper measure of damages for fraud under New York law. Santos's complaint did not identify any monetary loss he suffered as a result of Kimmel's deception. He was paid for each video he made; the deception consisted of Kimmel's false identity and intent, not of any failure to pay. The court cited *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* for the principle that a plaintiff must allege actual out-of-pocket loss to state a fraud claim, and concluded that Santos had not done so. Without an allegation of concrete monetary loss attributable to the fraud, the fraudulent inducement claim could not survive.

## Affirmance of the District Court's Dismissal of All State Law Claims

Having addressed each of Santos's three state law claims, the court affirmed the District Court's dismissal of all of them. The breach of contract claim failed because Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and could not overcome Illinois's strong presumption against third-party beneficiary enforcement. The breach of implied contract claim failed because Santos did not plead the essential terms of the alleged implied contract in nonconclusory language, nor did he allege a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service. And the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege the out-of-pocket loss that New York law requires for fraud claims. Notably, the court reached these conclusions without needing to resolve the Copyright Act preemption question that the District Court had relied on for the contract claims. By affirming on independent state law grounds, the Second Circuit provided a more robust basis for its decision—one that would stand regardless of how the preemption question might ultimately be resolved.
