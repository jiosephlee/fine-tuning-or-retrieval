Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Case Background, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## The Parties

This case involves a dispute between former Congressman George Santos and several defendants connected to the late-night television program *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* ("JKL"). The primary defendant is Jimmy Kimmel, the host of the show. Also named as defendants are American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. ("ABC"), the network that airs JKL, and The Walt Disney Company, ABC's parent company. Together, these parties form the basis of a dispute that spans copyright law, contract law, and fraud—though, as we will see, the legal questions resolve largely at the pleading stage.

## The Cameo Platform and Kimmel's Use of Fictitious Names

The facts giving rise to this dispute center on the Cameo platform. Cameo is a service through which users can pay celebrities or public figures to record short, personalized video messages. In this case, Kimmel, using various fictitious names, submitted requests to Santos for personalized videos through the Cameo platform. Santos fulfilled each of these requests, apparently without knowing that the requests originated with Kimmel or that the videos would be used on a late-night comedy show. This factual backdrop—Kimmel's use of false identities to solicit the videos—is important because Santos later argued that Kimmel's misrepresentations should undermine the fair use defense and support state law claims for fraudulent inducement. As the opinion explains, however, these arguments ultimately did not succeed.

## The "Will Santos Say It?" Segments

After Santos recorded and delivered the personalized videos, Kimmel aired them on JKL as part of a series of segments titled "Will Santos Say It?" These segments were mocking in nature, presenting the videos as part of a comedic commentary on Santos. The District Court characterized the purpose of the allegedly infringing use as "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Santos himself did not dispute this characterization of the segments' purpose, though he argued that this purpose was also his original purpose in making the videos—an argument the Second Circuit rejected. The comedic and critical character of the segments is central to the court's fair use analysis, which we will examine in detail in the next chapter.

## Santos's Copyright Registrations

Santos registered the videos with the Copyright Office. The copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created—a fact relevant to the second fair use factor, which considers the nature of the copyrighted work. The Second Circuit took judicial notice of these registrations, noting that courts may take judicial notice of "copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office's registry." This procedural point is important: even at the pleading stage, when a court evaluates a motion to dismiss, it may look to publicly available copyright registrations to inform its analysis without converting the motion into one for summary judgment.

## Santos's Claims

Santos brought four claims. First, he asserted a claim for copyright infringement, arguing that Kimmel and the other defendants' unauthorized airing of the videos on JKL infringed his exclusive rights under the Copyright Act. Second, he brought a claim for breach of contract, alleging that Kimmel's use of the videos violated the Cameo Terms of Service to which users must agree when using the platform. Third, he asserted a claim for breach of implied contract, arguing that an implied-in-fact contract existed between himself and the defendants that was breached when the videos were aired on JKL. Fourth, he brought a claim for fraudulent inducement, arguing that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to request the videos constituted fraud that induced Santos into creating and delivering the videos.

These claims arise under different bodies of law: the copyright infringement claim is a federal claim under the Copyright Act, while the remaining three claims are state law claims. The distinction between federal and state law claims matters because the Copyright Act can, in certain circumstances, preempt state law claims that are "equivalent" to claims for copyright infringement—a question the Second Circuit noted but did not need to resolve, as it found the state law claims failed for independent reasons.

## The District Court's Dismissal Under Rule 12(b)(6)

The United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.) dismissed Santos's complaint in its entirety under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. Rule 12(b)(6) allows a defendant to move to dismiss a complaint for "failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted." At this stage, the court does not weigh evidence or make factual findings. Instead, it accepts the factual allegations in the complaint as true and asks whether those allegations, if proven, would entitle the plaintiff to legal relief.

The District Court concluded that Santos's copyright claims were barred by the fair use doctrine, which serves as an affirmative defense to copyright infringement. The court also concluded that Santos's state law claims either were preempted by the Copyright Act or independently failed to state a claim under state law. The Second Circuit affirmed on slightly different reasoning: it agreed that the copyright claims were barred by fair use and that each of the state law claims failed to state a claim, but it did not need to reach the preemption question because the state law claims failed for other reasons.

## Standard of Review on Appeal

The Second Circuit reviews a district court's dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) de novo, meaning the court of appeals independently assesses the complaint without deferring to the district court's reasoning. In conducting this review, the court considers "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint." This means the appellate court's analysis is confined to the four corners of the complaint and certain closely related materials.

In addition to the complaint itself, the court may take judicial notice of certain publicly available facts. Here, the court took judicial notice of Santos's copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office's registry. This is significant because the registrations contained information—specifically, the publication dates of the videos—that was relevant to the fair use analysis. The ability to consider such materials at the pleading stage allows courts to resolve certain issues, including fair use, even before any factual discovery has occurred. Indeed, the Second Circuit noted that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage, a point that proved central to the outcome of this case.

# Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Defense

## Fair Use as a Flexible Affirmative Defense Under the Copyright Act

Former Congressman George Santos's appeal required the Second Circuit to address a fundamental question in copyright law: when does the unauthorized use of a copyrighted work constitute infringement, and when is it excused as "fair use"? The fair use doctrine operates as an affirmative defense to copyright infringement. As the court explained, it "excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote." This formulation captures the tension at the heart of copyright law. The Copyright Act grants creators exclusive rights in their works, but those rights are not absolute. Without some mechanism for flexibility, the enforcement of copyright could paradoxically suppress the very expressive and creative activity the law is designed to foster.

The court emphasized that fair use is "a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context." This flexibility is what allowed the court to evaluate Kimmel's use of Santos's videos—a use that involved deception in its procurement but served a commentary function in its execution—under a doctrine designed to accommodate a wide range of secondary uses. The court's discussion makes clear that fair use is not a narrow technical exemption but a broad, context-sensitive standard that courts apply by weighing multiple factors.

## The Four Fair Use Factors Under 17 U.S.C. § 107

The Copyright Act sets out four non-exclusive factors that courts consider in determining whether fair use has been established. Under 17 U.S.C. § 107, these are: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. The court noted that these factors are "non-exclusive," meaning courts may also consider other relevant circumstances, though the four statutory factors provide the core analytical framework.

The court's opinion in this case focused primarily on the first and fourth factors, consistent with the general observation that these two factors often carry the greatest weight in fair use analysis. The second factor received briefer treatment, and the third factor was not separately addressed in the court's reasoning. This selective treatment is itself instructive: the factors are not a mechanical checklist in which each must receive equal attention, but rather a set of considerations that courts weigh together, with some factors taking on greater significance depending on the facts of the case.

## First Factor: Purpose and Character of the Use and the Transformative Use Test

The first fair use factor—the "purpose and character of the use"—asks whether the secondary user has done something with the copyrighted work that adds new meaning, message, or purpose. The court explained that in assessing this factor, it "focus[es] chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative, i.e., whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." The key distinction is between a use that merely replaces the original work and one that gives it a new function or character.

In this case, the District Court had found that the purpose of Kimmel's use was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Santos did not dispute this characterization of the secondary use's purpose. The transformative nature of the use was therefore apparent: Santos created the videos to convey personalized messages of encouragement, while Kimmel aired them as part of a comedic segment that mocked Santos's willingness to say outrageous things for payment. The purpose of the secondary use was fundamentally different from, and did not supersede, the purpose of the original creation.

## Santos's Argument That Kimmel "Instigated" the Creation of the Videos and Was Their "Designer"

Santos advanced a creative argument to resist the conclusion that Kimmel's use was transformative. He maintained that Kimmel "instigated the creation of the original Works" by submitting the video requests through Cameo using fictitious names, and that Kimmel was therefore the "designer" of the videos' original purpose. Under this theory, because Kimmel conceived of the videos' purpose from the outset, the use could not be transformative—Kimmel was simply using the videos for the very purpose he had designed them to serve.

This argument reflects an attempt to collapse the distinction between the original work and the secondary use by tracing the origin of the original work back to the secondary user. If Kimmel was the architect of the videos' purpose, Santos argued, then Kimmel's later use of the videos on his show was not a transformation of that purpose but a continuation of it. The argument has a certain logical appeal: when the secondary user procures the creation of the original work, the usual assumption that the original creator's purpose and the secondary user's purpose are different may seem unsettled.

## The Objective Standard for Transformative Use: The Perspective of a Reasonable Observer vs. Subjective Intent

The court rejected Santos's instigation argument, relying on a critical principle of fair use analysis: the transformative character of a use is assessed from the perspective of a reasonable observer, not from the subjective intent of either the copyright holder or the secondary user. Citing the Supreme Court's decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), the court explained that "the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use."

This objective standard is significant for several reasons. First, it means that a copyright holder cannot defeat a fair use defense simply by claiming that the secondary user's purpose was identical to the original purpose. Second, it means that a secondary user cannot defeat the defense by claiming subjective transformative intent if an objective observer would not perceive the use as transformative. The inquiry is about what the use communicates and accomplishes, not about what was in the minds of the parties.

Applying this standard, the court found that Santos's own complaint undermined his argument. Santos had alleged that the videos conveyed "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." A reasonable observer, the court concluded, would understand the videos as carrying this message—not as communicating a willingness to say absurd things for money. Because Kimmel's use on JKL conveyed a different message—commentary and mockery of that willingness—the use was transformative regardless of who instigated the creation of the videos.

## Santos's Bad Faith Argument Based on Kimmel's False Representations and Harper & Row

Santos raised a separate challenge under the first fair use factor, arguing that Kimmel's false representations—his use of fictitious names to submit the Cameo requests—demonstrated bad faith and should nullify the fair use defense. In support, Santos cited *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539 (1985), in which the Supreme Court stated that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing."

The argument had surface plausibility. Kimmel had, after all, deceived Santos by using false identities to procure the videos. If fair use requires good faith, Santos's argument went, then Kimmel's deception should disqualify him from invoking the defense. This argument attempted to import a moral dimension into the fair use analysis—using the doctrine's flexibility to account for the equities of how the copyrighted work was obtained.

## The Court's Rejection of the Bad Faith Argument and the Lack of Market Usurpation

The court rejected Santos's bad faith argument for two reasons. First, the court observed that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from the *Harper & Row* statement that fair use presupposes good faith. This is an important point for students to note: Supreme Court language about good faith in fair use, once articulated, has not been reaffirmed and has effectively been narrowed or abandoned in subsequent jurisprudence. The court did not elaborate extensively on this point, but the citation signals that Santos's argument relied on precedent that no longer carries the weight it once may have had.

Second, and more substantively, the court looked at what Santos's complaint actually alleged about the purpose of Kimmel's use. The complaint, the court observed, painted "a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary." Citing *Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, 756 F.3d 73 (2d Cir. 2014), the court explained that the relevant concern is whether the secondary user acted to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work. Santos's complaint did not allege that Kimmel intended to supplant Santos's commercial rights in the videos; it alleged that Kimmel intended to mock and criticize Santos. Citing *Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015), the court recognized that uses motivated by criticism and commentary are consistent with fair use, even when the procurement of the work involved deception.

The court therefore agreed with the District Court that the first factor "strongly supports a finding of fair use." The combination of a transformative purpose and the absence of any intent to supplant the original work's commercial value—despite the deception involved in obtaining the videos—made the first factor decisively favorable to Kimmel.

## Second Factor: Nature of the Copyrighted Work and the Publication Timing of the Videos

The second fair use factor—the "nature of the copyrighted work"—considers whether the work is factual or creative, and whether it is published or unpublished, among other characteristics. The court's treatment of this factor was brief but turned on a specific factual point established by the copyright registrations. The court took judicial notice of the copyright registrations, as published in the Copyright Office's registry, which established that the videos "were published on the same day they were created."

This timing matters because the fair use analysis draws a distinction between published and unpublished works. Unpublished works generally receive greater protection under the second factor, since the copyright holder has a strong interest in controlling the first public appearance of their work. Here, however, the videos were published simultaneously with their creation, meaning they were already available to the public when Kimmel used them. This factual finding, established by the copyright registrations themselves, undercut any argument that the second factor weighed against fair use on the ground that the works were unpublished.

## Fourth Factor: Market Harm—Usurpation of a Market vs. Criticism or Parody Under Campbell and Hachette

The fourth fair use factor examines "the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work." The court clarified the proper framing of this inquiry, explaining that the question is not whether the secondary work damages the market for the original—through, for example, parody or criticism that devalues it—but whether the secondary work "usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." This distinction is critical. A use that criticizes or parodies a copyrighted work may well reduce its market value, but that is not the kind of harm the fourth factor is designed to capture. The factor targets uses that substitute for the original work in its existing or potential markets.

The court cited *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), for the proposition that "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." In other words, if the copyright holder would never have licensed the kind of use at issue—because creators do not typically authorize parodies or critical lampoons of their own work—then the secondary use has not usurped a potential market. Santos could not claim that Kimmel's mocking use had deprived him of a licensing market for parody or criticism of his own videos, because such a market is not realistically available to a copyright holder.

The court concluded that Santos had not suffered market harm within the meaning of the fourth factor. Kimmel's segments on JKL did not offer a competing substitute for Santos's Cameo videos. Someone who wanted a personalized video message from Santos would not substitute a JKL clip of Santos being mocked. The uses served different functions entirely.

## Fair Use as a Basis for Dismissal at the Pleading Stage Under TCA Television Corp.

A notable feature of this case is that the fair use defense was evaluated and upheld at the pleading stage—on a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6)—rather than after a developed factual record at summary judgment or trial. The court explained that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage, citing *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168 (2d Cir. 2016).

This is an important procedural point. Fair use is often thought of as a fact-intensive inquiry that resists early resolution. But the court's analysis demonstrates that when the facts alleged in the complaint itself establish the elements of fair use, a court may resolve the defense as a matter of law at the motion-to-dismiss stage. The court noted that it needed only to consider "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue" to decide the question of fair use, citing *Cariou v. Prince*, 714 F.3d 694 (2d Cir. 2013). Both the original works and the allegedly infringing uses were described in the complaint and were available for comparison. The transformative purpose was evident from Santos's own allegations about the videos' message and the District Court's characterization of Kimmel's purpose, which Santos did not dispute. The market harm analysis turned on the nature of the use as commentary rather than substitution, a conclusion that followed from the face of the complaint.

## Affirmance of the District Court's Dismissal of the Copyright Claims

Having addressed Santos's challenges to the District Court's analysis of the fair use factors, the court affirmed the dismissal of Santos's copyright claims. The first factor strongly supported fair use because Kimmel's use was transformative—adding a new purpose of sarcastic commentary—and Santos's bad faith argument failed both because the Supreme Court had distanced itself from the *Harper & Row* good faith language and because the complaint did not allege that Kimmel intended to supplant Santos's commercial rights. The second factor did not weigh against fair use because the videos were published on the same day they were created. The fourth factor favored fair use because Kimmel's use did not usurp Santos's market but instead offered criticism and parody, the kind of use that falls outside the notion of a potential licensing market under *Campbell*.

The court's analysis illustrates how the fair use doctrine, with its flexible, multi-factor approach, can resolve copyright disputes even at the pleading stage when the relevant facts are clear from the complaint itself. The court's opinion teaches that the doctrine's flexibility is not a license for unconstrained judicial discretion but a structured framework that, applied carefully, can distinguish between uses that supplant the original work and uses that transform it into something new.

# State Law Claims: Contract, Implied Contract, and Fraudulent Inducement

## Overview of Santos's Three State Law Claims and the District Court's Preemption Ruling

In addition to his copyright claims, Santos brought three state law causes of action against Kimmel and the other defendants: breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. These claims arose from the same factual backdrop as the copyright claims—Kimmel's use of fictitious names to request personalized Cameo videos from Santos, which were then aired on JKL as part of the mocking "Will Santos Say It?" segments. The District Court dismissed the breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act, and it dismissed the fraudulent inducement claim for failure to allege out-of-pocket loss. On appeal, Santos challenged each of these rulings, arguing that his contract claims were "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and thus not preempted, and that his fraudulent inducement claim was improperly dismissed.

## The Copyright Act Preemption Question and Why the Court Declined to Resolve It

The Copyright Act contains an express preemption provision that governs when state law claims are displaced by federal copyright law. The District Court concluded that Santos's two contract claims were preempted because they were equivalent to copyright infringement claims—the rights they sought to protect fell within the scope of copyright's exclusive rights. On appeal, Santos contested this conclusion, invoking the Second Circuit's standard that state law claims must be "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" to avoid preemption.

The Second Circuit, however, chose not to resolve the preemption question. This is a significant procedural and doctrinal choice. The court explained that it "need not resolve this issue" because Santos's state law contract claims "fail to state a claim for relief under state law." This reflects an important principle of judicial economy: when a claim fails on an independent ground, courts need not address every argument raised by the parties. By proceeding directly to the merits of the state law claims, the court avoided a potentially complex preemption analysis and provided a cleaner, more straightforward basis for affirmance. The practical effect was that even if Santos were correct about preemption, his claims would still fail because they did not satisfy the pleading requirements of the underlying state law.

## Direct Breach of Contract Claim: Santos as a Non-Party to the Cameo Terms of Service

Santos's first state law claim was for direct breach of contract, based on the Terms of Service that govern the Cameo platform. Santos alleged that Kimmel, by using fictitious names and misrepresenting his identity when submitting video requests, breached provisions of the Cameo Terms of Service. A critical threshold problem with this claim, however, was that Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service. The Terms of Service constituted an agreement between Cameo and its users—people like Kimmel who registered on the platform and submitted requests. Santos, as the talent fulfilling those requests, did not himself agree to the Terms of Service. He therefore sought to enforce a contract to which he was a stranger, raising the question whether he could do so as a third-party beneficiary.

## Illinois Law on Third-Party Beneficiaries and the Presumption Against Conferring Benefits to Noncontracting Parties

The court applied Illinois law to the Terms of Service, apparently because the agreement specified Illinois as the governing law. Under Illinois law, third-party beneficiary claims face a substantial barrier: there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." This presumption means that courts will not lightly infer that a contract was intended to benefit someone who is not a party to it. To overcome this presumption, the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration." This is a demanding standard—it requires the contracting parties' intent to benefit the third party to be so clear and unmistakable that it is essentially explicit in the contract.

The court found that Santos identified nothing close to such an express declaration in the Terms of Service. He could point to no language in the relevant provisions indicating that they were intended to be enforceable by him or other noncontracting parties. Without such language, the strong Illinois presumption held, and Santos could not enforce the Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary.

## The Significance of Third-Party Beneficiary Language in Other Provisions of the Terms of Service

The court's analysis was bolstered by an important textual observation about the Terms of Service as a whole. While the provisions Santos sought to enforce did not contain third-party beneficiary language, other provisions of the same agreement did. This contrast was legally significant because it demonstrated that the parties to the Terms of Service knew how to draft provisions that allowed enforcement by third parties when they so intended. The presence of third-party beneficiary language in some provisions, and its absence in the provisions Santos relied on, supported a strong inference that the parties did not intend the provisions Santos invoked to be enforceable by nonparties. This interpretive principle—sometimes called the *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* canon, though the court did not label it as such—allowed the court to conclude that the omission of third-party beneficiary language from the relevant provisions was deliberate rather than accidental.

## Breach of Implied Contract Under New York Law: Intent of the Parties and Surrounding Circumstances

Santos's second state law claim was for breach of an implied-in-fact contract. Unlike an express contract, which is defined by its written or spoken terms, an implied-in-fact contract is inferred from the conduct and circumstances of the parties. The court applied New York law to this claim, drawing on the principle that the determination of whether an implied-in-fact contract was formed depends on "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances." Under New York law, courts look to the parties' behavior and the context of their interactions to determine whether, despite the absence of an explicit agreement, the parties in fact understood themselves to be bound by contractual obligations.

## Failure to Plead Essential Terms of the Implied Contract in Nonconclusory Language

Even under this more flexible standard, Santos's claim failed. The court held that Santos's complaint did not "allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties' . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." This requirement—that a plaintiff plead essential contract terms in nonconclusory language—serves an important pleading function. It ensures that the defendant has adequate notice of the contractual obligations it allegedly breached and that the court can assess whether a legally enforceable agreement existed. Conclusory allegations—those that merely assert legal conclusions without supporting factual detail—are insufficient under the pleading standards applied here. Santos's complaint, the court noted, failed to plead the essential terms of the putative implied contract that the defendants allegedly breached, leaving the court without enough to conclude that an enforceable implied contract existed.

## Failure to Allege a Meeting of the Minds That Kimmel Would Adhere to the Terms of Service

In addition to the failure to plead essential terms, the court identified a separate and equally fatal defect in the implied contract claim: Santos did not plausibly allege "an indication of a meeting of the minds of the parties constituting an agreement" that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service. A "meeting of the minds"—a shared understanding of the essential terms of the agreement—is a fundamental element of contract formation under New York law. Without it, there is no enforceable contract, express or implied. The court found nothing in Santos's complaint that would allow a reasonable inference that Kimmel and Santos had reached a mutual understanding about adherence to the Terms of Service. This deficiency was particularly significant given that the Terms of Service were an agreement between Kimmel and Cameo, not between Kimmel and Santos, and Santos pointed to no conduct or circumstance suggesting that Kimmel had separately agreed with Santos to follow those terms.

## Fraudulent Inducement Claim Under New York Law and the Out-of-Pocket Loss Requirement

Santos's third and final state law claim was for fraudulent inducement. This claim was based on Kimmel's use of fictitious names and false representations when submitting the Cameo video requests—the deception that led Santos to create the videos in the first place. Under New York law, a fraudulent inducement claim requires the plaintiff to allege, among other elements, that he suffered an out-of-pocket loss as a result of the fraud. This out-of-pocket loss requirement is a distinctive feature of New York fraud law. It means that the plaintiff must demonstrate actual monetary loss—the difference between what he paid and what he received—rather than relying on other measures of damage such as benefit-of-the-bargain or expectation damages. The requirement serves as a gatekeeping function, ensuring that fraud claims rest on concrete financial harm rather than speculative injury.

## Santos's Failure to Allege Actual Out-of-Pocket Loss as Required by Lama Holding Co. and Connaughton

The court agreed with the District Court that Santos failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket loss. In *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, the New York Court of Appeals established that out-of-pocket loss is the proper measure of damages for fraud under New York law. Santos's complaint did not identify any monetary loss he suffered as a result of Kimmel's deception. He was paid for each video he made; the deception consisted of Kimmel's false identity and intent, not of any failure to pay. The court cited *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.* for the principle that a plaintiff must allege actual out-of-pocket loss to state a fraud claim, and concluded that Santos had not done so. Without an allegation of concrete monetary loss attributable to the fraud, the fraudulent inducement claim could not survive.

## Affirmance of the District Court's Dismissal of All State Law Claims

Having addressed each of Santos's three state law claims, the court affirmed the District Court's dismissal of all of them. The breach of contract claim failed because Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and could not overcome Illinois's strong presumption against third-party beneficiary enforcement. The breach of implied contract claim failed because Santos did not plead the essential terms of the alleged implied contract in nonconclusory language, nor did he allege a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service. And the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege the out-of-pocket loss that New York law requires for fraud claims. Notably, the court reached these conclusions without needing to resolve the Copyright Act preemption question that the District Court had relied on for the contract claims. By affirming on independent state law grounds, the Second Circuit provided a more robust basis for its decision—one that would stand regardless of how the preemption question might ultimately be resolved.
