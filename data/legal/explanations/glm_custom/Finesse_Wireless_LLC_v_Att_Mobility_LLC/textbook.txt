Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Case Background, Procedural Posture, and Standards of Review

## The Technology: Intermodulation Products, Passive Intermodulation Products, and PIM Cancellation

To understand this case, a law student must first grasp the underlying technology that the patents address. Radios transmit and receive signals on different frequencies to avoid interference with one another. However, when transmit signals encounter certain obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors—they can combine to form new frequencies known as "intermodulation products" (IMPs). These IMPs are unwanted signals that can interfere with the radio's ability to receive the signals it actually wants to capture.

IMPs caused by passive obstacles are specifically referred to as "passive intermodulation products" (PIM). The distinction between internal and external PIM is relevant to understanding the patents: internal PIM is caused by mechanical issues inside the radio itself, while external PIM is caused by sources outside the radio. Both the '134 and '775 patents address methods for mitigating the interference that IMPs create, and the accused products in this case employ a feature called "PIM cancellation" (PIM-C) to reduce this interference. Understanding this technological context is essential because the infringement disputes in this case turn on whether the accused radios perform the specific signal-processing steps that the patent claims require.

## The '134 and '775 Patents: General Subject Matter and Asserted Claims

Finesse Wireless LLC owns two patents at issue in this case: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the "'134 patent") and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the "'775 patent"). Both patents relate to methods for mitigating IMP interference in radios, though they claim different specific approaches.

The '134 patent claims a method for reducing IMP interference by isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals" and then canceling out the interference generating signals using a computed estimate of the IMPs. Claim 1 of the '134 patent is representative and recites a multi-step method: over-sampling a passband of received signals (which includes both signals of interest and interference generating signals) to create a bit stream, isolating signals of interest using decimating filters, isolating source signals that generate IMPs, computing estimates of those IMPs, canceling out the inband IMPs, and performing phase and amplitude adjustments on estimations of the IMP interfering signals in a closed loop manner. A critical limitation for the infringement analysis is limitation 1[a], which requires over-sampling a passband of received signals that includes both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals."

The '775 patent claims a method for reducing IMP interference in a receiver by digitally multiplying three signals in seven multiplications to generate IMP cancellation signals. Claim 1 is representative and requires, among other things, generating IMP cancellation signals based on a power series description of a non-linear process, including generating an n-th order cancellation signal by digitally multiplying and filtering three signals S1, S2, and S3 in seven specific combinations: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. The key disputed limitation on appeal concerns whether the accused radios perform all seven of these multiplications.

Finesse asserted claims 1–3 of the '134 patent and claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36 of the '775 patent.

## Parties, Infringement Allegations, and Nokia's Intervention

Finesse Wireless LLC, as the patent owner, sued AT&T Mobility LLC for allegedly infringing the asserted claims of both patents by using the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature in Nokia radios. This is an important procedural point: the infringement allegations were directed at AT&T's *use* of accused radios that incorporated the PIM-C feature, not at AT&T's manufacture of those radios. Nokia of America Corporation, which manufactured the accused radios, intervened in the case. Intervention is a procedural mechanism that allows a non-party with a significant interest in the litigation to join as a party. Nokia's interest was clear: its radios and their PIM-C feature were the accused technology, and an adverse ruling could have significant consequences for Nokia's business. Together, AT&T and Nokia are referred to as the Appellants in the Federal Circuit appeal.

## Trial Proceedings: Jury Verdict and Damages Award

The case proceeded to trial before a jury in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. At trial, Finesse presented its infringement case primarily through the testimony of its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who opined that the accused Nokia radios infringe the asserted claims of both patents. The jury returned a verdict finding all asserted claims of both the '134 and '775 patents valid and infringed. The jury also awarded Finesse $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages for the remaining life of both patents. A lump-sum damages award, as opposed to a running royalty, is a single payment that compensates the patentee for the accused infringer's use of the patented technology over a defined period. The size of this award underscores the commercial significance of the PIM-C technology at issue.

## Post-Trial Motions: JMOL and New Trial

After the jury verdict, the Appellants filed several post-trial motions. They moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, arguing that no reasonable jury could have found infringement based on the evidence presented at trial. They also moved for JMOL on damages and, in the alternative, for a new trial on damages. JMOL is a procedural device available under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50 by which a party challenges the legal sufficiency of the evidence supporting a jury verdict. A new trial motion, governed by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 59, asks the court to set aside the verdict and conduct a new trial, typically on grounds such as errors in the trial proceedings or a verdict that is against the weight of the evidence.

The district court denied all of these motions. With respect to JMOL of noninfringement for the '134 patent, the court acknowledged that the "modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the 'interference generating signals'" and that Dr. Wells testified the PIM model is generated in the GROOT FPGA (downstream of the RF ADC), but the court was persuaded by Finesse's argument that the modeled PIM path is not the "interference generating signals" and that Dr. Wells was actually referring to two input signals labeled x1 and x2. With respect to the '775 patent, the district court rejected Appellants' argument that "S1, S2 and S3" must be unique signals as waived, and found that using two signals plus a copy of one of those signals was sufficient to meet the claim construction of three separately identifiable signals, and that three distinct multiplications were sufficient to show infringement because each corresponded to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. The district court also denied JMOL on damages and the motion for a new trial on damages. All of these denials form the basis of the appeal.

## Federal Circuit Jurisdiction

The United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit has jurisdiction over this appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), which vests the Federal Circuit with exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from district court decisions in cases arising under the patent laws of the United States. Because Finesse's claims allege infringement of U.S. patents, the case arises under the patent laws, and the Federal Circuit—not the regional circuit court (here, the Fifth Circuit)—is the proper appellate forum.

However, a critical nuance of Federal Circuit appellate practice is that while the Federal Circuit has jurisdiction over patent appeals, it does not always apply its own procedural law to every issue on appeal. For procedural matters that are not unique to patent law, the Federal Circuit borrows the law of the regional circuit in which the district court sits. This distinction becomes important in determining the standards of review that govern the appeal.

## Standards of Review

The standards of review are central to understanding how the Federal Circuit evaluated the district court's decisions. Different standards apply to different types of rulings, and the applicable standard can significantly affect the outcome of the appeal.

### Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL)

The Federal Circuit reviews a district court's denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit—in this case, the Fifth Circuit. The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL de novo. This means the appellate court gives no deference to the district court's decision and independently evaluates whether the evidence was legally sufficient to support the verdict. The standard for granting JMOL is demanding: the court will reverse "only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." This is a high bar because the appellate court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party (here, Finesse, as the verdict-winner) and must respect the jury's role as factfinder. Nevertheless, as this case demonstrates, the standard can be met when the evidence supporting the verdict is internally inconsistent or insufficient as a matter of law.

### Substantial Evidence for Jury Infringement Findings

Closely related to the JMOL standard is the substantial evidence standard for reviewing a jury's factual findings of infringement. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence "if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." This standard is less demanding than the JMOL standard in one sense—it asks only whether *some* reasonable evidence supports the verdict—but it operates in tandem with JMOL review. When a party moves for JMOL challenging a jury's infringement finding, the appellate court asks whether substantial evidence supports that finding. If not, JMOL should have been granted.

### Claim Construction

The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction de novo, meaning the appellate court independently determines the correct interpretation of claim language without deferring to the district court's construction. Any underlying factual findings that support the district court's claim construction, however, are reviewed for clear error. This two-part standard reflects the Supreme Court's framework from *Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc.* (2015), which recognizes that while claim construction is ultimately a question of law reviewed de novo, a district court may rely on factual findings (such as the meaning of a technical term based on extrinsic evidence like expert testimony or dictionary definitions) that are reviewed under the deferential clear error standard. In this case, the district court construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean "separately identifiable" but not "unique" signals, and this construction was not challenged as erroneous on appeal.

### Denial of a New Trial

The Federal Circuit reviews a district court's denial of a motion for a new trial under the law of the regional circuit. The Fifth Circuit reviews such denials for abuse of discretion. Under this deferential standard, the appellate court will not reverse unless the district court's decision was based on a clearly erroneous view of the law, a clearly erroneous assessment of the evidence, or amounts to a manifest injustice. The abuse of discretion standard gives substantial deference to the district court, which is in a better position to assess the conduct of the trial and the weight of the evidence.

In this case, because the Federal Circuit reverses the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents, it vacates the damages award without needing to reach the merits of the damages-related issues. When the underlying liability finding is reversed, any damages award predicated on that liability cannot stand.

# The '134 Patent: Infringement Theory and the Insufficiency of Self-Contradictory Expert Testimony

## The '134 Patent Claims: Structure and Key Limitations

The '134 patent claims a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. As the court explains, the patent's approach involves isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals" and then canceling out the interference generating signals using a computed estimate of the IMPs. Claim 1, which the court treats as representative, recites six steps: (a) over-sampling a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, where the received signals include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" capable of generating IMPs in-band of the signals of interest; (b) isolating the signals of interest in the bit stream using decimating filters; (c) isolating the source signals that generate the IMPs using decimating filters; (d) computing an estimate of each IMP from the source signals; (e) canceling out the in-band IMPs using those estimates; and (f) performing phase and amplitude adjustment on the estimated IMP interfering signals in a closed-loop manner, including performing sub-sample phase shifts.

Understanding the patent's claimed method requires grasping the basic problem it addresses. Radios avoid signal interference by operating on different frequencies, but their transmit signals can combine when encountering passive obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors—to form new frequencies called intermodulation products. IMPs caused by passive obstacles are called "passive intermodulation products" (PIM). The '134 patent's method seeks to mitigate this PIM interference by sampling the relevant signals, isolating them, computing IMP estimates, and using those estimates to cancel the interference.

## Key Limitation 1[a]: The Sampling Requirement

The central dispute on appeal concerns limitation 1[a], which requires "over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals." This limitation imposes a specific structural requirement: the receiver must sample a passband of signals that includes both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals." As the party alleging infringement, Finesse bore the burden of proving that the accused Nokia radios meet this limitation—that is, that their receiver samples a passband of received signals containing both categories of signals.

This burden matters because patent infringement is a question of fact that the patentee must prove, and when the patentee relies on expert testimony to establish that an accused product meets a claim limitation, that testimony must provide sufficient evidence for a reasonable jury to find infringement. If the expert's testimony is internally inconsistent or fails to map the accused product onto the claimed limitations, the testimony may be legally insufficient to support a verdict.

## Dr. Wells' Initial Infringement Theory

At trial, Finesse's infringement case depended on the testimony of its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the '134 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document that depicts how the accused radios operate. According to Dr. Wells, the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) in the accused radios is a receiver that samples the claimed "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" to create a bit stream. Both categories of signals, according to Dr. Wells, appear on what the Nokia document labels as the "red path" entering the RF ADC.

Dr. Wells was consistent and specific in mapping the claimed terms onto the Nokia document. He testified that the "signals of interest" correspond to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference signal and that the "interference generating signals" correspond to the modeled PIM path. The Nokia document's legend confirms that the red path represents the "DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path." Dr. Wells' expert report similarly stated that the "DL (TX) reference" signal meets the agreed construction of "signals of interest" and that the "modeled PIM signal" is the "interference generating signal." This mapping was the cornerstone of Finesse's infringement theory: the RF ADC samples both the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, thereby satisfying limitation 1[a]'s requirement that the receiver over-sample a passband including both categories of signals.

## The Fundamental Flaw: Signal Generation Timing

On cross-examination, Appellants' counsel exposed a critical problem with Dr. Wells' theory. The modeled PIM path—the signal Dr. Wells identified as the "interference generating signals"—is generated downstream of the RF ADC, inside a component called the GROOT FPGA. This means the modeled PIM path is created after the RF ADC has already performed its sampling. The logical implication is devastating to Finesse's theory: the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because that signal does not exist at the time sampling occurs. A receiver cannot sample a signal that has not yet been generated.

This timing problem directly undermines limitation 1[a]. That limitation requires the receiver to over-sample a passband of received signals that includes both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." If the "interference generating signals" (as Dr. Wells defined them—the modeled PIM path) are not generated until after sampling, then the receiver cannot possibly sample a passband that includes them. The accused radios simply cannot meet the claim limitation under Dr. Wells' own mapping.

The district court acknowledged this problem at the JMOL stage. It recognized that "the modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the 'interference generating signals'" and that "Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA." The court further acknowledged that if the modeled PIM path is the claimed "interference generating signals," then limitation 1[a] cannot be met. This concession should have been the end of the matter, but the court nevertheless found sufficient evidence for the jury to find infringement.

## Dr. Wells' Contradictory Pivot

Confronted with the timing problem on cross-examination, Dr. Wells attempted to shift his theory. When counsel pointed out that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because it is generated downstream, Dr. Wells pivoted, testifying that "there's two signals on that red path because we know that because there's the x1 and the x2." These references to x1 and x2—signals appearing in the Nokia document—represented a new theory: perhaps x1 and x2, rather than the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, are the two signals the RF ADC samples.

But Dr. Wells never completed this pivot. He did not testify that x1 maps onto the "signals of interest" or that x2 maps onto the "interference generating signals," or vice versa. He did not explain how x1 and x2 correspond to the claimed categories of signals. Instead, he continued to testify, repeatedly and consistently, that the two signals on the red path are the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. When asked directly whether x1 is the modeled PIM path, Dr. Wells responded only that "x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path"—a statement that does not map x1 or x2 onto either claimed signal category.

The result was a muddle. Dr. Wells had one clear theory (DL(TX) reference = signals of interest; modeled PIM path = interference generating signals) that was logically impossible given the signal generation timing, and a vague, incomplete alternative reference to x1 and x2 that never connected to the claim language. His testimony oscillated between these two positions without resolution, leaving the jury without a coherent infringement theory.

## The District Court's Erroneous Finding

Despite the confusion in Dr. Wells' testimony, the district court found that "Dr. Wells testified that there are two signals entering the RF ADC, and that those are signals of interest and the interference generating signals. Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2." The Federal Circuit rejected this finding as unsupported by the record. The court noted that "there is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony." Dr. Wells never testified that x1 and x2 correspond to the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The district court's finding required it to infer a mapping that Dr. Wells himself never articulated—a mapping that was, at best, ambiguous and, at worst, directly contradicted by his repeated testimony that the two signals on the red path are the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path.

This is a significant point for understanding the limits of a court's role in evaluating expert testimony at the JMOL stage. While a court reviewing a jury verdict must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, it cannot manufacture testimony that the expert never gave. The court may draw reasonable inferences from the evidence, but it cannot bridge gaps in an expert's testimony by supplying the missing elements of his infringement theory.

## Finesse's Appeal Argument and the Federal Circuit's Rejection

On appeal, Finesse attempted to rehabilitate Dr. Wells' testimony by arguing that he was "laboring under the misimpression that x1 and x2 correspond to DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path." According to Finesse, when Dr. Wells referred to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path as the two signals on the red path, he was really referring to x1 and x2 all along, because he misunderstood the relationship between the labels in the Nokia document's legend and the signals x1 and x2.

The Federal Circuit found this argument unconvincing for several reasons. First, Dr. Wells' testimony was "confusing and unclear even after he was made aware of his alleged misimpression." He continued to testify that the modeled PIM path is the "interference generating signals" and never clarified that he meant x1 and x2 when he said DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path. Second, Finesse's argument required piecing together fragments of Dr. Wells' testimony from pages ninety pages apart in the record—one statement that "x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path" and another that "this red path, it includes the signals of interest, it includes the interference generating signals." The court declined to construct an infringement theory by assembling disparate snippets of testimony that were never connected by the expert himself. Third, even if Dr. Wells had testified that x1 and x2 are the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," he repeatedly and contradictorily testified that the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path correspond to the claimed signals, and offered no clear explanation for the contradiction.

## Application of Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.

The Federal Circuit's decision rests on an important principle of patent litigation evidence: when the party bearing the burden of proof relies on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, the court may conclude that the evidence is legally insufficient to support a jury verdict. The court drew directly from its precedent in Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp., 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008). In that case, the plaintiff's expert opined that the accused device contacted a vessel in "three dimensions," but on cross-examination admitted that the device only contacts the vessel in "two places." The court held that "no reasonable jury could have found that the device literally met this limitation based on [the expert's] opinion, given his contradictory testimony."

The principle has particular force in patent infringement cases, where the patentee bears the burden of proving that the accused product meets every limitation of the asserted claims. Expert testimony is often the primary means of establishing this, particularly when the accused technology is complex. But expert testimony is not a blank check. When an expert's testimony is internally contradictory—when it offers one infringement theory that is logically impossible and then vaguely gestures toward an alternative without completing the necessary analysis—the testimony cannot support a verdict. The jury cannot be expected to resolve contradictions in the expert's own testimony and then construct a coherent infringement theory from the pieces.

This principle also reflects the broader standard of review. The Federal Circuit reviews a jury's infringement finding for substantial evidence, meaning evidence "if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." The question is not whether any evidence supports the verdict, but whether a reasonable jury could reach the verdict based on the evidence. Self-contradictory expert testimony fails this standard because it does not provide the jury with a reliable basis for finding infringement. The jury would have to guess which version of the expert's testimony to believe and then fill in the gaps the expert left unexplained.

## Reversal of JMOL Denial: No Substantial Evidence of Infringement

Applying these principles, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the '134 patent. The court held that the jury's infringement verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. Dr. Wells' initial theory—that the RF ADC samples both the DL(TX) reference (signals of interest) and the modeled PIM path (interference generating signals)—was logically impossible because the modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling occurs. His attempted pivot to x1 and x2 was incomplete and contradictory, as he never mapped those signals onto the claimed categories and continued to testify that the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path were the relevant signals. Under Johns Hopkins, a party bearing the burden of proof cannot sustain a verdict on such self-contradictory testimony.

The reversal illustrates the critical importance of a coherent, internally consistent expert theory in patent infringement litigation. The patentee's expert must do more than identify components of the accused product that seem related to claim limitations; he must clearly and specifically map each claim limitation onto the accused product in a manner that is logically consistent. When an expert's testimony contains a fundamental flaw—such as claiming that a receiver samples a signal that does not exist at the time of sampling—and the expert's attempts to correct that flaw only produce further contradictions, the testimony is insufficient as a matter of law. The district court's denial of JMOL was therefore error, and the Federal Circuit reversed.

# The '775 Patent: Claim Construction and Prosecution History

## The '775 Patent Claims: Seven Multiplications of Three Signals

The '775 patent claims a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in a receiver that is co-located with a transmitter. The method works by generating IMP cancellation signals ("ICSs") continuously and in near real time using copies of the transmitter's signals. The key claim limitation at the heart of this appeal requires that the ICSs be generated based on a power series description of a non-linear process, and specifically that an n-th order ICS be generated by digitally multiplying and filtering three signals—S1, S2, and S3—in seven specific third-order combinations: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. Claim 1 is representative and recites these seven multiplications as part of the method for performing interference cancellation.

This seven-multiplication requirement became the central dispute on appeal. The parties did not contest that the accused Nokia radios use PIM cancellation technology, but they sharply disputed whether the accused radios perform the specific seven multiplications of three signals as required by the claim. Understanding this dispute requires examining both how the district court construed the phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3" and whether the prosecution history narrowed the scope of that phrase.

## The District Court's Claim Construction: "Separately Identifiable" but Not "Unique"

At summary judgment, the district court construed the phrase "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean signals that are "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique." This construction was significant because it meant the claimed three signals did not have to be three distinct, different input signals; they could include copies or repetitions of the same underlying signal, so long as they could be separately identified in the accused device.

The district court's construction drew on both intrinsic and extrinsic evidence. Examining the intrinsic evidence—the patent specification and claims—the court concluded that nothing in the patent limited "S1, S2 and S3" to unique input signals. The court then credited inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence, finding that the inventor testified "S1, S2 and S3" represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals. This extrinsic evidence reinforced the court's reading of the intrinsic record. The result was a construction that allowed for some flexibility in how the three signals are identified, while still requiring that they be separately identifiable rather than completely indistinguishable.

## Appellants' Prosecution History Argument: Amendment from "Two or Three" to "Three"

Appellants argued that even under the district court's construction, Finesse had surrendered claim scope over products that use only two signals through actions taken during prosecution of the patent before the Patent Office. The argument centered on a claim amendment Finesse made to overcome a prior art rejection. The original claims had recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals." The examiner rejected those claims as obvious over a prior art reference called Filipovic, which teaches generating cancellation signals using two inputs, in combination with secondary references. In response to that rejection, Finesse amended the claims to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven multiplications.

Appellants seized on this amendment to argue that Finesse narrowed its claims from covering products using "two or three" signals to covering only products using "three" signals. Under this theory, by amending the claims to recite three signals instead of "two or three," Finesse explicitly disclaimed coverage of products that use only two unique input signals—precisely the kind of product AT&T and Nokia accused the patents of covering. This is a classic prosecution history estoppel argument: the applicant amended the claims to overcome prior art, and the amendment narrowed the scope of the claim, thereby surrendering the territory between the original and amended claim language.

## The Federal Circuit's Rejection: Distinguishing Filipovic on Active Versus Passive IMPs

The Federal Circuit rejected Appellants' prosecution history estoppel argument. The court agreed that the examiner had rejected the original "two or three signals" claims as obvious over Filipovic, and that Finesse had amended the claims to recite "three signals." But the court looked closely at how Finesse overcame the rejection. The court noted that Finesse overcame the rejection "in part by explaining 'Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs' and not passive IMPs that the claimed method also addresses." In other words, the distinguishing feature Finesse relied on was not the number of signals but the type of IMPs being addressed—active versus passive.

This distinction matters for the estoppel analysis. Prosecution history estoppel bars recapture of subject matter surrendered during prosecution, but courts must identify what was actually surrendered. If the applicant overcame prior art by arguing a distinction unrelated to the amended limitation, the amendment may not constitute a surrender of the specific scope at issue. Here, the Federal Circuit found that Finesse's primary argument for distinguishing Filipovic was based on the active/passive IMP distinction, not on the number of signals. The court cited the Notice of Allowance as further support that the patent was allowed based on this distinction.

## Application of Continental Circuits: No "Clear and Unambiguous" Disavowal

The Federal Circuit placed the prosecution history argument in the framework established by *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019). Under *Continental Circuits*, a narrowing amendment rises to the level of a disclaimer of claim scope only if it constitutes a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal. The court held that Finesse's amendment from "two or three signals" to "three signals" did not meet this high bar. The court reasoned that the amendment, "[a]t most," requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals. The court found no clear and unambiguous disavowal of claim scope over products with two unique input signals, as would be required to find that Finesse surrendered coverage of such products.

This holding illustrates an important principle for claim construction and prosecution history estoppel: not every narrowing amendment disclaims all subject matter that might fall between the original and amended language. The court must identify what the applicant argued to distinguish the prior art and determine whether the amendment itself constituted a clear and unmistakable surrender of the specific scope the accused infringer is trying to exclude. Here, because Finesse overcame the rejection primarily by distinguishing Filipovic on the active/passive IMP ground, the amendment to "three signals" did not clearly and unambiguously disavow coverage of products using two unique input signals.

## Three "Separately Identifiable" Signals Versus Three "Unique" Input Signals

The distinction between "separately identifiable" and "unique" signals is central to understanding why the Federal Circuit rejected Appellants' argument. Under the district court's construction, "three signals S1, S2 and S3" need only be separately identifiable, not unique. The Federal Circuit accepted this construction and explained its practical consequence: using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, could satisfy the requirement for three separately identifiable signals. Two unique input signals, when one is duplicated, can yield three separately identifiable signals even though there are only two unique signals underlying them.

This distinction is what made Appellants' estoppel argument important but ultimately insufficient. Appellants needed to show that Finesse surrendered claim scope over products using two unique input signals. But the district court's construction—separately identifiable but not unique—meant that products using two unique signals could still infringe if those two signals, plus a copy, produced three separately identifiable signals. The Federal Circuit's rejection of the estoppel argument preserved this construction, leaving the door open to infringement by products with two unique input signals, so long as three separately identifiable signals could be found.

However, the Federal Circuit ultimately reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the '775 patent on a different ground: the insufficiency of the evidence that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document that listed only three distinct multiplications—x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2', and x1·|x2|·|x2|—and there was no evidence that any multiplications were repeated to produce all seven claimed combinations. Moreover, Dr. Wells never testified how those three distinct multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications. The court held that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications, and thus the infringement verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. The prosecution history argument, though rejected, illuminates the claim construction framework within which the court evaluated the infringement evidence, and the distinction between "separately identifiable" and "unique" signals remains an important element of the court's analysis.

# The '775 Patent: Substantial Evidence of the Seven Claimed Multiplications

## Dr. Wells' Reliance on Nokia Technical Documents

The '775 patent claims a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in a receiver by digitally multiplying three signals—designated S1, S2, and S3—in seven specific multiplications to generate IMP cancellation signals. Claim 1, the representative claim, requires "generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 and S1×S2×S3 and S1×S1×S3 and S2×S2×S3 and S1×S3×S3 and S2×S3×S3." The claim thus enumerates seven distinct third-order multiplications, each a unique combination of the three signals. To prove infringement, Finesse bore the burden of showing that the accused Nokia radios perform all seven of these multiplications.

At trial, Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document depicting how the accused radios operate. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios multiply three separately identifiable signals and equated three distinct multiplications listed in the Nokia document to the seven multiplications recited in the claim. This testimony formed the core of Finesse's infringement case for the '775 patent. The jury found all asserted claims infringed, and the district court denied Appellants' motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement. On appeal, the Federal Circuit's task was to determine whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, a reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform all seven claimed multiplications.

## The Three Distinct Multiplications in the Nokia Document

The Nokia document on which Dr. Wells relied does not show seven multiplications of three signals. Instead, it lists only three distinct multiplications: x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|. Critically, these three multiplications do not involve straightforward multiplication of signals x1 and x2 alone. Rather, they incorporate modulus operations (denoted by vertical bars, e.g., |x1|) and complex conjugate operations (denoted by a prime, e.g., x2'). These mathematical operations transform the signals before multiplication, meaning the three multiplications in the Nokia document are not simply multiplications of the signals themselves but of modified versions of those signals.

This distinction matters because the claim recites multiplication of "three signals S1, S2 and S3"—the signals themselves—not their modulus or complex conjugate. The presence of modulus and complex conjugate operations in the Nokia document created a mapping problem: Dr. Wells needed to explain how x1, |x1|, x2, x2', |x2|, and combinations thereof correspond to the three signals S1, S2, and S3 in a way that satisfies all seven claimed multiplications. As the Federal Circuit observed, the document "does not show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications" but "only lists three distinct multiplications," and there was "no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed."

## Dr. Wells' Failure to Map the Three Multiplications onto the Seven Claimed Multiplications

Perhaps the most significant gap in Finesse's infringement case was Dr. Wells' failure to explain how the three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document map onto the seven multiplications required by limitation 1[d]. The claim explicitly enumerates seven multiplications: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. Dr. Wells identified only three multiplications in the accused radios and offered no testimony connecting those three to the seven claimed combinations. The Federal Circuit noted that "Dr. Wells did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications."

This evidentiary gap is dispositive. In a patent infringement case, the patentee's expert must provide a clear and coherent explanation of how the accused device practices each claim limitation. Where the expert identifies fewer operations than the claim requires and does not explain the correspondence between what the accused device does and what the claim recites, the jury is left to speculate. Speculation is not substantial evidence. The Federal Circuit has consistently held that an expert's conclusory or unexplained testimony cannot sustain an infringement verdict, and Dr. Wells' testimony here fell short of the reasoned explanation required.

## The District Court's Reasoning

The district court denied JMOL on the '775 patent, finding that three distinct multiplications were sufficient to support the jury's infringement verdict. The court reasoned that when two unique signals are used, only three distinct multiplications are produced, and each of those three corresponds to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications. In other words, the district court accepted a one-to-many mapping in which a single multiplication in the accused device could satisfy multiple distinct multiplications recited in the claim.

The district court also rejected Appellants' argument that "S1, S2 and S3" must be unique signals, finding that argument waived because Appellants failed to raise it at the Rule 50(a) stage. The court concluded that using two signals plus a copy of one of those signals was sufficient to meet its construction of three "separately identifiable" signals. On the uniqueness issue specifically, the Federal Circuit agreed with the district court that Finesse had not surrendered claim scope over two-signal products during prosecution. The examiner had rejected claims reciting "digitally multiplying two or three signals" as obvious over the Filipovic reference, but Finesse overcame that rejection by arguing that Filipovic was limited to active IMPs, not passive IMPs. Although Finesse amended the claims to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven multiplications, the Federal Circuit found this amendment did not amount to a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of claim scope over products using two unique input signals. At most, the amendment required three separately identifiable signals without requiring any specific number of unique input signals.

However, the district court's acceptance of the one-to-many mapping for the seven multiplications was a separate and ultimately fatal problem. Even if three separately identifiable signals could be drawn from two unique inputs, the court's reasoning did not address the absence of evidence that all seven claimed multiplications are actually performed in the accused radios.

## Finesse's Flawed Mapping on Appeal

On appeal, Finesse attempted to supply the mapping that Dr. Wells never provided at trial. Finesse argued that the three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document could be mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications by assigning S1, S2, and S3 to various combinations of x1, x2, their complex conjugates, and their moduli. The Federal Circuit found this mapping "flawed." The problem is that Finesse's mapping requires S3 to be mapped simultaneously onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|. This contradicts the district court's claim construction, which required S1, S2, and S3 to be "separately identifiable" signals. A single signal S3 cannot simultaneously be x2, x2', and |x2|—these are different mathematical objects. The complex conjugate and modulus of a signal are transformations of that signal, not the signal itself, and Finesse's attempt to collapse them into a single claim term defies the construction the district court adopted.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit emphasized that Finesse's mapping evidence was never presented to the jury. Finesse offered its mapping argument for the first time in its appellate briefing, citing portions of the trial record and the Nokia document. But the jury never heard Dr. Wells explain how the three multiplications in the accused radios correspond to the seven claimed multiplications. Evidence presented for the first time on appeal cannot sustain a jury verdict, because the jury's finding must rest on the evidence presented at trial. The Federal Circuit noted that "Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury." This principle flows directly from the substantial evidence standard: the court asks whether a reasonable jury could have found infringement based on the evidence presented at trial, not based on arguments constructed post hoc by appellate counsel.

## The "Ten Multiplications" Argument at Oral Argument

At oral argument, Finesse advanced yet another theory. Finesse pointed to the Nokia document's reference to ten multiplications—labeled a0 through a9 in a polynomial p(n)—and argued that the accused radios perform not just seven multiplications but ten, thus necessarily encompassing the seven claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit found this argument equally unavailing. Nowhere did Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications. Nor did they provide a mapping from the ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse had previously argued (at trial and in briefing) covered the seven claimed multiplications. The argument thus raised more questions than it answered: if the accused radios perform ten multiplications, which seven are the claimed ones? How do those seven relate to the three that Dr. Wells identified at trial? Finesse offered no coherent explanation.

This argument illustrates a recurring problem in Finesse's case: the infringement theories multiplied, but none was ever presented coherently to the jury. The Federal Circuit observed that neither Finesse nor Dr. Wells "explain[ed] which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications" or "provide[d] a mapping from the ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications." Without such an explanation, the ten-multiplication reference in the Nokia document is simply an unexplained data point, not substantial evidence of infringement.

## Evidence Never Presented to the Jury Cannot Support the Verdict

The Federal Circuit's analysis underscores a fundamental principle of appellate review of jury verdicts: the sufficiency of the evidence is measured by what was presented at trial. Finesse's difficulties on appeal stemmed largely from its attempts to fill evidentiary gaps with arguments that the jury never heard. Whether characterized as a "clarification" of Dr. Wells' testimony or as a new theory based on ten multiplications, Finesse's appellate arguments could not cure the deficiencies in its trial proof. The court emphasized that Dr. Wells "did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications" and that Finesse's mapping was "never presented to the jury." The jury's verdict must stand or fall on the evidence before it, and the evidence before this jury did not establish that the accused radios perform all seven claimed multiplications.

## Reversal of JMOL Denial: No Substantial Evidence of Infringement

The Federal Circuit reviews a jury's finding of infringement for substantial evidence, asking whether "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." Applying this standard, the court held that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. The only evidence at trial—Dr. Wells' testimony and the Nokia document—showed three distinct multiplications involving modulus and complex conjugate operations, not the seven straightforward signal multiplications recited in the claim. Dr. Wells never explained the mapping from the three to the seven, and Finesse's post-hoc mapping attempts on appeal were both flawed and untimely.

Because the jury's infringement verdict for the '775 patent was not supported by substantial evidence, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the '775 patent. This ruling, combined with the court's reversal of JMOL denial for the '134 patent, meant that the entire damages award was vacated, as there was no underlying infringement to support it. The case illustrates the rigorous demands of the substantial evidence standard in patent infringement trials: an expert's testimony must clearly and coherently connect the accused device to each claim limitation, and gaps in trial proof cannot be repaired through appellate argument.

# Damages, Vacatur, and Final Disposition

## The Jury's Damages Award

After finding all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents valid and infringed, the jury awarded Finesse Wireless LLC $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages. This award covered the remaining life of both patents. A lump-sum damages award, as opposed to a running royalty, is a single payment that compensates the patentee for the accused infringer's use of the patented technology over a defined period. Here, the jury determined that AT&T's use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature in Nokia radios justified nearly $166.4 million in compensation for the entirety of the remaining patent terms.

The size and structure of this award became significant on appeal, but not for the reasons one might expect. The Federal Circuit did not assess whether the amount was reasonable or whether the lump-sum format was appropriate. Instead, the damages award's fate was tied entirely to the court's resolution of the infringement issue.

## Appellants' Post-Trial Motions on Damages

Following the verdict, Appellants moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on damages or, in the alternative, for a new trial on damages. These were distinct motions seeking different forms of relief. A JMOL motion challenges whether the evidence was legally sufficient to support the jury's verdict—here, whether the damages award was supported by adequate evidence. A motion for a new trial, by contrast, asks the court to retry the damages question, typically because the verdict was against the weight of the evidence, the damages were excessive, or some legal error tainted the proceedings. The alternative structure reflected Appellants' fallback strategy: if the court would not overturn the damages verdict entirely, it should at least order a fresh determination.

## The District Court's Denial of Both Damages Motions

The district court denied both the JMOL on damages and the motion for a new trial. With respect to JMOL, the court concluded that the evidence was sufficient to support the jury's damages award. With respect to the new trial motion, the court concluded that the verdict did not warrant disturbing under the applicable standard. These rulings left the $166 million lump-sum award intact and formed part of the basis for Appellants' appeal.

## The Federal Circuit's Standard of Review

The Federal Circuit applied regional circuit law—here, Fifth Circuit law—to its review of the denial of a new trial on damages. The Fifth Circuit reviews a denial of a new trial for abuse of discretion. This deferential standard means the appellate court will not overturn the district court's decision lightly; it must conclude that the district court exercised its discretion in a way that was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on an error of law. The Federal Circuit noted this standard at the outset of its damages discussion, signaling that it would evaluate the district court's ruling under a framework that affords substantial respect to the trial court's judgment.

As it happens, however, the court did not need to apply this standard to resolve the damages question.

## Vacatur of the Damages Award

The Federal Circuit's resolution of the damages issue was straightforward but consequential. Because the court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both the '134 and '775 patents, there was no longer any infringement liability to support a damages award. Damages in patent cases are a remedy that flows from a finding of infringement; they are not an independent cause of action. When the underlying infringement verdict cannot stand, the damages award built upon it must also fall.

The court accordingly vacated the damages award. Vacatur is distinct from reversal. To reverse a judgment means to overturn it, typically substituting the appellate court's judgment for that of the lower court. To vacate means to set aside or nullify the judgment, leaving the matter as if the vacated determination had never been made. Here, the court vacated rather than reversed because the proper disposition was not to substitute a different damages figure but to eliminate the damages award altogether, given that no infringement had occurred.

This outcome illustrates an important structural principle in patent litigation: the damages phase depends on the liability phase. The Federal Circuit did not evaluate whether the jury's $166 million award was excessive, whether the lump-sum format was appropriate, or whether the district court erred in denying the new trial motion. None of those questions mattered once the court determined that no reasonable jury could have found infringement. The damages award was a derivative remedy, and when the infringement finding was withdrawn, the damages award lost its foundation.

## Final Disposition

The court's final disposition was concise: "For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award." The court entered its judgment as "REVERSED AND VACATED" and awarded costs to Defendants-Appellants AT&T and Nokia.

The award of costs to the prevailing parties on appeal reflects the general principle that the successful appellant is entitled to recover the costs of the appeal from the losing party. This does not include attorney's fees, which are not at issue here, but covers items such as filing fees and other expenses associated with the appellate proceedings.

The disposition brought the case to a close at the appellate level, though the practical effect was to send a strong signal to the district court that the infringement claims could not proceed on the evidence presented. By reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement, the Federal Circuit effectively directed entry of judgment in favor of AT&T and Nokia on the infringement question, and by vacating the damages award, it eliminated the financial consequence of the jury's now-overturned verdict.
