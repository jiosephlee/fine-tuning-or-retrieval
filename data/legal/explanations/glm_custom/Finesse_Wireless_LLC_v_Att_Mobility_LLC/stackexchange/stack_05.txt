Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why was the argument that S1, S2, and S3 must be "unique" signals found waived, and what is the Rule 50(a) waiver requirement?
Question:
The district court rejected AT&T's argument that "S1, S2 and S3" must be unique signals as "waived" because they failed to raise it at the Rule 50(a) stage. I'm confused about the procedural mechanics here. What is the Rule 50(a) stage, and what exactly must a party do to preserve an issue for JMOL? Is there a difference between what must be argued in a Rule 50(a) motion before the case goes to the jury versus a Rule 50(b) motion after the verdict? If you don't raise an argument in 50(a), are you completely barred from raising it in 50(b) and on appeal, or are there exceptions?
Answer:
## Answer

The district court found AT&T's "unique signals" argument waived because AT&T did not raise that specific argument in its pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion, and Rule 50(b) only permits renewal of arguments already preserved at the Rule 50(a) stage.

### What Rule 50(a) Is and Why It Matters

Rule 50(a) governs motions for judgment as a matter of law made **before the case is submitted to the jury**—typically at the close of the opposing party's evidence and again at the close of all evidence. The movant argues that, based on the evidence presented at trial, no reasonable jury could find for the non-moving party on a particular claim or issue.

Rule 50(b), by contrast, is a **renewed** JMOL motion filed **after the jury verdict**. Critically, Rule 50(b) is not a new motion—it is a renewal of the earlier Rule 50(a) motion. This means a party may only raise in a Rule 50(b) motion the **specific grounds** that were raised in its Rule 50(a) motion. Arguments not raised at the Rule 50(a) stage are waived and cannot be raised in a Rule 50(b) motion or on appeal.

The rationale is straightforward: the Rule 50(a) motion gives the district court the opportunity to resolve the issue before the jury deliberates (potentially avoiding an unnecessary verdict and retrial), and it alerts the opposing party to the argument so they have a chance to cure any evidentiary deficiency before the case goes to the jury.

### What Happened Here

At the Rule 50(b) stage (post-verdict JMOL), AT&T argued that "S1, S2 and S3" must be **unique** signals, meaning the accused radios—which use only two unique signals (x1 and x2) plus a copy of one—cannot infringe. The district court found this argument was **not raised** in AT&T's Rule 50(a) motion and therefore was waived. The court then applied its earlier summary judgment construction—that "S1, S2 and S3" need only be "separately identifiable," not unique—and held that two signals plus a copy satisfied that construction. 

Because the argument was waived, the district court did not consider the merits of whether "S1, S2 and S3" must be unique. The waiver was procedural: AT&T failed to raise the specific argument at the required stage.

### Is There a Difference Between 50(a) and 50(b) Arguments?

Yes, but only in timing and posture—not in substance. In a Rule 50(a) motion, you ask the court to take the issue from the jury before deliberations. In a Rule 50(b) motion, you ask the court to overturn the verdict. But for preservation purposes, the **substantive arguments must be the same**. You cannot add new grounds for JMOL after the verdict that you did not raise before it.

### Are There Exceptions?

The opinion does not discuss exceptions to the waiver rule, and the Federal Circuit (applying Fifth Circuit law, as noted at the outset of the JMOL discussion) did not address the issue in detail because it ultimately reversed on other grounds. The general rule in the Fifth Circuit and most circuits is strict: failure to raise an argument in a Rule 50(a) motion waives it for Rule 50(b) and appeal purposes. Some circuits have recognized narrow exceptions—for example, where an argument is implicit in a Rule 50(a) motion, or where the argument could not have been made before the verdict—but these are limited and were not invoked here.

Notably, the Federal Circuit addressed AT&T's **related** prosecution history estoppel argument on the merits (disagreeing with AT&T that Finesse "surrendered claim scope over two signals" by amending claims during prosecution), and it independently agreed with AT&T that no reasonable jury could find the seven claimed multiplications were performed. That latter ground was sufficient to reverse the JMOL denial, making the waiver of the "unique signals" argument ultimately immaterial to the outcome on appeal.
