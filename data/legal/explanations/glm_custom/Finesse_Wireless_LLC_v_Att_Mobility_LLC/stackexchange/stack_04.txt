Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What does it mean for signals to be "separately identifiable" but not "unique" in patent claim construction?
Question:
The district court construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in the '775 patent to be "separately identifiable" but not "unique." The Federal Circuit seemed to accept this construction. I'm struggling with what this actually means in practice. If S1, S2, and S3 can be the same signal (not unique), in what sense are they "separately identifiable"? Can you give an example of signals that are separately identifiable but not unique? Is this saying you can use two distinct signals and a copy of one of them, as the district court found? What is the practical significance of this distinction for infringement analysis?
Answer:
## What "Separately Identifiable" but Not "Unique" Means

The distinction is about whether the claim requires three *distinct* signals, or merely three signals that can be pointed to individually even if some are identical copies of each other.

**"Not unique"** means S1, S2, and S3 do not have to be three different signals. S1 could equal S2, or S2 could equal S3, etc. The claim does not require three distinct input signals.

**"Separately identifiable"** means you must still be able to identify three signals in the accused device that correspond to S1, S2, and S3 — even if one is a copy of another. The signals must be distinguishable as separate inputs to the multiplication step, but they need not carry different information.

## The Concrete Example from the Opinion

The district court gave exactly the example you asked about: using two distinct signals plus a copy of one of them satisfies the "three separately identifiable signals" construction. As the court found, "using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals." J.A. 38. So if a device uses signals x1 and x2, and feeds a copy of x1 back in as a third input, you have three separately identifiable signals (x1, x2, copy of x1) even though only two are unique.

The Federal Circuit accepted this construction, holding that Finesse's amendment "requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals." (citing J.A. 968–69).

## The Practical Significance for Infringement

This construction matters because it determines whether a device using only two distinct input signals can infringe at all. If "three signals" meant three *unique* signals, AT&T could have argued non-infringement simply by showing the accused Nokia radios use only two inputs (x1 and x2). The "separately identifiable" construction closes off that argument — but it opened a different one.

The practical catch, and the reason the Federal Circuit ultimately reversed, is that even if you can meet the "three signals" limitation with two unique signals plus a copy, you still must perform all seven claimed multiplications. The claim requires seven specific third-order products: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. When only two unique signals are used, some of these seven products collapse into duplicates of each other (e.g., if S1 = S3, then S1×S1×S3 = S1×S1×S1 and S1×S3×S3 = S1×S1×S1, and S2×S2×S3 = S2×S2×S1, which is the same as S1×S2×S2). The district court found that three distinct multiplications could each "correspond to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications," and that was sufficient. J.A. 38–39.

The Federal Circuit rejected the infringement verdict on a different ground: Finesse's expert never clearly mapped the accused device's three multiplications onto the seven claimed multiplications, and the Nokia document showed operations involving modulus and complex conjugate operations — not the straightforward multiplications the claim recites. The court held "no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications."

So the distinction's practical significance is this: "separately identifiable but not unique" broadens the claim to cover devices with fewer than three distinct inputs, but it does not relieve the patentee of proving that all seven claimed multiplications are actually performed. The construction helps the patentee clear the threshold issue of signal count, but the patentee still bears the burden of showing each claimed multiplication occurs.
