Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why does the Federal Circuit apply Fifth Circuit law for JMOL review but its own law for claim construction?
Question:
In this opinion, the court says it reviews the denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit (Fifth Circuit), but then says it reviews claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings for clear error. I understand the Federal Circuit has nationwide jurisdiction over patent appeals, but I'm confused about how it decides which body of procedural law to apply. Why is JMOL review governed by regional circuit law while claim construction follows Federal Circuit precedent? Is there a principled basis for this split, or is it just historical accident?
Answer:
## The distinction turns on whether the issue is substantive patent law or procedural.

The Federal Circuit's split authority is not historical accident — it follows directly from Congress's decision to give the Federal Circuit exclusive jurisdiction over patent appeals for the purpose of achieving **national uniformity in patent law**, not to create a unified body of civil procedure.

### Substantive patent law → Federal Circuit law

Claim construction is a matter of substantive patent law. The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings for clear error under its own precedent. The opinion states this directly:

> "We review claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings supporting the district court's construction for clear error." *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016).

Because the meaning of patent claims is a question of patent law that affects patent rights nationwide, the Federal Circuit applies its own law to ensure that a patent claim means the same thing regardless of where the case is heard. This is the core reason the court exists.

### Procedural matters → Regional circuit law

JMOL standards, by contrast, are procedural. They govern how a district court evaluates the sufficiency of evidence at trial — a question of civil procedure, not patent law. The Federal Circuit applies the law of the regional circuit (here, the Fifth Circuit) because:

1. **No need for national uniformity.** JMOL standards are the same whether the case involves patents, contracts, or torts. There is no patent-specific reason to depart from regional circuit law.

2. **Respect for regional procedural expertise.** District courts operate within regional circuits and apply those circuits' procedural rules. The Federal Circuit borrows the regional circuit's standard to maintain consistency between how a district court applies JMOL and how that decision is reviewed on appeal.

The opinion applies this framework:

> "We review a district court's denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit." *Id.* (citing Fifth Circuit law for the de novo, "strongly and overwhelmingly" standard).

Notably, the court applies the same approach to new trial motions — also procedural — by reviewing under Fifth Circuit law for abuse of discretion.

### The principled basis

The Federal Circuit's mandate is to unify patent law, not procedure. When an issue is one that Congress intended to be uniform nationwide (claim construction, infringement, validity, damages methodology), the Federal Circuit applies its own law. When an issue is procedural and has no patent-specific dimension (JMOL sufficiency of evidence, new trial motions), the Federal Circuit defers to the regional circuit, which has general supervisory authority over the district courts within its territory.

Both propositions are stated in the same cited case — *Wi-LAN* — which itself reflects the Federal Circuit's settled understanding that its jurisdiction over patent cases does not extend to creating a freestanding body of procedural law for matters within the regional circuits' traditional domain.
