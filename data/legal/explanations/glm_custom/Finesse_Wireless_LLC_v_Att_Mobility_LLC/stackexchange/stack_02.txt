Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### When is expert testimony so self-contradictory that it cannot support a jury verdict?
Question:
The court reverses the JMOL denial on the '134 patent because Dr. Wells' testimony was "confusing and unclear" and he gave contradictory testimony about whether the "interference generating signals" mapped to the modeled PIM path or to x1 and x2. The court cites Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. for the proposition that self-contradictory expert testimony cannot support a jury verdict. But I thought credibility determinations and weighing conflicting testimony were jury functions. At what point does contradictory expert testimony cross the line from a credibility issue for the jury to insufficient evidence as a matter of law? Is there a specific test, or is it just case-by-case judgment?
Answer:
## When Self-Contradictory Expert Testimony Fails as a Matter of Law

Your instinct is right that credibility determinations are generally for the jury, and courts are reluctant to override that role. But there is a meaningful boundary, and the Federal Circuit's opinion here illustrates where it falls.

### The Governing Standard

The key distinction is between **conflicting or impeached testimony**—which remains a jury question—and **self-contradictory testimony on a dispositive issue from the party bearing the burden of proof, where the expert never reconciles the contradiction and no other evidence fills the gap**. The latter is insufficient as a matter of law.

The court articulates this principle through *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008): "When the party with the burden of proof . . . rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard." The court applies this under the substantial evidence framework—"if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." The question becomes whether a reasonable jury could actually find infringement when the only evidence on a claim limitation is internally inconsistent and unresolved.

### Why Dr. Wells' Testimony Crossed the Line

The problem was not simply that Dr. Wells was confused or impeached on a peripheral point. The contradiction went to the heart of limitation 1[a] of the '134 patent—whether the accused radios sample a passband containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." This was the element Finesse had to prove, and Dr. Wells was their only source of evidence on it.

The contradiction had a specific structure:

1. **Direct testimony mapped "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path.** Dr. Wells said so repeatedly—in his expert report, on direct, and under cross-examination. The court notes this was "consistent and repeated."

2. **Physical reality made that mapping impossible.** The modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling occurs. As the court explains, "the modeled PIM path cannot be the claimed 'interference generating signals'" because "signals cannot be sampled before they are generated."

3. **When confronted, the expert pivoted but never resolved the contradiction.** Dr. Wells mentioned x1 and x2 as "the two inputs" on the red path, but "did not testify that x1 and x2 map onto the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals' and instead continued to testify 'the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.'" He "repeatedly testified the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path correspond to the claimed signals, and offered no clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony."

4. **Finesse's attempted reconstruction was the court's, not the expert's.** Finesse tried to stitch together testimony from pages ninety pages apart in the record to argue Dr. Wells really meant x1 and x2 all along. The court rejected this: "There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony. Dr. Wells never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals.'"

This is what distinguishes the case from an ordinary credibility question. The jury was not choosing between two coherent accounts; it was asked to construct a theory of infringement that the expert himself never articulated.

### The Line, Stated as a Principle

Synthesizing from the opinion, the boundary seems to turn on several factors working together:

- **The contradiction concerns a claim limitation the burden-bearing party must prove**, not a collateral or cumulative issue.
- **The expert's testimony is the only evidence** supporting that limitation—there is no independent basis for the jury to find infringement.
- **The contradiction is fundamental**, not merely a matter of emphasis or degree—e.g., the expert's original mapping is physically impossible given the operation of the accused device.
- **The expert fails to reconcile or clarify** the contradiction when given the opportunity, leaving the jury with no coherent infringement theory to credit.
- **The party's attempt to salvage the testimony requires the factfinder to construct a theory the expert never actually articulated**, piecing together fragments of testimony.

When all of these are present, the court treats the testimony as failing the substantial evidence standard rather than presenting a credibility question. The jury cannot perform its factfinding function when the evidence it is asked to credit does not coherently state what the party must prove.

### What Would Have Kept This in the Jury's Hands

The opinion suggests, by contrast, that had Dr. Wells clearly testified x1 and x2 correspond to "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," and offered an explanation for his earlier references to the modeled PIM path, the jury could have credited that testimony over the conflicting statements. The issue was not that he gave contradictory answers—it was that **he never gave the jury a coherent, reconciled account to credit in the first place**. The court's phrase captures it: "This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict."

### Is There a Bright-Line Test?

There is no formal multi-factor test announced in this opinion. The court frames it as a case-specific application of the substantial evidence standard, guided by *Johns Hopkins*. But the pattern described above gives you a workable framework for identifying when contradictory expert testimony becomes legally insufficient rather than merely impeached.
