Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # The '134 Patent: Infringement Theory and the Insufficiency of Self-Contradictory Expert Testimony

## The '134 Patent Claims: Structure and Key Limitations

The '134 patent claims a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. As the court explains, the patent's approach involves isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals" and then canceling out the interference generating signals using a computed estimate of the IMPs. Claim 1, which the court treats as representative, recites six steps: (a) over-sampling a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, where the received signals include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" capable of generating IMPs in-band of the signals of interest; (b) isolating the signals of interest in the bit stream using decimating filters; (c) isolating the source signals that generate the IMPs using decimating filters; (d) computing an estimate of each IMP from the source signals; (e) canceling out the in-band IMPs using those estimates; and (f) performing phase and amplitude adjustment on the estimated IMP interfering signals in a closed-loop manner, including performing sub-sample phase shifts.

Understanding the patent's claimed method requires grasping the basic problem it addresses. Radios avoid signal interference by operating on different frequencies, but their transmit signals can combine when encountering passive obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors—to form new frequencies called intermodulation products. IMPs caused by passive obstacles are called "passive intermodulation products" (PIM). The '134 patent's method seeks to mitigate this PIM interference by sampling the relevant signals, isolating them, computing IMP estimates, and using those estimates to cancel the interference.

## Key Limitation 1[a]: The Sampling Requirement

The central dispute on appeal concerns limitation 1[a], which requires "over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals." This limitation imposes a specific structural requirement: the receiver must sample a passband of signals that includes both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals." As the party alleging infringement, Finesse bore the burden of proving that the accused Nokia radios meet this limitation—that is, that their receiver samples a passband of received signals containing both categories of signals.

This burden matters because patent infringement is a question of fact that the patentee must prove, and when the patentee relies on expert testimony to establish that an accused product meets a claim limitation, that testimony must provide sufficient evidence for a reasonable jury to find infringement. If the expert's testimony is internally inconsistent or fails to map the accused product onto the claimed limitations, the testimony may be legally insufficient to support a verdict.

## Dr. Wells' Initial Infringement Theory

At trial, Finesse's infringement case depended on the testimony of its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringe the asserted claims of the '134 patent, relying on a Nokia technical document that depicts how the accused radios operate. According to Dr. Wells, the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) in the accused radios is a receiver that samples the claimed "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" to create a bit stream. Both categories of signals, according to Dr. Wells, appear on what the Nokia document labels as the "red path" entering the RF ADC.

Dr. Wells was consistent and specific in mapping the claimed terms onto the Nokia document. He testified that the "signals of interest" correspond to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference signal and that the "interference generating signals" correspond to the modeled PIM path. The Nokia document's legend confirms that the red path represents the "DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path." Dr. Wells' expert report similarly stated that the "DL (TX) reference" signal meets the agreed construction of "signals of interest" and that the "modeled PIM signal" is the "interference generating signal." This mapping was the cornerstone of Finesse's infringement theory: the RF ADC samples both the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, thereby satisfying limitation 1[a]'s requirement that the receiver over-sample a passband including both categories of signals.

## The Fundamental Flaw: Signal Generation Timing

On cross-examination, Appellants' counsel exposed a critical problem with Dr. Wells' theory. The modeled PIM path—the signal Dr. Wells identified as the "interference generating signals"—is generated downstream of the RF ADC, inside a component called the GROOT FPGA. This means the modeled PIM path is created after the RF ADC has already performed its sampling. The logical implication is devastating to Finesse's theory: the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because that signal does not exist at the time sampling occurs. A receiver cannot sample a signal that has not yet been generated.

This timing problem directly undermines limitation 1[a]. That limitation requires the receiver to over-sample a passband of received signals that includes both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." If the "interference generating signals" (as Dr. Wells defined them—the modeled PIM path) are not generated until after sampling, then the receiver cannot possibly sample a passband that includes them. The accused radios simply cannot meet the claim limitation under Dr. Wells' own mapping.

The district court acknowledged this problem at the JMOL stage. It recognized that "the modeled PIM path is the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the 'interference generating signals'" and that "Dr. Wells also testified that the PIM Model is generated in the GROOT FPGA." The court further acknowledged that if the modeled PIM path is the claimed "interference generating signals," then limitation 1[a] cannot be met. This concession should have been the end of the matter, but the court nevertheless found sufficient evidence for the jury to find infringement.

## Dr. Wells' Contradictory Pivot

Confronted with the timing problem on cross-examination, Dr. Wells attempted to shift his theory. When counsel pointed out that the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because it is generated downstream, Dr. Wells pivoted, testifying that "there's two signals on that red path because we know that because there's the x1 and the x2." These references to x1 and x2—signals appearing in the Nokia document—represented a new theory: perhaps x1 and x2, rather than the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, are the two signals the RF ADC samples.

But Dr. Wells never completed this pivot. He did not testify that x1 maps onto the "signals of interest" or that x2 maps onto the "interference generating signals," or vice versa. He did not explain how x1 and x2 correspond to the claimed categories of signals. Instead, he continued to testify, repeatedly and consistently, that the two signals on the red path are the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. When asked directly whether x1 is the modeled PIM path, Dr. Wells responded only that "x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path"—a statement that does not map x1 or x2 onto either claimed signal category.

The result was a muddle. Dr. Wells had one clear theory (DL(TX) reference = signals of interest; modeled PIM path = interference generating signals) that was logically impossible given the signal generation timing, and a vague, incomplete alternative reference to x1 and x2 that never connected to the claim language. His testimony oscillated between these two positions without resolution, leaving the jury without a coherent infringement theory.

## The District Court's Erroneous Finding

Despite the confusion in Dr. Wells' testimony, the district court found that "Dr. Wells testified that there are two signals entering the RF ADC, and that those are signals of interest and the interference generating signals. Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2." The Federal Circuit rejected this finding as unsupported by the record. The court noted that "there is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony." Dr. Wells never testified that x1 and x2 correspond to the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The district court's finding required it to infer a mapping that Dr. Wells himself never articulated—a mapping that was, at best, ambiguous and, at worst, directly contradicted by his repeated testimony that the two signals on the red path are the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path.

This is a significant point for understanding the limits of a court's role in evaluating expert testimony at the JMOL stage. While a court reviewing a jury verdict must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, it cannot manufacture testimony that the expert never gave. The court may draw reasonable inferences from the evidence, but it cannot bridge gaps in an expert's testimony by supplying the missing elements of his infringement theory.

## Finesse's Appeal Argument and the Federal Circuit's Rejection

On appeal, Finesse attempted to rehabilitate Dr. Wells' testimony by arguing that he was "laboring under the misimpression that x1 and x2 correspond to DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path." According to Finesse, when Dr. Wells referred to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path as the two signals on the red path, he was really referring to x1 and x2 all along, because he misunderstood the relationship between the labels in the Nokia document's legend and the signals x1 and x2.

The Federal Circuit found this argument unconvincing for several reasons. First, Dr. Wells' testimony was "confusing and unclear even after he was made aware of his alleged misimpression." He continued to testify that the modeled PIM path is the "interference generating signals" and never clarified that he meant x1 and x2 when he said DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path. Second, Finesse's argument required piecing together fragments of Dr. Wells' testimony from pages ninety pages apart in the record—one statement that "x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path" and another that "this red path, it includes the signals of interest, it includes the interference generating signals." The court declined to construct an infringement theory by assembling disparate snippets of testimony that were never connected by the expert himself. Third, even if Dr. Wells had testified that x1 and x2 are the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," he repeatedly and contradictorily testified that the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path correspond to the claimed signals, and offered no clear explanation for the contradiction.

## Application of Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.

The Federal Circuit's decision rests on an important principle of patent litigation evidence: when the party bearing the burden of proof relies on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, the court may conclude that the evidence is legally insufficient to support a jury verdict. The court drew directly from its precedent in Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp., 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008). In that case, the plaintiff's expert opined that the accused device contacted a vessel in "three dimensions," but on cross-examination admitted that the device only contacts the vessel in "two places." The court held that "no reasonable jury could have found that the device literally met this limitation based on [the expert's] opinion, given his contradictory testimony."

The principle has particular force in patent infringement cases, where the patentee bears the burden of proving that the accused product meets every limitation of the asserted claims. Expert testimony is often the primary means of establishing this, particularly when the accused technology is complex. But expert testimony is not a blank check. When an expert's testimony is internally contradictory—when it offers one infringement theory that is logically impossible and then vaguely gestures toward an alternative without completing the necessary analysis—the testimony cannot support a verdict. The jury cannot be expected to resolve contradictions in the expert's own testimony and then construct a coherent infringement theory from the pieces.

This principle also reflects the broader standard of review. The Federal Circuit reviews a jury's infringement finding for substantial evidence, meaning evidence "if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." The question is not whether any evidence supports the verdict, but whether a reasonable jury could reach the verdict based on the evidence. Self-contradictory expert testimony fails this standard because it does not provide the jury with a reliable basis for finding infringement. The jury would have to guess which version of the expert's testimony to believe and then fill in the gaps the expert left unexplained.

## Reversal of JMOL Denial: No Substantial Evidence of Infringement

Applying these principles, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the '134 patent. The court held that the jury's infringement verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. Dr. Wells' initial theory—that the RF ADC samples both the DL(TX) reference (signals of interest) and the modeled PIM path (interference generating signals)—was logically impossible because the modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling occurs. His attempted pivot to x1 and x2 was incomplete and contradictory, as he never mapped those signals onto the claimed categories and continued to testify that the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path were the relevant signals. Under Johns Hopkins, a party bearing the burden of proof cannot sustain a verdict on such self-contradictory testimony.

The reversal illustrates the critical importance of a coherent, internally consistent expert theory in patent infringement litigation. The patentee's expert must do more than identify components of the accused product that seem related to claim limitations; he must clearly and specifically map each claim limitation onto the accused product in a manner that is logically consistent. When an expert's testimony contains a fundamental flaw—such as claiming that a receiver samples a signal that does not exist at the time of sampling—and the expert's attempts to correct that flaw only produce further contradictions, the testimony is insufficient as a matter of law. The district court's denial of JMOL was therefore error, and the Federal Circuit reversed.
