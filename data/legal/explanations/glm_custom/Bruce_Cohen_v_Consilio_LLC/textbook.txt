Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The Parties and the Employment Relationship

This case arises from an employment dispute between Bruce Cohen and his employer, Consilio, LLC. Consilio is a nationwide legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. Cohen began working for Consilio in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. The term "hourly" is significant: it means Cohen was not exempt from overtime protections and was entitled to be compensated for each hour worked, including any overtime hours, under applicable wage laws. As a "licensed attorney document reviewer," Cohen performed document review work—a common practice in legal proceedings where attorneys examine large volumes of documents for relevance, privilege, and other issues. The nature of Cohen's work and his hourly compensation structure form the backdrop for the wage law claims at the center of this case.

## Consilio's July 2019 Overtime Policy Change

In July 2019, Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers announcing a change to its overtime compensation practices. Effective August 2019, Consilio instituted what it described as a "new premium overtime policy." Despite the label "premium overtime," the policy actually eliminated overtime premium pay. Instead, Cohen and his fellow employees would be paid "at [their] base pay rate" for all hours worked, meaning they would receive the same hourly rate regardless of whether they worked standard or overtime hours. This policy change is the factual foundation of Cohen's claims. Under Minnesota wage law, the question of whether an employer may prospectively eliminate overtime premium pay for hourly employees—and what notice and remedies are required when doing so—became the central legal dispute.

## Cohen's August 2020 Lawsuit

In August 2020, Cohen sued Consilio on behalf of himself and a "putative class" of similarly situated employees. A putative class action is a lawsuit in which the plaintiff purports to represent a group of similarly affected individuals, but the court has not yet certified the class under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23. Cohen alleged that Consilio's actions violated three Minnesota statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101 and 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, and 177.27. Each of these statutes governs different aspects of wage payment and employer obligations under Minnesota law. The MPWA addresses the timing and payment of wages; the MFLSA is Minnesota's counterpart to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act and governs overtime pay requirements; and the MWTA addresses wage theft, including employer notice obligations regarding changes to compensation.

## Remedies Sought

Cohen sought four categories of relief: overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Overtime pay represents the additional compensation Cohen alleged he and the putative class were owed for hours worked beyond the standard workweek. Liquidated damages are a fixed monetary award designed to compensate for losses that are difficult to quantify and, in many wage statutes, serve a compensatory and sometimes punitive function. Statutory penalties are specific monetary penalties set by statute for violations, which may be payable to the employee or to a government official depending on the statutory scheme. Injunctive relief is an equitable remedy in which a court orders a party to do or refrain from doing a particular act—in this case, Cohen sought an order compelling Consilio to rescind its unlawful overtime policy. The distinction among these remedies becomes important because Consilio ultimately satisfied some but not all of them, and the remaining disputes centered on penalties and injunctive relief.

## Consilio's Payment and the Parties' Stipulations

After Cohen filed suit, Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers a total of $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under Cohen's MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. Following these payments, the parties entered into a stipulation—a formal agreement filed with the court—in which they acknowledged that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit" and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." This stipulation effectively resolved the overtime wage and liquidated damages components of Cohen's MFLSA claim. What remained in dispute was whether Cohen could obtain statutory penalties under the three statutes and injunctive relief.

## The Dispute Over Penalties

With the overtime and liquidated damages claims satisfied, the central dispute narrowed to penalties. Cohen argued he was entitled to $172,080 in "average daily wage penalties" under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio countered that the applicable statutes did not authorize Cohen to obtain penalties. This dispute over penalties is the primary issue before the court on appeal. The question of who may seek penalties under each statute—and whether penalties are available to private plaintiffs at all—requires careful statutory interpretation, which the Eighth Circuit addresses in the substantive sections of the opinion.

## The Parties' Agreement to Resolve Through Summary Judgment

Rather than continue with discovery and litigation on the remaining penalty and injunctive relief disputes, the parties agreed that the most efficient path forward would be for the district court to hear and decide Consilio's summary judgment motion on all three statutory claims before any further proceedings. The parties acknowledged that if the district court granted Consilio's summary judgment motion in full, no claims would remain and the case would be fully resolved. This procedural strategy effectively condensed the remaining issues into a single legal question suitable for resolution by the court, since the parties had stipulated to the key facts regarding payment and the only open questions were legal ones about the availability of penalties under the respective statutes.

## The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment and Cohen's Appeal

The district court granted Consilio's motion for summary judgment on all three claims—MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA. Summary judgment is a procedural mechanism under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56 by which a court resolves a case when there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Because the parties had stipulated to the relevant facts, the district court's role was to determine the legal questions: whether the statutes at issue permitted Cohen to obtain the penalties he sought. The district court concluded they did not, and dismissed all claims. Cohen then appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, challenging the district court's rulings on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims, as well as his claim for injunctive relief.

## Standard of Review on Appeal

On appeal, the Eighth Circuit reviews a district court's grant of summary judgment de novo. De novo review means the appellate court examines the legal question anew, giving no deference to the district court's conclusions. As the court explained, it views "the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn from the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party"—here, Cohen, since Consilio was the moving party who sought summary judgment. Under this standard, summary judgment is proper if there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Because the parties had stipulated to the key facts, the appeal turned entirely on questions of statutory interpretation—legal questions that the Eighth Circuit would review fresh, without deferring to the district court's reasoning.

# The MPWA Claim: Statutory Interpretation and the Commissioner's Exclusive Role in Seeking Penalties

## The Statutory Framework: § 181.171 as the Enforcement Mechanism

Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) required the Eighth Circuit to engage in a focused exercise in statutory interpretation. The MPWA is a collection of Minnesota statutes governing the payment of wages, and Cohen's claim centered on two specific provisions: § 181.171 and § 181.101. Section 181.171 serves as the enforcement mechanism for certain provisions of the MPWA. It provides that "[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations" of specified MPWA provisions, including § 181.101. When an employer "is found to have violated" such a provision, it is "liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." Critically for this case, § 181.171 also states that an employer who is found to have violated one of the enumerated sections "is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated." The question before the court was whether § 181.101, the specific provision Cohen alleged Consilio violated, actually "provides for" civil penalties that an aggrieved employee may recover — or whether the authority to seek penalties belongs exclusively to the Commissioner of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry.

## Cohen's Argument: Plain Language of § 181.101

Cohen argued that the "plain language and unambiguous meaning" of § 181.101 authorized employees to seek what he called "average daily wage penalties." Under this theory, § 181.171's reference to "civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated" would incorporate whatever penalties § 181.101 makes available, and those penalties would be recoverable by the aggrieved employee who brings the civil action. This was an attractive reading for Cohen because it would mean he, as a private plaintiff, could pursue penalties directly against Consilio — penalties he valued at $172,080. The court, however, concluded that a careful reading of § 181.101 did not support this interpretation.

## The Court's Plain Language Analysis of § 181.101(a)

The court began its analysis with the text of § 181.101(a), applying the well-established Minnesota principle that "[w]hen the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect." The court observed that § 181.101(a) references penalties only in connection with the Commissioner — not in connection with employees. This was the decisive textual feature of the statute.

The court identified several structural elements of § 181.101(a) that tied penalties exclusively to the Commissioner's authority. First, penalties become available only if the Commissioner first "serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." This is a procedural prerequisite that only the Commissioner can initiate. Second, only after that demand has been served and payment is not made within ten days may "the commissioner" — and the statute uses that specific term — "charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." The statute thus places the power to assess and collect penalties squarely with the Commissioner. Third, the statute directs that any "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned." The court emphasized this language: it is the Commissioner who collects, and the collected funds then flow to the employee. The employee does not independently seek or recover penalties under this provision.

## The Employee's Reserved Right: Prosecuting a Claim for Wages, Not Penalties

The court acknowledged that § 181.101(a) does preserve certain rights for employees. The statute provides that "[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages" and that the section "provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times." But the court read these provisions as reserving to employees the right to pursue wages themselves — not penalties. The statute's references to penalties, by contrast, are consistently and exclusively tied to the Commissioner's actions. The employee's role under § 181.101(a) is to benefit from penalties the Commissioner collects, but the employee has no statutory authority to seek those penalties directly.

## The Court's Comparison to Other Minnesota Statutes

To reinforce its reading of § 181.101, the court turned to a comparison with other Minnesota wage statutes — a common and powerful tool in statutory interpretation. The court examined § 181.13(a), which governs discharged employees, and § 181.14, subdivision 2, which governs employees who quit or resign. In both of those statutes, the Minnesota legislature expressly empowered the aggrieved employee to collect penalties. Section 181.13(a), for example, provides that upon a discharged employee's demand, the employee may recover unpaid wages and "may charge and collect a penalty." Section 181.14, subdivision 2, contains similar language for employees who resign. In these provisions, the legislature used explicit language granting the employee the authority to seek penalties directly.

The contrast with § 181.101 was stark. Where §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14 expressly authorize employees to "charge and collect a penalty," § 181.101(a) contains no such grant. Instead, § 181.101(a) consistently assigns the penalty function to the Commissioner. The court drew the inference that this was a deliberate legislative choice.

## The "Knows How" Principle of Legislative Inference

The court's comparison to §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14 rested on a recognized principle of statutory interpretation sometimes called the "knows how" doctrine. As the Minnesota Supreme Court explained in *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022), "the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so." The logic is straightforward: if the legislature has demonstrated in other statutes that it knows how to grant employees the power to seek penalties directly, its failure to do so in a particular statute is presumed to be intentional. The legislature could have written § 181.101(a) to give employees the right to "charge and collect a penalty," just as it did in §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14. It did not. The court therefore concluded that the legislature chose not to empower employees with that authority under § 181.101.

## The Court's Holding and Affirmance of Summary Judgment

Based on this analysis, the court held that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101. Because Cohen, as a private employee, lacked statutory authority to pursue these penalties, his MPWA claim for penalties could not succeed as a matter of law. The district court's grant of summary judgment to Consilio on this claim was therefore proper. The court's reasoning illustrates a methodical approach to statutory interpretation: it began with the plain language of the statute at issue, examined how that language assigned roles and responsibilities, compared the statute to related provisions where the legislature had acted differently, and drew a reasoned inference about legislative intent. The result was a conclusion that the MPWA's penalty provision under § 181.101 is enforced through the Commissioner, not through private civil actions by aggrieved employees.

# The MFLSA Claim: Standing, Mootness, and the Jurisdictional Question of Civil Penalties Payable to the Government

## The Stipulation That Narrowed the MFLSA Claim

Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) began as a straightforward overtime case. He alleged that Consilio failed to pay overtime wages owed to him and similarly situated employees, and he sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Before the district court ruled on summary judgment, however, Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded pursuant to Cohen's MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages.

The parties then stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit" and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." This stipulation eliminated the core of Cohen's MFLSA claim—the unpaid overtime and the corresponding liquidated damages. What remained was Cohen's demand for statutory penalties: he argued he was entitled to $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA. Critically, the parties agreed that any statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen personally.

This procedural posture set the stage for a jurisdictional question that neither party anticipated: whether a plaintiff who has been fully compensated for his personal losses may still pursue a claim for civil penalties that would be paid to the government rather than to himself.

## The Court's Sua Sponte Jurisdictional Concern

The Eighth Circuit raised its concern about jurisdiction on its own initiative, noting that "[a]t this stage of the proceedings, we have a serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue." This is a significant procedural move. Federal courts are generally passive institutions; they decide the questions presented by the parties. But jurisdiction is different. A court's subject-matter jurisdiction—its power to hear a case at all—may be raised at any time, by any party, or by the court sua sponte (on its own motion). The court's concern arose because Cohen had already received everything he personally claimed he was owed, and the only remaining relief he sought—statutory penalties—would not flow to him but to the Commissioner.

The jurisdictional question thus had two dimensions, both rooted in Article III of the United States Constitution, which limits federal judicial power to actual "Cases" and "Controversies." The first dimension is standing: whether Cohen had a sufficiently concrete and personal stake in the litigation when it began. The second is mootness: whether events occurring after the lawsuit was filed—namely, Consilio's payment of all overtime and liquidated damages—had so diminished Cohen's stake that the case was no longer a live controversy. The court addressed both, ultimately concluding that it could not resolve the jurisdictional question on the existing record and that remand was necessary.

## Standing: A Plaintiff Must Demonstrate It for Each Claim and Each Form of Relief

The court began its jurisdictional analysis with the fundamental principle that "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought." This requirement comes from the Supreme Court's decision in *Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017), which itself drew on *Davis v. Federal Election Commission*, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008). The rule is demanding: it is not enough for a plaintiff to have standing for one claim or one remedy. If a plaintiff seeks multiple forms of relief, he must independently satisfy the standing requirements for each.

For a plaintiff to establish Article III standing, he must generally show three things: (1) that he has suffered an "injury in fact"—a concrete and particularized harm that is actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical; (2) that the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct; and (3) that a favorable court decision is likely to redress the injury. In Cohen's case, the overtime wages and liquidated damages claims clearly satisfied these requirements at the outset: Cohen alleged he was not paid overtime he was owed, that Consilio caused the nonpayment, and that a court order requiring payment would remedy the harm. But the penalty claim presented a different question. If the penalties are payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen, what is Cohen's personal injury, and how does an order directing payment to the Commissioner redress that injury?

The court did not definitively answer these questions. Instead, it flagged them as serious and turned to the related doctrine of mootness to explain why the answer might depend on facts not yet developed.

## The Relationship Between Standing and Mootness

The court drew on its own precedent to explain the connection between standing and mootness, quoting *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021): "[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." This formulation captures an essential idea: standing and mootness are not entirely separate doctrines but rather two points on a single continuum. Standing asks whether the plaintiff had a sufficient personal stake when the lawsuit was filed. Mootness asks whether that stake still exists at the time the court decides the case. If events have intervened to eliminate the plaintiff's personal interest, the case becomes moot, and the court loses jurisdiction.

The two doctrines thus operate as bookends on the life of a lawsuit. Standing is assessed at the outset; mootness is assessed throughout. The court's analysis of Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim sat at the intersection of these doctrines. Even if Cohen had standing to seek penalties when he filed suit, the question was whether Consilio's payment of all personal compensation—combined with the fact that penalties would go to the Commissioner—had so eroded Cohen's personal stake that the claim was now moot.

## Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw: Civil Penalties as Redress for Citizen Plaintiffs

The court's most important analytical tool came from the Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000). In *Laidlaw*, environmental plaintiffs sued a polluter under the Clean Water Act, which authorized civil penalties payable to the United States Treasury. The defendant argued that because the penalties would be paid to the government rather than to the plaintiffs, the plaintiffs lacked standing: how could a payment to the Treasury redress the plaintiffs' injuries? The Supreme Court rejected this argument. It held that civil penalties, "even if they are paid to the government," may "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because such penalties may "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." In other words, the redress came not from the money itself but from the behavioral change the penalty was designed to produce. If the defendant stopped polluting because of the threat of penalties, the plaintiffs' injury—the ongoing harm from the pollution—would be remedied.

The Eighth Circuit cited *Laidlaw* for the proposition that Cohen's claim for MFLSA penalties might still be viable—if Consilio's violative overtime policy remained in place. The logic would be parallel: even though Cohen would not personally receive the penalty money, the penalty might deter Consilio from continuing its unlawful overtime policy, thereby redressing Cohen's ongoing injury from that policy. But the court immediately noted the limiting principle from *Laidlaw*: the claim "might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." This passage from *Laidlaw* itself quoted *United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Export Association*, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968), and sets a high bar: the defendant must show that the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur, not merely that it has not recurred.

## Mootness and the Voluntary Cessation Doctrine

The court then introduced a critical doctrinal qualification: "[i]t is well settled that 'a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice.'" This principle, drawn from *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982), is known as the voluntary cessation doctrine. It addresses a common scenario: a defendant, facing litigation, voluntarily stops the conduct the plaintiff has challenged. The defendant then argues the case is moot because the conduct has ceased. The voluntary cessation doctrine prevents defendants from unilaterally stripping federal courts of jurisdiction by simply stopping the challenged behavior, only to resume it after the lawsuit is dismissed.

The doctrine reflects a concern about manipulation of the judicial process. If a defendant could moot a case simply by ceasing the challenged conduct, the defendant could engage in the conduct, wait for a lawsuit, stop temporarily, have the case dismissed, and then resume. To prevent this, courts place the burden on the defendant to show that the conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The standard is demanding—"absolutely clear"—reflecting the court's skepticism toward claims of voluntary compliance.

In Cohen's case, the voluntary cessation doctrine was directly relevant. Consilio had already changed its overtime policy once—in August 2019, it eliminated overtime premium pay. The question on remand would be whether the policy that formed the basis of Cohen's claim was still in place. If Consilio had abandoned the policy, the court would need to determine whether it was "absolutely clear" that Consilio would not reinstate it. If the policy remained, the claim for penalties might still be viable under the reasoning of *Laidlaw*.

## The Disputed Factual Question

The court identified a genuine factual dispute that precluded it from resolving the jurisdictional question: "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute." This was the pivotal fact. If the policy remained in effect, Cohen's claim for civil penalties could still be viable because the penalties might deter continued violations, affording Cohen redress for an ongoing injury under *Laidlaw*. If the policy was no longer in place and it was "absolutely clear" that Consilio's wrongful conduct would not recur, the MFLSA penalty claim might be moot, because there would be no ongoing or threatened injury for the penalties to redress.

The court's inability to resolve this factual question on the existing record was central to its disposition. The court noted that "federal courts are not 'license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest.'" The quotation from *Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. at 192, underscores the point: jurisdiction is not a matter of judicial discretion but of constitutional necessity. If the parties lack a continuing stake in the outcome, the court must dismiss, regardless of whether it would be efficient or desirable to decide the merits.

## Vacatur and Remand for a Jurisdictional Determination

Given the unresolved factual question and the serious jurisdictional concern, the court concluded that it could not decide the MFLSA penalty issue. Instead, it vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment as to Cohen's MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction. This disposition is noteworthy for several reasons.

First, the court did not affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim. It did not hold that Cohen lacked standing or that his claim was moot. It simply held that it could not resolve the jurisdictional question on the record before it and that the district court should address the issue in the first instance.

Second, by vacating rather than affirming, the court restored the MFLSA claim to its pre-summary-judgment posture. This means that on remand, the district court may need to take evidence on whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place—a factual inquiry that could involve further discovery, hearings, or other proceedings.

Third, the court's disposition reflects the hierarchical relationship between jurisdiction and the merits. A court must satisfy itself of its jurisdiction before reaching the merits of a claim. Here, the court determined that it could not properly evaluate its jurisdiction without further factual development, and it declined to decide the merits in the absence of that threshold determination.

## Judge Kobes's Special Concurrence

Judge Kobes concurred specially, expressing a distinct perspective on the jurisdictional question. His concurrence is brief but raises important points.

First, Judge Kobes stated that he was "not convinced that Cohen has standing to seek statutory penalties under *Friends of the Earth*." He acknowledged that "[m]aybe he does," but noted "there are differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." This is a significant observation. In *Laidlaw*, the civil penalties were authorized under a federal environmental statute (the Clean Water Act) and were payable to the United States Treasury. The MFLSA penalties, by contrast, are authorized under a Minnesota state statute and are payable to a state Commissioner. Judge Kobes did not enumerate the specific differences he found relevant, and he noted that "[n]either party briefed the issue," but his concurrence signals that the *Laidlaw* framework may not map neatly onto every statutory penalty scheme. The differences between federal citizen-suit penalties and state labor-law penalties may matter for the standing analysis, and the district court on remand may need to address this question.

Second, Judge Kobes agreed that "Cohen's claim for MFLSA statutory penalties might be moot," and therefore concurred with vacating the judgment and remanding the case. His agreement with the majority's disposition reflects a shared view that the jurisdictional question cannot be resolved on the current record.

Third, Judge Kobes offered a practical recommendation: "On remand, the district court may decide the mootness question first." This suggestion reflects a strategic choice about the sequence in which jurisdictional issues should be addressed. Because both standing and mootness present jurisdictional barriers, the district court could choose to address either one first. Judge Kobes's recommendation to begin with mootness is pragmatic: if the claim is moot, the court lacks jurisdiction regardless of whether Cohen had standing, and there is no need to resolve the more difficult and potentially more far-reaching standing question. Mootness, in this context, may turn on a relatively straightforward factual inquiry—whether Consilio's policy is still in place—whereas the standing question may require the court to wrestle with the applicability of *Laidlaw* to state-law penalty provisions, a question neither party has briefed.

## The Broader Significance of the Court's Disposition

The court's treatment of the MFLSA claim illustrates several important principles of federal jurisdiction that law students should understand. First, jurisdiction is not a technicality to be glossed over; it is a constitutional threshold that federal courts must cross before reaching the merits. The court's willingness to raise the issue sua sponte, despite neither party having identified it, reflects the principle that jurisdiction is a duty of the court, not a right of the parties.

Second, the relationship between standing and mootness is dynamic, not static. A plaintiff's stake in the litigation may change over time as facts develop, and a case that was properly before the court at its inception may become moot as events unfold. The court's analysis demonstrates how a stipulation—here, that Cohen had been fully compensated—can transform the jurisdictional landscape of a case.

Third, the *Laidlaw* framework represents an important but nuanced expansion of the redressability requirement. When a plaintiff seeks penalties payable to the government rather than to himself, redress may still be found in the deterrent effect of those penalties on the defendant's ongoing or future conduct. But this theory of redressability depends on the existence of an ongoing or threatened injury. If the wrongful conduct has ceased and cannot reasonably be expected to recur, the deterrent rationale disappears, and the claim may become moot.

Fourth, the voluntary cessation doctrine protects the integrity of the judicial process by preventing defendants from unilaterally mooting cases through temporary compliance. The doctrine places a heavy burden on the defendant—the conduct must be "absolutely clear" not to recur—reflecting the court's skepticism about claims of voluntary reform.

Finally, the court's decision to vacate and remand, rather than to decide the jurisdictional question itself, reflects the principle that district courts are the primary fact-finders in the federal system. When a jurisdictional question depends on disputed facts—here, whether Consilio's overtime policy remains in place—the appellate court may be unable to resolve it on the existing record and must remand for the district court to develop the necessary factual basis.

# The MWTA Claim and Injunctive Relief: Procedural Default and the Failure to Properly Raise Claims

## Cohen's MWTA Claim: Statutory Notice Requirements and the Consequence of Inadequate Briefing

Cohen's remaining claims under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) and for injunctive relief met a common fate: each failed not on the substantive merits of the underlying legal theory, but because of procedural deficiencies in how Cohen presented and preserved those claims. The court's treatment of these claims illustrates fundamental principles of appellate advocacy and procedural default that every law student should understand.

### The MWTA Claim and Statutory Notice

Cohen's MWTA claim was based on an allegation that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of a change in its overtime policy. The relevant statutory provisions, Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4), outline employer obligations regarding wage statements and notice of changes to compensation policies. Cohen alleged that Consilio's implementation of the new overtime policy in August 2019—without proper notice—violated these provisions.

### The District Court's Limited Discussion

On appeal, Cohen complained that the district court "included little to no discussion" of his MWTA claim. This objection is notable for what it reveals about appellate practice. A party's complaint that a lower court failed to address a claim does not, without more, establish reversible error. The appellate court's review focuses on whether the district court reached the correct result, not whether it wrote extensively about every argument presented to it. Here, the Eighth Circuit did not dwell on the district court's brevity, instead examining whether the record supported dismissal on the basis that was presented.

### The Stipulation's Effect on the MWTA Claim

A critical fact complicated Cohen's MWTA claim. The parties had stipulated that Consilio had compensated Cohen for all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under his MFLSA claim. This stipulation that Cohen "received all pay Consilio owes him" narrowed the scope of what remained in dispute. With his wage claims satisfied, Cohen sought penalties under the MWTA—but failed to identify any statutory authority authorizing such penalties. This omission proved fatal to his claim.

### The Absence of Statutory Authority for Civil Penalties

The most significant deficiency in Cohen's MWTA claim was his failure to point to any statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under the MWTA provisions he cited. Unlike his MFLSA claim (where statutory penalties were at least theoretically available, though payable to the Commissioner), Cohen identified no provision in the MWTA that would entitle him to the penalties he sought. The court noted this absence without extended discussion, treating it as a straightforward deficiency. This portion of the opinion underscores a foundational principle of statutory interpretation: a claimant must ground their request for relief in the text of the statute they allege was violated. Without identifying a statutory basis for penalties, Cohen's MWTA claim could not survive summary judgment.

### The Waiver Principle: Milligan v. City of Red Oak

The Eighth Circuit's disposition of the MWTA claim relies on a core principle of appellate practice: arguments not adequately briefed are waived. Quoting Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 340 (8th Cir. 2000), the court reiterated the rule that "[I]nasmuch as [defendant's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." The court applied this rule to Cohen's MWTA claim, finding "no meaningful argument in support of reversal."

The waiver principle serves several important functions. First, it preserves judicial economy by ensuring courts do not expend resources addressing arguments that parties have not seen fit to develop. Second, it respects the adversarial system by holding parties accountable for the arguments they choose to make—and for those they choose not to make. Third, it provides fairness to opposing parties, who should not be surprised by theories advanced for the of an appellate decision. A party cannot simply assert a claim and expect the court to construct the legal support for it; the party must affirmatively demonstrate why the law entitles them to relief.

## The Injunctive Relief Claim: New Theories and the Requirement of Amended Pleadings

### Cohen's Shifting Request for Injunctive Relief

Cohen's claim for injunctive relief failed for a different but equally instructive procedural reason. In his complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief. However, by the time of summary judgment briefing, Cohen had changed course and requested different relief: an affirmative injunctive order requiring Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. This shift created a procedural mismatch between the pleadings and the briefing. The district court—understandably, in the Eighth Circuit's view—did not consider the new form of injunctive relief because it had not been properly raised.
