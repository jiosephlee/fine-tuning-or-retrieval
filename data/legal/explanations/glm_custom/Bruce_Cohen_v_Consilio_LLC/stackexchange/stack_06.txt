Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Can a plaintiff seek different injunctive relief in a summary judgment brief than what was requested in the complaint?
Question:
Cohen originally sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief in his complaint, but then requested different relief—withdrawing the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing. The court says this issue is not properly before it, citing InfoDeli, LLC for the proposition that "new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs" and referencing Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a). I'm confused about the line between modifying a claim and raising an entirely new one. Why is requesting different injunctive relief treated as a "new claim" rather than just a refinement of the existing claim? Would this have been permissible if Cohen had sought leave to amend under Rule 15(a)? And what should a plaintiff do when they realize during litigation that the relief they originally requested isn't quite what they need?
Answer:
## Why Different Injunctive Relief Is Treated as a New Claim

The short answer is that the complaint defines the scope of the claims and the relief sought. When a plaintiff requests substantively different injunctive relief in a summary judgment brief than what was pleaded in the complaint, the defendant has not had fair notice of—nor an opportunity to respond to—that new request in the pleadings themselves. The court in *Cohen v. Consilio* treated Cohen's request to withdraw the 2019 policy as a new claim rather than a refinement because it was a materially different form of injunctive relief from the "certain specific forms of injunctive relief" he had originally pleaded. The court characterized Cohen as having "change[d] course and request[ed] different relief," which is qualitatively different from narrowing or clarifying relief already within the complaint's four corners.

The distinction matters because of the notice function of pleadings. A defendant is entitled to know, from the complaint, what relief the plaintiff is seeking. Litigation briefs are not pleadings—they are argumentative documents. As the Eighth Circuit put it in *InfoDeli, LLC*, cited by the court: "New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs." The problem is not that Cohen's new request was related to the same underlying dispute; it is that he sought to expand or alter the relief through a vehicle that does not put the opposing party on formal notice of the change.

## Why Rule 15(a) Is the Proper Mechanism

The court's citation to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) signals the correct path. Rule 15(a)(2) permits a party to amend its pleading "only with the opposing party's written consent or the court's leave," and courts "should freely give leave when justice so requires." If Cohen had sought leave to amend his complaint to request withdrawal of the 2019 policy, the district court could have evaluated whether to permit the amendment—considering factors such as undue delay, prejudice to Consilio, and futility. That process would have given Consilio notice of the new relief and an opportunity to respond before the issue was decided. By bypassing Rule 15(a) and requesting the new relief for the first time in his summary judgment briefing, Cohen deprived the district court of the procedural mechanism for evaluating whether the new claim should be added at all.

## What a Plaintiff Should Do Instead

When a plaintiff realizes during litigation that the relief originally requested does not capture what they actually need, the proper step is to **seek leave to amend the complaint under Rule 15(a)**. This ensures that:

1. **The new relief is formally pleaded**, placing the defendant on notice.
2. **The defendant has an opportunity to respond**, including by opposing the amendment if it is prejudiced.
3. **The court can evaluate the amendment** under the standard Rule 15(a) factors before the new claim is litigated on the merits.

The key takeaway from *Cohen* is that the complaint—not the briefing—defines the universe of claims and relief before the court. A summary judgment motion tests the claims as pleaded; it is not a vehicle for asserting new ones. If your understanding of the appropriate remedy evolves during discovery, amend the complaint rather than springing the new request on the court and opposing counsel in a brief.
