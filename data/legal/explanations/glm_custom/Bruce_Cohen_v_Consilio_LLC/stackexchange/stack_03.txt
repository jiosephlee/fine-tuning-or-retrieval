Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does a plaintiff have Article III standing to seek civil penalties that are payable to the government rather than to the plaintiff?
Question:
I'm struggling with the standing analysis in this case. Cohen is seeking statutory penalties under the MFLSA, but the parties agree these penalties would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen personally. The court cites Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw for the proposition that civil penalties paid to the government can still provide redress to citizen plaintiffs because they deter future violations. But Judge Kobes's concurrence expresses skepticism about whether Laidlaw really applies given differences between the penalties at issue there and those under the MFLSA. How does the redressability prong of standing work when the plaintiff doesn't personally receive any money? Is the deterrence of future violations really sufficient injury to satisfy Article III, or does this depend on the specific statutory context?
Answer:
## Article III Redressability When Penalties Flow to the Government

The short answer, as the Eighth Circuit frames it here, is that **yes, civil penalties payable to the government can satisfy the redressability prong of standing—but only when the plaintiff is seeking redress for an ongoing or threatened injury, and the penalties serve to deter that ongoing conduct.** The court derives this principle from *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000).

### The Redressability Logic from *Laidlaw*

The standard redressability inquiry asks whether a favorable decision is likely to redress the plaintiff's injury. When a plaintiff personally receives money, the redressability chain is straightforward: the defendant pays the plaintiff, and the plaintiff's monetary injury is addressed. When penalties flow to the government, the chain is less direct but, per *Laidlaw*, still functional: civil penalties imposed on the defendant **deter ongoing and future violations**, and it is the cessation of those violations that redresses the plaintiff's injury.

The Eighth Circuit quotes *Laidlaw* directly on this point:

> Civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because such penalties may "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones."

The key is that the plaintiff's injury is not the loss of the penalty money—it is the ongoing unlawful conduct itself. The penalties redress that injury by incentivizing the defendant to stop.

### Why the Court Remanded Rather Than Resolving Standing

The court did not definitively hold that Cohen has standing for the MFLSA penalties. Instead, it identified a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction" and remanded for the district court to examine the issue in the first instance. The reason is that the same logic supporting standing under *Laidlaw* also creates a **mootness problem** once the underlying conduct ceases.

The court explains that "the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." Here, Cohen has already been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he is owed. What remains in dispute is **whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place.** This factual question is dispositive:

- **If the policy remains**, then Cohen is still threatened with injury from ongoing unlawful conduct, and the deterrence rationale from *Laidlaw* supports standing—the penalties would incentivize Consilio to discontinue the policy.
- **If the policy is no longer in place** and it is "absolutely clear" the wrongful conduct will not recur, then there is nothing left to deter, the claim "may in fact be moot," and the court lacks jurisdiction.

The court thus treats the standing/mootness question as turning on the same factual predicate: the existence of ongoing or threatened unlawful conduct that the penalties would deter.

### Judge Kobes's Concurrence: A Statutory-Context Caveat

Judge Kobes concurs specially to flag that the transferability of *Laidlaw* to this context is not straightforward. He notes "there are differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA" and that "[n]either party briefed the issue."

This is a meaningful caveat. *Laidlaw* involved civil penalties under the Clean Water Act's citizen-suit provision, a statute specifically designed to allow citizens to enforce environmental laws. The MFLSA, by contrast, may not share the same enforcement architecture, and the differences could affect whether the deterrence rationale applies with equal force. However, because the parties did not brief the standing question, and because the claim might be moot in any event, Judge Kobes agrees that remand is the appropriate course.

### Bottom Line

The opinion stands for the proposition that **penalties payable to the government can satisfy Article III redressability when the plaintiff is injured by ongoing unlawful conduct and the penalties would deter that conduct.** But the opinion does not definitively resolve whether that standard is met here. Instead, it ties the standing inquiry to the factual question of whether the defendant's violative conduct is ongoing—making the standing analysis and the mootness analysis two sides of the same coin. If there is no continuing violation to deter, there is no ongoing injury to redress, and the court lacks jurisdiction regardless of whether *Laidlaw* would otherwise apply.
