Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Can a federal appeals court raise Article III jurisdictional concerns like mootness sua sponte when neither party has briefed the issue?
Question:
In this case, the Eighth Circuit vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment on Cohen's MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its own jurisdiction, specifically whether Cohen's claim for statutory penalties is moot. The court says it has a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction" even though Cohen was apparently still actively pursuing this claim and neither party raised the issue. I always thought courts generally decide the issues parties present to them. Under what authority can a federal court raise jurisdictional concerns on its own? Is there a distinction between standing and mootness in terms of when courts must raise them sua sponte versus when they can choose to? And does the fact that penalties under the MFLSA are payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen affect the analysis?
Answer:
Yes. Federal courts have an independent obligation to ensure that Article III jurisdiction exists, and this obligation does not depend on the parties raising the issue. The Eighth Circuit's action here is consistent with that principle.

The opinion grounds its sua sponte inquiry in the fundamental relationship between standing and mootness. Quoting *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, the court explains that "[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." The court then applies this framework to Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim, noting that while Cohen may have had standing when he filed suit, subsequent events—specifically, Consilio's payment of all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages to Cohen—raised a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction" as to whether his claim for statutory penalties remained live.

On your question about the distinction between standing and mootness: the opinion treats them as two points on the same continuum of Article III justiciability rather than as categorically different inquiries. Standing is assessed at the outset of litigation; mootness is the continuing obligation to confirm that the personal stake that supported standing at the outset persists throughout the case. Both are jurisdictional requirements that a court must satisfy before exercising its authority, and neither can be waived or conferred by party agreement. The court's quotation of *Laidlaw*—that federal courts are not "license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest"—captures the principle that jurisdiction is not a matter of party consent but of judicial power.

The opinion does not suggest that a court has discretion to *ignore* a jurisdictional defect simply because the parties have not flagged it. To the contrary, the court raises the mootness concern on its own initiative, explains why the record is insufficient to resolve it, and remands for the district court to make the jurisdictional determination in the first instance. The remand reflects the court's view that it cannot adjudicate the MFLSA penalty claim without first confirming that an Article III controversy still exists.

As for the fact that MFLSA statutory penalties are payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen, the opinion addresses this directly through *Laidlaw*. The court acknowledges that "civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may 'afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct'" because such penalties "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." Thus, the fact that penalties flow to the Commissioner does not automatically defeat standing or mootness. However, it does shape the analysis: because Cohen has already received all personal monetary compensation (wages and liquidated damages), the viability of his penalty claim depends on whether Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in place. If the policy continues, the deterrent function of civil penalties may keep the claim alive. If the policy has been abandoned and it is "absolutely clear" the wrongful conduct will not recur, the claim may be moot.

Judge Kobes's concurrence adds a wr worth noting. While agreeing that the penalty claim "might be moot," Judge Kobes expresses skepticism about whether Cohen has standing to seek statutory penalties at all under *Laidlaw*, noting "differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." The concurrence does not resolve this standing question, suggesting only that "the district court may decide the mootness question first." This illustrates the practical point that standing and mootness can blur when the plaintiff's personal stake in the remedy is indirect—as it is here, where penalties go to the Commissioner rather than to the plaintiff. The majority resolves this ambiguity by focusing on mootness and remanding for factual development, while Judge Kobes leaves the door open for the district court to also examine standing.

In short: the court's sua sponte raise of the mootness issue is not a departure from the adversarial norm but an exercise of the court's independent obligation to confirm its own jurisdiction. The opinion does not distinguish between standing and mootness in terms of when courts *must* versus *may* raise them—both are treated as non-negotiable Article III prerequisites that the court is responsible for ensuring. The Commissioner-penalty structure does not automatically resolve the jurisdictional question, but it does make the analysis turn on whether an ongoing violation provides Cohen with the continuing personal interest that Article III requires.
