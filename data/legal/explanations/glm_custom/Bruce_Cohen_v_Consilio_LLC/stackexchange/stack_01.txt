Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What is the 'legislature knows how' canon of statutory interpretation and how is it applied?
Question:
In this opinion, the court concludes that only the Commissioner—not the employee—can seek "average daily wage penalties" under Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act (§ 181.101). Part of the court's reasoning relies on the fact that other Minnesota statutes (§§ 181.13 and 181.14) explicitly authorize employees to seek penalties, so the legislature's failure to include similar language in § 181.101 must be intentional. The court cites this as the "legislature knows how" canon, referencing Lagasse v. Horton. I understand the basic idea that expressio unius est exclusio alterius, but I'm confused about how this canon works in practice. Is this a standalone canon or just an application of negative implication? And how do courts decide when it's appropriate to infer legislative intent from the presence of language in one statute but absence in another, versus assuming the legislature just made a drafting oversight?
Answer:
---

## What the "Legislature Knows How" Canon Is

The "legislature knows how" canon is a specialized form of negative implication that operates *across statutes* rather than within a single statutory text. The basic idea, as the Minnesota Supreme Court explained in *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022), is that "the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so." When the legislature has demonstrated that it knows how to draft language achieving a specific result—and then omits that language from a related statute addressing the same subject—courts infer that the omission was deliberate rather than accidental.

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the Eighth Circuit applied this canon to hold that only the Commissioner of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry—not the employee—may seek "average daily wage penalties" under Minn. Stat. § 181.101.

## How the Canon Differs from Ordinary *Expressio Unius*

You're right to sense the connection to *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, but the "legislature knows how" canon has a distinct analytical structure:

- **Expressio unius** typically operates *within* a single statute: if a provision lists A, B, and C, the omission of D implies exclusion of D.
- **"Legislature knows how"** operates *across* statutes: if Statute 1 explicitly grants a particular right or power, and Statute 2—addressing the same general subject—omits that grant, the omission is presumed intentional.

The opinion makes this clear. The court didn't just look at § 181.101 in isolation. It compared § 181.101 to **sister provisions** in the same Payment of Wages Act: §§ 181.13 and 181.14, which govern wages owed to discharged employees and employees who quit or resign, respectively. Both of those statutes explicitly provide that the employee "may charge and collect a penalty." § 181.101, by contrast, references only the Commissioner's authority to "charge and collect" penalties, with collected funds then paid to the employee. The contrast across these related provisions is what makes the inference powerful.

## How the Court Applies It Here

The court's reasoning proceeds in three steps:

1. **The plain language of § 181.101 references only the Commissioner.** The statute provides that "if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." Any money collected "must be paid to the employee concerned." The employee's role is limited to "prosecuting a claim for wages"—not to seeking penalties directly.

2. **The legislature has demonstrated it knows how to grant employees penalty-seeking authority.** In §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subd. 2—provisions dealing with the same general subject of unpaid wages—the legislature explicitly gave employees the power to "charge and collect a penalty." The presence of this specific language in related statutes shows the legislature had a available template it could have used in § 181.101.

3. **The absence of similar language in § 181.101 is therefore presumed intentional.** Because the legislature knew how to empower employees to seek penalties and chose not to do so in § 181.101, the court infers that § 181.101 deliberately reserves penalty-seeking authority to the Commissioner alone.

As the court put it: "the Minnesota legislature, if it wished, knew how to empower an aggrieved employee with a statutory right to obtain penalties."

## When Courts Will Infer Intent vs. Drafting Oversight

The opinion doesn't lay out a formal multi-factor test, but the reasoning suggests the key considerations courts look to:

- **Relatedness of the statutes.** The canon has force when the statutes being compared address the same general subject matter. Here, §§ 181.101, 181.13, and 181.14 are all provisions of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act, dealing with wage collection. This makes the comparison meaningful.

- **Specificity of the omitted language.** The language at issue isn't vague or incidental. The legislature used a specific, recognizable formulation—"may charge and collect a penalty"—in the comparison statutes. Its complete absence from § 181.101 is hard to attribute to carelessness.

- **Plain language of the target statute.** The court begins with the text of § 181.101 itself, confirming that it "only references the Commissioner regarding penalties." The canon then *reinforces* what the plain text already suggests. The court isn't using the canon to create ambiguity where none exists; it's using it to confirm that the statute means what it appears to say.

- **The overall statutory structure.** § 181.171, the general enforcement provision, says employees may recover "civil penalties or damages . . . provided for in the section violated." So the employee's right to penalties depends on whether the *specific section* violated actually provides for them. § 181.101 provides penalties only to the Commissioner, not to the employee. The "legislature knows how" canon confirms this structural reading.

In short, the canon is most persuasive when the statutory texts are closely related, the omitted language is specific and deliberate-looking, the target statute's plain text already points in the same direction, and the overall statutory architecture supports the inference. When these conditions are met—as the court found here—courts treat the omission as intentional rather than as a drafting oversight.
