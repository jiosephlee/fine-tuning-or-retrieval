Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Standing to Seek Penalties Payable to the State: Laidlaw's Reach After Full Compensation

When a plaintiff sues for unpaid wages and wins full compensation, what remains of the case? For most litigants, the answer is straightforward: the dispute is over, and the court loses jurisdiction. But when the plaintiff also seeks civil penalties payable not to himself but to the state, the question becomes considerably more complicated. The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* highlights this doctrinal tension—and suggests that federal courts have yet to fully work through the implications of the Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.* for penalty claims that survive full monetary compensation.

## The Factual Setup

Bruce Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney working as a document reviewer for Consilio, sued his employer under three Minnesota wage statutes after Consilio eliminated its overtime premium pay policy in 2019. Before summary judgment, Consilio paid Cohen and his colleagues over $256,000 to cover all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been "paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed."

What remained were Cohen's requests for statutory penalties—$172,080 in "average daily wage penalties" under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act, $42,000 under the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act. The parties agreed that any MFLSA statutory penalties, unlike wages and liquidated damages, would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. The district court granted Consilio summary judgment on all three penalty claims. Cohen appealed.

## The Eighth Circuit's Mootness Analysis

The most interesting part of the opinion is the court's treatment of the MFLSA penalty claim. The court acknowledged a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue"—and properly so. The parties had stipulated that Cohen received full compensation for his wages and liquidated damages. If the only remaining remedy is a penalty payable to the state, does Cohen still have a personal stake in the outcome?

The court's answer was cautious and, in a sense, procedural. It did not resolve the question directly. Instead, it identified a factual dispute that it deemed dispositive: whether Consilio's violative overtime policy remained in place. Citing *Laidlaw*, the court explained that civil penalties payable to the government can "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs" by encouraging defendants to discontinue ongoing violations and deterring future ones. But a claim for such penalties "might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." Because the record was unclear whether Consilio had actually changed its policy, the court vacated the grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction in the first instance.

This framing is analytically sound but leaves the central doctrinal question unanswered. The court implicitly accepted that *Laidlaw* can support a plaintiff's standing to pursue penalties payable to the government—at least where there is an ongoing violation. But the court did not address the harder question: whether a plaintiff who has already been made whole retains Article III standing to pursue penalties that flow entirely to the state, even if the underlying conduct is ongoing.

## Laidlaw's Logic and Its Limits

*Laidlaw* is the natural starting point, and the Eighth Circuit was right to begin there. In *Laidlaw*, environmental plaintiffs challenged a polluter's wastewater permit violations under the Clean Water Act's citizen-suit provision. The defendant argued the case was moot because it had achieved compliance with its permit and paid a civil penalty to the government. The Supreme Court held that the case was not moot, reasoning that civil penalties payable to the government could redress the plaintiffs' injury because such penalties "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones."

But *Laidlaw* involved a citizen-suit provision specifically designed to allow private plaintiffs to enforce public rights. The Court's redressability analysis rested on the deterrence function of penalties in the context of ongoing unlawful conduct. The MFLSA presents a somewhat different posture. Cohen is not bringing a citizen suit; he is bringing an individual employment claim under a statute that provides for penalties payable to the Commissioner. The connection between the penalty and Cohen's personal injury is more attenuated than in *Laidlaw*, where the statutory scheme contemplated private enforcement as the primary mechanism for achieving compliance.

Judge Kobes's special concurrence gestures at exactly this concern. He expressed skepticism—without resolving the question—that *Laidlaw*'s logic extends to MFLSA penalties, noting "there are differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." He declined to develop the point because neither party briefed the standing issue. But his concurrence is a signal that at least one member of the panel sees a meaningful distinction between *Laidlaw* and the present case.

The distinction matters. In *Laidlaw*, the civil penalty was the primary remedy available to the citizen-plaintiff; it was the mechanism through which Congress chose to vindicate the public interest. In Cohen's case, the MFLSA provides individual remedies—wages and liquidated damages—that directly redress the employee's injury, and penalties that serve a more traditional regulatory function. Once the employee has been made whole, the penalty claim begins to look less like redress for a personal injury and more like a private attorney general action seeking to enforce public law. Whether Article III permits such an action after the plaintiff's personal injury has been fully remediated is an open and difficult question.

## The Mootness-Status Distinction

The court's opinion also illustrates the fuzzy boundary between standing and mootness in this context. The court framed the issue primarily as one of mootness—whether Consilio's voluntary cessation of its violative policy (if it had indeed ceased) made the case moot under *Laidlaw*'s "absolutely clear" standard. But the underlying question is arguably one of standing: whether Cohen ever had, or continues to have, a personal injury traceable to the penalty provision that is redressable by the penalty itself.

Standing and mootness share the same core requirement: a continuing personal stake in the litigation. As the Eighth Circuit noted, "the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." But conflating the two can obscure the analytically prior question. If Cohen lacks standing to pursue penalties payable to the Commissioner—because such penalties do not redress his personal injury—then mootness is beside the point. The court's decision to remand for a jurisdictional determination is prudent, but the district court will need to confront this threshold question.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The Eighth Circuit's opinion does not resolve the debate, but it places the issue squarely before the district court on remand. And the question is not unique to the MFLSA. Many state and federal statutes provide for civil penalties payable to the government as part of a broader remedial scheme that also includes individual compensation. If plaintiffs who have been fully compensated for their personal injuries lose standing to pursue such penalties, a significant enforcement mechanism may be lost—or, at minimum, shifted entirely to state regulators who may lack the resources or incentive to pursue every violation.

On the other hand, allowing plaintiffs to retain standing after full compensation risks converting federal courts into forums for what are essentially public law enforcement actions, brought by plaintiffs with no personal stake in the outcome beyond a generalized desire to see the law enforced. That is precisely the kind of "generalized grievance" that Article III is designed to exclude. The challenge for courts going forward will be to identify a principled line between penalty claims that genuinely redress an ongoing personal injury—as *Laidlaw* contemplated—and penalty claims that have become untethered from any personal stake.

Judge Kobes's concurrence also flags a practical concern worth noting: the remand extends to attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest, all of which the district court will need to revisit if it determines that Cohen's MFLSA claim survives jurisdictional scrutiny. This is a reminder that the standing question does not exist in isolation; it has cascading consequences for the entire remedial package.

## Conclusion

*Cohen v. Consilio* is a modest decision that opens a significant door. The Eighth Circuit declined to resolve whether a fully compensated plaintiff retains Article III standing to pursue statutory penalties payable to the state, but it identified the right doctrinal framework and signaled that the question demands serious engagement. The district court on remand will have the first crack at it. Whether the issue eventually reaches the Supreme Court may depend on whether lower courts develop a coherent approach—or a circuit split—in applying *Laidlaw* to penalty claims that outlive the plaintiff's personal injury.
