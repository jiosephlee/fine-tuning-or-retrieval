Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Voluntary Cessation and the Burden of Proving Non-Recurrence in Wage-and-Hour Cases

When an employer pays back wages but leaves its disputed pay policy in place, does a former employee's claim for statutory penalties survive? The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* reframes this familiar question through the lens of the voluntary cessation doctrine, reminding employers that partial compensation does not automatically moot a case—and reminding plaintiffs that the boundary between a live controversy and an academic one can be razor-thin.

The case matters because it sits at the intersection of two increasingly common litigation dynamics. First, employers facing wage-and-hour class actions frequently tender full payment of owed wages and liquidated damages to named plaintiffs, attempting to undercut the litigation's economic engine while preserving their policy position. Second, the statutory penalty regimes embedded in state labor laws create enforcement incentives that persist even after individual plaintiffs have been made whole. The Eighth Circuit's decision doesn't resolve these tensions, but it usefully identifies the doctrinal pressure points.

## The Procedural Posture and the Court's Holding

Bruce Cohen, an hourly attorney document reviewer at Consilio, sued under three Minnesota wage statutes after the company eliminated overtime premium pay in 2019. Consilio subsequently paid Cohen and similarly situated employees over $256,000 in overtime wages and liquidated damages under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). The parties stipulated that Cohen had received all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. What remained were Cohen's claims for statutory penalties under the MFLSA, the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), plus a request for injunctive relief.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment on the MPWA and MWTA claims, holding that the MPWA's penalty provision reserves enforcement to the Commissioner of the Department of Labor and Industry rather than empowering private plaintiffs, and that Cohen had waived meaningful argument on the MWTA claim by failing to develop it. But on the MFLSA claim, the court took a different course. Rather than resolve whether Cohen could pursue statutory penalties payable to the Commissioner, the court flagged a "serious concern" about its jurisdiction and remanded for the district court to determine whether the claim was moot under the voluntary cessation doctrine.

## The Voluntary Cessation Framework in the Penalty Context

The court's analysis draws directly from *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), the Supreme Court's leading voluntary cessation decision. Under *Laidlaw*, a defendant's voluntary cessation of challenged conduct does not automatically moot a case; the claim survives unless the defendant can demonstrate that it is "absolutely clear" the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The Eighth Circuit applied this framework straightforwardly: Consilio had paid the wages and liquidated damages, but whether its violative overtime policy remained in place was disputed. If the policy persists, Cohen's penalty claim may still be viable because penalties can "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." If the policy has been abandoned and recurrence is "absolutely clear," the claim may be moot.

This framing is doctrinally sound but practically freighted. The *Laidlaw* standard is famously demanding, and for good reason. As the Supreme Court noted in *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283 (1982), a defendant who simply stops the challenged practice while litigation is pending can too easily resume it once the court's jurisdiction lapses. The "absolutely clear" standard is designed to prevent precisely that maneuver.

## The Employment Context and the Problem of Policy Resurrection

In the environmental context from which *Laidlaw* emerged, cessation often involves tangible, verifiable changes—a polluter shuts down a discharge pipe, dismantles equipment, or permanently alters a manufacturing process. The recurrence inquiry, while fact-intensive, at least concerns physical realities that can be documented and monitored.

The employment context presents a structurally different problem. An employer's pay policy is an administrative artifact. It can be changed by a memo, reinstated by a different memo, and applied inconsistently across offices, departments, or job classifications. Consilio's policy change—eliminating overtime premium pay for hourly licensed attorney document reviewers—was implemented by a company-wide email. There is no physical infrastructure to dismantle, no equipment to remove. The policy can be revived as easily as it was announced.

This asymmetry should, in principle, make it harder for employers to satisfy the "absolutely clear" standard in wage-and-hour cases. The Eighth Circuit's observation that "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute" suggests the court recognized this dynamic. If the policy is merely suspended rather than rescinded, the *Laidlaw* standard would seem to require more than a representation that the employer has no current intention to reinstate it.

Yet the opinion leaves the factual inquiry entirely to the district court on remand, and the record below is unclear about what exactly Consilio has done with the policy since the litigation began. The court's careful avoidance of the factual question is appropriate at the summary judgment stage, but it leaves the doctrinal framework somewhat unmoored from the practical realities that make voluntary cessation inquiries in employment cases so slippery.

## Standing, Penalties Payable to the State, and the Concurrence's Concern

A deeper question lurks in the background of the remand: whether Cohen has standing to pursue civil penalties that are payable to the Commissioner rather than to himself. The parties agreed that any MFLSA statutory penalties would be paid to the government, not to Cohen. The majority invoked *Laidlaw*'s recognition that civil penalties payable to the government can still "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs" by deterring ongoing and future violations, citing 528 U.S. at 186. This is consistent with the broader doctrine that statutory penalties can redress informational or deterrence injuries even when the plaintiff does not receive the funds directly.

Judge Kobes's special concurrence flags a meaningful concern. The penalties at issue in *Laidlaw* were civil penalties under the Clean Water Act, a statute with a robust citizen-suit provision and a well-developed statutory framework for private enforcement of environmental laws. The MFLSA's penalty provisions may operate differently, and the differences between the two statutory schemes could bear on whether penalties payable to the Commissioner actually redress a private plaintiff's injury. Judge Kobes is careful not to resolve this question—he notes that neither party briefed it—but his concurrence signals that the standing question is not as straightforward as the majority's citation to *Laidlaw* might suggest.

This is an important observation. The Supreme Court's standing jurisprudence has tightened considerably since *Laidlaw*, particularly with respect to whether a plaintiff's injury is sufficiently redressable by the relief sought. If MFLSA penalties are designed primarily as regulatory enforcement tools rather than as redress for individual employees' injuries, the standing analysis may require more than a bare assertion that penalties deter future violations. The district court on remand will need to grapple not only with mootness but potentially with the more fundamental question of whether Cohen can invoke the penalty provisions at all—a question the Eighth Circuit conspicuously declined to answer.

## Strategic Implications for Employers and Plaintiffs

The decision creates a strategic landscape that both sides will need to navigate carefully. For employers, the opinion underscores that paying back wages and liquidated damages is necessary but not sufficient to moot a wage-and-hour action. If the employer maintains the disputed policy, the *Laidlaw* standard provides the plaintiff with a continuing basis to pursue penalties and, potentially, injunctive relief. An employer seeking to moot a case through voluntary cessation in this context would need to do more than simply pay what is owed—it would need to either rescind the policy or provide concrete, enforceable assurances that the policy will not be reinstated.

For plaintiffs, the opinion is a double-edged sword. The remand preserves the MFLSA penalty claim, but it also opens the door to a mootness finding if the district court determines that the policy has been abandoned. Plaintiffs in similar cases should anticipate this inquiry and develop a factual record about the policy's current status, the employer's stated intentions, and the structural incentives that might lead to reinstatement. A mere stipulation that wages have been paid, without more, may not suffice to keep the case alive.

The more fundamental question—whether an employee has standing to pursue penalties payable to the state—remains unresolved in the Eighth Circuit. Judge Kobes's concurrence ensures that this issue will be litigated on remand, and the district court's resolution of it could provide the basis for further appellate review. Employers and plaintiffs alike should watch that question closely, because it has implications well beyond the Minnesota wage statutes at issue in this case. If employees lack standing to pursue civil penalties payable to regulatory authorities, the enforcement architecture of state wage-and-hour laws may look significantly different than many practitioners currently assume.

## Conclusion

*Cohen v. Consilio* is not a landmark decision, but it is a useful one. It applies the voluntary cessation doctrine to the wage-and-hour context in a way that is doctrinally faithful to *Laidlaw* while leaving the hardest questions for the district court. The opinion's most significant contribution may be the one it was most reluctant to make: surfacing the standing problem that arises when private plaintiffs seek penalties payable to the government. That question, now teed up for the district court, may ultimately prove more consequential than the mootness inquiry that prompted the remand.
