Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Who Can Enforce Penalties? The Eighth Circuit Limits Private Actions Under Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act

When an employer eliminates overtime pay and a employee sues, the question of who can enforce what remedy matters enormously—not just for the parties, but for the architecture of wage-and-hour law. In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided October 6, 2025, the Eighth Circuit answered one question with clarity and punted on another, with potentially significant consequences for private enforcement of Minnesota's labor statutes.

The case arose straightforwardly. Consilio, a legal document review company, told its Minnesota-based hourly attorney document reviewers in July 2019 that it was eliminating overtime premium pay. Bruce Cohen sued under three Minnesota statutes: the Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Before summary judgment, Consilio paid Cohen and his colleagues all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA—Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. What remained were Cohen's claims for statutory penalties: $172,080 in "average daily wage penalties" under the MPWA, $42,000 under the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the MWTA. The parties agreed that if Consilio won summary judgment on all penalty claims, the case would be fully resolved. Consilio won, and Cohen appealed.

## The MPWA Holding: Only the Commissioner May Seek Penalties

The most consequential part of the opinion is the Eighth Circuit's interpretation of the MPWA. Cohen argued that § 181.101's plain language authorized employees to seek average daily wage penalties. The court disagreed, and its reasoning is worth examining closely.

Section 181.171, the MPWA's civil action provision, allows a person to bring a civil action for violations of certain MPWA sections, including § 181.101. An employer found to have violated those sections is "liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated." The question, then, was what penalties § 181.101 itself provides for—and to whom they are available.

The court's answer is that § 181.101(a) references only the Commissioner of Labor in connection with penalties. The statute provides that if the Commissioner serves a demand for payment on behalf of an employee and payment is not made within ten days, "the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." Any money collected "must be paid to the employee concerned." As for employees themselves, the statute reserves only the ability to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages." The statute thus creates a substantive right to payment of wages, but the enforcement mechanism for penalties runs through the Commissioner, not through private litigation.

The Eighth Circuit reinforced this reading with a classic structural canon: the legislature knows how to accomplish a particular objective when it wishes to do so. The court pointed to §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subd. 2—provisions governing discharged employees and those who resign—which expressly provide that the employee "may charge and collect a penalty." The contrast is telling. Where the Minnesota legislature wanted to empower employees to seek penalties directly, it said so. Where it did not, courts should not supply what the legislature withheld.

## The "Legislature Knows How" Canon in Context

This use of the "legislature knows how" canon is textbook statutory interpretation, and it reflects an increasingly common judicial posture toward penalty provisions in wage-and-hour law. The logic is straightforward: when a legislature has demonstrated in adjacent provisions that it knows how to confer a private right to collect penalties, the absence of that conferral in another provision is not accidental but deliberate. The *Lagasse v. Horton* citation (982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022)) anchors the court's approach in Minnesota Supreme Court precedent, which itself drew from *In re Hubbard* (778 N.W.2d 313 (Minn. 2010)). This is not a novel interpretive move, but its application here has real teeth.

The analytical strength of this approach is that it takes the statutory text seriously without resorting to freewheeling policy arguments. The court did not say that private penalty actions are a bad idea; it said that the Minnesota legislature, by providing express employee penalty rights in §§ 181.13 and 181.14 while omitting them from § 181.101, made a choice. That choice must be respected. The court's citation to *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.* (570 F. Supp. 3d 681 (D. Minn. 2021)) shows that the District of Minnesota had already reached the same conclusion, and the Eighth Circuit's affirmance gives that interpretation binding force within the circuit.

One might quibble with the premise that legislative silence is always deliberate. Drafting is imperfect, and statutes are often the product of compromise rather than design. But the court's response is implicit in its method: when adjacent provisions speak directly to the same question, the inference of intentional differentiation is strongest. Here, §§ 181.13 and 181.14 deal with the same general subject—wage payment and penalties—and were enacted within the same statutory framework. The contrast is close enough that the inference of deliberate differentiation is reasonable.

## The MFLSA Claim: A Mootness Puzzle

The court's treatment of Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim is less satisfying. Having stipulated that he received all overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA, Cohen sought only statutory penalties—penalties that, the parties agreed, are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen himself. The Eighth Circuit flagged a "serious concern" about its jurisdiction.

The court's analysis draws on *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* (528 U.S. 167 (2000)), which held that civil penalties payable to the government can still provide redress to private plaintiffs by encouraging defendants to discontinue unlawful conduct and deterring future violations. But the court also invoked the standing-to-mootness continuum from *Sisney v. Kaemingk* and *Laidlaw*'s voluntariness doctrine: a claim for penalties might become moot if it is "absolutely clear" that the wrongful conduct will not recur. Because the record was unclear whether Consilio's violative overtime policy remained in place, the court vacated and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction.

This is a cautious approach, perhaps appropriately so, but it leaves the law in an uncertain posture. The court recognized that penalties payable to the government can support standing under *Laidlaw*, but it declined to decide whether Cohen actually had standing or whether his claim was moot. Judge Kobes, concurring specially, expressed skepticism that *Laidlaw* straightforwardly applies given the differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA—a point neither party briefed. The result is a remand that may produce further litigation on a threshold jurisdictional question rather than on the merits.

## The MWTA and Injunctive Relief Claims: Waiver and Procedural Missteps

The remaining claims received brief treatment. Cohen's MWTA claim alleged that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of its overtime policy change, but Cohen pointed to no statutory authority for civil penalties under the MWTA, and the court deemed the argument waived for lack of meaningful briefing. His injunctive relief claim foundered on a procedural defect: Cohen sought specific injunctive remedies in his complaint, then requested different relief—withdrawal of the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing. The court, citing *InfoDeli, LLC* and Rule 15(a), declined to consider the new request because it was not properly raised.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The MPWA holding is the opinion's center of gravity, and its implications are significant. By holding that only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101, the Eighth Circuit has narrowed the enforcement toolkit available to private plaintiffs under one of Minnesota's core wage statutes. Employees who suffer wage violations can still recover unpaid wages through private action, and they can seek penalties under §§ 181.13 and 181.14 where applicable. But the penalty mechanism in § 181.101—a provision that, unlike the discharge-specific §§ 181.13 and 181.14, applies broadly to wage payment demands—remains exclusively in the Commissioner's hands.

This division of enforcement authority is not inherently problematic. Administrative enforcement through the Commissioner may be more efficient and better suited to systemic violations. But it raises a practical question: will the Commissioner's office have the resources and inclination to pursue penalties that private litigants cannot? If not, the practical effect of the court's holding may be to reduce the deterrent value of § 181.101's penalty provision. That is a policy judgment for the Minnesota legislature, not the courts, but it is one the legislature may want to revisit.

More broadly, the opinion reflects a judicial trend toward strict construction of statutory penalty provisions against private rights of action. The "legislature knows how" canon is not new, but its application in the wage-and-hour context has gained traction as plaintiffs increasingly seek penalties—sometimes substantial ones, as Cohen's $172,080 demand illustrates—under statutes that may not clearly authorize them. Courts across the country have shown growing reluctance to infer private enforcement rights where legislatures have not expressly provided them. The Eighth Circuit's approach here is consistent with that trend, and the opinion provides a clean template for similar analyses under other Minnesota statutes—and potentially under analogous provisions in other states.

The MFLSA remand, meanwhile, leaves open a question that litigants and courts in the Eighth Circuit will need to address: when an employee has been fully compensated for wages and liquidated damages but seeks only penalties payable to the state, does *Laidlaw* standing survive? The answer may depend on facts that develop on remand—specifically, whether Consilio's policy remains in place. But the broader question of whether *Laidlaw*'s reasoning about deterrent-effect redress extends cleanly to state wage-and-hour penalty statutes deserves more sustained briefing than it received here.

## Bottom Line

*Cohen v. Consilio* is a case about who gets to enforce the law. The Eighth Circuit's answer, at least under the MPWA, is that the legislature assigned that role to the Commissioner, not to private litigants. The reasoning is sound and the textual analysis is careful. But the opinion also exposes the awkward position that arises when an employee has been made whole and only penalties remain: the jurisdictional questions get harder, and the courts get more cautious. On remand, the District of Minnesota will have the first crack at resolving the mootness puzzle—and the answer may shape how private plaintiffs approach penalty claims under Minnesota's labor statutes for years to come.
