Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 3: # The MFLSA Claim: Standing, Mootness, and the Jurisdictional Question of Civil Penalties Payable to the Government

## The Stipulation That Narrowed the MFLSA Claim

Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) began as a straightforward overtime case. He alleged that Consilio failed to pay overtime wages owed to him and similarly situated employees, and he sought overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Before the district court ruled on summary judgment, however, Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded pursuant to Cohen's MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages.

The parties then stipulated that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit" and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." This stipulation eliminated the core of Cohen's MFLSA claim—the unpaid overtime and the corresponding liquidated damages. What remained was Cohen's demand for statutory penalties: he argued he was entitled to $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA. Critically, the parties agreed that any statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen personally.

This procedural posture set the stage for a jurisdictional question that neither party anticipated: whether a plaintiff who has been fully compensated for his personal losses may still pursue a claim for civil penalties that would be paid to the government rather than to himself.

## The Court's Sua Sponte Jurisdictional Concern

The Eighth Circuit raised its concern about jurisdiction on its own initiative, noting that "[a]t this stage of the proceedings, we have a serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue." This is a significant procedural move. Federal courts are generally passive institutions; they decide the questions presented by the parties. But jurisdiction is different. A court's subject-matter jurisdiction—its power to hear a case at all—may be raised at any time, by any party, or by the court sua sponte (on its own motion). The court's concern arose because Cohen had already received everything he personally claimed he was owed, and the only remaining relief he sought—statutory penalties—would not flow to him but to the Commissioner.

The jurisdictional question thus had two dimensions, both rooted in Article III of the United States Constitution, which limits federal judicial power to actual "Cases" and "Controversies." The first dimension is standing: whether Cohen had a sufficiently concrete and personal stake in the litigation when it began. The second is mootness: whether events occurring after the lawsuit was filed—namely, Consilio's payment of all overtime and liquidated damages—had so diminished Cohen's stake that the case was no longer a live controversy. The court addressed both, ultimately concluding that it could not resolve the jurisdictional question on the existing record and that remand was necessary.

## Standing: A Plaintiff Must Demonstrate It for Each Claim and Each Form of Relief

The court began its jurisdictional analysis with the fundamental principle that "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought." This requirement comes from the Supreme Court's decision in *Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017), which itself drew on *Davis v. Federal Election Commission*, 554 U.S. 724, 734 (2008). The rule is demanding: it is not enough for a plaintiff to have standing for one claim or one remedy. If a plaintiff seeks multiple forms of relief, he must independently satisfy the standing requirements for each.

For a plaintiff to establish Article III standing, he must generally show three things: (1) that he has suffered an "injury in fact"—a concrete and particularized harm that is actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical; (2) that the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct; and (3) that a favorable court decision is likely to redress the injury. In Cohen's case, the overtime wages and liquidated damages claims clearly satisfied these requirements at the outset: Cohen alleged he was not paid overtime he was owed, that Consilio caused the nonpayment, and that a court order requiring payment would remedy the harm. But the penalty claim presented a different question. If the penalties are payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen, what is Cohen's personal injury, and how does an order directing payment to the Commissioner redress that injury?

The court did not definitively answer these questions. Instead, it flagged them as serious and turned to the related doctrine of mootness to explain why the answer might depend on facts not yet developed.

## The Relationship Between Standing and Mootness

The court drew on its own precedent to explain the connection between standing and mootness, quoting *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021): "[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." This formulation captures an essential idea: standing and mootness are not entirely separate doctrines but rather two points on a single continuum. Standing asks whether the plaintiff had a sufficient personal stake when the lawsuit was filed. Mootness asks whether that stake still exists at the time the court decides the case. If events have intervened to eliminate the plaintiff's personal interest, the case becomes moot, and the court loses jurisdiction.

The two doctrines thus operate as bookends on the life of a lawsuit. Standing is assessed at the outset; mootness is assessed throughout. The court's analysis of Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim sat at the intersection of these doctrines. Even if Cohen had standing to seek penalties when he filed suit, the question was whether Consilio's payment of all personal compensation—combined with the fact that penalties would go to the Commissioner—had so eroded Cohen's personal stake that the claim was now moot.

## Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw: Civil Penalties as Redress for Citizen Plaintiffs

The court's most important analytical tool came from the Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000). In *Laidlaw*, environmental plaintiffs sued a polluter under the Clean Water Act, which authorized civil penalties payable to the United States Treasury. The defendant argued that because the penalties would be paid to the government rather than to the plaintiffs, the plaintiffs lacked standing: how could a payment to the Treasury redress the plaintiffs' injuries? The Supreme Court rejected this argument. It held that civil penalties, "even if they are paid to the government," may "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because such penalties may "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." In other words, the redress came not from the money itself but from the behavioral change the penalty was designed to produce. If the defendant stopped polluting because of the threat of penalties, the plaintiffs' injury—the ongoing harm from the pollution—would be remedied.

The Eighth Circuit cited *Laidlaw* for the proposition that Cohen's claim for MFLSA penalties might still be viable—if Consilio's violative overtime policy remained in place. The logic would be parallel: even though Cohen would not personally receive the penalty money, the penalty might deter Consilio from continuing its unlawful overtime policy, thereby redressing Cohen's ongoing injury from that policy. But the court immediately noted the limiting principle from *Laidlaw*: the claim "might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." This passage from *Laidlaw* itself quoted *United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Export Association*, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968), and sets a high bar: the defendant must show that the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur, not merely that it has not recurred.

## Mootness and the Voluntary Cessation Doctrine

The court then introduced a critical doctrinal qualification: "[i]t is well settled that 'a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice.'" This principle, drawn from *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982), is known as the voluntary cessation doctrine. It addresses a common scenario: a defendant, facing litigation, voluntarily stops the conduct the plaintiff has challenged. The defendant then argues the case is moot because the conduct has ceased. The voluntary cessation doctrine prevents defendants from unilaterally stripping federal courts of jurisdiction by simply stopping the challenged behavior, only to resume it after the lawsuit is dismissed.

The doctrine reflects a concern about manipulation of the judicial process. If a defendant could moot a case simply by ceasing the challenged conduct, the defendant could engage in the conduct, wait for a lawsuit, stop temporarily, have the case dismissed, and then resume. To prevent this, courts place the burden on the defendant to show that the conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The standard is demanding—"absolutely clear"—reflecting the court's skepticism toward claims of voluntary compliance.

In Cohen's case, the voluntary cessation doctrine was directly relevant. Consilio had already changed its overtime policy once—in August 2019, it eliminated overtime premium pay. The question on remand would be whether the policy that formed the basis of Cohen's claim was still in place. If Consilio had abandoned the policy, the court would need to determine whether it was "absolutely clear" that Consilio would not reinstate it. If the policy remained, the claim for penalties might still be viable under the reasoning of *Laidlaw*.

## The Disputed Factual Question

The court identified a genuine factual dispute that precluded it from resolving the jurisdictional question: "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute." This was the pivotal fact. If the policy remained in effect, Cohen's claim for civil penalties could still be viable because the penalties might deter continued violations, affording Cohen redress for an ongoing injury under *Laidlaw*. If the policy was no longer in place and it was "absolutely clear" that Consilio's wrongful conduct would not recur, the MFLSA penalty claim might be moot, because there would be no ongoing or threatened injury for the penalties to redress.

The court's inability to resolve this factual question on the existing record was central to its disposition. The court noted that "federal courts are not 'license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest.'" The quotation from *Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. at 192, underscores the point: jurisdiction is not a matter of judicial discretion but of constitutional necessity. If the parties lack a continuing stake in the outcome, the court must dismiss, regardless of whether it would be efficient or desirable to decide the merits.

## Vacatur and Remand for a Jurisdictional Determination

Given the unresolved factual question and the serious jurisdictional concern, the court concluded that it could not decide the MFLSA penalty issue. Instead, it vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment as to Cohen's MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction. This disposition is noteworthy for several reasons.

First, the court did not affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim. It did not hold that Cohen lacked standing or that his claim was moot. It simply held that it could not resolve the jurisdictional question on the record before it and that the district court should address the issue in the first instance.

Second, by vacating rather than affirming, the court restored the MFLSA claim to its pre-summary-judgment posture. This means that on remand, the district court may need to take evidence on whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place—a factual inquiry that could involve further discovery, hearings, or other proceedings.

Third, the court's disposition reflects the hierarchical relationship between jurisdiction and the merits. A court must satisfy itself of its jurisdiction before reaching the merits of a claim. Here, the court determined that it could not properly evaluate its jurisdiction without further factual development, and it declined to decide the merits in the absence of that threshold determination.

## Judge Kobes's Special Concurrence

Judge Kobes concurred specially, expressing a distinct perspective on the jurisdictional question. His concurrence is brief but raises important points.

First, Judge Kobes stated that he was "not convinced that Cohen has standing to seek statutory penalties under *Friends of the Earth*." He acknowledged that "[m]aybe he does," but noted "there are differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." This is a significant observation. In *Laidlaw*, the civil penalties were authorized under a federal environmental statute (the Clean Water Act) and were payable to the United States Treasury. The MFLSA penalties, by contrast, are authorized under a Minnesota state statute and are payable to a state Commissioner. Judge Kobes did not enumerate the specific differences he found relevant, and he noted that "[n]either party briefed the issue," but his concurrence signals that the *Laidlaw* framework may not map neatly onto every statutory penalty scheme. The differences between federal citizen-suit penalties and state labor-law penalties may matter for the standing analysis, and the district court on remand may need to address this question.

Second, Judge Kobes agreed that "Cohen's claim for MFLSA statutory penalties might be moot," and therefore concurred with vacating the judgment and remanding the case. His agreement with the majority's disposition reflects a shared view that the jurisdictional question cannot be resolved on the current record.

Third, Judge Kobes offered a practical recommendation: "On remand, the district court may decide the mootness question first." This suggestion reflects a strategic choice about the sequence in which jurisdictional issues should be addressed. Because both standing and mootness present jurisdictional barriers, the district court could choose to address either one first. Judge Kobes's recommendation to begin with mootness is pragmatic: if the claim is moot, the court lacks jurisdiction regardless of whether Cohen had standing, and there is no need to resolve the more difficult and potentially more far-reaching standing question. Mootness, in this context, may turn on a relatively straightforward factual inquiry—whether Consilio's policy is still in place—whereas the standing question may require the court to wrestle with the applicability of *Laidlaw* to state-law penalty provisions, a question neither party has briefed.

## The Broader Significance of the Court's Disposition

The court's treatment of the MFLSA claim illustrates several important principles of federal jurisdiction that law students should understand. First, jurisdiction is not a technicality to be glossed over; it is a constitutional threshold that federal courts must cross before reaching the merits. The court's willingness to raise the issue sua sponte, despite neither party having identified it, reflects the principle that jurisdiction is a duty of the court, not a right of the parties.

Second, the relationship between standing and mootness is dynamic, not static. A plaintiff's stake in the litigation may change over time as facts develop, and a case that was properly before the court at its inception may become moot as events unfold. The court's analysis demonstrates how a stipulation—here, that Cohen had been fully compensated—can transform the jurisdictional landscape of a case.

Third, the *Laidlaw* framework represents an important but nuanced expansion of the redressability requirement. When a plaintiff seeks penalties payable to the government rather than to himself, redress may still be found in the deterrent effect of those penalties on the defendant's ongoing or future conduct. But this theory of redressability depends on the existence of an ongoing or threatened injury. If the wrongful conduct has ceased and cannot reasonably be expected to recur, the deterrent rationale disappears, and the claim may become moot.

Fourth, the voluntary cessation doctrine protects the integrity of the judicial process by preventing defendants from unilaterally mooting cases through temporary compliance. The doctrine places a heavy burden on the defendant—the conduct must be "absolutely clear" not to recur—reflecting the court's skepticism about claims of voluntary reform.

Finally, the court's decision to vacate and remand, rather than to decide the jurisdictional question itself, reflects the principle that district courts are the primary fact-finders in the federal system. When a jurisdictional question depends on disputed facts—here, whether Consilio's overtime policy remains in place—the appellate court may be unable to resolve it on the existing record and must remand for the district court to develop the necessary factual basis.
