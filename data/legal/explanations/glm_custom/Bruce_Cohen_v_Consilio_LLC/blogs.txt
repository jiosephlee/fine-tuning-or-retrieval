Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Who Can Enforce Penalties? The Eighth Circuit Limits Private Actions Under Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act

When an employer eliminates overtime pay and a employee sues, the question of who can enforce what remedy matters enormously—not just for the parties, but for the architecture of wage-and-hour law. In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided October 6, 2025, the Eighth Circuit answered one question with clarity and punted on another, with potentially significant consequences for private enforcement of Minnesota's labor statutes.

The case arose straightforwardly. Consilio, a legal document review company, told its Minnesota-based hourly attorney document reviewers in July 2019 that it was eliminating overtime premium pay. Bruce Cohen sued under three Minnesota statutes: the Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Before summary judgment, Consilio paid Cohen and his colleagues all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA—Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. What remained were Cohen's claims for statutory penalties: $172,080 in "average daily wage penalties" under the MPWA, $42,000 under the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the MWTA. The parties agreed that if Consilio won summary judgment on all penalty claims, the case would be fully resolved. Consilio won, and Cohen appealed.

## The MPWA Holding: Only the Commissioner May Seek Penalties

The most consequential part of the opinion is the Eighth Circuit's interpretation of the MPWA. Cohen argued that § 181.101's plain language authorized employees to seek average daily wage penalties. The court disagreed, and its reasoning is worth examining closely.

Section 181.171, the MPWA's civil action provision, allows a person to bring a civil action for violations of certain MPWA sections, including § 181.101. An employer found to have violated those sections is "liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated." The question, then, was what penalties § 181.101 itself provides for—and to whom they are available.

The court's answer is that § 181.101(a) references only the Commissioner of Labor in connection with penalties. The statute provides that if the Commissioner serves a demand for payment on behalf of an employee and payment is not made within ten days, "the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." Any money collected "must be paid to the employee concerned." As for employees themselves, the statute reserves only the ability to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages." The statute thus creates a substantive right to payment of wages, but the enforcement mechanism for penalties runs through the Commissioner, not through private litigation.

The Eighth Circuit reinforced this reading with a classic structural canon: the legislature knows how to accomplish a particular objective when it wishes to do so. The court pointed to §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subd. 2—provisions governing discharged employees and those who resign—which expressly provide that the employee "may charge and collect a penalty." The contrast is telling. Where the Minnesota legislature wanted to empower employees to seek penalties directly, it said so. Where it did not, courts should not supply what the legislature withheld.

## The "Legislature Knows How" Canon in Context

This use of the "legislature knows how" canon is textbook statutory interpretation, and it reflects an increasingly common judicial posture toward penalty provisions in wage-and-hour law. The logic is straightforward: when a legislature has demonstrated in adjacent provisions that it knows how to confer a private right to collect penalties, the absence of that conferral in another provision is not accidental but deliberate. The *Lagasse v. Horton* citation (982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022)) anchors the court's approach in Minnesota Supreme Court precedent, which itself drew from *In re Hubbard* (778 N.W.2d 313 (Minn. 2010)). This is not a novel interpretive move, but its application here has real teeth.

The analytical strength of this approach is that it takes the statutory text seriously without resorting to freewheeling policy arguments. The court did not say that private penalty actions are a bad idea; it said that the Minnesota legislature, by providing express employee penalty rights in §§ 181.13 and 181.14 while omitting them from § 181.101, made a choice. That choice must be respected. The court's citation to *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.* (570 F. Supp. 3d 681 (D. Minn. 2021)) shows that the District of Minnesota had already reached the same conclusion, and the Eighth Circuit's affirmance gives that interpretation binding force within the circuit.

One might quibble with the premise that legislative silence is always deliberate. Drafting is imperfect, and statutes are often the product of compromise rather than design. But the court's response is implicit in its method: when adjacent provisions speak directly to the same question, the inference of intentional differentiation is strongest. Here, §§ 181.13 and 181.14 deal with the same general subject—wage payment and penalties—and were enacted within the same statutory framework. The contrast is close enough that the inference of deliberate differentiation is reasonable.

## The MFLSA Claim: A Mootness Puzzle

The court's treatment of Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim is less satisfying. Having stipulated that he received all overtime wages and liquidated damages under the MFLSA, Cohen sought only statutory penalties—penalties that, the parties agreed, are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen himself. The Eighth Circuit flagged a "serious concern" about its jurisdiction.

The court's analysis draws on *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* (528 U.S. 167 (2000)), which held that civil penalties payable to the government can still provide redress to private plaintiffs by encouraging defendants to discontinue unlawful conduct and deterring future violations. But the court also invoked the standing-to-mootness continuum from *Sisney v. Kaemingk* and *Laidlaw*'s voluntariness doctrine: a claim for penalties might become moot if it is "absolutely clear" that the wrongful conduct will not recur. Because the record was unclear whether Consilio's violative overtime policy remained in place, the court vacated and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction.

This is a cautious approach, perhaps appropriately so, but it leaves the law in an uncertain posture. The court recognized that penalties payable to the government can support standing under *Laidlaw*, but it declined to decide whether Cohen actually had standing or whether his claim was moot. Judge Kobes, concurring specially, expressed skepticism that *Laidlaw* straightforwardly applies given the differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA—a point neither party briefed. The result is a remand that may produce further litigation on a threshold jurisdictional question rather than on the merits.

## The MWTA and Injunctive Relief Claims: Waiver and Procedural Missteps

The remaining claims received brief treatment. Cohen's MWTA claim alleged that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of its overtime policy change, but Cohen pointed to no statutory authority for civil penalties under the MWTA, and the court deemed the argument waived for lack of meaningful briefing. His injunctive relief claim foundered on a procedural defect: Cohen sought specific injunctive remedies in his complaint, then requested different relief—withdrawal of the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing. The court, citing *InfoDeli, LLC* and Rule 15(a), declined to consider the new request because it was not properly raised.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The MPWA holding is the opinion's center of gravity, and its implications are significant. By holding that only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101, the Eighth Circuit has narrowed the enforcement toolkit available to private plaintiffs under one of Minnesota's core wage statutes. Employees who suffer wage violations can still recover unpaid wages through private action, and they can seek penalties under §§ 181.13 and 181.14 where applicable. But the penalty mechanism in § 181.101—a provision that, unlike the discharge-specific §§ 181.13 and 181.14, applies broadly to wage payment demands—remains exclusively in the Commissioner's hands.

This division of enforcement authority is not inherently problematic. Administrative enforcement through the Commissioner may be more efficient and better suited to systemic violations. But it raises a practical question: will the Commissioner's office have the resources and inclination to pursue penalties that private litigants cannot? If not, the practical effect of the court's holding may be to reduce the deterrent value of § 181.101's penalty provision. That is a policy judgment for the Minnesota legislature, not the courts, but it is one the legislature may want to revisit.

More broadly, the opinion reflects a judicial trend toward strict construction of statutory penalty provisions against private rights of action. The "legislature knows how" canon is not new, but its application in the wage-and-hour context has gained traction as plaintiffs increasingly seek penalties—sometimes substantial ones, as Cohen's $172,080 demand illustrates—under statutes that may not clearly authorize them. Courts across the country have shown growing reluctance to infer private enforcement rights where legislatures have not expressly provided them. The Eighth Circuit's approach here is consistent with that trend, and the opinion provides a clean template for similar analyses under other Minnesota statutes—and potentially under analogous provisions in other states.

The MFLSA remand, meanwhile, leaves open a question that litigants and courts in the Eighth Circuit will need to address: when an employee has been fully compensated for wages and liquidated damages but seeks only penalties payable to the state, does *Laidlaw* standing survive? The answer may depend on facts that develop on remand—specifically, whether Consilio's policy remains in place. But the broader question of whether *Laidlaw*'s reasoning about deterrent-effect redress extends cleanly to state wage-and-hour penalty statutes deserves more sustained briefing than it received here.

## Bottom Line

*Cohen v. Consilio* is a case about who gets to enforce the law. The Eighth Circuit's answer, at least under the MPWA, is that the legislature assigned that role to the Commissioner, not to private litigants. The reasoning is sound and the textual analysis is careful. But the opinion also exposes the awkward position that arises when an employee has been made whole and only penalties remain: the jurisdictional questions get harder, and the courts get more cautious. On remand, the District of Minnesota will have the first crack at resolving the mootness puzzle—and the answer may shape how private plaintiffs approach penalty claims under Minnesota's labor statutes for years to come.



# Standing to Seek Penalties Payable to the State: Laidlaw's Reach After Full Compensation

When a plaintiff sues for unpaid wages and wins full compensation, what remains of the case? For most litigants, the answer is straightforward: the dispute is over, and the court loses jurisdiction. But when the plaintiff also seeks civil penalties payable not to himself but to the state, the question becomes considerably more complicated. The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* highlights this doctrinal tension—and suggests that federal courts have yet to fully work through the implications of the Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.* for penalty claims that survive full monetary compensation.

## The Factual Setup

Bruce Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney working as a document reviewer for Consilio, sued his employer under three Minnesota wage statutes after Consilio eliminated its overtime premium pay policy in 2019. Before summary judgment, Consilio paid Cohen and his colleagues over $256,000 to cover all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been "paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed."

What remained were Cohen's requests for statutory penalties—$172,080 in "average daily wage penalties" under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act, $42,000 under the MFLSA, and $42,000 under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act. The parties agreed that any MFLSA statutory penalties, unlike wages and liquidated damages, would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. The district court granted Consilio summary judgment on all three penalty claims. Cohen appealed.

## The Eighth Circuit's Mootness Analysis

The most interesting part of the opinion is the court's treatment of the MFLSA penalty claim. The court acknowledged a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue"—and properly so. The parties had stipulated that Cohen received full compensation for his wages and liquidated damages. If the only remaining remedy is a penalty payable to the state, does Cohen still have a personal stake in the outcome?

The court's answer was cautious and, in a sense, procedural. It did not resolve the question directly. Instead, it identified a factual dispute that it deemed dispositive: whether Consilio's violative overtime policy remained in place. Citing *Laidlaw*, the court explained that civil penalties payable to the government can "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs" by encouraging defendants to discontinue ongoing violations and deterring future ones. But a claim for such penalties "might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." Because the record was unclear whether Consilio had actually changed its policy, the court vacated the grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction in the first instance.

This framing is analytically sound but leaves the central doctrinal question unanswered. The court implicitly accepted that *Laidlaw* can support a plaintiff's standing to pursue penalties payable to the government—at least where there is an ongoing violation. But the court did not address the harder question: whether a plaintiff who has already been made whole retains Article III standing to pursue penalties that flow entirely to the state, even if the underlying conduct is ongoing.

## Laidlaw's Logic and Its Limits

*Laidlaw* is the natural starting point, and the Eighth Circuit was right to begin there. In *Laidlaw*, environmental plaintiffs challenged a polluter's wastewater permit violations under the Clean Water Act's citizen-suit provision. The defendant argued the case was moot because it had achieved compliance with its permit and paid a civil penalty to the government. The Supreme Court held that the case was not moot, reasoning that civil penalties payable to the government could redress the plaintiffs' injury because such penalties "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones."

But *Laidlaw* involved a citizen-suit provision specifically designed to allow private plaintiffs to enforce public rights. The Court's redressability analysis rested on the deterrence function of penalties in the context of ongoing unlawful conduct. The MFLSA presents a somewhat different posture. Cohen is not bringing a citizen suit; he is bringing an individual employment claim under a statute that provides for penalties payable to the Commissioner. The connection between the penalty and Cohen's personal injury is more attenuated than in *Laidlaw*, where the statutory scheme contemplated private enforcement as the primary mechanism for achieving compliance.

Judge Kobes's special concurrence gestures at exactly this concern. He expressed skepticism—without resolving the question—that *Laidlaw*'s logic extends to MFLSA penalties, noting "there are differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." He declined to develop the point because neither party briefed the standing issue. But his concurrence is a signal that at least one member of the panel sees a meaningful distinction between *Laidlaw* and the present case.

The distinction matters. In *Laidlaw*, the civil penalty was the primary remedy available to the citizen-plaintiff; it was the mechanism through which Congress chose to vindicate the public interest. In Cohen's case, the MFLSA provides individual remedies—wages and liquidated damages—that directly redress the employee's injury, and penalties that serve a more traditional regulatory function. Once the employee has been made whole, the penalty claim begins to look less like redress for a personal injury and more like a private attorney general action seeking to enforce public law. Whether Article III permits such an action after the plaintiff's personal injury has been fully remediated is an open and difficult question.

## The Mootness-Status Distinction

The court's opinion also illustrates the fuzzy boundary between standing and mootness in this context. The court framed the issue primarily as one of mootness—whether Consilio's voluntary cessation of its violative policy (if it had indeed ceased) made the case moot under *Laidlaw*'s "absolutely clear" standard. But the underlying question is arguably one of standing: whether Cohen ever had, or continues to have, a personal injury traceable to the penalty provision that is redressable by the penalty itself.

Standing and mootness share the same core requirement: a continuing personal stake in the litigation. As the Eighth Circuit noted, "the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." But conflating the two can obscure the analytically prior question. If Cohen lacks standing to pursue penalties payable to the Commissioner—because such penalties do not redress his personal injury—then mootness is beside the point. The court's decision to remand for a jurisdictional determination is prudent, but the district court will need to confront this threshold question.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The Eighth Circuit's opinion does not resolve the debate, but it places the issue squarely before the district court on remand. And the question is not unique to the MFLSA. Many state and federal statutes provide for civil penalties payable to the government as part of a broader remedial scheme that also includes individual compensation. If plaintiffs who have been fully compensated for their personal injuries lose standing to pursue such penalties, a significant enforcement mechanism may be lost—or, at minimum, shifted entirely to state regulators who may lack the resources or incentive to pursue every violation.

On the other hand, allowing plaintiffs to retain standing after full compensation risks converting federal courts into forums for what are essentially public law enforcement actions, brought by plaintiffs with no personal stake in the outcome beyond a generalized desire to see the law enforced. That is precisely the kind of "generalized grievance" that Article III is designed to exclude. The challenge for courts going forward will be to identify a principled line between penalty claims that genuinely redress an ongoing personal injury—as *Laidlaw* contemplated—and penalty claims that have become untethered from any personal stake.

Judge Kobes's concurrence also flags a practical concern worth noting: the remand extends to attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest, all of which the district court will need to revisit if it determines that Cohen's MFLSA claim survives jurisdictional scrutiny. This is a reminder that the standing question does not exist in isolation; it has cascading consequences for the entire remedial package.

## Conclusion

*Cohen v. Consilio* is a modest decision that opens a significant door. The Eighth Circuit declined to resolve whether a fully compensated plaintiff retains Article III standing to pursue statutory penalties payable to the state, but it identified the right doctrinal framework and signaled that the question demands serious engagement. The district court on remand will have the first crack at it. Whether the issue eventually reaches the Supreme Court may depend on whether lower courts develop a coherent approach—or a circuit split—in applying *Laidlaw* to penalty claims that outlive the plaintiff's personal injury.



# The Waiver Trap: Procedural Missteps in Employment Class Action Appeals

When an employer pays everything it owes before judgment, what's left? In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit confronted that question and largely answered "not much"—but the opinion's most instructive lessons for practitioners lie not in its statutory interpretation of Minnesota wage law, but in the procedural pitfalls that swallowed two of Cohen's remaining claims. The decision serves as a cautionary tale about the consequences of underdeveloped arguments and shifting legal theories, particularly in class actions where the named plaintiff's choices bind putative class members.

## The MWTA Claim: Silence as Concession

Cohen alleged that Consilio violated the Minnesota Wage Theft Act by failing to provide statutorily required notice of its overtime policy change. The district court dismissed the claim with little discussion, and Cohen protested that the court had shortchanged his argument. But the Eighth Circuit was unmoved. The panel noted that Cohen had stipulated to receiving all pay Consilio owed him, and—critically—that he "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute." Citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, the court treated the absence of supporting legal authority and meaningful argument as a waiver, declining to disturb the district court's dismissal.

This is a familiar but easily forgotten principle: appellate courts are not research assistants. The *Milligan* rule—that unsupported assertions are waived—is not a technicality; it is a structural feature of adversarial litigation. The burden falls on the appellant to identify both the factual basis and the legal authority supporting each claim. In the class action context, this burden takes on added weight. A named plaintiff who fails to develop a statutory theory does not merely forfeit his own recovery; he may foreclose it for the entire class, assuming certification was or would be granted. Cohen's MWTA claim required him to identify a specific penalty provision in the statute that created a private right to recover. His failure to do so—whether by oversight or strategic miscalculation—proved fatal.

## The Injunctive Relief Claim: The Right Remedy Through the Wrong Vehicle

Cohen's injunctive relief claim illustrates a different but equally common procedural trap: changing course mid-litigation without amending the pleadings. In his complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief. By the time he filed his summary judgment opposition, however, he had pivoted to requesting a different remedy—an affirmative order that Consilio rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The district court, understandably, did not address this newly formulated request, and the Eighth Circuit declined to consider it, citing *InfoDeli, LLC* for the proposition that "[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs" and invoking Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a).

The court's reasoning here is straightforward and doctrinally sound. Summary judgment briefing is not a vehicle for reformulating claims. Rule 15(a) provides a clear mechanism for amending pleadings—before trial, leave to amend should be "freely given when justice so requires"—and parties who bypass that mechanism cannot expect appellate courts to rescue them. The district court was entitled to evaluate Cohen's request for injunctive relief based on what he pleaded, not what he argued at the eleventh hour.

From a strategic standpoint, the shift suggests that Cohen's litigation theory evolved as the case progressed—perhaps as discovery revealed that his originally pleaded injunctive relief was unworkable or that the policy he challenged had been modified. That kind of evolution is normal in complex litigation, but the procedural mechanism matters. An amended complaint would have given Consilio fair notice and an opportunity to respond. Raising the issue in summary judgment briefing instead denied the district court a clean record and deprived the appellate court of a properly developed question.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The Eighth Circuit's treatment of these two claims reinforces several principles that practitioners should heed, particularly in wage-and-hour class actions where the stakes extend beyond the named plaintiff.

First, the *Milligan* waiver rule applies with full force on appeal. Appellate briefs must identify the statutory basis for each form of relief sought, including penalties. General invocations of a statute are insufficient. Counsel must trace the specific subsection that creates the right of action and the specific penalty provision that authorizes recovery—failure to do so is treated as a concession that no such authority exists.

Second, *InfoDeli* and Rule 15(a) together establish that summary judgment briefing is not a substitute for the pleadings. When litigation strategy shifts, the complaint must follow. This is especially important in the class action context, where class certification motions and merits briefing often proceed on parallel tracks. A named plaintiff who changes his injunctive relief theory without amending the complaint risks losing the claim entirely—and may face arguments that the class representative is inadequate under Rule 23(a)(4) for failing to prosecute the class's claims consistently.

Third, the opinion's remand of the MFLSA claim on mootness grounds—while not the focus of this post—casts a shadow over the remaining claims. The court's concern about whether Consilio's voluntary cessation of its overtime policy rendered Cohen's penalty claim moot underscores the fragility of penalty-only claims once the underlying conduct has ceased. Practitioners pursuing such claims should develop a robust record on whether the challenged policy remains in effect, as the burden of showing that wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur falls on the defendant.

## Conclusion

*Cohen v. Consilio* is, at its core, a case about what remains when the substantive claims have been satisfied. The Eighth Circuit's answer is: procedurally proper claims, adequately briefed and properly pleaded. Cohen's MWTA and injunctive relief claims failed not because they lacked merit, but because they were not presented in the form the adversarial system requires. For employment class action practitioners, the lesson is clear. Develop every statutory theory from the outset, identify the specific penalty provisions authorizing recovery, and when litigation strategy shifts, amend the complaint. The waiver trap is easy to fall into and nearly impossible to escape on appeal.



# Voluntary Cessation and the Burden of Proving Non-Recurrence in Wage-and-Hour Cases

When an employer pays back wages but leaves its disputed pay policy in place, does a former employee's claim for statutory penalties survive? The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* reframes this familiar question through the lens of the voluntary cessation doctrine, reminding employers that partial compensation does not automatically moot a case—and reminding plaintiffs that the boundary between a live controversy and an academic one can be razor-thin.

The case matters because it sits at the intersection of two increasingly common litigation dynamics. First, employers facing wage-and-hour class actions frequently tender full payment of owed wages and liquidated damages to named plaintiffs, attempting to undercut the litigation's economic engine while preserving their policy position. Second, the statutory penalty regimes embedded in state labor laws create enforcement incentives that persist even after individual plaintiffs have been made whole. The Eighth Circuit's decision doesn't resolve these tensions, but it usefully identifies the doctrinal pressure points.

## The Procedural Posture and the Court's Holding

Bruce Cohen, an hourly attorney document reviewer at Consilio, sued under three Minnesota wage statutes after the company eliminated overtime premium pay in 2019. Consilio subsequently paid Cohen and similarly situated employees over $256,000 in overtime wages and liquidated damages under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). The parties stipulated that Cohen had received all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. What remained were Cohen's claims for statutory penalties under the MFLSA, the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), plus a request for injunctive relief.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment on the MPWA and MWTA claims, holding that the MPWA's penalty provision reserves enforcement to the Commissioner of the Department of Labor and Industry rather than empowering private plaintiffs, and that Cohen had waived meaningful argument on the MWTA claim by failing to develop it. But on the MFLSA claim, the court took a different course. Rather than resolve whether Cohen could pursue statutory penalties payable to the Commissioner, the court flagged a "serious concern" about its jurisdiction and remanded for the district court to determine whether the claim was moot under the voluntary cessation doctrine.

## The Voluntary Cessation Framework in the Penalty Context

The court's analysis draws directly from *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), the Supreme Court's leading voluntary cessation decision. Under *Laidlaw*, a defendant's voluntary cessation of challenged conduct does not automatically moot a case; the claim survives unless the defendant can demonstrate that it is "absolutely clear" the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The Eighth Circuit applied this framework straightforwardly: Consilio had paid the wages and liquidated damages, but whether its violative overtime policy remained in place was disputed. If the policy persists, Cohen's penalty claim may still be viable because penalties can "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." If the policy has been abandoned and recurrence is "absolutely clear," the claim may be moot.

This framing is doctrinally sound but practically freighted. The *Laidlaw* standard is famously demanding, and for good reason. As the Supreme Court noted in *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283 (1982), a defendant who simply stops the challenged practice while litigation is pending can too easily resume it once the court's jurisdiction lapses. The "absolutely clear" standard is designed to prevent precisely that maneuver.

## The Employment Context and the Problem of Policy Resurrection

In the environmental context from which *Laidlaw* emerged, cessation often involves tangible, verifiable changes—a polluter shuts down a discharge pipe, dismantles equipment, or permanently alters a manufacturing process. The recurrence inquiry, while fact-intensive, at least concerns physical realities that can be documented and monitored.

The employment context presents a structurally different problem. An employer's pay policy is an administrative artifact. It can be changed by a memo, reinstated by a different memo, and applied inconsistently across offices, departments, or job classifications. Consilio's policy change—eliminating overtime premium pay for hourly licensed attorney document reviewers—was implemented by a company-wide email. There is no physical infrastructure to dismantle, no equipment to remove. The policy can be revived as easily as it was announced.

This asymmetry should, in principle, make it harder for employers to satisfy the "absolutely clear" standard in wage-and-hour cases. The Eighth Circuit's observation that "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute" suggests the court recognized this dynamic. If the policy is merely suspended rather than rescinded, the *Laidlaw* standard would seem to require more than a representation that the employer has no current intention to reinstate it.

Yet the opinion leaves the factual inquiry entirely to the district court on remand, and the record below is unclear about what exactly Consilio has done with the policy since the litigation began. The court's careful avoidance of the factual question is appropriate at the summary judgment stage, but it leaves the doctrinal framework somewhat unmoored from the practical realities that make voluntary cessation inquiries in employment cases so slippery.

## Standing, Penalties Payable to the State, and the Concurrence's Concern

A deeper question lurks in the background of the remand: whether Cohen has standing to pursue civil penalties that are payable to the Commissioner rather than to himself. The parties agreed that any MFLSA statutory penalties would be paid to the government, not to Cohen. The majority invoked *Laidlaw*'s recognition that civil penalties payable to the government can still "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs" by deterring ongoing and future violations, citing 528 U.S. at 186. This is consistent with the broader doctrine that statutory penalties can redress informational or deterrence injuries even when the plaintiff does not receive the funds directly.

Judge Kobes's special concurrence flags a meaningful concern. The penalties at issue in *Laidlaw* were civil penalties under the Clean Water Act, a statute with a robust citizen-suit provision and a well-developed statutory framework for private enforcement of environmental laws. The MFLSA's penalty provisions may operate differently, and the differences between the two statutory schemes could bear on whether penalties payable to the Commissioner actually redress a private plaintiff's injury. Judge Kobes is careful not to resolve this question—he notes that neither party briefed it—but his concurrence signals that the standing question is not as straightforward as the majority's citation to *Laidlaw* might suggest.

This is an important observation. The Supreme Court's standing jurisprudence has tightened considerably since *Laidlaw*, particularly with respect to whether a plaintiff's injury is sufficiently redressable by the relief sought. If MFLSA penalties are designed primarily as regulatory enforcement tools rather than as redress for individual employees' injuries, the standing analysis may require more than a bare assertion that penalties deter future violations. The district court on remand will need to grapple not only with mootness but potentially with the more fundamental question of whether Cohen can invoke the penalty provisions at all—a question the Eighth Circuit conspicuously declined to answer.

## Strategic Implications for Employers and Plaintiffs

The decision creates a strategic landscape that both sides will need to navigate carefully. For employers, the opinion underscores that paying back wages and liquidated damages is necessary but not sufficient to moot a wage-and-hour action. If the employer maintains the disputed policy, the *Laidlaw* standard provides the plaintiff with a continuing basis to pursue penalties and, potentially, injunctive relief. An employer seeking to moot a case through voluntary cessation in this context would need to do more than simply pay what is owed—it would need to either rescind the policy or provide concrete, enforceable assurances that the policy will not be reinstated.

For plaintiffs, the opinion is a double-edged sword. The remand preserves the MFLSA penalty claim, but it also opens the door to a mootness finding if the district court determines that the policy has been abandoned. Plaintiffs in similar cases should anticipate this inquiry and develop a factual record about the policy's current status, the employer's stated intentions, and the structural incentives that might lead to reinstatement. A mere stipulation that wages have been paid, without more, may not suffice to keep the case alive.

The more fundamental question—whether an employee has standing to pursue penalties payable to the state—remains unresolved in the Eighth Circuit. Judge Kobes's concurrence ensures that this issue will be litigated on remand, and the district court's resolution of it could provide the basis for further appellate review. Employers and plaintiffs alike should watch that question closely, because it has implications well beyond the Minnesota wage statutes at issue in this case. If employees lack standing to pursue civil penalties payable to regulatory authorities, the enforcement architecture of state wage-and-hour laws may look significantly different than many practitioners currently assume.

## Conclusion

*Cohen v. Consilio* is not a landmark decision, but it is a useful one. It applies the voluntary cessation doctrine to the wage-and-hour context in a way that is doctrinally faithful to *Laidlaw* while leaving the hardest questions for the district court. The opinion's most significant contribution may be the one it was most reluctant to make: surfacing the standing problem that arises when private plaintiffs seek penalties payable to the government. That question, now teed up for the district court, may ultimately prove more consequential than the mootness inquiry that prompted the remand.
