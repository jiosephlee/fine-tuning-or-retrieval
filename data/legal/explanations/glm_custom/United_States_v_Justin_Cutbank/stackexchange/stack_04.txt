Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What is the standard for determining if an evidentiary error was "harmless" versus requiring reversal?
Question:
In Cutbank, the Eighth Circuit addressed multiple evidentiary rulings and ultimately held that even if there were errors, they were harmless. The court cited United States v. DeMarce for the standard: "we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." The court also noted that "the government's evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial" and that the errors "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict."

I'm confused about how the harmless error analysis works in practice. The court seems to be weighing the strength of the government's case against the potential impact of the errors. Is this a quantitative assessment (how much evidence supported the conviction) or a qualitative one (how important was the erroneous evidence to the prosecution's theory)? Also, the court mentions "cumulative error" separately—how does the cumulative error doctrine differ from individual harmless error analysis? If each individual error is harmless, can they still add up to require reversal under cumulative error doctrine?
Answer:
## Harmless Error: A Qualitative Assessment of Impact, Not a Quantitative Tally

The harmless error analysis as applied in *Cutbank* is primarily **qualitative**, though the court uses the overall strength of the government's properly admitted evidence as a benchmark. The standard the Eighth Circuit applies, quoting *United States v. DeMarce*, asks whether "the jury **may have been substantially swayed** by the improperly admitted evidence." This is not a counting exercise. The court is not asking whether the government had *enough* evidence without the erroneous admission (a quantitative question), but rather whether the erroneously admitted evidence itself had a substantial influence on the jury's verdict (a qualitative one).

The *Cutbank* opinion illustrates how this works in practice. The court says the government's evidence "was substantial" and therefore the errors "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict." Read carefully, this is not simply saying "there was a lot of other evidence, so the error doesn't matter." Rather, the substantiality of the properly admitted evidence—D.F.'s testimony, C.F.'s testimony, physical evidence including the rifle with Cutbank's DNA, and his conduct during arrest—provides the context against which the court assesses whether the erroneously admitted evidence (if any of the rulings were in fact error) could have *substantially swayed* the jury. The inquiry focuses on the marginal impact of the error: given everything else the jury properly heard, could this additional evidence have changed the outcome?

## Cumulative Error: A Separate, More Demanding Inquiry

The cumulative error doctrine addresses a distinct concern. Individual harmless error analysis examines each error **in isolation**. But the cumulative error doctrine recognizes that even when no single error is sufficient to require reversal, the **combined weight** of multiple errors may undermine confidence in the verdict and require a new trial.

The *Cutbank* court cites *United States v. Keys* for this standard: "We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is **substantial prejudice** to the defendant." This is a separate analytical step. After concluding that any individual evidentiary errors were harmless under *DeMarce*, the court then separately considers whether those errors, considered *together*, caused substantial prejudice.

So yes—individually harmless errors **can** in principle add up to require reversal. That is the entire purpose of the cumulative error doctrine. The doctrine exists because the effect of errors can be synergistic rather than merely additive: multiple minor errors may collectively paint a picture for the jury that is more prejudicial than any single error viewed in isolation would suggest.

## How the Two Standards Relate in *Cutbank*

The court's structure in *Cutbank* shows the two-step framework in action:

1. **Individual harmless error**: For each evidentiary ruling, the court asks whether the error (assuming arguendo it was error) substantially swayed the jury. The court finds it did not, because the properly admitted evidence was substantial and the erroneous evidence had little marginal impact.

2. **Cumulative error**: The court then asks whether the errors, taken together, caused substantial prejudice. The court finds they did not.

The court dispatches this second step very briefly—a single sentence—because Cutbank presented no developed argument for why the combined effect of the errors should matter when each individually did not. The opinion notes that Cutbank "provid[ed] no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments," which explains the terse treatment.

## Practical Takeaway

For a law student trying to apply this framework:

- **Individual harmless error**: Ask whether the erroneously admitted evidence, viewed against the backdrop of the entire trial record, likely had a substantial influence on the jury's verdict. The strength of the government's case matters because it helps you assess the marginal impact of the error—but the question is about the error's effect, not the sufficiency of the remaining evidence.

- **Cumulative error**: Ask whether the errors, viewed together, created substantial prejudice even if each alone was harmless. This requires arguing that the errors reinforced each other in a way that materially affected the fairness of the trial.

In *Cutbank*, the court found neither threshold crossed. The government's evidence was strong—eyewitness testimony, DNA on the weapon, and Cutbank's own conduct—and the challenged evidentiary rulings (on 404(b) evidence, confrontation/limiting instructions, and impeachment) were either not errors at all or had negligible impact on the verdict, both individually and cumulatively.
