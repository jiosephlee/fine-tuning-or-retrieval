Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement apply when no separate felony conviction was obtained?
Question:
In Cutbank, the PSR recommended a four-level sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because Cutbank possessed the firearm "in connection with another felony offense." The commentary to the guideline states this applies "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." The district court adopted the PSR's factual findings without objection and applied the enhancement.

I'm confused about how this enhancement works procedurally. If Cutbank wasn't actually charged or convicted of the "other felony offense" (here, apparently domestic abuse, kidnapping, or assault), how can the sentencing court use those uncharged acts to enhance his sentence? Doesn't this raise Apprendi/Blakely concerns? The court mentions the standard of review is "clear error" for factual findings—does this mean the judge alone determines whether the other felony occurred by a preponderance of evidence? Also, what constitutes "in connection with"? Is there a causal nexus requirement, or is mere temporal or spatial proximity enough?
Answer:
## How § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Works Without a Separate Conviction

Your question touches on several distinct issues. Here's how they break down:

### No separate charge or conviction is required

The guideline commentary is explicit: the enhancement applies "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). The Eighth Circuit quoted this language directly and treated it as dispositive. The "other felony offense" is a sentencing fact, not an element of the offense of conviction. The sentencing court is not adjudicating guilt of a separate crime; it is determining whether the conduct surrounding the offense of conviction triggers an advisory guidelines adjustment.

### Apprendi/Blakely concerns

The opinion doesn't explicitly analyze Apprendi or Blakely, likely because the issue wasn't properly before the court—Cutbank's argument on appeal was that the enhancement produced a sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)," i.e., a substantive reasonableness challenge, not a Sixth Amendment challenge.

That said, the Apprendi/Blakely framework explains why this enhancement survives. The statutory maximum under the Armed Career Criminal Act is life imprisonment. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement raised Cutbank's *advisory guidelines range* (from an offense level of 34 to 35), but it did not increase the sentence beyond the statutory maximum authorized by the jury's verdict. After *United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), the guidelines are advisory, so judicial factfinding that increases the guidelines range—but not the statutory maximum—does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

### The standard: preponderance of the evidence, reviewed for clear error

You asked whether the judge alone determines whether the other felony occurred. Yes. At sentencing, the district court makes factual findings by a preponderance of the evidence. The appellate court then reviews those findings for clear error. The Eighth Circuit cited *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929–30 (8th Cir. 2013), for that standard.

Critically here, Cutbank did not object to the PSR's factual findings. The district court adopted them without objection, and the Eighth Circuit noted that the court therefore "did not err, much less clearly err" in finding the gun was used in connection with another felony. The absence of objection made the appellate court's review straightforward—there was no contested factual dispute to resolve.

### "In connection with": the facts speak for themselves

The opinion does not articulate an abstract "in connection with" test or discuss whether temporal/spatial proximity alone would suffice. It didn't need to. The facts found in the PSR—and adopted without objection—established far more than mere proximity. Cutbank pointed the firearm at D.F.'s face, directed her into a closet at gunpoint, held her there for what she perceived as hours, struck her across the face with the gun, and threatened her life. The firearm was the instrumentality of the assault and kidnapping. On these facts, the "in connection with" element was satisfied because the gun was used to facilitate, intimidate, and carry out the other felony conduct.

### The procedural posture matters

One reason this case reads as straightforward is the procedural posture. Cutbank did not object to the PSR's factual findings, did not raise an Apprendi/Blakely argument, and on appeal challenged only the substantive reasonableness of his sentence under § 3553(a). The Eighth Circuit treated the enhancement as procedurally proper (facts adopted without objection, clear error standard satisfied) and then addressed the substantive reasonableness claim, finding the within-range sentence of 292 months reasonable given Cutbank's extensive criminal history and the circumstances of the offense.
