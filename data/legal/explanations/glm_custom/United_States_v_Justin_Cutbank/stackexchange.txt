Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why is prior possession of the same firearm considered "intrinsic evidence" rather than 404(b) propensity evidence?
Question:
In United States v. Cutbank, the defendant filed a pretrial motion to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle, arguing this was propensity evidence barred by Rule 404(b). The district court ruled it was "intrinsic evidence" relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession. The Eighth Circuit agreed, citing United States v. Battle for the principle that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control."

I'm confused about the line between intrinsic evidence and 404(b) propensity evidence. I thought Rule 404(b) bars evidence of prior bad acts used to show propensity, but here the witnesses are describing seeing the defendant with the same gun on prior occasions. How does the fact that it's the *same weapon* make this intrinsic rather than propensity evidence? If the witnesses had said they saw him with a *different* gun on prior occasions, would that analysis change? What is the test for determining whether evidence is "intrinsic" versus requiring 404(b) analysis?
Answer:
## The "same weapon" distinction is doing the work here

The key to understanding this case is recognizing what the government had to prove and how the testimony related to that burden.

### What Rule 404(b) actually bars

Rule 404(b)(1) bars evidence of prior bad acts **"to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character."** The concern is the inferential chain: the defendant did bad thing X in the past, therefore he has a propensity to do things like X, therefore he probably did the charged conduct. That reasoning is forbidden.

But Rule 404(b) is not a general bar on mentioning anything the defendant did before the charged conduct. It applies to acts that are **separate from the charged offense** and offered for some non-propensity purpose. Evidence that directly proves an element of the charged offense is a different matter entirely.

### Why prior possession of the *same* firearm is intrinsic

Cutbank was charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm. An element the government had to prove was that Cutbank **possessed** the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle found along his flight path. The testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen Cutbank with that *same rifle* before November 19–20 was offered to prove that element—specifically, that Cutbank owned or controlled the weapon, rather than just happening to pick it up that night.

As the Eighth Circuit explained, quoting *United States v. Battle*: **"Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon."** 774 F.3d at 511. The testimony wasn't being used to say "Cutbank is a gun guy, so he probably had a gun that night." It was being used to say "this specific weapon belonged to or was controlled by Cutbank," which is a direct element of the offense. That makes it intrinsic evidence, not 404(b) other-acts evidence.

### Why a *different* weapon would change the analysis

If the witnesses had testified that they previously saw Cutbank with some *other* firearm—a different rifle, a handgun, anything other than the sawed-off Marlin .22—that testimony would not directly prove Cutbank possessed the charged weapon. It would only be relevant through the propensity inferential chain: Cutbank has a history of possessing guns, therefore he probably possessed *this* gun. That is precisely the reasoning Rule 404(b)(1) prohibits.

A different-weapon scenario could potentially come in under a 404(b)(2) purpose—knowledge, intent, or absence of mistake, for example—but it would require the 404(b) analysis the defendant sought here: notice, a proper purpose, 403 balancing, and a limiting instruction. The "same weapon" fact is what keeps this testimony on the intrinsic side of the line.

### The test

The distinction turns on whether the evidence is offered to prove an element of the charged offense through direct inference, or whether it requires a propensity inference to connect the prior act to the charged conduct. When the prior act involves the **same instrumentality** as the charged offense and directly bears on an element (here, possession and control), it is intrinsic. When the prior act is separate and can only be connected to the charged conduct through a character-based inference, 404(b) governs.

This is why the district court's framing—"intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession"—was accurate. The testimony about seeing Cutbank with the same rifle on prior occasions wasn't painting a picture of bad character; it was establishing his ongoing dominion and control over the very weapon the indictment charged him with possessing.

### How does a witness testifying at trial satisfy Confrontation Clause concerns about their prior statements to police?
Question:
In Cutbank's case, the defendant requested a jury instruction that D.F.'s statements to police could not be considered as substantive evidence until D.F. herself testified. The government argued the statements established context for police officer actions. The Eighth Circuit held there was no Confrontation Clause problem because "the hearsay declarants actually appear in court and testify in person," citing United States v. Charboneau.

I'm struggling with this. If D.F. made statements to police that were testimonial in nature (since they were made to law enforcement during an investigation), wouldn't admitting those statements through the officers raise Crawford issues? How does D.F. later testifying at trial cure the Confrontation Clause problem with her prior statements? Is the court saying that because D.F. was available for cross-examination about her statements, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied regardless of whether the statements themselves were testimonial? Also, what exactly is the distinction between using D.F.'s statements to police as "context for officer actions" versus as "substantive evidence of Cutbank's actions"?
Answer:
## How the Confrontation Clause Is Satisfied When the Declarant Testifies

Your confusion is understandable, but it stems from treating *Crawford v. Washington* as if it created an absolute bar on admitting testimonial hearsay. It did not. *Crawford* bars admission of testimonial hearsay **when the declarant does not appear at trial**—because in that situation, the defendant has no opportunity to cross-examine the declarant. When the declarant *does* appear and is subject to cross-examination, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied because the core guarantee—the right to confront the witness—has been honored.

### The Doctrine

*Crawford* held that the Confrontation Clause bars "testimonial" out-of-court statements offered against a criminal defendant unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination. 541 U.S. 36 (2004). But this rule addresses the situation where the prosecution attempts to substitute a prior statement for live testimony. Where the declarant takes the stand and testifies, the defendant can confront them—examining them about the events, their prior statements, inconsistencies, and anything else relevant. That opportunity is what the Confrontation Clause protects.

This is exactly the principle the Eighth Circuit applied here. The court cited *United States v. Charboneau* for the rule that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied "when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person." 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). D.F. testified at trial. Cutbank's counsel had the opportunity to cross-examine her about her statements to police, her recollection of events, and any inconsistencies. That live cross-examination is what the Confrontation Clause demands, and it was available here.

### The Hearsay Dimension

You are right that if D.F.'s statements to police were testimonial, admitting them through the officers *for their truth* would raise *Crawford* concerns if D.F. had not testified. But the government argued a different theory: the statements were not offered for their truth but to "establish the context for police officer actions and their knowledge at the time." This is a non-hearsay purpose—a statement offered to show its effect on the listener (here, why officers took certain investigative steps) rather than to prove the truth of what was said.

The distinction matters:

- **"Substantive evidence of Cutbank's actions"**: The jury would be asked to believe D.F.'s prior statements were *true*—that Cutbank did what she described. This is classic hearsay and would require both a hearsay exception and Confrontation Clause compliance.
- **"Context for officer actions"**: The jury would learn what officers were told to understand why they went where they went, searched where they searched, and acted as they did. The truth of D.F.'s accusations is not the point; the officers' state of knowledge is.

The court noted that "the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial," accepting the government's framing. Combined with D.F.'s testimony, there was no Confrontation Clause problem.

### Answering Your Core Question

> Is the court saying that because D.F. was available for cross-examination about her statements, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied regardless of whether the statements themselves were testimonial?

Essentially, yes. The testimonial character of a prior statement matters most under *Crawford* when the prosecution tries to admit it *in lieu of* the declarant's live testimony. When the declarant appears, testifies, and is subject to cross-examination about both the events and any prior statements, the defendant has the confrontation that the Clause guarantees. The court did not need to determine whether D.F.'s statements to police were testimonial because the precondition that triggers heightened *Crawford* scrutiny—the declarant's absence from trial—was not met.

### Why the Limiting Instruction Was Denied

Cutbank requested a limiting instruction that the jury could not consider D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence "until D.F. herself testified." The court declined, and the Eighth Circuit found no abuse of discretion. The reasoning was twofold: the statements were not admitted as hearsay (they were offered for context), and D.F. did in fact testify, satisfying the Confrontation Clause under *Charboneau*. The court also noted that a limiting instruction need not be given at the time evidence is admitted if an appropriate instruction is provided at the close of trial, citing *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014).

In short, *Crawford* is not implicated when the declarant is on the stand and available for cross-examination. The concern you identify would be valid if D.F. had not testified and the officers relayed her accusatory statements for their truth. But here, D.F. appeared, and the statements were offered for a non-hearsay purpose. Both doors were closed.

### When can extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement be excluded for lack of authentication?
Question:
In Cutbank, the defense wanted to cross-examine C.F. about prior statements she made to a police investigator that were inconsistent with her trial testimony. The court allowed questions about what C.F. said but sustained the government's objection to defense counsel reading portions of the police report into the record, because the report was not authored by C.F. and lacked proper foundation. The Eighth Circuit cited United States v. Cumbie for the principle that "extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated."

I'm confused about the authentication requirement here. If the defense is trying to impeach C.F. with her own prior inconsistent statements, why does it matter who authored the police report? Is the issue that a police report containing a witness's statements is hearsay within hearsay? Could the defense have called the officer who wrote the report to authenticate it, or would that still be improper because the officer didn't personally witness C.F.'s statements? What would have been the proper way to introduce C.F.'s prior inconsistent statements from the police report?
Answer:
## Authentication is a threshold requirement for extrinsic evidence, regardless of the impeachment purpose

The short answer is that authentication and hearsay are distinct requirements, and authentication comes first. Even when extrinsic evidence is offered for a permissible non-hearsay purpose—impeachment by prior inconsistent statement—it still must be authenticated. The defense was not trying to introduce the police report for its truth; it was trying to use the report to prove that C.F. previously made inconsistent statements. That impeachment purpose doesn't bypass the authentication requirement.

### Why authorship matters for authentication

Authentication under Federal Rule of Evidence 901 requires the proponent to "produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is." Here, the defense was offering the police report as evidence of what C.F. actually said to the investigator. But the report was authored by the investigator, not by C.F. When defense counsel simply read portions of the report to C.F. on cross-examination, there was no foundation establishing that the report accurately recorded C.F.'s statements—or even that C.F. had made any statements at all. The document itself had not been authenticated as a reliable record of C.F.'s words.

This is why the court allowed defense counsel to question C.F. directly about what she said to police but drew the line at reading the report into the record. Asking the witness "Did you tell the investigator X?" is examination of the witness about her own prior statements. Reading a third-party document that purportedly records those statements is introducing extrinsic evidence, and extrinsic evidence must be authenticated.

### Why this is not primarily a hearsay-within-hearsay problem

The student asks whether the issue is hearsay within hearsay. That is a related concern but not the one the Eighth Circuit identified. The opinion cites *United States v. Cumbie* for the proposition that extrinsic evidence used for impeachment "must still be authenticated." The court framed the issue as authentication, not hearsay.

This makes sense because impeachment by prior inconsistent statement is generally a non-hearsay use of the statement—the statement is offered not for its truth but to show the inconsistency. However, authentication is a separate threshold requirement. A document offered for a non-hearsay purpose still must be shown to be what the proponent claims it is. The defense claimed the report contained C.F.'s prior statements, but without authentication, there was no basis for that claim in the record.

### Could the defense have called the officer?

Yes. The officer who interviewed C.F. and wrote the report could have authenticated it. The officer would not need to have witnessed the underlying events—the relevant authentication question is whether the officer personally conducted the interview and accurately recorded C.F.'s statements. An officer who interviewed C.F. can testify that C.F. made specific statements to them and that the report reflects those statements. That testimony authenticates both the report and, more directly, the prior inconsistent statements themselves.

In fact, calling the officer would have been cleaner than trying to introduce the report as a document. The officer's testimony that "C.F. told me she did not see the gun that night" is extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement, authenticated by the witness who heard it firsthand. The report itself might raise additional hearsay concerns if offered as a document, but the officer's testimony about what C.F. said would be admissible for impeachment purposes.

### The proper procedure

The proper approach under Federal Rule of Evidence 613 would have been:

1. **Examine C.F. on cross-examination** about the specific prior statements, giving her an opportunity to explain or deny them. The court permitted this.
2. **If C.F. denied or did not distinctly admit making the statements**, introduce extrinsic evidence through the officer who conducted the interview, authenticating the statements by firsthand testimony.
3. **If the defense wanted to introduce the report itself**, lay a foundation through the officer—establishing that the officer wrote the report, interviewed C.F., and accurately recorded her statements—or through another authentication method under Rule 901.

The district court's ruling was consistent with this framework. It allowed the first step and required proper foundation for the second. As the Eighth Circuit noted, this was not an abuse of discretion, and in any event, any error would have been harmless given the substantial evidence of Cutbank's guilt.

### What is the standard for determining if an evidentiary error was "harmless" versus requiring reversal?
Question:
In Cutbank, the Eighth Circuit addressed multiple evidentiary rulings and ultimately held that even if there were errors, they were harmless. The court cited United States v. DeMarce for the standard: "we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence." The court also noted that "the government's evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial" and that the errors "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict."

I'm confused about how the harmless error analysis works in practice. The court seems to be weighing the strength of the government's case against the potential impact of the errors. Is this a quantitative assessment (how much evidence supported the conviction) or a qualitative one (how important was the erroneous evidence to the prosecution's theory)? Also, the court mentions "cumulative error" separately—how does the cumulative error doctrine differ from individual harmless error analysis? If each individual error is harmless, can they still add up to require reversal under cumulative error doctrine?
Answer:
## Harmless Error: A Qualitative Assessment of Impact, Not a Quantitative Tally

The harmless error analysis as applied in *Cutbank* is primarily **qualitative**, though the court uses the overall strength of the government's properly admitted evidence as a benchmark. The standard the Eighth Circuit applies, quoting *United States v. DeMarce*, asks whether "the jury **may have been substantially swayed** by the improperly admitted evidence." This is not a counting exercise. The court is not asking whether the government had *enough* evidence without the erroneous admission (a quantitative question), but rather whether the erroneously admitted evidence itself had a substantial influence on the jury's verdict (a qualitative one).

The *Cutbank* opinion illustrates how this works in practice. The court says the government's evidence "was substantial" and therefore the errors "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict." Read carefully, this is not simply saying "there was a lot of other evidence, so the error doesn't matter." Rather, the substantiality of the properly admitted evidence—D.F.'s testimony, C.F.'s testimony, physical evidence including the rifle with Cutbank's DNA, and his conduct during arrest—provides the context against which the court assesses whether the erroneously admitted evidence (if any of the rulings were in fact error) could have *substantially swayed* the jury. The inquiry focuses on the marginal impact of the error: given everything else the jury properly heard, could this additional evidence have changed the outcome?

## Cumulative Error: A Separate, More Demanding Inquiry

The cumulative error doctrine addresses a distinct concern. Individual harmless error analysis examines each error **in isolation**. But the cumulative error doctrine recognizes that even when no single error is sufficient to require reversal, the **combined weight** of multiple errors may undermine confidence in the verdict and require a new trial.

The *Cutbank* court cites *United States v. Keys* for this standard: "We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is **substantial prejudice** to the defendant." This is a separate analytical step. After concluding that any individual evidentiary errors were harmless under *DeMarce*, the court then separately considers whether those errors, considered *together*, caused substantial prejudice.

So yes—individually harmless errors **can** in principle add up to require reversal. That is the entire purpose of the cumulative error doctrine. The doctrine exists because the effect of errors can be synergistic rather than merely additive: multiple minor errors may collectively paint a picture for the jury that is more prejudicial than any single error viewed in isolation would suggest.

## How the Two Standards Relate in *Cutbank*

The court's structure in *Cutbank* shows the two-step framework in action:

1. **Individual harmless error**: For each evidentiary ruling, the court asks whether the error (assuming arguendo it was error) substantially swayed the jury. The court finds it did not, because the properly admitted evidence was substantial and the erroneous evidence had little marginal impact.

2. **Cumulative error**: The court then asks whether the errors, taken together, caused substantial prejudice. The court finds they did not.

The court dispatches this second step very briefly—a single sentence—because Cutbank presented no developed argument for why the combined effect of the errors should matter when each individually did not. The opinion notes that Cutbank "provid[ed] no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments," which explains the terse treatment.

## Practical Takeaway

For a law student trying to apply this framework:

- **Individual harmless error**: Ask whether the erroneously admitted evidence, viewed against the backdrop of the entire trial record, likely had a substantial influence on the jury's verdict. The strength of the government's case matters because it helps you assess the marginal impact of the error—but the question is about the error's effect, not the sufficiency of the remaining evidence.

- **Cumulative error**: Ask whether the errors, viewed together, created substantial prejudice even if each alone was harmless. This requires arguing that the errors reinforced each other in a way that materially affected the fairness of the trial.

In *Cutbank*, the court found neither threshold crossed. The government's evidence was strong—eyewitness testimony, DNA on the weapon, and Cutbank's own conduct—and the challenged evidentiary rulings (on 404(b) evidence, confrontation/limiting instructions, and impeachment) were either not errors at all or had negligible impact on the verdict, both individually and cumulatively.

### How does the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement apply when no separate felony conviction was obtained?
Question:
In Cutbank, the PSR recommended a four-level sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because Cutbank possessed the firearm "in connection with another felony offense." The commentary to the guideline states this applies "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." The district court adopted the PSR's factual findings without objection and applied the enhancement.

I'm confused about how this enhancement works procedurally. If Cutbank wasn't actually charged or convicted of the "other felony offense" (here, apparently domestic abuse, kidnapping, or assault), how can the sentencing court use those uncharged acts to enhance his sentence? Doesn't this raise Apprendi/Blakely concerns? The court mentions the standard of review is "clear error" for factual findings—does this mean the judge alone determines whether the other felony occurred by a preponderance of evidence? Also, what constitutes "in connection with"? Is there a causal nexus requirement, or is mere temporal or spatial proximity enough?
Answer:
## How § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Works Without a Separate Conviction

Your question touches on several distinct issues. Here's how they break down:

### No separate charge or conviction is required

The guideline commentary is explicit: the enhancement applies "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). The Eighth Circuit quoted this language directly and treated it as dispositive. The "other felony offense" is a sentencing fact, not an element of the offense of conviction. The sentencing court is not adjudicating guilt of a separate crime; it is determining whether the conduct surrounding the offense of conviction triggers an advisory guidelines adjustment.

### Apprendi/Blakely concerns

The opinion doesn't explicitly analyze Apprendi or Blakely, likely because the issue wasn't properly before the court—Cutbank's argument on appeal was that the enhancement produced a sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)," i.e., a substantive reasonableness challenge, not a Sixth Amendment challenge.

That said, the Apprendi/Blakely framework explains why this enhancement survives. The statutory maximum under the Armed Career Criminal Act is life imprisonment. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement raised Cutbank's *advisory guidelines range* (from an offense level of 34 to 35), but it did not increase the sentence beyond the statutory maximum authorized by the jury's verdict. After *United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), the guidelines are advisory, so judicial factfinding that increases the guidelines range—but not the statutory maximum—does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

### The standard: preponderance of the evidence, reviewed for clear error

You asked whether the judge alone determines whether the other felony occurred. Yes. At sentencing, the district court makes factual findings by a preponderance of the evidence. The appellate court then reviews those findings for clear error. The Eighth Circuit cited *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929–30 (8th Cir. 2013), for that standard.

Critically here, Cutbank did not object to the PSR's factual findings. The district court adopted them without objection, and the Eighth Circuit noted that the court therefore "did not err, much less clearly err" in finding the gun was used in connection with another felony. The absence of objection made the appellate court's review straightforward—there was no contested factual dispute to resolve.

### "In connection with": the facts speak for themselves

The opinion does not articulate an abstract "in connection with" test or discuss whether temporal/spatial proximity alone would suffice. It didn't need to. The facts found in the PSR—and adopted without objection—established far more than mere proximity. Cutbank pointed the firearm at D.F.'s face, directed her into a closet at gunpoint, held her there for what she perceived as hours, struck her across the face with the gun, and threatened her life. The firearm was the instrumentality of the assault and kidnapping. On these facts, the "in connection with" element was satisfied because the gun was used to facilitate, intimidate, and carry out the other felony conduct.

### The procedural posture matters

One reason this case reads as straightforward is the procedural posture. Cutbank did not object to the PSR's factual findings, did not raise an Apprendi/Blakely argument, and on appeal challenged only the substantive reasonableness of his sentence under § 3553(a). The Eighth Circuit treated the enhancement as procedurally proper (facts adopted without objection, clear error standard satisfied) and then addressed the substantive reasonableness claim, finding the within-range sentence of 292 months reasonable given Cutbank's extensive criminal history and the circumstances of the offense.

### How do ACCA statutory penalties interact with the Armed Career Criminal Guideline when they produce different offense levels?
Question:
In Cutbank, the jury found the defendant had three or more prior violent felony convictions, triggering Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) enhanced penalties under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The PSR calculated a total offense level of 35, but Cutbank argued that the Armed Career Criminal Guideline (USSG § 4B1.4) would have produced a total offense level of 34. The court noted that "the Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies" under USSG § 4B1.4(b).

I'm having trouble understanding the relationship between the ACCA statute and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline. The ACCA sets minimum statutory penalties (15-year minimum), but how does that translate into a guidelines calculation? Is the § 4B1.4 guideline simply the guidelines' attempt to operationalize the ACCA, and if so, why would there be a discrepancy between the offense level produced by the general firearm guidelines (with enhancements) versus the ACCA guideline? When the court says "the greater offense level applies," is this a cross-reference rule within the guidelines themselves? Could the lower ACCA guideline level ever control, or does the enhancement structure always produce a higher level?
Answer:
## How ACCA and the Armed Career Criminal Guideline Interact

You're essentially right that § 4B1.4 is the Guidelines' mechanism for operationalizing the ACCA, but the key to understanding the discrepancy is recognizing that a defendant sentenced under the ACCA can have **two independent guideline calculations running in parallel**, and the Guidelines themselves provide a rule for which one controls.

### Two Paths to an Offense Level

When a defendant qualifies as an armed career criminal under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), the sentencing court may calculate the offense level through two separate routes:

1. **The general firearm guideline path (§ 2K2.1):** This is the default guideline for felon-in-possession offenses. It starts with a base offense level and then applies specific offense characteristics — here, enhancements for a sawed-off firearm, an obliterated serial number, reckless flight creating risk of death, and possession in connection with another felony. In Cutbank's case, these stacked to produce a total offense level of **35**.

2. **The Armed Career Criminal Guideline path (§ 4B1.4):** This guideline provides a self-contained calculation for armed career criminals, with its own base offense level and enhancements tied to the ACCA's predicate-conviction structure. In Cutbank's case, this path would have produced a total offense level of **34**.

### Why the Discrepancy Exists

The two paths produce different numbers because they are structured differently. Section 2K2.1 builds the offense level through a series of specific offense characteristics tied to the circumstances of the firearm possession — the type of weapon, the serial number, the defendant's conduct during flight, and the connection to other felonies. Section 4B1.4, by contrast, calculates the offense level primarily by reference to the defendant's status as an armed career criminal and the number and nature of qualifying predicate convictions. These are simply different enhancement structures, and there is no reason they will always converge on the same number. In Cutbank's case, the § 2K2.1 enhancements (particularly the four-level enhancement for possession in connection with another felony) pushed the general guideline calculation one level higher than the § 4B1.4 calculation.

### The "Greater Offense Level Applies" Rule

The court's statement that "the Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies" refers to **USSG § 4B1.4(b)**, which is an internal Guidelines cross-reference rule — not a statutory command from the ACCA itself. Section 4B1.4(b) directs the sentencing court to compare the offense level produced under § 4B1.4 with the offense level produced under the otherwise applicable guideline (here, § 2K2.1 with its enhancements) and to **use whichever is greater**. This functions as a one-way floor: the armed career criminal designation can never *reduce* the offense level below what the general guideline would produce, but it can *raise* it if the § 4B1.4 calculation comes out higher.

### Could the Lower Level Ever Control?

No. Under § 4B1.4(b), the lower of the two offense levels will never control. The greater always governs the advisory range calculation. If the § 4B1.4 path produces a higher level than § 2K2.1, the § 4B1.4 level applies. If — as in Cutbank's case — the § 2K2.1 path produces a higher level, that level applies. The defendant cannot elect the lower calculation, and the court is not free to choose between them; the Guidelines mandate the greater.

### The Statutory Minimum Operates Separately

It's worth keeping distinct the Guidelines calculation (which produces an advisory range) from the ACCA's statutory minimum (which produces a mandatory floor). The 15-year mandatory minimum under § 924(e)(1) operates as a statutory constraint on the sentence regardless of what the Guidelines range says. If the Guidelines range calculated under the "greater offense level" rule fell below the statutory minimum, the statutory minimum would control as the actual sentence. But in Cutbank's case, the Guidelines range of 292–365 months already exceeded the 15-year (180-month) minimum, so the statutory minimum did not affect the calculation.

### Summary

- **§ 4B1.4 is the Guidelines' implementation of the ACCA**, but it runs alongside the general § 2K2.1 calculation.
- **Discrepancies arise because the two provisions use different enhancement structures** — one tied to the circumstances of the offense, the other tied to the defendant's armed career criminal status.
- **§ 4B1.4(b) is a Guidelines cross-reference rule** that resolves the discrepancy by mandating the greater of the two offense levels.
- **The lower level can never control** under this framework.
- **The ACCA's statutory minimum operates independently** as a floor on the actual sentence, separate from the Guidelines range calculation.

### Can a defendant challenge a within-guidelines sentence by arguing the guidelines calculation itself violates § 3553(a)?
Question:
In Cutbank, the defendant argued that applying the four-level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement resulted in a within-range sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" that a sentence "be sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The Eighth Circuit rejected this, holding that the district court correctly calculated the guidelines range, considered the § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a within-range sentence that is presumed reasonable.

I'm confused about the boundaries between procedural and substantive reasonableness challenges. Cutbank seems to be arguing that the guidelines enhancement itself produces a sentence that is greater than necessary—this sounds like a substantive challenge to the reasonableness of the sentence. But the court treats it as if the guidelines calculation was proper and the sentence is presumptively reasonable. Can a defendant ever argue that a correctly calculated guidelines range is substantively unreasonable because the enhancements stack in a way that violates § 3553(a)? Or is the proper procedure to argue for a variance under § 3553(a) after accepting the guidelines calculation? What would Cutbank have needed to show to successfully challenge the sentence as substantively unreasonable?
Answer:
## Can a defendant challenge a within-guidelines sentence by arguing the guidelines calculation violates § 3553(a)?

**No—not in the way Cutbank framed it.** A correctly calculated guidelines range does not "violate" § 3553(a). The guidelines calculation is a procedural step governed by the Sentencing Guidelines themselves; § 3553(a) governs the sentence the court ultimately imposes. The proper path is to accept the guidelines calculation (or challenge it as procedurally wrong) and then argue for a variance under § 3553(a).

### The sequential framework

*Gall* establishes a specific sequence: the district court must first "correctly calculat[e] the applicable Guidelines range," then consider the § 3553(a) factors, then consider arguments for departure or variance, and finally impose a sentence. Appellate review follows the same order. As the Eighth Circuit noted, quoting *Rosales-Mireles*, "Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range."

This means a defendant's challenge to the guidelines calculation is always a **procedural** challenge—either the court applied the right enhancements to the right facts or it didn't. Cutbank's argument that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" conflates the guidelines calculation (step one) with the § 3553(a) analysis (step two). The guidelines math is either correct or incorrect; it doesn't "violate" § 3553(a) because § 3553(a) doesn't govern how guidelines are calculated. It governs what sentence the court imposes after the range is determined.

### What the Eighth Circuit actually did in Cutbank

The court followed the *Gall* sequence precisely:

1. **Procedural check:** The court confirmed the district court "correctly calculated" the guidelines range. The PSR's factual statements were adopted without objection, and the facts established the gun was used in connection with another felony, so the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was properly applied. No procedural error.

2. **§ 3553(a) consideration:** The court noted the district court "reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance." Because the district court was aware of Cutbank's arguments, the appellate court "presume[d] that the district court considered and rejected them."

3. **Substantive reasonableness:** The within-range sentence is "presume[d] ... reasonable on appeal." The court then examined whether the presumption was overcome given Cutbank's extensive criminal history (nearly a dozen prior felonies, many involving violence and firearms) and the seriousness of the offense (domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault in the victim's home in the presence of her children, and injuring a police officer). The court concluded the sentence was not substantively unreasonable.

### Can a within-range sentence ever be substantively unreasonable? Yes, but the burden is steep.

A defendant can argue that a within-range sentence is substantively unreasonable under § 3553(a)—that's exactly what substantive reasonableness review is for. But the defendant must **rebut the presumption of reasonableness** that attaches to within-range sentences. The way to do this is not by arguing that the guidelines calculation "violates" § 3553(a), but by pointing to specific § 3553(a) factors that make the within-range sentence greater than necessary—factors the district court failed to adequately account for.

The practical vehicle for this is a **variance request**. The defendant argues: "The guidelines range is calculated correctly, but given the § 3553(a) factors—here, the nature and circumstances of the offense, my history and characteristics, and the need for the sentence—the court should vary below the range." If the district court rejects the variance and imposes a within-range sentence, the defendant can then challenge that sentence as substantively unreasonable on appeal.

### What would Cutbank have needed to show?

To succeed, Cutbank would have needed to:

1. **Concede or properly challenge the guidelines calculation.** If the calculation is correct (as it was here), procedurally challenging it will fail. The opinion makes clear the facts in the PSR established the gun was used in connection with another felony, and the court adopted those facts without objection.

2. **Make a developed variance argument under § 3553(a).** Cutbank needed to identify specific § 3553(a) factors showing why a below-range sentence was sufficient—but not greater than necessary—despite the correctly calculated range. Vague invocations of the "not greater than necessary" principle are not enough.

3. **Rebut the presumption of reasonableness on appeal.** Even if the district court rejected a variance argument, the appellate court presumes a within-range sentence is reasonable. Cutbank would have needed to show that the § 3553(a) factors, taken together, render the within-range sentence unreasonable. Given his extensive criminal history and the violent nature of the offense, this would have been extraordinarily difficult.

The opinion's recitation of the facts underscores why Cutbank's challenge failed on the merits: nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms; domestic abuse with a firearm pointed at the victim's face; holding the victim at gunpoint in a closet; committing the offense in the presence of her children; and biting a police officer while resisting arrest. These facts make the within-range sentence difficult to characterize as "greater than necessary."

### Bottom line

The guidelines calculation and the § 3553(a) analysis are sequential, not overlapping. A guidelines enhancement either was or was not properly applied as a matter of Guidelines interpretation and factfinding—that's procedural. Whether the resulting sentence is reasonable in light of all the § 3553(a) factors is substantive. Cutbank's mistake was framing a substantive challenge (the sentence is too long) as a procedural one (the guidelines calculation "conflicts with" § 3553(a)). The proper approach is to accept the correctly calculated range and argue for a variance, then challenge the substance of the within-range sentence if the district court rejects the variance. But overcoming the presumption of reasonableness for a within-range sentence requires far more than a general assertion that the sentence is "greater than necessary."
