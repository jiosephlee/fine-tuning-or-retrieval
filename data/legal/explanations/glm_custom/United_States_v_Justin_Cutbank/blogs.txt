Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Same Gun, Different Night: The Intrinsic Evidence Doctrine and Rule 404(b) in Felon-in-Possession Cases

When a defendant is charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm, the government must prove that he knowingly possessed the weapon. But what if witnesses testify that they saw him with the same gun days, weeks, or even months before the charged offense? Is that proof of a course of possession—or is it propensity evidence that the defendant is a gun-toting criminal, introduced to prejudice the jury? The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank* illustrates a doctrinal fault line that circuits have papered over with relatively little scrutiny: the so-called "same weapon" rule, under which prior possession of the firearm charged in the indictment is classified not as Rule 404(b) other-acts evidence but as intrinsic evidence of the offense itself.

The classification matters enormously. Evidence designated as intrinsic escapes the procedural safeguards that Rule 404(b) imposes on prosecutions seeking to introduce prior bad acts. The government need not provide pretrial notice under Rule 404(b)(2)(A). The defense cannot demand a pretrial hearing on whether the probative value of the evidence is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice under Rule 403. And perhaps most importantly, the jury receives no limiting instruction explaining that it may consider the evidence only for the permitted purpose and not to infer criminal disposition. The "intrinsic" label is, in practical terms, a free pass.

## The Cutbank Facts and the Eighth Circuit's Reasoning

Justin Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm after a harrowing night in which he held his girlfriend at gunpoint in a closet for hours, threatened her life, struck her with the gun, and then fled—leading to a standoff with police during which he bit an officer and attempted to grab the officer's weapon. Police recovered a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle along the path between the girlfriend's home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself. DNA on the gun matched Cutbank.

Before trial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from his girlfriend, D.F., and her housemate, C.F., that they had previously observed him with the same sawed-off rifle. He argued this was propensity evidence barred by Rule 404(b). The district court rejected the argument, classifying the testimony as intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, citing its prior decision in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), for the proposition that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon."

At trial, D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the incident. C.F. testified that she had seen the gun used during the incident "once or twice" before. The panel found this testimony squarely within the *Battle* rule and noted that Cutbank cited no authority to the contrary.

## The Doctrinal Boundary and Its Problems

The distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence is deceptively simple in formulation and maddeningly imprecise in application. Intrinsic evidence is evidence of acts that are "part of the same transaction" as the charged offense. Extrinsic evidence—governed by Rule 404(b)—is evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts offered to prove propensity. The classic formulation comes from *United States v. Williams*, 900 F.2d 823, 825 (5th Cir. 1990), which describes intrinsic evidence as "evidence concerning the background, circumstances, and details of the charged offense." But courts have stretched this concept considerably in the firearms context.

The "same weapon" rule as articulated in *Battle* and now applied in *Cutbank* treats prior possession of the identical firearm charged in the indictment as intrinsic to the offense of possession. The logic is that possession is not a discrete instant but a continuing state; evidence that a defendant possessed the gun on prior occasions helps establish that he possessed it on the charged date. This reasoning has surface plausibility. If the government must prove knowing possession and the defendant's defense is that he merely stumbled upon the weapon or was framed, evidence that he possessed the same gun previously is probative of ownership and control.

But the doctrine's application in cases like *Cutbank* reveals two significant problems.

The first is temporal indeterminacy. The "handful of times" and "once or twice" that the witnesses described are untethered from any specific date. The testimony establishes only that Cutbank had possessed the rifle at some unspecified points before November 2020. If the prior possession occurred days or even weeks before the charged offense, the inference of continuous possession is reasonably strong. But if the prior observations were months or years earlier, the chain of inference becomes considerably more attenuated. The doctrine as articulated in *Battle* and now *Cutbank* contains no temporal limitation—no outer boundary beyond which prior possession is too remote to qualify as intrinsic. This is a striking omission, given that Rule 404(b) case law routinely treats temporal proximity as a key factor in the balancing analysis. By classifying prior possession as intrinsic regardless of how remote in time, courts effectively immunize the evidence from the very scrutiny that remoteness would ordinarily invite.

The second problem is the evasion of Rule 403 balancing. When evidence is classified as intrinsic, it is generally not subject to the prejudice-versus-probative-value analysis that Rule 403 demands for 404(b) evidence. Yet the concerns that animate Rule 403 are fully present. Testimony that a defendant possessed a sawed-off rifle—a particularly menacing weapon—"a handful of times" before the charged offense invites the jury to infer exactly what Rule 404(b) forbids: that the defendant is the sort of person who carries dangerous weapons and therefore probably committed the charged offense. The probative value of this evidence—establishing ownership and control—is often substantial, but so is the prejudicial effect. In a case like *Cutbank*, where the government also had DNA evidence linking the defendant to the gun and two eyewitnesses who identified the weapon, the marginal probative value of the prior-possession testimony was likely modest. The prejudice, by contrast, was considerable: the jury heard that Cutbank was not merely a felon caught with a gun but a repeat sawed-off-rifle possessor.

## The Harmless Error Safety Valve and Its Limits

The Eighth Circuit's affirmance of the evidentiary rulings did not rest solely on the intrinsic evidence doctrine. The panel also invoked the harmless error doctrine, noting that the government's evidence was "substantial"—DNA on the gun, eyewitness identification, recovery of the weapon along the flight path—and that any error "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict." This alternative ground for affirmance is common in cases involving evidentiary challenges, and it underscores a structural feature of appellate review in felon-in-possession cases: when the government's physical evidence is strong, courts have little incentive to police the boundary between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence carefully. The defendant would have been convicted regardless, so the doctrinal error—if error there was—becomes harmless.

But harmless error is not a substitute for sound doctrine. The rule announced in *Battle* and applied in *Cutbank* shapes pretrial practice in every felon-in-possession case in the Eighth Circuit, not just those with overwhelming physical evidence. In closer cases—where the gun is recovered without fingerprints, where eyewitness identification is shaky, or where the defense theory is accidental or momentary possession—the automatic admission of prior-possession testimony as intrinsic evidence could tip the balance. And because the evidence is classified as intrinsic, the defense has no meaningful pretrial mechanism to exclude it or to obtain a limiting instruction tailored to its actual probative function.

## Downstream Implications

The *Cutbank* decision, while unpublished in effect if not in form, reinforces a trend that should give defense counsel and district judges pause. The "same weapon" rule as currently articulated treats temporal remoteness as irrelevant, conflates possession with ownership, and bypasses the careful balancing that the Rules of Evidence were designed to ensure. Several circuits have recognized versions of this doctrine, though the precise formulations vary. The Fifth Circuit, for instance, has suggested that prior possession of the same weapon is admissible when it is "not too distant in time," implicitly acknowledging that remoteness matters. The Eighth Circuit's broader formulation—that prior possession is "directly relevant" regardless of temporal proximity—leaves district courts with no analytical tools to exclude even decades-old prior possession of the same firearm.

The better approach would be for courts to treat prior possession of the same weapon as 404(b) evidence subject to a context-sensitive analysis, or at minimum to require district courts to make a finding of temporal proximity before classifying such evidence as intrinsic. The continuous possession theory has genuine evidentiary value, but it should be tested against the realities of each case rather than applied as a categorical rule. When prior possession is close in time to the charged offense, the intrinsic label may well be appropriate. When it is remote, the evidence should pass through the filter of Rule 403 and the notice requirements of Rule 404(b)—not because the evidence is irrelevant, but because the procedures that surround 404(b) exist precisely to ensure that relevant evidence does not become a vehicle for improper propensity reasoning.

None of this changes the outcome in *Cutbank*. The evidence against him was overwhelming, and the within-guidelines sentence of 292 months—driven by his extensive violent history and the circumstances of the offense—was amply supported. But the doctrinal shortcut the panel endorsed is worth watching. In the Eighth Circuit and beyond, the "same weapon" rule has become a settled feature of felon-in-possession prosecutions. The question is whether courts will continue to apply it categorically or begin to ask the harder questions about remoteness, prejudice, and the purpose for which prior-possession evidence is actually offered. *Cutbank* suggests the categorical approach remains very much in force.



## Deferred Limiting Instructions and the Confrontation Clause: When Is 'Later' Good Enough?

The Eighth Circuit's affirmance of Justin Cutbank's felon-in-possession conviction in *United States v. Cutbank* addressed a range of evidentiary questions, but one issue deserves particular attention: the court's approval of the district court's decision to defer a limiting instruction on out-of-court statements until the close of trial, even when the defendant specifically requested an immediate instruction before trial began. The decision highlights an underexamined tension between the Confrontation Clause's confrontation guarantee and the practical sequencing of evidence in criminal trials—specifically, whether jurors can be trusted to treat police testimony about a victim's statements as mere context rather than substantive proof of guilt during the interval before the victim takes the stand.

## The Factual and Procedural Background

The facts of Cutbank's offense are harrowing. After a night of drinking and methamphetamine use, Cutbank held his girlfriend, D.F., at gunpoint in a closet for what she described as hours, threatening her life and striking her with the rifle. He eventually fled, and police found him barricaded in a garage half a mile away, where he resisted arrest and bit an officer. The sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle was recovered along the path between the two locations.

Before trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be instructed that it could not consider D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence of his actions until D.F. herself testified to the events. The government countered that the statements would establish context for the officers' actions and their knowledge at the time, that the officers' testimony would not constitute hearsay, and that a later instruction from the bench, if needed, would be customary and sufficient. The district court denied the pretrial request, and the case proceeded to trial.

## The Eighth Circuit's Reasoning

The Eighth Circuit affirmed under *United States v. Bennett*, which holds that a district court "need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial." The panel reviewed the district court's decision for abuse of discretion and found none. The court noted that "the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial," and, separately, that D.F. ultimately testified, satisfying the Confrontation Clause under *United States v. Charboneau*, which holds that the Clause is satisfied "when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person." There was no abuse of discretion.

## Two Paths, One Formalist Answer

The court's reasoning proceeds along two distinct but related paths, and it is worth examining whether either adequately addresses the concern Cutbank raised. First, the court determined that no hearsay was admitted—the officers' testimony about D.F.'s statements was apparently offered for the non-hearsay purpose of establishing context for their actions and knowledge at the time. If the statements were not offered for their truth, the Confrontation Clause, which restricts only the admission of testimonial hearsay, does not apply at all. This is a clean doctrinal answer, but it rests on a particular view of how jurors process evidence.

The second path is more interesting. The court cited *Charboneau* for the proposition that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant ultimately testifies in person. This framing treats the Confrontation Clause as a binary question—either the declarant appears for cross-examination or does not—and gives no weight to the temporal sequencing of testimony. Under this view, so long as D.F. took the stand at some point during the trial, any Confrontation Clause concern arising from officers testifying about her out-of-court statements was cured.

But Cutbank's pretrial request was not simply about the Confrontation Clause in the abstract. It was about the risk that jurors would hear police officers recount D.F.'s statements—describing Cutbank's actions, his threats, his paranoia—and treat those statements as substantive evidence of guilt before D.F. was available for cross-examination. The concern is not that D.F. never testified; it is that in the interval between the officers' testimony and D.F.'s testimony, jurors may have formed impressions based on statements they understood, consciously or not, as proof of what happened, not merely as context for why officers went where they went.

## The Limits of Deferred Limiting Instructions

The *Bennett* framework assumes that a limiting instruction given at the close of trial can effectively cabin the jury's use of evidence admitted days or weeks earlier. This assumption is not unique to the Eighth Circuit; it reflects a broader federal practice that prioritizes the smooth flow of evidence over frequent judicial interruptions. But the assumption is not self-evidently correct. Decades of research on hindsight bias and the difficulty of disregarding information once heard suggest that deferred limiting instructions may be less effective than contemporaneous ones, particularly when the evidence in question is emotionally charged—like a police officer describing a victim's account of being held at gunpoint in a closet.

The Eighth Circuit's opinion does not engage with this concern. It treats the availability of a later instruction as sufficient without examining whether the instruction, when given, could effectively undo whatever impression the officers' testimony had already created. The court also does not address whether the government's non-hearsay characterization of the officers' testimony was accurate in practice—whether jurors were likely to treat the officers' recounting of D.F.'s statements as context for police conduct or as substantive proof of Cutbank's actions. The doctrinal label "not offered for the truth" may do little to constrain how jurors actually use the evidence.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The decision has several implications for trial practice in the Eighth Circuit. First, it confirms that defendants cannot compel contemporaneous limiting instructions as a matter of right; the timing of such instructions remains firmly within the district court's discretion. Second, it reinforces the view that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied by the declarant's eventual appearance at trial, regardless of the order of witnesses. Third, and perhaps most significantly, it signals that courts will accept the government's characterization of police testimony about victim statements as non-hearsay context evidence without closely examining whether jurors are likely to treat it as such.

The downstream effect is that defendants in the Eighth Circuit who face trials where officers testify about victim statements before the victim takes the stand have limited tools to protect against the risk of jurors treating those statements as substantive evidence. The pretrial motion Cutbank filed—asking for an instruction that the statements could not be considered as substantive evidence until the declarant testified—was a reasonable attempt to address this risk. The Eighth Circuit's rejection of that request, without engaging with the underlying concern, leaves defendants with little recourse beyond objecting at trial and hoping the court provides an adequate instruction at closing.

The deeper question is whether the Confrontation Clause's guarantee of confrontation is adequately protected by a framework that focuses solely on whether the declarant ultimately testifies, without regard to the order of proof. The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." When officers testify about a victim's out-of-court statements before the victim testifies, the defendant cannot confront the declarant at the moment the evidence is presented. The Eighth Circuit's answer—that eventual confrontation is sufficient—may be doctrinally correct under existing precedent, but it reflects a formalist approach to the Confrontation Clause that gives little weight to the practical realities of how jurors process evidence and how trial sequencing affects the meaningfulness of cross-examination.

*Cutbank* is unlikely to be the last word on this issue. The question of when limiting instructions must be given remains committed to district court discretion, and the Eighth Circuit's precedent provides little pressure on trial courts to provide contemporaneous instructions even when defendants specifically request them. But the court's approval of deferred instructions in a case where the defendant flagged the Confrontation Clause concern before trial suggests that, at least in this circuit, "later" is almost always good enough. Whether that adequately protects defendants is a question the court has yet to take seriously.



# Reading Police Reports During Cross-Examination: Authentication Requirements for Impeachment by Prior Inconsistent Statement

When defense counsel cross-examines a witness about prior statements to law enforcement, a natural instinct is to reach for the police report itself—to read the relevant portions into the record and force the witness to reconcile her trial testimony with what she previously said. The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank* serves as a useful reminder that this instinct can run aground on authentication requirements, and that the rules governing impeachment by prior inconsistent statement do not carve out a special exception for law enforcement reports.

## The Factual and Procedural Background

Justin Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and sentenced to 292 months under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The charges stemmed from a harrowing November 2020 incident in which Cutbank, after drinking and using methamphetamine, held his girlfriend at gunpoint in a closet for hours, struck her with the gun, and later barricaded himself in a garage a half mile away, where he bit a police officer and grabbed the officer's weapon during the struggle. DNA on a sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle found along the route between the two locations matched Cutbank.

At trial, the government called C.F., a housemate of the victim, who testified that she had seen Cutbank with the gun used during the incident "once or twice" before. On cross-examination, defense counsel sought to undermine this testimony by confronting C.F. with prior statements she had made to a police investigator. The district court allowed questions about what C.F. said to police but sustained the government's objections when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the resulting police report into the record without proper foundation, because the report was not authored by C.F.

## The Authentication Requirement Under *Cumbie*

The Eighth Circuit affirmed, relying on *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), which holds that "although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated." The panel reviewed the claim—despite some uncertainty about whether it was preserved—and found it lacking "whether preserved or not."

The court's reasoning tracks the structure of the Federal Rules of Evidence. Rule 613(b) permits extrinsic evidence of a witness's prior inconsistent statement, but only if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny the statement and an adverse party is given an opportunity to examine the witness about it. Rule 901, however, imposes a separate authentication requirement: to introduce extrinsic evidence, the proponent must produce sufficient evidence to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. A police report, as a document authored by a law enforcement officer based on statements made by the witness, does not authenticate itself simply because the witness made the underlying statements. The report is hearsay as to the witness's statements—or at minimum, it is a document whose authorship and accuracy must be established before it can be read to the jury as extrinsic evidence of what the witness previously said.

The distinction the panel draws is important and often overlooked in practice. There is a meaningful difference between asking a witness, "Did you tell Officer Smith on November 20 that you had never seen the gun before?" and reading to the jury from a police report: "The witness stated she had never seen the firearm." The former is classic cross-examination on prior inconsistent statements, testing the witness's credibility through her own responses. The latter introduces a document as extrinsic evidence—and that document must be authenticated, whether through the testimony of its author, a stipulation, or another method satisfying Rule 901.

## The Panel's Criticism of the Government

Worth noting is the panel's pointed rebuke of the government's briefing. The court observed that Cutbank "did not refer to this ruling" in his appellate brief, yet the government's brief argued that Cutbank's "next claim is that the district court 'thwarted' his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession." The panel responded: "We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal."

This is a welcome reminder that appellees should resist the temptation to address arguments the appellant did not pursue. The instinct to get ahead of potential objections or to preemptively characterize an appellant's position is understandable, but the Eighth Circuit's reaction illustrates the risks. Injecting issues that the appellant has abandoned or never raised can clutter the appellate record, irritate the panel, and potentially prejudice the appellee's own cause by drawing the court's attention to unfavorable rulings that might otherwise have passed without comment. The better practice is to address the arguments the appellant actually makes and let the court decide what issues are properly before it.

## Doctrinal Implications and Practical Takeaways

The authentication requirement for police reports used in impeachment is not new, but *Cutbank* reinforces it in a context where the temptation to cut corners is strong. Defense counsel frequently encounter situations where a witness's trial testimony differs from what appears in a police report, and the report seems like the most efficient way to establish the inconsistency. But *Cumbie* and *Cutbank* make clear that efficiency cannot substitute for foundation.

Several practical lessons follow. First, defense counsel who wish to use a police report for impeachment should plan their authentication strategy before trial. If the report's author is available, calling the author as a witness—or at least securing a stipulation from the government—can satisfy Rule 901. Second, counsel can often achieve the same impeachment effect through careful questioning of the witness about her prior statements, without introducing the report at all. This approach sidesteps authentication issues entirely and may be more effective, as it forces the witness to confront her own words rather than relying on an officer's recording of them. Third, if the witness denies making the prior statement, Rule 613(b)'s extrinsic evidence door opens—but the authentication requirement still must be met before the report itself comes in.

The broader doctrinal point is that the rules of evidence apply with equal force to impeachment evidence as to substantive evidence. Courts have long recognized that the impeachment context does not create a free-floating exception to hearsay, authentication, or other evidentiary requirements. The Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause guarantees the right to cross-examine, but it does not guarantee the right to introduce unauthenticated documents during that cross-examination. Defense counsel must navigate both sets of constraints simultaneously.

## Conclusion

*Cutbank* is unlikely to reshape evidentiary doctrine, but it is a clean illustration of a recurring problem in firearms and domestic violence trials, where witness recollections shift and police reports become the critical point of reference. The Eighth Circuit's message is straightforward: if you want to read from a police report during cross-examination, authenticate it first. And if you are the government, do not manufacture issues the defendant did not raise. Both pieces of advice are sound, and both are too often ignored.



# When Guidelines Enhancements Clash with the Parsimony Principle: The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Problem in Armed Career Criminal Cases

The Armed Career Criminal Act is one of the most powerful sentencing tools the federal government possesses. It transforms a felon-in-possession offense carrying a ten-year maximum into one with a fifteen-year minimum and a maximum of life. But what happens when guidelines enhancements stack on top of ACCA's already severe penalties to produce an advisory range that dwarfs what the statute alone would require? That was the question Justin Cutbank raised in *United States v. Cutbank*, No. 24-1225 (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025), and the Eighth Circuit's answer reveals a structural tension in federal sentencing that deserves more attention than the court gave it.

## The Factual Backdrop and the Evidentiary Issues

The facts of Cutbank's offense are grim. After a night of drinking and methamphetamine use, Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint in a closet for hours, beat her with the sawed-off rifle, terrorized her teenage sons and housemate, and then fled. He barricaded himself in a nearby garage, resisted arrest, and bit a police officer in the face and neck. Police recovered the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle along the likely flight path, and DNA tied Cutbank to the weapon. A jury convicted him under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e).

Cutbank raised a series of evidentiary challenges on appeal — all of which the Eighth Circuit dispatched with little ceremony. The most interesting of these involved testimony from the victim and her housemate that they had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle on prior occasions. Cutbank argued this was inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The court correctly rejected this argument, relying on *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), which holds that prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic evidence relevant to proving ownership and control. This is a well-established principle in the Eighth Circuit and elsewhere, and the panel was right to affirm it without much fanfare.

The remaining evidentiary arguments — involving a denied request for a contemporaneous limiting instruction on hearsay, testimony about the victim's sister being killed by a gun offered in response to cross-examination about prior inconsistent statements, and the authentication requirement for a police report used to impeach a witness — were all resolved in the district court's favor under deferential abuse-of-discretion review. The court also applied the cumulative-error doctrine and found that even assuming arguendo that any individual ruling was erroneous, the evidence of guilt was so overwhelming that no error could have substantially swayed the verdict. This section of the opinion is straightforward and unlikely to trouble future litigants.

## The Sentencing Question: § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Meets the Parsimony Principle

The sentencing issue is where this case gets interesting, even though the court treated it almost as an afterthought.

The Presentence Investigation Report calculated Cutbank's total offense level at 35. This figure reflected a base offense level of 26 (for a sawed-off firearm with two prior crime-of-violence convictions), plus a three-level enhancement for an obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury during flight, and a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm "in connection with another felony offense." With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory range was 292 to 365 months. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months — the bottom of the range.

Cutbank did not dispute the factual basis for the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. The PSR detailed his conduct — holding his girlfriend at gunpoint, assaulting her, preventing her from leaving — which plainly constitutes state felony offenses of domestic assault and false imprisonment. The enhancement applies regardless of whether charges are brought or convictions obtained. USSG § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). On the facts, the four-level bump was unassailable.

Instead, Cutbank made a more structural argument. He contended that the enhancement produced a within-guidelines sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" — the parsimony provision — which requires that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary" to achieve the purposes of sentencing. The key to understanding this argument is the interaction between the ACCA guideline and the firearms guideline.

The Armed Career Criminal Guideline, USSG § 4B1.4, produces its own offense level calculation for ACCA-eligible defendants. In Cutbank's case, § 4B1.4 would have yielded a total offense level of 34, producing an advisory range of 262 to 327 months at Criminal History Category VI. But because the § 2K2.1 enhancements pushed the offense level to 35, the greater offense level governed. See USSG § 4B1.4(b). The four-level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was thus the mechanism by which Cutbank's advisory range exceeded what the ACCA guideline alone would have produced — by approximately thirty months at the bottom of the range. Cutbank's argument, in essence, was that the ACCA already accounts for his dangerousness and criminal history through its enhanced statutory penalties and corresponding guideline, and that the additional four-level bump for the same underlying conduct that triggered ACCA in the first place produces a range that is "greater than necessary."

This is not a frivolous observation. The parsimony principle is, after all, the overarching constraint that Congress imposed on federal sentencing. It is the one statutory command that applies to every sentence, in every case, regardless of the guideline calculation. And stacked enhancements — particularly when they layer on top of an already-enhanced statutory scheme like ACCA — can produce advisory ranges that seem to exceed what any reasonable person would consider the minimum necessary sentence. Cutbank's fifteen-year statutory minimum was already a severe consequence for firearm possession. His 292-month sentence — nearly twenty-five years — is approaching a level that many would consider more retributive than necessary, particularly given that the underlying conduct involved no fatality.

## The Eighth Circuit's Response: Procedural Correctness Subsuming Substantive Challenge

The Eighth Circuit's treatment of this argument is revealing, though ultimately unsatisfying. The panel did not engage with the substance of Cutbank's parsimony argument at all. Instead, it recited the familiar procedural framework: the district court correctly calculated the guidelines range, considered the § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a within-range sentence. Such sentences are presumed reasonable on appeal. *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). The district court "was aware of [Cutbank's] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them." *United States v. Masood*, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). The court did not need to provide a "mechanical recitation" of the factors it weighed. *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024). And given Cutbank's extensive criminal history and the violent circumstances of the offense, the sentence was substantively reasonable.

This approach illustrates a structural feature of post-Booker sentencing that merits scrutiny. The parsimony principle is supposed to function as an independent constraint on sentence length — a ceiling beyond which a sentence becomes "greater than necessary" regardless of what the guidelines say. But the Eighth Circuit's analytical framework effectively subsumes the parsimony inquiry into the presumption of reasonableness. If the guidelines range is correctly calculated and the sentence falls within it, the parsimony argument is never reached on its merits. The court treats the guidelines calculation as the starting and ending point of the substantive reasonableness inquiry, with the presumption of reasonableness doing all the heavy lifting.

This is not unique to the Eighth Circuit. The presumption of reasonableness for within-range sentences has been endorsed by the Supreme Court and adopted across the circuits. But Cutbank's case exposes the cost of this approach: a defendant who argues that a correctly calculated guidelines range is itself "greater than necessary" faces a nearly insurmountable burden. He must overcome not only the district court's discretionary sentencing determination but also an appellate presumption that the range itself is reasonable. The parsimony principle, in practice, functions less as an independent constraint and more as a rhetorical flourish — a statutory aspiration that is always acknowledged and never applied.

## The Broader Problem of Stacked Enhancements in ACCA Cases

There is also a deeper structural issue that the panel did not address. The ACCA and the guidelines enhancements under § 2K2.1 are not fully independent calibrations of sentencing severity. The ACCA already reflects a congressional judgment that certain recidivist firearm offenders are especially dangerous and warrant dramatically enhanced penalties. The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement then adds another layer of severity for the same underlying conduct — the defendant's use of the firearm in connection with a felony — that may have contributed to the ACCA triggering analysis or that reflects the same dangerousness that ACCA already punishes. When the guidelines then instruct that the greater of the two offense levels governs, the result is that the firearms guideline's enhancement effectively overrides the ACCA guideline's own calibration.

This is not technically double-counting in the conventional sense, because the ACCA and § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) serve distinct purposes — one targets recidivism, the other targets the connection between the firearm and the underlying felony. But the practical effect is cumulative punishment for overlapping aspects of the same conduct, and the guidelines provide no mechanism for assessing whether the combined result is proportional. The parsimony principle could, in theory, serve as that mechanism — but only if courts are willing to take it seriously as an independent constraint on advisory ranges.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The Eighth Circuit's opinion in Cutbank is unlikely to change sentencing practice in any direct way. It applies well-established precedent to routine facts and reaches a predictable result. But it highlights a question that will continue to arise as long as the guidelines permit stacking: can a defendant ever successfully argue that a correctly calculated within-range sentence exceeds what the parsimony principle permits?

The answer, at least in the Eighth Circuit, appears to be no — or at least not in a case like Cutbank's, where the underlying conduct is violent and the criminal history is extensive. The presumption of reasonableness, combined with the deferential abuse-of-discretion standard for sentencing determinations, creates a formidable barrier. A defendant might have a better argument in a case where the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement is based on a non-violent felony, where the ACCA predicate offenses are less serious, or where the guidelines range dramatically exceeds the ACCA's statutory minimum. But even then, the court's framework — treating correct calculation as the prerequisite for the presumption, and the presumption as the practical end of the substantive inquiry — leaves little room for a successful parsimony challenge.

For defense attorneys in the Eighth Circuit and similar jurisdictions, the takeaway is sobering. The parsimony argument in the context of stacked enhancements is theoretically alive but practically moribund. The most promising path may be to press the argument at sentencing rather than on appeal — asking the district court to vary downward based on the cumulative effect of enhancements that produce a range exceeding what § 3553(a) requires — and to build a record that specifically addresses the proportionality concerns that the guidelines do not. Whether any district court will be willing to grant such a variance in an ACCA case remains an open question, but Cutbank suggests that the alternative — winning the argument on appeal — is a decidedly uphill battle.
