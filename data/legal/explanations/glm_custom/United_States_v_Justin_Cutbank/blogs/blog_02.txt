Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## Deferred Limiting Instructions and the Confrontation Clause: When Is 'Later' Good Enough?

The Eighth Circuit's affirmance of Justin Cutbank's felon-in-possession conviction in *United States v. Cutbank* addressed a range of evidentiary questions, but one issue deserves particular attention: the court's approval of the district court's decision to defer a limiting instruction on out-of-court statements until the close of trial, even when the defendant specifically requested an immediate instruction before trial began. The decision highlights an underexamined tension between the Confrontation Clause's confrontation guarantee and the practical sequencing of evidence in criminal trials—specifically, whether jurors can be trusted to treat police testimony about a victim's statements as mere context rather than substantive proof of guilt during the interval before the victim takes the stand.

## The Factual and Procedural Background

The facts of Cutbank's offense are harrowing. After a night of drinking and methamphetamine use, Cutbank held his girlfriend, D.F., at gunpoint in a closet for what she described as hours, threatening her life and striking her with the rifle. He eventually fled, and police found him barricaded in a garage half a mile away, where he resisted arrest and bit an officer. The sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle was recovered along the path between the two locations.

Before trial, Cutbank requested that the jury be instructed that it could not consider D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence of his actions until D.F. herself testified to the events. The government countered that the statements would establish context for the officers' actions and their knowledge at the time, that the officers' testimony would not constitute hearsay, and that a later instruction from the bench, if needed, would be customary and sufficient. The district court denied the pretrial request, and the case proceeded to trial.

## The Eighth Circuit's Reasoning

The Eighth Circuit affirmed under *United States v. Bennett*, which holds that a district court "need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial." The panel reviewed the district court's decision for abuse of discretion and found none. The court noted that "the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial," and, separately, that D.F. ultimately testified, satisfying the Confrontation Clause under *United States v. Charboneau*, which holds that the Clause is satisfied "when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person." There was no abuse of discretion.

## Two Paths, One Formalist Answer

The court's reasoning proceeds along two distinct but related paths, and it is worth examining whether either adequately addresses the concern Cutbank raised. First, the court determined that no hearsay was admitted—the officers' testimony about D.F.'s statements was apparently offered for the non-hearsay purpose of establishing context for their actions and knowledge at the time. If the statements were not offered for their truth, the Confrontation Clause, which restricts only the admission of testimonial hearsay, does not apply at all. This is a clean doctrinal answer, but it rests on a particular view of how jurors process evidence.

The second path is more interesting. The court cited *Charboneau* for the proposition that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant ultimately testifies in person. This framing treats the Confrontation Clause as a binary question—either the declarant appears for cross-examination or does not—and gives no weight to the temporal sequencing of testimony. Under this view, so long as D.F. took the stand at some point during the trial, any Confrontation Clause concern arising from officers testifying about her out-of-court statements was cured.

But Cutbank's pretrial request was not simply about the Confrontation Clause in the abstract. It was about the risk that jurors would hear police officers recount D.F.'s statements—describing Cutbank's actions, his threats, his paranoia—and treat those statements as substantive evidence of guilt before D.F. was available for cross-examination. The concern is not that D.F. never testified; it is that in the interval between the officers' testimony and D.F.'s testimony, jurors may have formed impressions based on statements they understood, consciously or not, as proof of what happened, not merely as context for why officers went where they went.

## The Limits of Deferred Limiting Instructions

The *Bennett* framework assumes that a limiting instruction given at the close of trial can effectively cabin the jury's use of evidence admitted days or weeks earlier. This assumption is not unique to the Eighth Circuit; it reflects a broader federal practice that prioritizes the smooth flow of evidence over frequent judicial interruptions. But the assumption is not self-evidently correct. Decades of research on hindsight bias and the difficulty of disregarding information once heard suggest that deferred limiting instructions may be less effective than contemporaneous ones, particularly when the evidence in question is emotionally charged—like a police officer describing a victim's account of being held at gunpoint in a closet.

The Eighth Circuit's opinion does not engage with this concern. It treats the availability of a later instruction as sufficient without examining whether the instruction, when given, could effectively undo whatever impression the officers' testimony had already created. The court also does not address whether the government's non-hearsay characterization of the officers' testimony was accurate in practice—whether jurors were likely to treat the officers' recounting of D.F.'s statements as context for police conduct or as substantive proof of Cutbank's actions. The doctrinal label "not offered for the truth" may do little to constrain how jurors actually use the evidence.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The decision has several implications for trial practice in the Eighth Circuit. First, it confirms that defendants cannot compel contemporaneous limiting instructions as a matter of right; the timing of such instructions remains firmly within the district court's discretion. Second, it reinforces the view that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied by the declarant's eventual appearance at trial, regardless of the order of witnesses. Third, and perhaps most significantly, it signals that courts will accept the government's characterization of police testimony about victim statements as non-hearsay context evidence without closely examining whether jurors are likely to treat it as such.

The downstream effect is that defendants in the Eighth Circuit who face trials where officers testify about victim statements before the victim takes the stand have limited tools to protect against the risk of jurors treating those statements as substantive evidence. The pretrial motion Cutbank filed—asking for an instruction that the statements could not be considered as substantive evidence until the declarant testified—was a reasonable attempt to address this risk. The Eighth Circuit's rejection of that request, without engaging with the underlying concern, leaves defendants with little recourse beyond objecting at trial and hoping the court provides an adequate instruction at closing.

The deeper question is whether the Confrontation Clause's guarantee of confrontation is adequately protected by a framework that focuses solely on whether the declarant ultimately testifies, without regard to the order of proof. The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." When officers testify about a victim's out-of-court statements before the victim testifies, the defendant cannot confront the declarant at the moment the evidence is presented. The Eighth Circuit's answer—that eventual confrontation is sufficient—may be doctrinally correct under existing precedent, but it reflects a formalist approach to the Confrontation Clause that gives little weight to the practical realities of how jurors process evidence and how trial sequencing affects the meaningfulness of cross-examination.

*Cutbank* is unlikely to be the last word on this issue. The question of when limiting instructions must be given remains committed to district court discretion, and the Eighth Circuit's precedent provides little pressure on trial courts to provide contemporaneous instructions even when defendants specifically request them. But the court's approval of deferred instructions in a case where the defendant flagged the Confrontation Clause concern before trial suggests that, at least in this circuit, "later" is almost always good enough. Whether that adequately protects defendants is a question the court has yet to take seriously.
