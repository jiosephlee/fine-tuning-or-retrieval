Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

## From Welfare Check to Warrant: The Expanding Scope of Consent in Home Entries

The Fourth Amendment's protections are at their zenith in the home, and the Supreme Court has long insisted that warrantless entry is the exception, not the rule. Yet the Seventh Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Coleman* illustrates how a seemingly narrow exception—consent to enter for a welfare check—can expand into something approaching a broad investigative authority once officers cross the threshold. The case deserves attention not so much for its voluntariness holding, which is defensible, but for its remarkably thin treatment of the scope of that consent. The opinion effectively transforms a mother's grudging "okay" into authorization for a full canvass of the home, a protective pat-down of the suspected aggressor, and custodial-style interviews of minor children—producing the very evidence used to secure a search warrant. The doctrinal question this raises is whether *Florida v. Jimeno*'s objective-reasonableness standard meaningfully constrains police activity once an officer is inside a home pursuant to a welfare-check consent, or whether it functions instead as a rubber stamp.

## The Facts and the Key Exchange

The facts are drawn from body camera footage. Around 4:00 a.m., Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police responded to a 911 call from a stepdaughter reporting that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and was still inside the home. When Kizer arrived, Lisa stepped onto the porch and told him her children were inside. Asked whether her kids were going to be okay, she waffled—"I hope so"—and when Kizer asked if he could enter, she paused and said, "um … I'd prefer you not." That initial refusal is critical to the voluntariness analysis, because it demonstrates Lisa's capacity to decline.

The conversation continued. Lisa went inside to get her shoes, Kizer grew concerned and called into the house, and she eventually returned. Kizer then asked whether Coleman was calm and whether the kids were okay. Lisa hesitated again: "I … I … I don't … They're … They're okay right this second." Kizer responded with the line that became the centerpiece of the appeal: "Okay, um, I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Lisa said "Okay." Kizer asked about the dogs; Lisa assured him they wouldn't bite and told him where the children and Coleman were. She then opened the door, brushed the dogs aside, and led the officers inside.

What happened next is the heart of the matter. The officers scanned the home, located Coleman in a separate bedroom, and engaged him in a brief discussion. Coleman permitted Kizer to conduct a protective pat-down. Officers then spoke with the two children, who reported that Coleman had been loud and aggressive, had pointed a gun at their mother, and had threatened to shoot her and burn the house down. Those statements—obtained during what the court characterized as a welfare check—formed the basis for the search warrant that ultimately uncovered three firearms and led to Coleman's conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

## Voluntariness: A Defensible Holding

Coleman's primary argument on appeal was that Lisa's consent was not voluntary. He focused on Officer Kizer's use of the word "need," contending that "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside" carried an implied assertion of authority that rendered the consent coerced. The Seventh Circuit rejected this argument, and on the record before it, the voluntariness holding is hard to challenge.

The court emphasized two factors visible on the body camera footage. First, Kizer's tone and demeanor were those of concern, not coercion. Second, Lisa had already demonstrated her ability to refuse—she had told Kizer she'd prefer he not enter—suggesting that her subsequent consent was a genuine choice rather than submission to authority. The court also noted that Lisa was never in custody, that the entire conversation lasted only a few minutes, and that Kizer asked only twice for permission to enter. While Lisa may have felt "some degree of pressure," the court concluded that no coercion was exerted.

This is a close case on the voluntariness question, and reasonable minds could differ. Kizer's use of "need" is not nothing—it carries at least a hint of official insistence, and the qualifier "almost" does only so much to soften it. But the court's totality-of-the-circumstances analysis is grounded in the video record, and the fact that Lisa initially refused is strong evidence that her later consent was a voluntary shift rather than a capitulation. The opinion's treatment of the "need" language—acknowledging it as a "plenty fair argument" but concluding it was not coercive in context—is appropriately calibrated. On the voluntariness question, the court's reasoning is sound.

## The Scope Problem: From Welfare Check to Investigation

The more troubling aspect of *Coleman* lies in its scope-of-consent analysis, which the court dispatches in a few brief sentences. The governing standard comes from *Florida v. Jimeno*: courts ask "what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer" and the person giving consent. The question is objective, focused on what a reasonable person would believe they had authorized.

Applying that standard, the Seventh Circuit concluded that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa's consent because "their stated purpose was to check the welfare of her children inside, including by confirming that Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else. Doing so necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions." The court saw "no infirmity" in the district court's finding that the officers respected the scope of Lisa's consent.

This is where the analysis falters. The court's language—"necessarily required"—does a lot of work without earning it. The question under *Jimeno* is not what the officers believed they needed to do, but what a reasonable person in Lisa's shoes would have understood she was authorizing. The court skips past this inquiry almost entirely, substituting the officers' perspective for the consenting party's.

Consider what Lisa actually authorized. When Kizer said he was "sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that"—where "that" referred to the children being "okay right this second"—a reasonable person might understand Lisa to have consented to officers entering and visually confirming the children's safety. Perhaps that consent extends to walking to the bedroom where Lisa said the children were located. But the court's holding sweeps far beyond that. It treats Lisa's consent as encompassing a full canvas of the entire home, including locating Coleman in a separate bedroom—a room Lisa had already identified as his but had not invited officers to enter. It encompasses a protective pat-down of Coleman (though that was separately consented to by Coleman himself). And it encompasses questioning the children about specific incidents of domestic violence—questions that went well beyond "are you okay?" and produced the detailed, incriminating statements that formed the probable cause for the search warrant.

The conflation of a welfare check with a preliminary criminal investigation is the crux of the problem. A welfare check, at its core, is a protective measure: officers enter to confirm that the people inside are not in immediate danger. A preliminary investigation, by contrast, is an evidence-gathering exercise aimed at building a criminal case. The two can overlap in practice, and in the domestic violence context they frequently do. But the Fourth Amendment demands that the scope of consent be assessed from the perspective of the person who gave it, not from the perspective of the officers who may have a broader investigative purpose in mind.

Nothing in the exchange between Kizer and Lisa suggests she understood herself to be authorizing a full canvass and interviews. She was hesitant throughout the encounter—waffling about whether the children were safe, initially refusing entry, and responding to Kizer's second request with a brief "Okay" that the court itself acknowledges was given under at least some degree of pressure. The objective-reasonableness inquiry under *Jimeno* is supposed to protect people in exactly this situation, by asking whether a reasonable person would have understood the scope of their consent to extend to the activities the officers actually undertook. The Seventh Circuit's opinion does not meaningfully engage with that question.

## The Doctrinal Implications

The downstream effects of this holding are significant. If consent to "come inside to make sure" the children are okay is understood to encompass a full canvass of the home, a protective pat-down of a suspect, and detailed questioning of minor children about specific criminal conduct, then welfare-check consent has been functionally converted into a general investigative warrant. Officers responding to domestic violence calls—which are among the most common 911 dispatches—will have broad authority to enter homes and gather evidence without any independent showing of probable cause or exigent circumstances.

This is particularly troubling because the domestic violence context creates structural pressures that make consent less reliable as a marker of voluntary agreement. The person answering the door may be a victim of the very violence that prompted the police response. She may be fearful, confused, or operating under the influence of the same dynamics of coercion and control that characterize the underlying abuse. The Seventh Circuit acknowledges that Lisa "may have felt some degree of pressure to let the police enter her home," but treats this as irrelevant to the scope inquiry. Yet pressure that falls short of legal coercion can still distort a person's understanding of what they are authorizing, and the scope of consent analysis ought to account for that distortion.

Moreover, the court's decision to rest its holding entirely on consent allows it to sidestep the more demanding doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery, both of which the government offered as alternative grounds. The court expressly declined to reach these arguments, noting that its consent ruling made them unnecessary. But the practical effect is that the court avoids the harder question—whether the circumstances justified a warrantless entry under a more demanding standard—by resolving the case under the least demanding standard available. If consent for a welfare check can be this elastic, the exigent circumstances doctrine may become a redundancy in domestic violence cases.

The opinion closes with an observation that Officer Kizer and his colleagues "exhibited sound and balanced decision making in delicate and difficult circumstances." That may well be true as a matter of police practice, and nothing in this commentary should be read as criticizing officers who respond to domestic violence calls in the middle of the night with genuine concern for the safety of those inside. But the Fourth Amendment is not a reasonableness-of-police-practice standard. It is a constraint on government power, and its scope-of-consent doctrine is designed to ensure that when people grant limited permission to enter their homes, the police do not take that as license to conduct a full investigation. The Seventh Circuit's opinion in *Coleman* gives too little weight to that constraint.
