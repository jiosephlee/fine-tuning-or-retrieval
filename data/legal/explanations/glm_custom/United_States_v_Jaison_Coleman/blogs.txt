Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# "We Almost Need to Come Inside": The Fine Line Between Urgency and Coercion in Voluntariness Analysis

Domestic violence calls are among the most volatile situations police face, and they routinely generate Fourth Amendment questions that sit at the intersection of officer safety, victim protection, and constitutional limits on warrantless entry. The Seventh Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Coleman* illustrates just how thin the doctrinal line can be—and how much the voluntariness analysis turns on linguistic nuance captured by a body camera.

The case arose from a 4:00 a.m. 911 call reporting that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill his wife, Lisa, and was still inside the family home. When Officer Jamie Kizer arrived, Lisa stepped onto the porch but hesitated when asked whether her children were safe. She initially refused the officer's request to enter, saying "I'd prefer you not." Minutes later, after Lisa reappeared and again equivocated about the children's safety, Officer Kizer said: "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Lisa responded "Okay," told the officers where the children and Coleman were located, warned them about the dogs, and opened the door. The officers entered, found Coleman, spoke with the children—who described their father threatening their mother with a gun—and used that information to secure a search warrant that ultimately turned up firearms.

Coleman moved to suppress, arguing Lisa never voluntarily consented to entry. The district court disagreed, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed.

## The "Need" Problem

The doctrinal flashpoint is Officer Kizer's use of the word "need." Coleman pressed the argument that framing a request to enter as something officers "need" to do transforms a request into an assertion of authority—the kind of statement that, under *Bumper v. North Carolina*, cannot produce voluntary consent. In *Bumper*, the Supreme Court held that consent obtained after an officer's claim of lawful authority is no consent at all. Coleman's argument has intuitive force: when an officer says "we need to come inside," a reasonable person might hear a command dressed in polite language, not a genuine choice.

The Seventh Circuit acknowledged this was "a plenty fair argument." That concession is notable. The court did not dismiss the linguistic concern but instead subordinated it to the totality-of-circumstances test that governs voluntariness analysis. The court found three factors decisive: Officer Kizer's tone was concerned rather than commanding, Lisa had demonstrated the ability to deny consent by doing exactly that moments earlier, and the entire exchange lasted only a few minutes with no physical coercion or custody. On that record, the district court's finding of voluntariness was not clearly erroneous.

The opinion's reasoning is defensible but raises a question the court did not fully resolve: does the totality-of-circumstances test adequately police the distinction between urgency and coercion when officers frame entry as a necessity?

## Urgency as a Double-Edged Sword

Officer Kizer's statement operated on two levels. Substantively, he expressed genuine concern for children who might be in danger—a concern that turned out to be well-founded. Doctrinally, however, the statement implied that the officers had a duty to enter, which Lisa was in no position to prevent. The word "almost" did some mitigating work—"we almost need to come inside" is softer than "we need to come inside"—but the overall structure of the sentence positioned entry as the natural and expected course of action, with Lisa's assent reduced to a procedural formality.

The court's reliance on Lisa's prior refusal as evidence that her later consent was voluntary is the strongest part of the analysis. A person who has demonstrated the ability to say no is harder to characterize as having been overwhelmed. But the opinion's own recitation of facts reveals why this factor may be less reassuring than it appears. Between Lisa's initial refusal and her eventual consent, Officer Kizer continued questioning her, she went back inside, he called into the house to check on her, and the conversation continued. The second request came after Lisa had been engaged with the officer for several minutes in the middle of the night, having just reported—or having had reported on her behalf—a domestic violence incident. In that context, repeated requests, even absent explicit coercion, can functionally narrow a person's perceived options.

## The Limits of Totality-of-Circumstances Review

The voluntariness doctrine has long struggled with this problem. The *Schneckloth* totality-of-circumstances test was designed to be flexible enough to account for the wide variety of encounters between police and citizens. But flexibility has a cost: it can make it difficult to identify specific linguistic patterns that should categorically invalidate consent. The Seventh Circuit's opinion illustrates this dynamic. The court acknowledged that "need" was a fair target, noted that Lisa may have felt "some degree of pressure," but concluded that the absence of coercion—rather than the absence of pressure—controlled the outcome.

This framing is consistent with precedent. The Supreme Court has never held that pressure alone invalidates consent; *Bumper* requires a claim of lawful authority, and the voluntariness test asks whether consent was the product of coercion, not merely influence. But the line between pressure and coercion is doing a great deal of work in cases like this one, and the totality-of-circumstances test provides little structured guidance for lower courts drawing it.

The opinion's deferential standard of review—clear error—further constrains the appellate court's ability to police that line. The panel emphasized that it was reviewing body camera footage and could not identify clear error in the district court's factual finding. This is an honest application of the governing standard, but it means that the voluntariness of consent in domestic violence cases will turn heavily on the district court's impressionistic assessment of tone, demeanor, and timing—assessments that are difficult to challenge on appeal.

## Scope and the Domestic Violence Context

The court's scope-of-consent analysis is straightforward and unlikely to generate controversy. Having consented to entry for the purpose of checking on the children, the officers' conduct inside—locating occupants, asking basic questions, and conducting a brief protective sweep—fell within what a reasonable person would understand Lisa to have authorized. The information gathered led to a warrant, breaking the causal chain between the initial entry and the firearm recovery.

But the domestic violence setting deserves more doctrinal attention than the opinion gives it. Victims of domestic violence are often operating under duress that is invisible to a body camera. Lisa's equivocation—she "hoped" the children were okay, hesitated, went back inside—could reflect fear of Coleman, fear of police, or some combination. The voluntariness test, focused as it is on police conduct, has no mechanism for accounting for the possibility that a domestic violence victim's consent to police entry is shaped by coercion from the very person the officers are investigating. This is not a criticism of the Seventh Circuit's analysis, which faithfully applies existing doctrine, but it is a limitation of the doctrine itself.

## Downstream Implications

*Coleman* will be cited primarily for the proposition that an officer's expression of urgency, even using language that frames entry as a "need," does not invalidate consent when the overall interaction is brief, non-custodial, and non-coercive. The case adds to a body of Seventh Circuit law that treats the totality-of-circumstances test as capacious enough to absorb a wide range of officer conduct.

The more significant doctrinal question is whether any circuit will adopt a categorical rule that specific linguistic patterns—statements that frame entry as a necessity rather than a request—render consent per se involuntary. The Seventh Circuit's approach leaves that door open by acknowledging the force of Coleman's argument before resolving the case on the facts. For now, officers in the Seventh Circuit can express urgency, even framing entry as something they "need" to do, so long as the surrounding circumstances demonstrate that the homeowner retained the practical ability to say no.

Whether that is sufficient protection for Fourth Amendment rights in the home—particularly in domestic violence cases where the homeowner's autonomy may be constrained by forces the body camera cannot capture—is a question the doctrine has yet to answer.



# The Reluctant Consenter: Third-Party Consent Doctrine and the Complex Dynamics of Domestic Violence

Few scenarios test Fourth Amendment doctrine more severely than the domestic violence call. Officers arrive at a private residence, often in the dead of night, with incomplete information about what is happening inside. They must make split-second decisions about whether to enter a home—among the most constitutionally protected spaces—based on conversations with people who may be frightened, traumatized, or operating under constraints not visible to police. The Seventh Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Jaison Coleman* presents a fact pattern that lies at the heart of this tension, and the court's analysis raises important questions about whether the existing consent framework adequately accounts for the distinctive dynamics of domestic violence.

## The Facts and the Court's Holding

The facts are straightforward enough, at least on the surface. At 4:00 a.m., Marshfield, Wisconsin police responded to a 911 call from a stepdaughter reporting that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and was still inside the home. Officer Jamie Kizer knocked and spoke with Lisa on the porch. When asked whether her children were safe, Lisa waffled—first saying they were in her bedroom, then saying she "hope[d] so." When Officer Kizer asked to enter, Lisa initially declined: "I'd prefer you not." After a pause, Lisa went inside to get shoes. When she returned, Officer Kizer tried again. The critical exchange:

> **Officer Kizer:** "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that."
>
> **Lisa:** "Okay."

Lisa then told the officers where the children were, where Coleman was likely located, and assured them the dogs would not bite. She opened the door and physically facilitated the officers' entry by brushing the dogs aside. Once inside, officers spoke with the children, who reported that Coleman had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened to shoot her and burn down the house. This information led to a warrant, which turned up firearms. Coleman was charged and convicted under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

The Seventh Circuit affirmed, holding that Lisa's consent was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances and that the officers' in-home actions fell within the scope of that consent. Judge Scudder, writing for the panel, emphasized two factors: first, that Officer Kizer's tone was that of "someone concerned, not someone coercing," and second, that Lisa had demonstrated her capacity to decline by initially refusing entry. The court also rejected Coleman's argument that Officer Kizer's use of the word "need" rendered the request coercive, reasoning that the "totality of the circumstances" showed an officer expressing urgency about child welfare without overstepping.

## The Dual Role Problem

What makes this case doctrinally interesting—and what the opinion does not fully engage with—is Lisa's dual role. She was simultaneously the alleged victim of Coleman's threats and the person whose consent legitimized the police entry. Under *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974), a third party with "common authority" over a shared space can consent to a search that binds a co-occupant. The Matlock framework treats the consentor as a rights-holder who can waive Fourth Amendment protections for a shared space. But Matlock's common-authority principle was developed in contexts where the consentor and the target of the search have aligned interests—roommates, co-tenants, family members cooperating with law enforcement against a suspect.

In the domestic violence context, the consentor and the victim may be the same person, and the "target" of the search is the person who has been exercising coercive control over her. This complicates the voluntariness analysis in ways that the Seventh Circuit's opinion acknowledges only in passing. The court notes that Lisa was not in custody, that Officer Kizer asked permission rather than asserting authority, and that she had demonstrated the ability to refuse. These are the standard *Figueroa-Espana* factors, applied in a straightforward manner. But they may not capture the full picture of constraint that a domestic violence victim may experience even when the primary aggressor is in another room.

## Does "I'd Prefer You Not" Mean What the Court Says It Means?

The court places considerable weight on Lisa's initial refusal as evidence of her autonomy. The reasoning is intuitive: if she could say no once, her later "okay" must reflect a genuine change of mind. But this inference, while reasonable, may be too quick. Research on domestic violence and trauma responses documents that victims often engage in partial compliance strategies—yielding on some matters while resisting on others—as a way of maintaining some sense of control in an unpredictable situation. Lisa's initial refusal may have been an assertion of autonomy; it may also have been a calculated effort to manage two sources of pressure simultaneously: the officer at the door and the husband inside. Her refusal bought time. Her eventual consent may have reflected a reassessment of which pressure was more dangerous to resist.

None of this is to say that Lisa's consent was involuntary as a constitutional matter. The Seventh Circuit's application of the totality-of-circumstances test is defensible and perhaps even compelled by existing doctrine. Officer Kizer was calm, respectful, and non-threatening. He asked rather than demanded. Lisa appeared to understand the exchange. Under the current framework, that is enough. But the question worth asking is whether the current framework is calibrated to detect the forms of duress that domestic violence victims may experience—duress that does not come from the officer at the door but from the situation itself.

## The Word "Need" and the Boundaries of Persuasion

Coleman's strongest argument centered on Officer Kizer's statement that "we almost need to come inside." The court acknowledged this was "a plenty fair argument" but concluded that the totality of the circumstances neutralized any coercive quality the word might carry. The court's reasoning tracks a well-established principle: isolated words do not determine voluntariness; context does. *United States v. Hicks*, 539 F.3d 566, 570-71 (7th Cir. 2008).

But there is a doctrinal tension here that deserves more attention. In *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543, 548-49 (1968), the Supreme Court held that consent obtained after an officer falsely claims to have a warrant is no consent at all. The logic is that when an officer asserts lawful authority to enter, any subsequent "consent" is merely acquiescence to a show of force. Officer Kizer did not claim to have a warrant, but his use of "need"—softened by "almost"—carried an implicit assertion that he had a duty to enter, which Lisa may have understood as a statement that he would enter regardless. The court's conclusion that Lisa heard this as a request rather than a pronouncement is plausible from the body camera footage, but it rests on an inference about her subjective understanding that the objective-reasonableness framework is not designed to capture.

## The Video Evidence Problem

The court's analysis, like much modern Fourth Amendment jurisprudence, relies heavily on body camera footage. Judge Scudder cited *United States v. Norville* for the proposition that video can "evaporate any factual dispute." There is no doubt the footage is valuable—it shows Officer Kizer's demeanor, Lisa's responses, and the sequence of events. But video has limits. It captures what is visible and audible, not what is felt. It cannot show the internal calculation of a domestic violence victim deciding whether to let police into a home where the person who threatened her is waiting in the next room. The court's confidence that Lisa "demonstrated she understood what was transpiring" is an inference from external behavior—composure, verbal responsiveness, a prior refusal. These are reasonable inferences, but they are inferences, and the court might have acknowledged their limits more candidly.

## Toward a Context-Sensitive Framework?

The harder question is whether Fourth Amendment doctrine should develop a more context-sensitive framework for evaluating consent from alleged victims in domestic violence situations. The current totality-of-circumstances test is flexible in theory but tends in practice to gravitate toward observable factors—officer tone, duration of encounter, explicit threats—that may not detect the subtle dynamics at play. A more context-sensitive approach might require courts to consider the relationship between the consentor and the target of the search, the nature of the underlying call, and whether the consentor had a realistic opportunity to make a free and uncoerced decision.

The risk, of course, is that such a framework could undermine the very law enforcement response that domestic violence victims need. If courts begin presuming that consent from a DV victim is suspect, police may resort to warrantless entry under exigent-circumstances doctrine—a path the government argued in the alternative here—or may simply wait for situations to escalate. The Seventh Circuit's opinion hints at this concern, noting that the government's exigent-circumstances and inevitable-discovery arguments provided alternative grounds for affirmance. If consent becomes harder to establish in DV contexts, the practical result may simply be a shift in doctrinal justifications rather than a meaningful change in police conduct.

## The Scope of Consent and the Protective Sweep

The court's scope analysis deserves brief comment. Having found consent to enter for the purpose of checking the children's welfare, the court held that the officers' subsequent actions—scanning the home, locating Coleman, conducting a protective pat-down, speaking with the children—fell within the "objective reasonableness" standard of *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991). This holding is unremarkable as far as it goes: if Lisa consented to entry for the purpose of ensuring her children's safety, confirming that the person who threatened her wife was not an active danger was a necessary component of that purpose. But the holding also illustrates how consent, once given, can expand to encompass a fairly broad investigation. The officers' welfare check became a protective sweep, which became a search, which produced evidence supporting a warrant for a more comprehensive search. Each step was reasonable in isolation; the aggregate is a significant intrusion into the home. Courts should remain attentive to whether consent for a limited welfare check is being used as a gateway to a broader investigation.

## Conclusion

*United States v. Coleman* is, on its own terms, a defensible application of established doctrine. Officer Kizer acted with restraint and professionalism in genuinely difficult circumstances. The Seventh Circuit's holding that Lisa consented voluntarily is well-supported by the record and consistent with precedent. But the case also exposes a gap in the doctrinal framework. The totality-of-circumstances test, with its emphasis on observable factors and officer conduct, may not fully capture the constraints under which domestic violence victims operate when police arrive at the door. Whether that gap should be closed through doctrinal development, legislative guidance, or simply greater judicial awareness is a question the courts will need to confront as domestic violence calls continue to present some of the most vexing Fourth Amendment scenarios. For now, the Seventh Circuit has provided a careful and honest application of the existing framework—even if the framework itself may not be equal to the task.



## From Welfare Check to Warrant: The Expanding Scope of Consent in Home Entries

The Fourth Amendment's protections are at their zenith in the home, and the Supreme Court has long insisted that warrantless entry is the exception, not the rule. Yet the Seventh Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Coleman* illustrates how a seemingly narrow exception—consent to enter for a welfare check—can expand into something approaching a broad investigative authority once officers cross the threshold. The case deserves attention not so much for its voluntariness holding, which is defensible, but for its remarkably thin treatment of the scope of that consent. The opinion effectively transforms a mother's grudging "okay" into authorization for a full canvass of the home, a protective pat-down of the suspected aggressor, and custodial-style interviews of minor children—producing the very evidence used to secure a search warrant. The doctrinal question this raises is whether *Florida v. Jimeno*'s objective-reasonableness standard meaningfully constrains police activity once an officer is inside a home pursuant to a welfare-check consent, or whether it functions instead as a rubber stamp.

## The Facts and the Key Exchange

The facts are drawn from body camera footage. Around 4:00 a.m., Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police responded to a 911 call from a stepdaughter reporting that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and was still inside the home. When Kizer arrived, Lisa stepped onto the porch and told him her children were inside. Asked whether her kids were going to be okay, she waffled—"I hope so"—and when Kizer asked if he could enter, she paused and said, "um … I'd prefer you not." That initial refusal is critical to the voluntariness analysis, because it demonstrates Lisa's capacity to decline.

The conversation continued. Lisa went inside to get her shoes, Kizer grew concerned and called into the house, and she eventually returned. Kizer then asked whether Coleman was calm and whether the kids were okay. Lisa hesitated again: "I … I … I don't … They're … They're okay right this second." Kizer responded with the line that became the centerpiece of the appeal: "Okay, um, I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Lisa said "Okay." Kizer asked about the dogs; Lisa assured him they wouldn't bite and told him where the children and Coleman were. She then opened the door, brushed the dogs aside, and led the officers inside.

What happened next is the heart of the matter. The officers scanned the home, located Coleman in a separate bedroom, and engaged him in a brief discussion. Coleman permitted Kizer to conduct a protective pat-down. Officers then spoke with the two children, who reported that Coleman had been loud and aggressive, had pointed a gun at their mother, and had threatened to shoot her and burn the house down. Those statements—obtained during what the court characterized as a welfare check—formed the basis for the search warrant that ultimately uncovered three firearms and led to Coleman's conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

## Voluntariness: A Defensible Holding

Coleman's primary argument on appeal was that Lisa's consent was not voluntary. He focused on Officer Kizer's use of the word "need," contending that "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside" carried an implied assertion of authority that rendered the consent coerced. The Seventh Circuit rejected this argument, and on the record before it, the voluntariness holding is hard to challenge.

The court emphasized two factors visible on the body camera footage. First, Kizer's tone and demeanor were those of concern, not coercion. Second, Lisa had already demonstrated her ability to refuse—she had told Kizer she'd prefer he not enter—suggesting that her subsequent consent was a genuine choice rather than submission to authority. The court also noted that Lisa was never in custody, that the entire conversation lasted only a few minutes, and that Kizer asked only twice for permission to enter. While Lisa may have felt "some degree of pressure," the court concluded that no coercion was exerted.

This is a close case on the voluntariness question, and reasonable minds could differ. Kizer's use of "need" is not nothing—it carries at least a hint of official insistence, and the qualifier "almost" does only so much to soften it. But the court's totality-of-the-circumstances analysis is grounded in the video record, and the fact that Lisa initially refused is strong evidence that her later consent was a voluntary shift rather than a capitulation. The opinion's treatment of the "need" language—acknowledging it as a "plenty fair argument" but concluding it was not coercive in context—is appropriately calibrated. On the voluntariness question, the court's reasoning is sound.

## The Scope Problem: From Welfare Check to Investigation

The more troubling aspect of *Coleman* lies in its scope-of-consent analysis, which the court dispatches in a few brief sentences. The governing standard comes from *Florida v. Jimeno*: courts ask "what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer" and the person giving consent. The question is objective, focused on what a reasonable person would believe they had authorized.

Applying that standard, the Seventh Circuit concluded that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa's consent because "their stated purpose was to check the welfare of her children inside, including by confirming that Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else. Doing so necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions." The court saw "no infirmity" in the district court's finding that the officers respected the scope of Lisa's consent.

This is where the analysis falters. The court's language—"necessarily required"—does a lot of work without earning it. The question under *Jimeno* is not what the officers believed they needed to do, but what a reasonable person in Lisa's shoes would have understood she was authorizing. The court skips past this inquiry almost entirely, substituting the officers' perspective for the consenting party's.

Consider what Lisa actually authorized. When Kizer said he was "sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that"—where "that" referred to the children being "okay right this second"—a reasonable person might understand Lisa to have consented to officers entering and visually confirming the children's safety. Perhaps that consent extends to walking to the bedroom where Lisa said the children were located. But the court's holding sweeps far beyond that. It treats Lisa's consent as encompassing a full canvas of the entire home, including locating Coleman in a separate bedroom—a room Lisa had already identified as his but had not invited officers to enter. It encompasses a protective pat-down of Coleman (though that was separately consented to by Coleman himself). And it encompasses questioning the children about specific incidents of domestic violence—questions that went well beyond "are you okay?" and produced the detailed, incriminating statements that formed the probable cause for the search warrant.

The conflation of a welfare check with a preliminary criminal investigation is the crux of the problem. A welfare check, at its core, is a protective measure: officers enter to confirm that the people inside are not in immediate danger. A preliminary investigation, by contrast, is an evidence-gathering exercise aimed at building a criminal case. The two can overlap in practice, and in the domestic violence context they frequently do. But the Fourth Amendment demands that the scope of consent be assessed from the perspective of the person who gave it, not from the perspective of the officers who may have a broader investigative purpose in mind.

Nothing in the exchange between Kizer and Lisa suggests she understood herself to be authorizing a full canvass and interviews. She was hesitant throughout the encounter—waffling about whether the children were safe, initially refusing entry, and responding to Kizer's second request with a brief "Okay" that the court itself acknowledges was given under at least some degree of pressure. The objective-reasonableness inquiry under *Jimeno* is supposed to protect people in exactly this situation, by asking whether a reasonable person would have understood the scope of their consent to extend to the activities the officers actually undertook. The Seventh Circuit's opinion does not meaningfully engage with that question.

## The Doctrinal Implications

The downstream effects of this holding are significant. If consent to "come inside to make sure" the children are okay is understood to encompass a full canvass of the home, a protective pat-down of a suspect, and detailed questioning of minor children about specific criminal conduct, then welfare-check consent has been functionally converted into a general investigative warrant. Officers responding to domestic violence calls—which are among the most common 911 dispatches—will have broad authority to enter homes and gather evidence without any independent showing of probable cause or exigent circumstances.

This is particularly troubling because the domestic violence context creates structural pressures that make consent less reliable as a marker of voluntary agreement. The person answering the door may be a victim of the very violence that prompted the police response. She may be fearful, confused, or operating under the influence of the same dynamics of coercion and control that characterize the underlying abuse. The Seventh Circuit acknowledges that Lisa "may have felt some degree of pressure to let the police enter her home," but treats this as irrelevant to the scope inquiry. Yet pressure that falls short of legal coercion can still distort a person's understanding of what they are authorizing, and the scope of consent analysis ought to account for that distortion.

Moreover, the court's decision to rest its holding entirely on consent allows it to sidestep the more demanding doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery, both of which the government offered as alternative grounds. The court expressly declined to reach these arguments, noting that its consent ruling made them unnecessary. But the practical effect is that the court avoids the harder question—whether the circumstances justified a warrantless entry under a more demanding standard—by resolving the case under the least demanding standard available. If consent for a welfare check can be this elastic, the exigent circumstances doctrine may become a redundancy in domestic violence cases.

The opinion closes with an observation that Officer Kizer and his colleagues "exhibited sound and balanced decision making in delicate and difficult circumstances." That may well be true as a matter of police practice, and nothing in this commentary should be read as criticizing officers who respond to domestic violence calls in the middle of the night with genuine concern for the safety of those inside. But the Fourth Amendment is not a reasonableness-of-police-practice standard. It is a constraint on government power, and its scope-of-consent doctrine is designed to ensure that when people grant limited permission to enter their homes, the police do not take that as license to conduct a full investigation. The Seventh Circuit's opinion in *Coleman* gives too little weight to that constraint.
