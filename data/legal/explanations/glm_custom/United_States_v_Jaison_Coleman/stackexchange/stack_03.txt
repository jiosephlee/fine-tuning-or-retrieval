Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### Does a prior refusal to consent make a later grant of consent more likely to be deemed voluntary?
Question:
In Coleman, Lisa initially refused entry ('I'd prefer you not'), but later consented after the officer asked again in a different way. The court used her initial refusal as evidence that she understood she could say no and that her later consent was voluntary. But I've also read cases where repeated requests after a refusal are considered coercive—the idea being that badgering someone into compliance undermines voluntariness. Here, the officer only asked twice and the conversation lasted only a few minutes. Is there a framework for determining when re-asking for consent after a refusal crosses into coercion? Does the fact that the second request was phrased differently matter?
Answer:
## Does a prior refusal make later consent more likely voluntary?

Yes, and the opinion in *Coleman* illustrates why. The court treats Lisa's initial refusal—"I'd prefer you not"—as evidence that she understood she had the right to say no, which in turn supports the conclusion that her later consent was an exercise of free will rather than the product of coercion. The logic is straightforward: a person who has already demonstrated the ability to refuse has shown that any subsequent agreement is not merely acquiescence to perceived authority.

## What is the framework for voluntariness?

The court applies the **totality of the circumstances** test, citing *Figueroa-Espana*, which lists several non-exhaustive factors:

1. Age, intelligence, and education
2. Whether the person was advised of constitutional rights
3. Duration of detention before consent
4. Whether consent was immediate or prompted by **repeated requests**
5. Whether physical coercion was used
6. Whether the person was in custody

The fourth factor directly addresses your question about re-asking. Notably, the court does not treat repeated requests as automatically coercive. Instead, it weighs the number of requests alongside the other factors—in particular, the duration of the encounter, the officer's tone, and whether the person was in custody.

## How does the court distinguish acceptable re-asking from coercive badgering?

The opinion does not draw a bright line. Instead, several features of the encounter weigh against a finding of coercion:

- **Only two requests.** The officer asked twice, not repeatedly over an extended period.
- **Brief duration.** The entire conversation lasted "but a few minutes."
- **No custody.** Lisa was never in custody.
- **Concerned tone, not coercive tone.** The court emphasizes that Officer Kizer's "tone and demeanor throughout was that of someone concerned, not someone coercing."
- **No explicit threats or physical coercion.**

The court also rejects Coleman's argument that the officer's use of the word "need" ("I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside") was inherently coercive. The court calls this focus "too narrow" and holds that the totality of circumstances—not isolated words—controls the voluntariness inquiry.

## Does the different phrasing of the second request matter?

It matters, but only as part of the totality analysis, not as a standalone rule. The second request differed from the first in two ways: it was framed as an expression of concern for the children's safety ("to make sure of that") rather than a direct request to enter, and it conveyed some urgency. The court found that this framing, combined with the officer's calm demeanor, placed the request on the permissible side of the line. The court reasoned that Officer Kizer "expressed concern for the safety of Lisa's children and relayed that concern with some urgency and intensity" but did not "overstep[] and coerc[e] her consent."

## Bottom line

There is no mechanical rule that re-asking after a refusal invalidates consent. The inquiry is fact-intensive and holistic. A prior refusal generally **helps** the government's voluntariness argument because it demonstrates the person knew they could refuse. Re-asking becomes problematic when, viewed alongside the other *Figueroa-Espana* factors—duration, custody, tone, physical coercion—it begins to look like badgering designed to wear down the person's will. *Coleman* falls on the permissible side because the encounter was short, the officer was calm, the requests were limited, and Lisa was never in custody.
