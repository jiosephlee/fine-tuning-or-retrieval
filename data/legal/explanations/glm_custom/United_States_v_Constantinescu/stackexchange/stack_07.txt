Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How does the "object of the fraud" requirement work, and what does it mean that defendants "had money in mind"?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the court quotes Kousisis for the proposition that a defendant commits securities fraud when "obtaining the victim's money or property" was "an object of his fraud." Later, the court says the indictment's allegation that defendants pumped and dumped to gain a profit shows they "had money in mind." I'm confused about what "object" means here. Is this a subjective test (what the defendant actually intended) or an objective test (what a reasonable person would infer from the scheme)? Also, the phrase "had money in mind" seems informal—does this refer to a specific legal standard? If the defendants had multiple objectives (like gaining social media followers, building their brand, AND making money), does the fraud statute require money to be the primary object, or just one of several objects?
Answer:
## The "object of the fraud" is the defendant's purpose, and it need only be *one* object among several

### Subjective, not objective

The opinion frames "object" as a question of the defendant's own purpose in carrying out the scheme—not what a reasonable person would infer from the scheme's design. The court states that "defendants' object was to obtain money, and they did so by fraudulently inducing their followers to purchase securities." It then quotes *Kousisis* for the proposition that the indictment's allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped to gain a profit shows that they 'had money in mind.'" 145 S. Ct. at 1397. This language—both "object" and "had money in mind"—is directed at what the defendants themselves sought to accomplish through their deception. The court is examining the indictment's allegations about the defendants' intent and the design of their scheme, not conducting a reasonable-person inquiry.

Note that "had money in mind" is a direct quotation from the Supreme Court's opinion in *Kousisis*, not informal gloss by the Fifth Circuit. It is simply the Court's shorthand for the same concept: the defendant's fraud was purposefully directed at obtaining the victim's money or property.

### "An object" means one of potentially several

The court quotes *Kousisis* for the standard that a defendant commits securities fraud when "obtaining the victim's money or property" was "**an object** of his fraud." The indefinite article "an" is doing real work here. The fraud statutes do not require that obtaining money be the sole purpose of the scheme, nor does the opinion suggest it must be the primary one. The court's reasoning supports this reading: it identifies the profit motive as sufficient based on the indictment's allegations that defendants pumped and dumped securities "to gain a profit," without ever suggesting that profit must have been the exclusive or dominant motive. Had the statute required money to be the *primary* object, one would expect the court to say so—or at minimum to address the possibility that the defendants had other goals alongside making money. It does neither.

So if defendants had multiple objectives—building a social media following, promoting their brand, and making money—the fraud statute is satisfied so long as obtaining the victims' money or property was *one* of the objects of the deception. The opinion does not require a hierarchy among those objects.

### How it works in practice

The opinion illustrates the standard through its application. The indictment alleged that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." The court treated this as sufficient because it showed the defendants' scheme was designed to obtain their followers' money by tricking them into buying securities at artificially inflated prices. The defendants' argument that they intended only to profit—not to harm victims—was rejected as "a distinction without a difference," because the fraud statutes focus on whether the defendant schemed to obtain the victim's money or property, not on whether the defendant sought to cause net economic loss. In other words, the "object" inquiry asks whether obtaining money was part of the defendant's purpose in deceiving the victim—and here, the indictment's allegation of a $114 million profit from the pump-and-dump scheme was enough to establish that it was.
