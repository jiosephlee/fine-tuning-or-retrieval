Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Why does the securities fraud statute (§ 1348) borrow concepts from mail and wire fraud statutes, and what does this mean for interpreting its elements?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the Fifth Circuit notes that 18 U.S.C. § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes" and that courts often rely on mail and wire fraud cases when analyzing securities fraud charges. The court then cites United States v. Greenlaw for this proposition. I don't understand why this borrowing occurs. Is it because the statutes have similar text? Are there legislative history reasons? And practically, does this mean that any development in mail/wire fraud jurisprudence automatically applies to securities fraud under § 1348? Are there any limits to this borrowing principle, or could a future Supreme Court decision about mail fraud completely change how § 1348 is interpreted?
Answer:
## Why § 1348 Borrows from Mail and Wire Fraud

The Fifth Circuit's statement that § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes" rests primarily on **textual parallelism**. All three statutes share the core phrase "scheme to defraud," and § 1348's two prongs closely track the structure of §§ 1341 and 1343: one prong prohibits schemes "to defraud," and the other prohibits schemes "to obtain... by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises... any money or property." Because the operative language is shared, courts treat the terms as having the same meaning unless there is reason to do otherwise.

The opinion doesn't discuss legislative history, so I won't speculate on that. The practical motivation the opinion does surface is **gap-filling**: the court notes there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit." When direct precedent on § 1348 is sparse, the well-developed body of mail and wire fraud law provides a ready interpretive resource for the shared terms—"scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud."

## What This Means in Practice

The opinion demonstrates the borrowing in action. The court derives the elements of § 1348 from *Greenlaw*, which itself drew on *Evans*—a mail/wire fraud case—for the definitions of "scheme to defraud" (a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value") and "intent to defraud" (intent to deceive and to cause harm from the deceit). The court then applies *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, both of which were mail/wire fraud cases, directly to the § 1348 context. So yes—developments in fraud jurisprudence at the Supreme Court level do shape how § 1348 is interpreted, at least in the Fifth Circuit.

## Limits on the Borrowing

The opinion indicates at least two limits:

1. **"Key concepts," not wholesale importation.** The court says § 1348 borrows "key concepts"—specifically "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud"—not every doctrinal development from mail/wire fraud. Elements unique to mail fraud (use of the mails) or wire fraud (use of interstate wires) have no analogue in § 1348, which instead requires a connection to securities.

2. **The property requirement constrains both statutes equally.** *Ciminelli* held that the fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests," not the right to valuable economic information. The court applies that limit to § 1348, distinguishing the indictment (which alleged a fraudulent-inducement scheme to deprive victims of *money*) from the rejected right-to-control theory. Similarly, *Kousisis* confirmed that the fraud statutes require that obtaining the victim's money or property be an "object" of the scheme, even if the victim suffers no net loss. These limits apply to § 1348 not because they are borrowed, but because the shared text carries the same substantive boundary.

So a future Supreme Court decision narrowing or expanding the "scheme to defraud" concept in the mail/wire fraud context would likely affect § 1348 in the Fifth Circuit—but only to the extent the decision interprets language that the two statutes actually share.
