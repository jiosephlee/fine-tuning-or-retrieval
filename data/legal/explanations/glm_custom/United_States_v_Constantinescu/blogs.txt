Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Navigating the Ciminelli-Kousisis Divide: Why the Fifth Circuit Got the Scheme-to-Defraud Element Right in Constantinescu

## Why This Case Matters

The Supreme Court's recent efforts to constrain federal fraud law have left lower courts with a delicate doctrinal task: distinguishing between fraud theories the Court has rejected and those it has preserved. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit navigated that divide with notable precision, reversing the dismissal of a securities fraud indictment against a group of social-media influencers accused of operating a "pump and dump" scheme that netted $114 million. The decision matters not only for its immediate holding—that the indictment alleged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme—but also for what it reveals about the practical administrability of the line the Supreme Court drew last Term between *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*.

At stake is a question that has troubled fraud doctrine for years: when does deception cross from merely depriving someone of information into the territory of criminal fraud? The Fifth Circuit's answer, grounded firmly in the text of the indictment, offers a clean and defensible application of the Supreme Court's recent framework—but it also exposes some lingering tensions that will continue to test lower courts.

## The Scheme and the District Court's Dismissal

The defendants—each with substantial social-media followings—allegedly held themselves out as skilled traders, posted misleading information about their trading positions and the prospects of certain securities, encouraged their followers to buy, and then secretly sold their own positions at the artificially inflated prices. The indictment charged conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. One defendant pleaded guilty; the rest moved to dismiss.

The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. In the district court's view, the indictment essentially alleged that defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information"—the sort of theory the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli*.

## The Fifth Circuit's Reversal

The Fifth Circuit's analysis proceeds in two moves, each tracking a recent Supreme Court decision.

First, the court distinguished *Ciminelli*, where the Supreme Court held that the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests and that the "right to valuable economic information" is not such an interest. The right-to-control theory advanced there—that a defendant commits fraud by depriving a victim of information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions—could not support a fraud conviction. But the Fifth Circuit observed that the indictment in *Constantinescu* did not rely on the right-to-control theory at all. Rather, it alleged that defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property." This, the court explained, is a straightforward "fraudulent-inducement theory," which the Supreme Court expressly validated in *Kousisis*: "a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."

Second, the court addressed the intent element. Defendants argued that they intended to profit, not to harm their followers, and that the followers received securities in exchange for their money. The court dispatched this argument by direct citation to *Kousisis*, which held that "a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain the victim's money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The followers' receipt of securities—even securities at market value—did not negate the fraud. As the court put it, the defendants' intent to profit and intent to harm were "a distinction without a difference," because the indictment alleged that their object was to obtain money from followers through fraudulent inducement.

## The Ciminelli-Kousisis Line: Administrable in Practice?

The Fifth Circuit's reasoning is sound, and the result seems clearly correct on the facts alleged. But the decision also highlights a structural tension in the post-*Ciminelli* landscape that courts will need to manage.

Consider the thinness of the doctrinal line. In *Ciminelli*, the defendant deceived a government official about how contracts were awarded; the victim was deprived of information needed to make an economic decision. In *Kousisis*, the defendant deceived a government agency about whether he was using a disadvantaged-business subcontractor; the victim was tricked into entering a contract and handing over money. In *Constantinescu*, the defendants deceived their followers about their trading positions and the prospects of certain securities; the victims were tricked into buying securities and handing over money.

The distinction the Fifth Circuit draws turns on what the indictment alleges the scheme was designed to obtain. If the indictment says the defendant sought to deprive the victim of information, that is *Ciminelli* and fails. If the indictment says the defendant sought to trick the victim into parting with money or property, that is *Kousisis* and stands. This is a framing-dependent distinction, and the Fifth Circuit was candid about it: the court noted that the indictment "does not mention the right-to-control theory" and instead alleges fraudulent inducement. The implication is that the same underlying conduct might be charged under either theory—and that the government's charging decisions matter enormously.

This is not necessarily a criticism. The Supreme Court itself recognized in *Kousisis* that the fraudulent-inducement theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory," and the Fifth Circuit faithfully applied that distinction. But the administrability of the line depends on courts looking past labels to the substance of what the government actually alleges. A defendant might argue that a purported fraudulent-inducement charge is really a right-to-control theory in disguise—particularly in cases where the victim received something of equal value in return and the "deception" concerned information relevant to the victim's decision-making. The Fifth Circuit's analysis, which looks at whether the indictment alleges the defendant induced victims to "part with their money or property," provides a workable test, but it will require careful application in closer cases than this one.

## The No-Net-Loss Principle and Its Reach

Perhaps the most significant doctrinal point in *Constantinescu* is the court's straightforward application of *Kousisis*'s holding that fraud does not require the victim to suffer net economic loss. The defendants argued that they intended to profit, not to harm anyone, and that their followers received securities in return. The Fifth Circuit rejected this without hesitation, citing *Kousisis* for the proposition that "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

This principle has broad implications. In the pump-and-dump context, victims did likely suffer losses—the securities they purchased were artificially inflated, and the price collapsed when defendants dumped their shares. But the Fifth Circuit's reasoning does not depend on that fact. It says, in effect, that even if the victims had received securities at fair market value, the fraud would still be complete. That is a broad reading of the fraud statutes, and it aligns with *Kousisis*'s rejection of any "mirror image" requirement between the defendant's gain and the victim's loss.

The question for future cases is how far this principle extends. If a defendant makes a material misrepresentation that induces someone to enter a transaction, and the transaction is otherwise economically fair, is it still criminal fraud? *Kousisis* and *Constantinescu* suggest yes. That is a powerful prosecutorial tool, and one that courts should be attentive to in cases that lack the egregious facts of a pump-and-dump scheme.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

*Constantinescu* is a clean application of the Supreme Court's recent fraud jurisprudence, but it also signals several things about the trajectory of federal fraud law.

First, the decision confirms that *Kousisis* gave the government a durable and broadly applicable theory of fraud liability. The fraudulent-inducement theory survives *Ciminelli* and can support prosecutions in a wide range of contexts where victims are tricked into parting with money or property, even if they receive something of value in return. Prosecutors now have a clear roadmap: charge the scheme as one to obtain money or property through deception, not as one to deprive victims of information.

Second, the decision suggests that the *Ciminelli* constraint may be narrower than some initially thought. The right-to-control theory is dead, but only when the government relies solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. Where the indictment alleges a traditional fraudulent-inducement scheme, *Ciminelli* poses no barrier. The Fifth Circuit's opinion makes this distinction explicit and administrable, at least on the facts before it.

Third, the case is a reminder that the sufficiency of an indictment is a low bar. The Fifth Circuit reviewed the dismissal de novo, took the indictment's allegations as true, and asked only whether the indictment alleged each element of the offense. At this stage, the government need not prove that the scheme actually worked or that victims actually lost money—it need only allege a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. The court's observation that defendants concede the indictment adequately alleges deception underscores how modest the threshold is.

Finally, the decision will likely embolden prosecutors in the social-media fraud space. The defendants in *Constantinescu* operated through platforms where influence and followership are the currency of persuasion. The Fifth Circuit's holding that inducing followers to purchase securities through misrepresentations states a fraud offense provides a clear legal foundation for pursuing similar schemes. Whether courts will extend this reasoning to other forms of social-media-driven investment fraud—crypto "rug pulls," for instance, or influencer-driven NFT promotions—remains to be seen, but the doctrinal framework is in place.

## Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit's decision in *Constantinescu* is a careful and well-reasoned application of the Supreme Court's recent fraud jurisprudence. The court correctly identified that the indictment alleged a fraudulent-inducement scheme, not a right-to-control theory, and correctly held that the intent element does not require the defendant to seek the victim's economic harm. The Ciminelli-Kousisis divide is not always easy to navigate, but the Fifth Circuit showed that it can be done with rigor. The harder cases—where the line between depriving victims of information and tricking them into parting with money is less clear—will test whether lower courts can maintain the distinction that the Supreme Court has drawn and that the Fifth Circuit has now applied.



# Profit Without Harm: Constantinescu and the Growing Reach of Fraudulent-Inducement Theory After Kousisis

When is a lie told for profit also a lie told to harm? That question sits at the heart of *United States v. Constantinescu*, a Fifth Circuit decision that reverses the dismissal of a securities-fraud indictment against a group of social-media influencers alleged to have orchestrated a "pump and dump" scheme. The case matters not because the facts are novel—pump-and-dump schemes are as old as the securities markets—but because it shows how quickly the Supreme Court's recent fraud jurisprudence is reshaping the boundaries of criminal liability in ways that may surprise defendants and prosecutors alike.

## The Scheme and the District Court's Dismissal

The defendants—eight influencers with large social-media followings—are alleged to have purchased securities, promoted them to their followers through false and misleading posts, and then secretly sold their positions at a profit, netting approximately $114 million. The superseding indictment charged conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. One defendant pleaded guilty; the rest moved to dismiss.

The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. In the district court's view, the indictment merely alleged that defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information"—the kind of non-property interest that the Supreme Court had held insufficient to sustain a fraud conviction in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023). The government appealed.

## The Fifth Circuit's Reversal: Distinguishing Ciminelli, Applying Kousisis

The Fifth Circuit's analysis is straightforward in its execution but significant in its implications. Judge Engelhardt's opinion first dispatches the *Ciminelli* argument. The indictment, the court observed, does not rely on a right-to-control theory at all. It alleges a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme: defendants used material misrepresentations to trick their followers into purchasing securities—that is, into parting with their money. Under the fraudulent-inducement theory that the Supreme Court reaffirmed in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025), that is sufficient. The victims' property interest is the money they handed over, not some abstract right to accurate information.

This portion of the analysis is clean and unremarkable. The district court appears to have misread the indictment by importing a right-to-control framework that the government did not actually advance. The Fifth Circuit corrected that error, and rightly so.

The more interesting—and more consequential—part of the opinion addresses the intent-to-defraud element. Defendants argued that even if the indictment alleged a scheme to obtain money, it did not allege an intent to *harm* their followers. After all, the followers received market-value securities in exchange for their money. The defendants' object was personal profit, not victim injury. Under a traditional understanding of fraud, which requires both deceit and resulting harm, this argument has considerable intuitive force.

The Fifth Circuit rejected it, relying directly on *Kousisis*. There, the Supreme Court held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain the victim's money or property "regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—whether or not the victim suffers a net pecuniary loss. The court found this dispositive: defendants' followers' receipt of market-value securities did not obviate the fraud charges. The intent to obtain money through deception was itself sufficient.

## Severing Fraud from Net Loss: Doctrinal Implications

This is where the opinion's brevity belies its doctrinal significance. The Fifth Circuit's application of *Kousisis* effectively severs the intent-to-defraud element from any requirement of intended economic harm. A defendant who tricks a victim into a transaction, receives the victim's money, and gives something of equivalent value in return has still committed fraud—so long as the deception was material and the defendant "had money in mind."

This is a substantial departure from the common-law fraud tradition, which generally requires both reliance and damages. At common law, a plaintiff who received exactly what he bargained for, at a fair price, would struggle to prove the injury element of a fraud claim. The criminal fraud statutes have always been broader than their common-law counterparts, but *Kousisis* and now *Constantinescu* make clear just how much broader. The government need not prove that the defendant intended to leave the victim worse off. It need only prove that the defendant intended to obtain the victim's money through deception.

The implications extend beyond securities fraud. Because Section 1348 borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes, the Fifth Circuit's reasoning applies with equal force to fraud prosecutions across the federal code. Any case involving a fraudulent-inducement theory—where a defendant misrepresents something material to induce a transaction—can now proceed without proof of intended net loss. That includes procurement fraud, real-estate transactions, consumer fraud, and a host of other contexts where a defendant provides something of value in exchange for the property obtained through deception.

## The Puffery Problem and the Limits of the Theory

The dissenting instinct—though no dissent was written here—would be to worry about where this leads. If fraud does not require intended harm, what distinguishes criminal fraud from aggressive sales tactics, commercial puffery, or the ordinary exaggerations that pervade marketplace communications? The question is not fanciful. Securities markets are full of commentators who promote stocks they hold while expressing optimism about future prices. Not all of them disclose their positions. Not all of them are right. The line between enthusiastic promotion and fraudulent inducement has always been blurry, and the *Kousisis* framework pushes that line closer to criminal liability.

The answer, such as it is, lies in the materiality requirement and the specific intent to deceive. The *Constantinescu* indictment alleges that defendants posted false information about their trading positions and the potential price of securities—that they were secretly selling while publicly claiming to be buying or holding, and that they coordinated to create artificial price inflation. Those are specific, actionable misrepresentations, not vague expressions of optimism. The opinion is careful to ground the fraud in the defendants' deception about their own positions and the deliberate manipulation of price through coordinated conduct. Puffery—subjective opinions about a security's prospects, vague claims about future performance—would not satisfy the material-misrepresentation element in the same way.

But the margin is thinner than it looks. The court's reasoning suggests that a defendant who materially misrepresents any fact bearing on a victim's decision to part with money has committed fraud, regardless of whether the victim received fair value. In a world where social-media influencers routinely promote products they hold, use, or stand to benefit from, the question of which misrepresentations are "material" enough to trigger criminal liability takes on outsized importance. The Fifth Circuit did not address that question, because the indictment's allegations were sufficiently specific. But future cases will press harder.

## What Constantinescu Signals

*Constantinescu* is, in one sense, a straightforward application of *Kousisis* to a familiar fact pattern. The Fifth Circuit corrected a district court that had overread *Ciminelli* and underappreciated the government's theory of the case. But the opinion also signals that the post-*Kousisis* fraud landscape is one where the absence of intended victim harm is no longer a meaningful defense. Defendants who obtain money through material deception will face fraud charges whether or not their victims suffered a loss, and whether or not the defendants intended one.

That is a significant expansion of criminal fraud liability, and it is happening with remarkably little doctrinal friction. The Supreme Court endorsed the fraudulent-inducement theory in *Kousisis*; the Fifth Circuit has now shown how readily it applies. Other circuits will likely follow. The real doctrinal work going forward will be at the margins—defining materiality, distinguishing deception from puffery, and identifying the point at which a transaction induced through misrepresentation becomes criminal fraud rather than a bad bargain. *Constantinescu* does not answer those questions, but it ensures they will be asked.



# From Boiler Rooms to Group Chats: Social Media Pump-and-Dump Schemes and the Adaptability of Traditional Fraud Statutes

When Congress enacted the securities fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, in the wake of Enron and WorldCom, it was responding to corporate accounting scandals perpetrated by insiders with fiduciary obligations to shareholders. The statute's language—prohibiting schemes to "defraud any person in connection with" a security—was broad enough to reach a wide range of misconduct, but the paradigmatic defendant was a corporate officer, auditor, or broker-dealer. Last month's Fifth Circuit decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* tests the outer boundaries of that paradigm. The alleged fraudsters were not corporate insiders or licensed professionals. They were social-media influencers—finfluencers, in current parlance—who used their follower counts to orchestrate pump-and-dump schemes that generated $114 million in illicit profits. The case matters not only for what it says about the reach of § 1348, but for what it reveals about the capacity of traditional fraud statutes to address decentralized, platform-mediated securities manipulation.

## The Scheme and the District Court's Dismissal

The facts alleged in the superseding indictment are straightforward in concept, if novel in execution. Eight defendants, each with substantial social-media followings across platforms, held themselves out as skilled traders. According to the government, they would purchase a security, promote it to their followers through false and misleading posts—misrepresenting their own trading positions and predicting price increases—and then sell their holdings once the followers' purchases drove up the price. The indictment alleged that this pump-and-dump cycle netted the defendants $114 million. One defendant pleaded guilty; the rest moved to dismiss.

The district court granted the motion, reasoning that the indictment failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. In the district court's view, the indictment amounted to an allegation that defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information"—the information necessary to make informed trading decisions—rather than money or property. This framing placed the case squarely within the territory the Supreme Court had recently declared off-limits in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023), where the Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory of fraud under which the government could establish wire fraud merely by showing that a defendant deprived a victim of valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions.

## The Fifth Circuit's Reversal: Ciminelli Distinguished, Kousisis Applied

The Fifth Circuit's reversal, authored by Judge Engelhardt, turned on a careful distinction between the right-to-control theory rejected in *Ciminelli* and the fraudulent-inducement theory the Supreme Court subsequently affirmed in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025). The distinction is doctrinally precise but practically intuitive. The right-to-control theory treats the victim's property as the right to control his assets; the fraudulent-inducement theory treats the victim's property as the money or property itself, which the defendant obtains through material misrepresentations.

The Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment alleged the latter, not the former. The indictment did not mention the right-to-control theory. It alleged that defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money. As the court put it, drawing on *Kousisis*, the indictment alleged a "fraudulent-inducement theory, under which a defendant 'uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.'" The followers' money was the object of the scheme; the deception was the means. This was not a case where the government was trying to prosecute the deprivation of information alone.

The intent-to-defraud element required a similarly careful parsing of recent Supreme Court precedent. Defendants argued that the indictment alleged only that they intended to profit, not that they intended to harm their followers. This argument implicitly relied on a net-loss requirement: if the followers received market-value securities in exchange for their money, and if the defendants did not specifically intend to leave the followers worse off, then there was no intent to defraud. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, citing *Kousisis* for the proposition that "a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he 'schem[es] to "obtain" the victim's "money or property," regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.'" The court noted that the defendants' profit motive—pumping and dumping to gain a profit—showed that they "had money in mind," which was sufficient to establish the object of the fraud. The followers' receipt of securities at market value did not negate the fraud, because a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.

## The Fiduciary Duty Question: Does It Matter Under § 1348?

One notable feature of *Constantinescu* is what the opinion does not discuss: fiduciary duty. In traditional securities fraud cases, the defendant's fiduciary obligation to the victim is often central. A corporate insider who trades on material nonpublic information violates Rule 10b-5 because he owes a duty to the shareholders from whom he effectively trades. A broker-dealer who recommends a security to a client while secretly selling his own position is committing fraud because he owes the client a fiduciary duty. The misrepresentation in these cases is actionable not merely because it is false, but because it breaches a preexisting legal obligation of trust and confidence.

The defendants in *Constantinescu* had no such relationship with their followers. They were not registered investment advisors. They were not broker-dealers. They had no contractual or fiduciary relationship with the people who followed their trading posts. They were, in essence, strangers who had amassed audiences on platforms that rewarded engagement and personality. And yet the Fifth Circuit saw no need to address whether the absence of a fiduciary duty posed any obstacle to prosecution under § 1348.

This is doctrinally significant. The securities fraud statute, unlike Rule 10b-5, does not require a fiduciary relationship as an element of the offense. Section 1348 prohibits schemes to defraud "any person" in connection with a security. The statute's text draws no distinction between fraudsters who owe duties to their victims and fraudsters who do not. The Fifth Circuit's opinion implicitly treats this textual breadth as sufficient. The scheme to defraud is established by the material misrepresentations and the intent to obtain money; the intent to defraud is established by the deception and the object of obtaining the followers' money. No fiduciary duty needed.

Whether this is unremarkable or troubling depends on one's view of the fraud statutes' proper scope. From one perspective, the result is straightforwardly correct: a defendant who lies to induce another person to buy a security has committed fraud, full stop. The absence of a fiduciary duty does not make the lie any less material or the victim's loss any less real. From another perspective, the case illustrates the open-ended quality of the federal fraud statutes, which can reach essentially any deception that results in the transfer of money, regardless of whether the defendant occupied a position of trust. The Supreme Court's recent decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* represent an effort to impose limits on this open-ended quality, but as *Constantinescu* demonstrates, those limits are modest. *Ciminelli* forecloses one theory; *Kousisis* opens—or rather, confirms—another.

## The Broader Trend: Influencer Fraud and the Limits of Existing Statutes

*Constantinescu* is not an isolated case. The Department of Justice has increasingly pursued influencer-driven fraud across contexts, from cryptocurrency pump-and-dump schemes to deceptive investment promotions on YouTube and TikTok. The SEC has likewise brought civil enforcement actions against social-media personalities for securities fraud, including the high-profile case against Barstool Sports founder Dave Portnoy. The phenomenon reflects a structural shift in how investment information circulates. Where investors once relied on broker-dealers, investment advisors, and financial media for guidance, they now increasingly turn to social-media personalities whose incentives are aligned with engagement rather than accuracy.

The question is whether existing statutes are adequate to address this shift or whether new regulatory frameworks are needed. The Fifth Circuit's decision in *Constantinescu* suggests that the existing tools are, at minimum, serviceable. The securities fraud statute reaches the conduct at issue without requiring the government to establish a fiduciary duty, and the fraudulent-inducement theory affirmed in *Kousisis* provides a workable framework for prosecutions in which the defendant's deception induced the victim to part with money. The government does not need to show that the victim suffered a net loss; it needs to show that the defendant obtained the victim's money through material misrepresentations. In the social-media context, where influencers can plausibly argue that their followers received something of value—the securities they purchased—the *Kousisis* framework is particularly important. It allows the government to focus on the deception and the transfer rather than getting bogged down in contested questions about whether the followers were ultimately harmed.

But there are limits to what traditional fraud statutes can do. The pump-and-dump scheme alleged in *Constantinescu* is relatively easy to characterize as fraud because it involves active misrepresentations about the defendants' own trading positions and the likely trajectory of the securities' prices. But not all social-media-driven market manipulation involves false statements. An influencer who truthfully discloses his position but omits to mention that he intends to sell immediately after his followers drive up the price may not be making a material misrepresentation at all. The line between persuasive promotion and fraudulent inducement is not always clear, and the fraud statutes require a material misrepresentation or omission. Where the conduct falls short of an actionable misstatement, the government may need to look to other tools—SEC Rule 10b-5's prohibition on manipulative devices, for example, or new disclosure requirements tailored to the social-media context.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The Fifth Circuit's decision is likely to have practical consequences beyond the immediate case. By confirming that the fraudulent-inducement theory applies in the securities fraud context and that the absence of a net-loss requirement extends to social-media-driven schemes, the opinion provides a roadmap for future prosecutions. The government can proceed under § 1348 by alleging that an influencer made material misrepresentations to induce followers to purchase securities, that the influencer's object was to obtain money, and that the deception was the means of obtaining it. The government does not need to allege a fiduciary duty, a net loss, or the deprivation of a traditional property interest beyond the money the followers invested.

The more interesting downstream question is whether *Ciminelli*'s limitation retains any practical force in the social-media context. *Ciminelli* held that the right-to-control theory cannot support a fraud conviction because the right to valuable economic information is not a traditional property interest. But in the social-media context, the deprivation of information—the right to make an informed trading decision—is often the primary harm. An influencer who fails to disclose a conflict of interest is depriving his followers of information they need to assess his recommendations. Under *Ciminelli*, that deprivation alone cannot support a fraud charge. Under *Kousisis*, if the influencer's nondisclosure or misrepresentation induced the followers to part with money, the charge can proceed. The doctrinal architecture thus channels prosecutors toward the fraudulent-inducement theory and away from the right-to-control theory, even in cases where the information deprivation is the more intuitively culpable aspect of the defendant's conduct.

This is not necessarily a problem. The fraudulent-inducement theory is a workable framework that focuses on the transfer of money, which is after all the ultimate object of most securities fraud. But it does mean that the fraud statutes are doing a particular kind of work in the social-media context—work that focuses on the inducement of investment rather than the manipulation of information. Whether that focus is adequate to address the full range of influencer-driven market manipulation remains to be seen. For now, the Fifth Circuit has confirmed that the existing framework is sufficient to reach the most clear-cut cases. The harder questions will come when the schemes are subtler and the misrepresentations less overt.



# Statutory Borrowing as Constitutional Adjudication: Section 1348's Reliance on Mail and Wire Fraud Precedent

When Congress enacted Section 1348 as part of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002, it created a criminal securities fraud statute that borrowed heavily—some might say reflexively—from the familiar architecture of the mail and wire fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit's decision last week in *United States v. Constantinescu* illustrates how deeply this cross-statutory borrowing has become embedded in the jurisprudence, and why it deserves more critical attention than it has received. The case is a straightforward pump-and-dump prosecution on its facts, but the court's analytical method raises a prior question: what does it mean for the legitimate scope of a criminal statute when its content is supplied, in significant part, by doctrines developed under different statutory schemes?

## The Case and the Borrowing Move

The defendants in *Constantinescu* were social-media influencers who allegedly profited $114 million by promoting securities they secretly intended to sell once their followers drove up the price. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it alleged only a deprivation of "potentially valuable economic information"—the theory the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023)—rather than a deprivation of a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed, holding that the indictment alleged a straightforward fraudulent-inducement scheme: defendants misrepresented their positions to trick followers into parting with money to buy securities.

What is notable about the Fifth Circuit's reasoning is not the result but the method. The court observed that "[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit" and that Section 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," so "courts have given the terms similar treatment," routinely "relying on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges." Having established this borrowing framework, the court then applied two recent Supreme Court wire fraud decisions—*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis v. United States* (2025)—to resolve the securities fraud claims before it. The *Kousisis* holding that fraud does not require the victim to suffer net economic loss was transposed directly into the Section 1348 context to reject the defendants' argument that they intended only to profit, not to harm.

The court's approach is entirely consistent with how most circuits have treated Section 1348. But the ease of the transplantation should give us pause.

## The Textual Problem

Section 1348 criminalizes any "scheme to defraud any person in connection with" a security and any scheme "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of" a security. The language parallels Sections 1341 and 1343, which prohibit schemes "to defraud" and schemes "to obtain money or property" by false pretenses. The textual parallelism is real, and it provides a plausible basis for looking to mail and wire fraud precedent for guidance on shared terms like "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud."

But textual parallelism is not textual identity. Section 1348 contains its own qualifying phrases—"in connection with any security" and "in connection with the purchase or sale of any security"—that have no analogues in the mail and wire fraud statutes. These phrases presumably narrow the statute's reach in ways that the general fraud statutes do not, and they may also inform the meaning of the shared terms in context. A "scheme to defraud" in connection with a security may not mean precisely the same thing as a "scheme to defraud" carried out through the mails, even if the words are identical.

The borrowing approach tends to flatten these contextual differences. When the Fifth Circuit says that courts "give the terms similar treatment," it effectively treats the shared language as having a single, transstatutory meaning—one fixed by the accumulated body of mail and wire fraud jurisprudence. This is a significant interpretive choice, and it is one that most courts have made without sustained justification.

## The Circularity Concern

The borrowing approach also creates a potential circularity problem, one that *Constantinescu* itself illustrates. The Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* constrained the scope of the federal fraud statutes by holding that the "right-to-control" theory—which treats a victim's right to make informed economic decisions as a protected property interest—cannot serve as the basis for a fraud conviction because the right to valuable economic information is not a "traditional property interest." That holding was rooted in the Court's understanding of the property requirement in the mail and wire fraud statutes, informed by common-law property concepts and historical usage.

When the Fifth Circuit borrows *Ciminelli* to construe Section 1348, it imports not just a doctrinal rule but a constitutional-adjacent limitation on criminal liability—one developed under a different statute with a different legislative history and a different regulatory context. The result is that Section 1348's scope is effectively determined by the boundaries the Supreme Court has drawn around mail fraud, even though Congress enacted Section 1348 years after the mail fraud statutes and in response to a very different problem—corporate and securities fraud in the post-Enron era.

This is not necessarily wrong. The statutes do share key terms, and the Supreme Court's fraud jurisprudence has long treated the various fraud provisions as a coherent family. But the circularity becomes apparent when one considers that the Court's recent fraud decisions—*Ciminelli*, *Kousisis*, and before them *Kelly v. United States* (the Bridgegate case)—have been preoccupied with limiting the apparently boundless reach of the general fraud statutes. Those limiting principles were developed with the mail and wire fraud statutes' potentially sweeping scope in mind. Section 1348, by contrast, is already cabined by its securities-specific nexus requirement. Importing limitations designed to rein in a broader statute into a statute that is already narrower may be doubly constraining in ways Congress did not contemplate—and may not have wanted.

## Fair Notice and Due Process

Perhaps the most serious concern is fair notice. The Due Process Clause requires that criminal statutes provide adequate warning of what conduct is prohibited. A defendant charged under Section 1348 who consults the statutory text will find language about schemes to defraud "in connection with" securities. He may not anticipate that his conduct will be evaluated under a body of doctrine developed primarily to address mail fraud schemes involving government contracts, construction bids, or healthcare billing—and that the line between lawful and unlawful conduct will be drawn by reference to Supreme Court decisions that never mention securities.

The defendants in *Constantinescu* did not seriously press this argument, and the Fifth Circuit did not address it. The court's approach—treating the fraud statutes as a unified interpretive field—has the virtue of doctrinal efficiency, and it produces a result that seems intuitively correct: a pump-and-dump scheme is fraud by any reasonable definition. But efficiency and intuition are not the same as textual fidelity, and the fair-notice question becomes more acute in closer cases where the conduct at issue looks less like classic fraud and more like aggressive marketing or puffery that happens to involve securities.

The concern is not hypothetical. Section 1348 has been used to prosecute insider trading, market manipulation, and various forms of misrepresentation by market participants. The scope of each application depends significantly on how "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud" are construed—and those constructions are increasingly supplied by the Supreme Court's general fraud jurisprudence rather than by securities law's own developed understandings of these concepts.

## Toward a More Principled Borrowing

None of this is to say that cross-statutory interpretation is inherently problematic. Courts routinely look to analogous statutes for interpretive guidance, and the textual overlap between Section 1348 and the general fraud statutes provides a reasonable basis for doing so. But the practice would benefit from greater analytical discipline. Courts borrowing from the mail and wire fraud tradition to construe Section 1348 should explain why the borrowed doctrine fits the statutory context—not simply assert that the statutes share key terms and therefore receive "similar treatment." They should consider whether Section 1348's securities-specific language narrows or alters the borrowed principles. And they should be candid about the fair-notice implications of transposing limitations—and expansions—developed under one criminal statute onto another.

The Fifth Circuit's opinion in *Constantinescu* reaches a sensible outcome through an analytical move that has become almost invisible in fraud jurisprudence. That invisibility is itself the problem. When the content of a criminal statute is determined largely by doctrines developed under other statutes, we should at least be able to see the seams. In *Constantinescu*, the seams are nowhere in sight.
