Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Is it legally problematic that the policy was cancelled before a human signed the certification documents?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the timeline seems important: APAC's computer automatically sends the cancellation request, GoAuto's computer automatically cancels the policy, and only later in the morning does an APAC employee stamp a signature onto the printed documents. So the policy was effectively cancelled before any human signed anything. The court didn't seem troubled by this temporal sequence. But if the statute requires a 'certifying' statement, and certification typically implies some human verification, how can the cancellation be valid before the certification is signed? Is there a legal doctrine that addresses retroactive validation of automated actions? Does it matter that the signature was purely ministerial rather than substantive?
Answer:
The short answer is that the court didn't need a retroactive validation doctrine because it held that **no signature was required in the first place**. The temporal gap between the automated cancellation and the later signature stamp was legally irrelevant because the statute, as interpreted by the court, was already satisfied at the time of cancellation.

## The statute requires a "statement certifying," not a signature

The key move in the court's reasoning is textual. Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium finance company to send the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four enumerated items. The court observed that "[t]he statute makes no explicit reference to a signature." The plaintiffs argued that the word "certifying" implicitly requires one, but the court rejected that reading, noting that the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity that effects cancellation by certifying—not a named individual employee.

The court supported this with the observation that Louisiana case law has never held a signature to be an independent statutory requirement. The closest case, *Benitez v. Elsayed*, mentioned the absence of a signature but only as one of several shortcomings, and the notice in that case also failed to "purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." Here, by contrast, the e-mailed notice contained the full certifying language. The court distinguished the two cases on exactly that basis: "Unlike in *Benitez*, each notice 'certifies' the relevant information."

So the certification was in the **content of the statement sent**, not in a human's act of signing it.

## The statutory requirements were met at the moment of cancellation

Once the court determined that no signature was required, the temporal sequence becomes straightforward. The statute requires two things before the insurer may cancel: (1) the insurer's "receipt" of the notice, and (2) that the notice contain a "statement certifying" the four enumerated items. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b)(i).

Both were satisfied when APAC's computer sent the e-mail containing the certifying language and GoAuto's computer received it. The court found receipt occurred at that point: "once the e-mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto's computer system, that computer system cancels the policy." The court explicitly embraced the automated process: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

The later signature stamp added nothing that the statute required. It was, as you suggest, ministerial—but the court's reasoning goes further than calling it ministerial. The court held it was **superfluous to statutory compliance**.

## No retroactive validation doctrine was needed

Because the statute was satisfied at the time of cancellation, there is nothing to retroactively validate. A retroactive validation doctrine would only be necessary if the cancellation were defective when performed and needed to be cured by a later act. The court's holding forecloses that premise: the cancellation was valid when it occurred because the e-mail containing the certifying statement had been received. The signature stamp that followed was legally irrelevant to the question of statutory compliance.

In sum, the court's analysis rests on a straightforward textual interpretation: the statute requires a certifying *statement* from the *company*, not a signature from a *person*. Because the certifying statement was received before the cancellation was effected, the statutory prerequisites were met, and the later human signature neither added to nor cured anything.
