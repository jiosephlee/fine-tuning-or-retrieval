Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The Parties and the Premium Financing Arrangement

The plaintiffs in this case are three former insureds of GoAuto Insurance Company: Kimberly Williams, Felita Wright, and Nicholas Jenkins. Williams and Wright are named plaintiffs in the action, while Jenkins became involved through a separate accident that forms part of the factual background. The defendant is GoAuto Insurance Company, the insurer that issued and ultimately cancelled the policies at issue. A third GoAuto insured, not a party to this appeal, was involved in the accident with Jenkins, but the details of that insured's participation in the litigation are not elaborated upon in the opinion.

A critical entity in this case is American Premium Assistance Company, LLC ("APAC"), a premium finance company. APAC is not a party to the appeal, but its role is central to the dispute. Each of the three insureds financed their GoAuto insurance policy through an agreement with APAC. The financing arrangement worked as follows: APAC paid GoAuto the entire premium upfront, and in return, the insureds made monthly installment payments to APAC. This arrangement allowed the insureds to obtain insurance coverage without paying the full premium at once, while GoAuto received its full premium immediately from APAC.

Each financing agreement granted APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the insurance policies upon the insureds' failure to pay the monthly installments. This power of attorney is the mechanism through which APAC could initiate cancellation with GoAuto when an insured defaulted. Each of the three policies at issue was cancelled as a result of nonpayment.

## The Underlying Accident and the Class Action Lawsuit

The litigation arose from two related sources. Williams and Jenkins initially filed a class action lawsuit against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court. Wright later became an additional plaintiff when an amended complaint was filed. The catalyst for Jenkins's involvement was an accident between Jenkins and a third GoAuto insured—one who was not a party to this appeal. The factual details of the accident itself are not developed in the opinion, but the accident served as the event that brought the cancellation issue to a head: the third insured's policy had been cancelled through the same APAC-financed process, and the accident presumably raised questions about whether that policy was effectively cancelled at the time of the accident.

The plaintiffs alleged that GoAuto had not effectively cancelled their policies under Louisiana law and had failed to act in good faith. The thrust of the complaint was that GoAuto's cancellation procedures did not comply with the governing Louisiana statute, which in turn meant the policies remained in effect and GoAuto's representations about their cancellation were false. This allegation supported claims of misrepresentation and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing.

## Procedural History: From State Court to Summary Judgment

Williams and Jenkins filed the class action in a Louisiana state court. GoAuto then removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana, invoking federal diversity jurisdiction. The basis for removal is not elaborated in the opinion, but the parties evidently satisfied the requirements for federal jurisdiction.

Once in federal court, the parties filed motions for summary judgment. Importantly, the summary judgment motions were "related solely to whether GoAuto's procedure for cancelling the financed insurance policies satisfied Louisiana law." This narrowing of the summary judgment stage is significant: the district court was not asked to resolve the full range of the plaintiffs' claims—misrepresentation, bad faith, and breach of fair dealing—in all their factual complexity. Instead, the parties agreed to focus on the threshold statutory question of whether GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana law. The answer to that question would logically determine whether the policies were effectively cancelled, which in turn would resolve whether GoAuto's subsequent representations about the cancellations were actionable.

## The District Court's Ruling

The district court granted GoAuto's motion for summary judgment, holding that GoAuto's procedures complied with Louisiana's insurance-premium finance statute. The district court reached this conclusion through a particular analytical route: it held that the APAC employee who delivered the printed cancellation notices fit the statutory definition of a "private carrier," relying on an analogy to a Fifth Circuit case about when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. The Fifth Circuit later described this approach as "imaginative and unconventional" and declined to endorse it, finding an alternative basis for affirmance. The district court's reasoning on the "private carrier" question is thus not the holding the appellate court adopted, but it is worth noting as the basis on which the district court rested its decision.

Having concluded that GoAuto's procedures satisfied the statute, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. The dismissal with prejudice meant that the plaintiffs could not refile the same claims against GoAuto. The plaintiffs—Williams, Jenkins, and Wright—then appealed to the Fifth Circuit.

## The Scope of the Appeal

On appeal, the plaintiffs raised three contentions. First, they argued that the district court erred in finding that the statutory procedures for delivery of the notice of cancellation were followed. Second, they challenged the district court's finding that GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests. Third, they contended that the district court should have granted their motion for partial summary judgment rather than GoAuto's motion.

These three contentions frame the appellate issues. The first concerns whether APAC's method of transmitting the cancellation notice to GoAuto satisfied the statute's requirements for delivery. The second concerns whether GoAuto's receipt of that notice—accomplished through automated computer systems rather than human action—satisfied the statute's requirement that the insurer "receive" the notice before cancelling. The third is a summary judgment procedural question: given the parties' cross-motions, the plaintiffs argue that they, not GoAuto, were entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

The Fifth Circuit's task was to interpret the Louisiana statute governing cancellation of financed insurance policies and determine whether GoAuto's procedures, as a matter of law, complied with it. Because the case was resolved on summary judgment, the relevant facts were not in dispute; the question was purely one of statutory interpretation and its application to undisputed procedures. The court ultimately affirmed the district court's judgment, though on reasoning that differed in part from the district court's approach.

# Louisiana's Statutory Framework for Cancellation of Financed Insurance Policies

## The Statutory Mechanism for Cancellation by a Premium Finance Company

When an insured finances insurance premiums through a premium finance company, Louisiana law provides a specific statutory mechanism for cancelling the policy upon default. The governing statute, Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), authorizes a premium finance company to request cancellation of an insurance policy "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" certain required information. The statute thus contemplates a two-step process: first, the premium finance company sends notice of cancellation to the insured upon default; second, the finance company sends to the insurer a copy of that notice along with a certification statement. Once the insurer receives this notice and statement, the statute provides that the insurer "shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract." La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

In this case, the financing arrangement involved American Premium Assistance Company, LLC ("APAC"), which paid GoAuto the full premium in exchange for monthly installment payments from the insureds. Each financing agreement granted APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the insurance policies upon failure to pay. Each of the plaintiffs' policies was cancelled as a result of nonpayment. The dispute centered on whether GoAuto's cancellation procedures satisfied the statutory requirements.

## The Four Certification Requirements

The statute specifies four items that the premium finance company must certify in its notice to the insurer:

1. **Valid power of attorney**: The premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as provided in the statute. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i).

2. **Uncured default**: The premium finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(ii).

3. **Notice sent to insured**: Upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured, specifying the date of sending by the premium finance company to the insured. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iii).

4. **Copies sent to interested parties**: Copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss that may occur thereunder, specifying the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, holders of a security interest in the insured property, or third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company has sent notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv).

In practice, APAC's cancellation request to GoAuto was sent on APAC's letterhead and certified all four elements: that the premium finance agreement contained a valid power of attorney, that the agreement was in default and the default had not been timely cured, that a ten-day notice of cancellation had been sent to the insured, and that copies of the notice had been sent to all persons shown by the agreement to have an interest in any loss.

## The Strict Adherence Doctrine

Louisiana courts apply a demanding standard when evaluating whether a premium finance company has properly exercised its statutory power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy. As the Fifth Circuit explained, quoting Louisiana appellate authority: "Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy." *Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Strict adherence means that the premium finance company and the insurer must follow each procedural step the statute prescribes. The court emphasized that "'[s]trict adherence' to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." In other words, strict adherence requires faithful compliance with what the statute actually says, but it does not authorize courts to supplement the statute with additional requirements that the legislature did not enact.

## The Dual Purpose of Strict Adherence

The strict adherence doctrine serves two identifiable functions. According to *Britten v. Reavis*, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987), strict adherence to the statute "(1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured." The first function addresses certainty: when the statutory procedures are followed precisely, all parties can know exactly when cancellation takes effect. The second function protects the insured: by requiring that notice be sent and a cure period be observed before cancellation occurs, the statute ensures that the insured has a meaningful opportunity to remedy the default and maintain coverage. These dual purposes explain why Louisiana courts hold finance companies and insurers to exacting procedural compliance.

## Consequences of Noncompliance

The consequence of failing to strictly adhere to the statute is severe. The court quoted *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990), for the proposition that "[a]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy." This all-or-nothing approach means that even a seemingly minor procedural defect can render a cancellation ineffective, leaving the insurer on the hook for a policy it believed had been terminated. It is against this backdrop that the plaintiffs in *Williams* argued that GoAuto's procedures fell short of statutory requirements. They pointed to two alleged defects: the absence of a signature on APAC's certification at the time of cancellation and the claim that GoAuto never properly "received" the cancellation request. The plaintiffs' strategy reflected the strict adherence doctrine: if they could identify any defect in the process, the cancellation would be ineffective as a matter of law.

## GoAuto's Cancellation Procedures

The court set out GoAuto's cancellation procedures in detail. The process unfolds in the following sequence. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail containing a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. This request is sent on APAC's letterhead and certifies the four items required by the statute. Upon receipt of that e-mail, GoAuto's computer system automatically cancels the policy—no human involvement is required for cancellation to take effect. Only later in the morning, after the policy has already been cancelled, does an APAC employee affix a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents. A GoAuto deponent explained the computer-to-computer interaction: APAC's system "makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation," and the GoAuto web service acts as "a listener" that performs the cancellation when called.

## Pre-2017 and Post-2017 Document Handling

The physical handling of cancellation documents changed in 2017. Prior to 2017, after an APAC employee stamped the printed cancellation documents with a signature, the employee would physically take the documents to a filing cabinet shared by GoAuto and APAC. The shared filing room, however, belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto Insurance Company itself. Since 2017, the signed cancellation document is physically taken to a GoAuto employee who stamps the document as "received" and then places it in the shared filing cabinet. The plaintiffs argued that this post-2017 procedure still did not satisfy the statutory requirement of "receipt" because the employee who affixes the "received" stamp is an APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee. The court rejected this argument, concluding that GoAuto's method of receiving the electronic notice and reacting to it through its computer system satisfied the statute. The court noted that the statute is "a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did."

# The Signature Requirement: Does 'Certifying' Mean Signing?

## Plaintiffs' Argument: The Timing of the Signature

The plaintiffs' central challenge to GoAuto's cancellation procedures targets the sequence of events surrounding the signature on APAC's certification. Under GoAuto's established procedures, when an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice that the policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment arrives by midnight on the tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail containing a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. Upon receipt of that e-mail, GoAuto's computer system cancels the policy automatically—no human involvement is required at this stage. Only later that morning does an APAC employee affix a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents.

The plaintiffs seized on this temporal gap. They argued that because no signature appears on APAC's certification until after the policy has already been cancelled by the computer system, the cancellation was premature and therefore ineffective. Their theory depends on a critical interpretive move: that the statutory requirement of "certifying" certain information necessarily implies that a human being must sign the certification before the insurer may act on it. If a signature is required before cancellation, and the signature comes only after cancellation, then every cancellation GoAuto processed through this automated system was invalid—or so the argument ran.

## The Statutory Text: Silence on Signatures

The Fifth Circuit began its analysis with the text of the governing statute. Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) specifies what a premium finance company must include when it requests cancellation of an insurance policy due to an uncured default. The statute requires the company to send the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four categories of information: that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; that the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; that a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured upon default; and that copies of the notice were sent to all persons shown by the agreement to have an interest in any loss.

Critically, the statute makes no explicit reference to a signature. Nothing in the statutory text states that the certification must be signed, that a named individual must attest to the certified information, or that any particular form of authentication is required. The plaintiffs' argument therefore required the court to find that the word "certifying" itself carries an implicit signature requirement—that to "certify" information, in this statutory context, is necessarily to sign one's name to it.

## Whether "Certifying" Implicitly Requires a Signature

The court's approach to this question illustrates a fundamental principle of statutory interpretation: courts generally will not read requirements into a statute that the legislature did not include. The Fifth Circuit noted that if a signature requirement exists, it must be found in the word "certifying," because the statute mentions nothing else that could supply one. The court then examined whether Louisiana courts had interpreted "certifying" in this statute to require a signature.

The court's inquiry was complicated by the relative scarcity of Louisiana case law directly addressing this question. As the court observed, "no Louisiana judicial opinion considering insurance-premium finance companies' procedures under the statute has addressed the need for a signature" with one limited exception. This absence of authority itself became part of the court's reasoning: without clear guidance from Louisiana courts that "certifying" demands a signature, the Fifth Circuit was reluctant to impose such a requirement on its own.

## Louisiana Case Law Review

The court surveyed the relevant Louisiana jurisprudence to determine whether any case had established or implied a signature requirement. The cases the court examined proved largely unhelpful to the plaintiffs' position, though each offered a slightly different angle on what the statute requires.

In **KMJ Services, Inc. v. Hood**, a Louisiana appellate court paraphrased the statutory requirement as needing "a statement certifying compliance with" the statute. This formulation focused on the content of the statement—what it must certify—rather than on any formal requirement such as a signature. The court's language treated the certification as a substantive representation that the statutory conditions had been met, not as a formal act requiring personal attestation.

In **Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd's Insurance Co.**, the court described the process as allowing cancellation "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company." This framing emphasized the insurer's receipt of the notice and statement as the triggering event, not the presence of a signature on the statement. The court's focus was on the transmission and receipt of the document, not on its form of authentication.

The court also cited **Lewis v. Julien**, which the Fifth Circuit described as "similarly unhelpful on this question." None of these cases directly addressed whether a signature was required for an effective certification under the statute.

## Benitez v. Elsayed: The One Case Mentioning Lack of Signature

The most relevant case for the plaintiffs' argument was **Benitez v. Elsayed**, a Louisiana appellate decision that mentioned the absence of a signature in its discussion of a deficient cancellation notice. In Benitez, the court noted that the notice "does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." This passage at first glance appears supportive of the plaintiffs' position, as it lists the lack of a signature among the notice's shortcomings.

However, the Fifth Circuit read Benitez more narrowly. The court emphasized that Benitez identified multiple deficiencies in the notice—it lacked a named employee, lacked a signature, and, critically, did not purport to "certify" any of the required statements at all. The Benitez court did not hold that the absence of a signature alone would disqualify a cancellation if the other statutory elements were satisfied. The lack of a signature was part of a constellation of problems, not an independently fatal defect. This distinction was essential to the Fifth Circuit's analysis, because it meant that Benitez could not be read as establishing a per se signature requirement.

## Distinguishing Benitez: Certification Was Present Here

The Fifth Circuit then drew a sharp distinction between the facts in Benitez and the facts before it. In Benitez, the notice failed to "certify" any of the required information—it did not contain the substantive representations the statute demands. In the present case, each notice from APAC to GoAuto explicitly certified the relevant information. APAC's request for cancellation, sent on APAC's letterhead, stated that "APAC certifies" four categories of information: the existence of a valid power of attorney, the existence of an uncured default, that a ten-day notice of cancellation had been sent to the insured, and that copies of the notice had been sent to all persons with an interest in any loss.

Thus, the deficiency that the Fifth Circuit appeared to view as most significant in Benitez—the total absence of any certification—was not present here. The APAC notices did contain substantive certifications of all four statutorily required categories. The only feature shared with the deficient notice in Benitez was the absence of an individual's signature and the failure to identify a specific employee. Because Benitez did not establish that these features alone rendered a cancellation ineffective, and because the APAC notices actually contained the certifications that Benitez's notice lacked, the Fifth Circuit found Benitez insufficient to support the plaintiffs' argument.

The court also noted that the notices in this record, like those in Benitez, did not identify any particular employee by printed name. But the court saw no reason, "without clearer Louisiana authority," that a company could not certify information without a named employee being identified. This reasoning reflects a practical concern: the statute's requirements focus on the substance of what is certified, not on the identity of the person doing the certifying.

## The Court's Reasoning: The Company, Not an Individual, Certifies

The Fifth Circuit's conclusion rested significantly on the statutory text's identification of who effects the certification. The statute states that it is "the insurance premium finance company" that effects cancellation by "certifying" certain information in a notice. The statute does not refer to an agent, an employee, or an authorized representative of the company performing the certification—it identifies the company itself as the certifying entity.

This textual choice by the legislature carried interpretive weight for the court. If the legislature had intended to require a personal attestation by a named individual, it could have said so. Instead, it assigned the certification function to the corporate entity. The court's reasoning suggests that when a statute identifies an entity rather than a person as the actor, the formal requirements for the act should be understood in light of how such entities operate. A company certifies through its systems and procedures, and the statute does not specify that those systems must include a human signature at any particular point in the process.

The court also considered the practical context in which these cancellations occur. The statute is, as the court described it, "a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did." The automated system by which APAC's computer sends the certification e-mail to GoAuto's computer, which then processes the cancellation, represents the kind of electronic, system-to-system transaction that modern insurance operations routinely employ. The statute's silence on signatures, combined with its identification of the company as the certifying party, allowed the court to conclude that the automated certification satisfied the statutory requirement.

## Conclusion: Strict Adherence Does Not Mean Adding Requirements

The Fifth Circuit tied its analysis together with a principle that has broader significance for statutory interpretation. Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to the statutory procedures for cancellation of financed insurance policies. The plaintiffs invoked this standard to argue that the absence of a signature constituted a defect that rendered the cancellations ineffective. The Fifth Circuit accepted the strict adherence standard but rejected the plaintiffs' interpretation of what it requires.

The court stated the principle directly: "'Strict adherence' to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." Strict adherence means that the procedures the legislature actually enacted must be followed exactly. It does not mean that courts may supplement those procedures with additional requirements—such as a signature—that the legislature did not include. The statute requires a statement certifying four categories of information, sent by the premium finance company to the insurer. APAC's notices contained exactly that. The absence of a human signature before cancellation did not constitute a deviation from the statutory procedure, because the statute contains no signature requirement to deviate from.

The court concluded: "We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer." This holding resolves the question presented and underscores a lesson that extends beyond this case: when a statute specifies what must be done but is silent on how a particular step must be authenticated, courts will generally not read in formal requirements the legislature omitted, even under a standard of strict adherence. The rigor of "strict adherence" operates to ensure compliance with what the statute actually says, not to expand the statute beyond its text.

# The Receipt Requirement: Did GoAuto Properly 'Receive' the Cancellation Request?

## The Plaintiffs' Argument: Shared Corporate Infrastructure Defeats Receipt

Having addressed whether APAC's certification required a signature, the Fifth Circuit turned to the plaintiffs' second principal argument: that GoAuto never properly "received" the cancellation request at all. The plaintiffs grounded this argument in the shared physical and corporate infrastructure between GoAuto and APAC, identifying two distinct periods in which different structural problems allegedly prevented receipt.

Before 2017, APAC employees would physically take the printed cancellation documents to a filing cabinet shared by GoAuto and APAC. The plaintiffs pointed out, however, that the shared filing room belonged not to GoAuto Insurance Company itself, but to GoAuto Management Services—a separate corporate entity. The implication was that physical possession by a related but distinct corporate entity did not constitute receipt by the insurer that had issued the policy.

After 2017, the procedure changed. The signed cancellation document was physically taken to a GoAuto employee who stamped it as "received" and placed it in a shared filing cabinet. But the plaintiffs contended that the employee who affixed the stamp was actually an APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee. In their view, this meant that even the post-2017 procedure did not result in GoAuto itself taking possession of the cancellation request. The shared corporate relationship between GoAuto and APAC thus permeated every step of the physical handling of cancellation documents, and the plaintiffs argued that this intertwining meant the statutory requirement of receipt by the insurer was never satisfied.

## The Plaintiffs' Interpretation: "Receipt" Means More Than "Sending"

The plaintiffs' structural argument was paired with a textual one. They emphasized that the Louisiana legislature used the word "receipt" in the statute, not merely "sending." Under the statute, the premium finance company must send the cancellation request to the insurer, but cancellation cannot proceed until the insurer has "receipt" of that request. The plaintiffs contended that this deliberate statutory choice required GoAuto to actually take possession of the notice before cancelling the policy. In other words, the statute created a two-step process: first, APAC sends the request; second, GoAuto receives it. The plaintiffs argued that the second step—actual possession by GoAuto—was a prerequisite to cancellation, and because of the shared infrastructure problems they identified, that second step never occurred.

## The Statutory Framework: What the Statute Says About Receipt

The statutory provision at the center of this dispute is Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), which provides: "Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract." This provision sits at the intersection of two stages in the cancellation process. The prior subsection, § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), governs what the premium finance company must send to the insurer: a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a certifying statement. The (b)(i) provision then addresses what happens next: once the insurer receives that notice and statement, the insurer "shall consider" cancellation to have been requested and "may proceed to cancel."

The statute thus envisions a sequence in which the premium finance company initiates the process by sending the required documents, and the insurer acts upon receiving them. The statutory text, however, does not specify what "receipt" means. It does not say whether receipt requires physical possession by a human being, electronic delivery to a computer system, or something else entirely. The statute also does not distinguish between receipt by the insurer's employee, receipt by the insurer's computer, or receipt by a related corporate entity acting on the insurer's behalf.

## The Absence of Definitional Guidance

The Fifth Circuit observed that the Louisiana Supreme Court had never defined "receipt" as used in § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The statute itself contains no definition of the term. Louisiana appellate courts had not provided meaningful guidance on the question either. This left the Fifth Circuit, sitting in diversity and applying Louisiana law, without a controlling state-court interpretation of a key statutory term.

This absence of authoritative state law created both a challenge and an opportunity. The challenge was that the Fifth Circuit, as a federal court predicting how the Louisiana Supreme Court would interpret its own statute, had to proceed cautiously. The opportunity was that the lack of contrary authority meant the court could rely on ordinary dictionary definitions and common sense to determine what "receipt" required in the modern business context. The court chose the latter path, noting that it was not free to add glosses to the statute that the Louisiana legislature had not included, but also not free to ignore the practical realities of how modern insurance companies conduct business.

## Black's Law Dictionary Definitions

To fill the definitional gap, the Fifth Circuit turned to Black's Law Dictionary, a standard reference for interpreting undefined legal terms. The dictionary defined "receipt" as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession." It defined "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." These definitions emphasized two features: the act of taking possession and the transfer from an outside source to the recipient.

The court accepted these definitions and concluded that GoAuto could satisfy the statutory requirement of receipt once it took "possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation." The dictionary definitions did not require that a human being physically handle the document, nor did they require that the recipient be a specific corporate entity rather than an agent or system acting on its behalf. The definitions spoke in terms of possession and transfer, concepts that could apply to electronic communications as readily as to physical documents.

## The Computerized Cancellation Process

The key to the court's analysis was its understanding of how GoAuto actually received and processed cancellation requests. The court quoted at length from a GoAuto deponent who explained the electronic interaction between APAC's and GoAuto's computer systems. The APAC system "makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date." The deponent described the GoAuto web service as being "like a listener. It's always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it's called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity."

This description was central to the court's reasoning. The APAC system sent the cancellation request electronically to GoAuto's system, which received it automatically and, upon receipt, cancelled the policy. The process was entirely automated. No human being at GoAuto reviewed the request, approved the cancellation, or even saw the notice before the policy was cancelled. The cancellation occurred in the early morning hours, triggered by the interaction between the two computer systems. Only later in the morning, after the policy had already been cancelled, did an APAC employee affix a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents and physically deliver them to a GoAuto employee for stamping and filing.

The plaintiffs' argument about shared infrastructure thus missed the critical point: the physical handling of documents that occurred later in the morning was not the moment of receipt. Receipt had already occurred electronically, when APAC's computer system transmitted the cancellation request to GoAuto's computer system and GoAuto's system took possession of it. The subsequent physical steps—stamping, signing, filing—were record-keeping measures, not the act of receipt itself.

## No Human Involvement Until After Cancellation

The court emphasized that no person was involved in the receipt and cancellation process until after the GoAuto computer system had already effected the cancellation. The court stated plainly: "No person is involved until later in the morning after the GoAuto computer system effects cancellation." At that later time, an APAC employee used a signature stamp on the printed documents, and the documents were physically transferred to a GoAuto employee for stamping and filing.

This temporal sequence was important for two reasons. First, it meant that the plaintiffs' arguments about which corporate entity employed the person who stamped the documents were beside the point. The stamping occurred after receipt and after cancellation, so it could not be the act of receipt the statute required. Second, it underscored the court's broader point that the statute was "a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did." The court was willing to recognize that receipt could be accomplished by a computer system acting on the insurer's behalf, just as it had long been accomplished by employees acting on the insurer's behalf.

## The Court's Conclusion: Electronic Receipt Satisfies the Statute

The Fifth Circuit concluded that GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfied the statute. The court framed its holding in straightforward terms: "What is necessary prior to cancellation is that the insurer had 'receipt' of notice of cancellation sent by the premium finance company." The court found that the electronic transmission from APAC's computer system to GoAuto's computer system constituted receipt under the ordinary meaning of that term. GoAuto's system took possession of the request from an outside source—APAC's system—and acted upon it by cancelling the policy.

The court's reasoning reflected several interpretive principles. First, it adhered to the statutory text, which required receipt but did not specify the manner of receipt. Second, it declined to add requirements that the legislature had not included, such as a signature requirement or human involvement. Third, it recognized the practical realities of modern business operations, in which computer-to-computer communication is a standard and accepted method of transferring information between entities. Fourth, it relied on dictionary definitions that were consistent with both traditional and electronic forms of receipt.

The court also noted that "strict adherence" to the statute, as required by Louisiana courts, was not an invitation for courts to add procedural steps the legislature had not enacted. This principle, which the court had invoked earlier in addressing the signature question, applied equally to the receipt question. The statute required receipt; it did not require receipt by a particular person, at a particular location, or in a particular format.

## The District Court's Alternative Reasoning: The "Private Carrier" Theory

Before concluding, the Fifth Circuit briefly addressed the district court's alternative reasoning. The district court had held that GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with the statute in a different way: it found that "the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier." The statute allows the premium finance company to send the cancellation request "by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail." The district court had reasoned that the APAC employee who physically delivered the cancellation documents to GoAuto qualified as a "private carrier."

The district court recognized an absence of Louisiana case law interpreting "private carrier" and found guidance in a Fifth Circuit case involving a railroad, Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App'x 453 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court's analogy was, as the Fifth Circuit described it, "imaginative and unconventional." Because the Fifth Circuit found that the e-mail transmission satisfied the statute independently, it declined to consider the validity of the district court's "private carrier" reasoning. The court's choice not to reach the alternative basis was consistent with the general principle that federal appellate courts should avoid deciding issues unnecessarily, particularly ones involving novel interpretations of state law. By resolving the case on the narrower ground of electronic receipt, the court left the meaning of "private carrier" in this statutory context for another day.

# Certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court and Disposition

## The Plaintiffs' Request for Certification

Having failed to persuade the Fifth Circuit that GoAuto's cancellation procedures violated Louisiana law, the Plaintiffs made a final request: that the court certify the unresolved questions of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court. Certification is a procedural mechanism by which a federal court, rather than deciding a novel or unsettled question of state law itself, refers the question to the highest court of the state for an authoritative answer. The Plaintiffs argued that certification was appropriate because the proper interpretation of the statutory terms at issue—particularly whether "certifying" requires a signature and what "receipt" means in the context of electronic communications—was important to Louisiana's insurance industry and statutory scheme.

This argument had surface appeal. The Louisiana Supreme Court had never defined "receipt" as used in the insurance-premium finance statute, and no Louisiana appellate court had directly addressed whether a signature is required for a premium finance company's certification. The questions presented themselves as genuinely novel issues of state law with potentially broad implications for how insurance companies and premium finance companies conduct cancellation procedures across the state.

## The Fifth Circuit's Posture on Certification

The Fifth Circuit, however, was not persuaded. To understand why, a law student must appreciate the federal appellate courts' general posture toward certification. Certification is a valuable tool, but federal courts do not invoke it lightly. The Fifth Circuit has described itself as "chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so." The court quoted its prior decision in *Jefferson v. Lead Industry Ass'n*, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997), which explained the court's reasoning: "the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often."

This language reveals an important institutional consideration. Certification is a discretionary courtesy extended by federal courts to state supreme courts. If federal courts overuse the mechanism, referring every novel state-law question to the state judiciary, they risk straining the relationship and exhausting the willingness of state courts to cooperate. Certification is therefore reserved for cases in which the federal court genuinely cannot resolve the state-law question with confidence—typically where the issue is truly indeterminate under existing state authority and the stakes are sufficiently high to warrant the state court's direct involvement.

## The Principle That Novelty Alone Is Insufficient

The Fifth Circuit applied this standard directly to the Plaintiffs' request. The court acknowledged that the Louisiana Supreme Court had not yet defined "receipt" under the statute, but it held that this fact alone was not enough. Quoting *Jefferson* again, the court stated that the mere fact "a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court 'is not sufficient to warrant certification.'" The court had already engaged in a careful analysis of the statutory text, relevant Louisiana appellate decisions, and standard legal dictionary definitions. On the basis of that analysis, the court was confident in its resolution of the interpretive questions. The absence of a definitive Louisiana Supreme Court ruling did not leave the issues so open-ended that the court was guessing at the answer; rather, the court found the existing legal materials sufficient to support a principled conclusion.

This distinction is an important one for law students to grasp. Certification is not a reflexive response whenever a state supreme court has not spoken on a particular issue. Federal courts sitting in diversity must often make Erie guesses—predicting how a state's highest court would rule based on the available sources of state law. Certification is reserved for those cases where such a prediction would be too speculative to be responsible, not merely those cases where the prediction has not yet been confirmed.

## The Court's Decline to Certify

Applying these principles, the court declined to certify the questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. The court had already concluded that the statutory text, read in light of existing Louisiana appellate authority and standard legal definitions, adequately supported its interpretation. The statute did not explicitly require a signature, Louisiana courts had not imposed such a requirement, and the term "receipt" was susceptible to a common-sense definition that encompassed GoAuto's electronic procedures. Because the court was able to resolve these questions with reasonable confidence, there was no compelling reason to defer to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

## Final Disposition

With the certification request denied, the court turned to the final disposition of the appeal. The court had already determined that GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana law in two critical respects: first, the premium finance company's certification did not require a signature to be effective, and second, GoAuto's electronic receipt of the cancellation notice satisfied the statutory requirement that the insurer have "receipt" of the notice before cancelling the policy.

Because GoAuto's procedures complied with the statute, the court held that the district court did not err in denying the Plaintiffs' motion for partial summary judgment. Recall that the parties had filed cross-motions for summary judgment in the district court focused solely on whether GoAuto's cancellation procedures satisfied Louisiana law. The district court had granted GoAuto's motion, holding that the procedures complied with state law, and had dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. The Fifth Circuit's conclusion that the procedures were statutorily compliant meant that the district court's summary judgment ruling was correct.

The court therefore affirmed the district court's judgment in its entirety. The district court's dismissal of all claims against GoAuto with prejudice stood. The Plaintiffs' core contention—that GoAuto had failed to effectively cancel their insurance policies and had thereby committed misrepresentations and breached the duty of good faith and fair dealing—failed because it was predicated on the premise that the cancellations were statutorily defective. Once the Fifth Circuit concluded that the cancellation procedures satisfied Louisiana law, the foundation for all of the Plaintiffs' claims was removed.

The court's disposition was succinct: "AFFIRMED." This single word confirmed that the district court's judgment was correct in all respects and that the Plaintiffs' appeal had failed.
