Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How does premium financing work and why does the finance company get power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the plaintiffs financed their insurance premiums through APAC, a premium finance company. The opinion says APAC paid the full premium to GoAuto upfront and the insureds repaid APAC in installments. The financing agreement gave APAC a power of attorney to cancel policies upon nonpayment. I'm confused about the basic mechanics here: Why would an insured agree to give a finance company the power to cancel their insurance? Is this a standard arrangement in the insurance industry? And what is the legal basis for allowing a third-party finance company to exercise cancellation rights that would normally belong to the insurer?
Answer:
**How Premium Financing Works in This Case**

According to the *Williams* opinion, the mechanics of premium financing are straightforward: the finance company (APAC) pays the insurer (GoAuto) the full premium upfront. In return, the insureds repay the finance company in monthly installments. Because APAC has fronted the money for the entire premium, the financing agreement grants APAC a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the insurance policies upon the insureds' failure to pay.

**Why an Insured Would Agree**

An insured would agree to give a finance company this power because it is the trade-off for being able to pay the premium over time rather than all at once. The opinion describes the arrangement as APAC paying the full premium "in return for monthly installment payments from the insureds." The power of attorney is a term of the financing agreement that protects the finance company's interest in the money it fronted: if the insured defaults on the installment payments, the finance company can initiate cancellation of the policy it financed, preventing the insured from continuing to receive coverage without paying for it.

**The Legal Basis for Third-Party Cancellation**

The legal basis is a specific state statute governing premium financing, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Under this law, a premium finance company may request cancellation of an insurance policy upon an uncured default by sending the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a statement certifying that:
1. The financing agreement contains a valid power of attorney;
2. The agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured;
3. A notice of cancellation was sent to the insured; and
4. Copies were sent to all interested parties.

The opinion emphasizes that the finance company does not unilaterally cancel the policy. Instead, upon receipt of the finance company's notice and certification, the insurer "shall consider that cancellation... has been requested... and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract." La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Thus, the right to cancel still formally belongs to the insurer, but the statute authorizes the finance company to trigger that cancellation through its power of attorney.

Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to these statutory procedures, which the Fifth Circuit noted serves two functions: (1) providing a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) granting a minimum time during which the default may be cured. The statutory framework is what permits the finance company to exercise these rights, and any defect in the process results in an ineffective cancellation.

**Is This Standard?**

While the opinion does not explicitly state whether this arrangement is "standard" across the entire insurance industry, it treats premium financing as an established practice governed by a specific Louisiana statutory scheme. The existence of detailed statutory procedures and prior Louisiana case law interpreting them indicates this is a recognized and regulated method of financing insurance premiums in Louisiana.

### Does 'strict adherence' to statutory cancellation requirements allow courts to add requirements not explicitly in the statute?
Question:
The court in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance cites Louisiana cases requiring 'strict adherence' to statutory requirements for cancelling financed insurance policies, but then concludes that strict adherence 'is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.' The plaintiffs argued that a signature should be required on the certification, but the statute doesn't explicitly mention one. How does the court reconcile 'strict adherence' with refusing to add a signature requirement? Is there a difference between strictly enforcing what the legislature wrote versus strictly enforcing what the legislature might have intended? Where is the line between strict adherence and judicial overreach?
Answer:
The court reconciles "strict adherence" with its refusal to imply a signature requirement by distinguishing between strictly enforcing the specific procedures the legislature enacted and imposing additional procedural formalities the legislature did not.

In Louisiana, the doctrine of strict adherence means that any defect in the statutory cancellation process renders the cancellation ineffective. The statute at issue requires the premium finance company to send a notice of cancellation together with "a statement certifying" four specific facts. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The statute, however, makes no mention of a signature.

The plaintiffs argued that the word "certifying" inherently requires a human signature before a policy can be cancelled. The Fifth Circuit rejected this interpretation. The court emphasized that the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity responsible for certifying the information, not a specific named employee. The court found "no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified." 

To answer your broader questions: yes, there is a critical difference between strictly enforcing what the legislature wrote versus strictly enforcing what the legislature might have intended. The court draws the line between strict adherence and judicial overreach at the four corners of the statutory text.

Strict adherence requires courts to ensure that every element explicitly detailed by the legislature is followed precisely. It does not give courts license to fill perceived gaps with additional formalities that the legislature omitted. The court's opinion reflects a textualist approach: it enforces what the legislature wrote, not what the legislature might have intended. As the court stated, strict adherence "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." 

If the court were to read "certifying" to require a signature simply because it seems like a prudent safeguard, it would be adding a procedural step to the statute based on an assumption of intent. The court views that as judicial overreach. Therefore, as long as the finance company's notice strictly complies with the text of the statute—by containing the required certification statement and ensuring the insurer takes "receipt" of the notice—the cancellation is valid, regardless of whether a human signature is affixed before the computer system processes the cancellation.

### Does the statutory term 'certifying' implicitly require a signature to be valid?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the plaintiffs argued that APAC's cancellation notices were invalid because they lacked a signature at the time the policy was cancelled. The statute requires the premium finance company to send a 'statement certifying' certain information, but doesn't explicitly mention a signature. The court found no Louisiana authority requiring one. I'm struggling with statutory interpretation here: Does the word 'certifying' have a settled legal meaning that implies a signature? The court cited Benitez v. Elsayed, which mentioned the lack of signature as a shortcoming, but didn't hold it was independently disqualifying. How do courts typically determine whether a statutory term implies procedural requirements like signatures that aren't explicitly stated?
Answer:
## Does "certifying" require a signature? Not under this statute.

The short answer is no — at least not under Louisiana's premium-financing statute. The Fifth Circuit held that "certifying" does not implicitly carry a signature requirement, and the reasoning is a useful illustration of how courts approach implied procedural requirements.

### The statutory text controls, and the statute is silent on signatures

The court's analysis starts and largely ends with the text. Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium finance company to send the insurer a "copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four enumerated items. The statute says nothing about a signature. The court made this explicit: "The statute makes no explicit reference to a signature. If there is a need for one, the word 'certifying' would have to create such a requirement."

That framing — identifying whether a specific term must bear the interpretive weight of creating an unspoken requirement — is central to the court's approach. When a statute prescribes specific procedural steps (here, the content of the certification, the method of delivery, and the timing relative to the insured's cure period), courts are generally reluctant to supplement those steps with additional requirements the legislature did not include.

### Strict adherence does not mean judicial supplementation

This is the point the student may find most counterintuitive. Louisiana courts demand "strict adherence" to the statutory cancellation procedures, and "any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation." That sounds like a standard that should cut in the plaintiffs' favor. But the Fifth Circuit drew a clear line between strict adherence to what the statute actually requires and strict adherence to a judicially expanded version of those requirements:

> "Strict adherence" to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.

In other words, the strict-adherence doctrine intensifies scrutiny of compliance with each statutory element, but it does not authorize courts to manufacture new elements. The absence of a signature requirement in the statute means there is no statutory element with which to strictly comply on that score.

### Benitez is distinguishable, not controlling

The student correctly notes that *Benitez v. Elsayed* mentioned the lack of a signature as one of several shortcomings in the cancellation notice. The Fifth Circuit addressed this head-on and explained why *Benitez* does not establish a signature requirement:

1. **The *Benitez* court identified multiple defects.** The notice in *Benitez* did not identify any employee, was not signed, *and* "does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." The signature was one item in a list of problems; the court did not isolate it as independently disqualifying.

2. **The notices here actually certify.** Unlike in *Benitez*, where the notice failed to "purport to certify" anything, APAC's notices expressly stated: "APAC certifies that:" followed by the four statutory items. The substantive act of certification was present even if a signature was not.

The court thus treated *Benitez* as a case about a notice that failed across the board, not as authority for the proposition that a signature is a standalone prerequisite. A mention of an absence in a list of shortcomings is not the same as a holding that the absence is fatal.

### The identity of the certifying party matters

A subtle but important point in the opinion: the statute designates "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity that effects cancellation by certifying. The court keyed on this: "we see no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified." Because the statute allocates the certifying duty to a corporate entity rather than to a natural person, requiring a human signature would be an awkward judicial addition. A corporate entity can certify through its systems and authorized processes; the statute does not specify that a human hand must sign.

### The broader interpretive principle

The approach here reflects a standard methodological sequence for implied-requirement questions:

1. **Start with the text.** Does the statute use the term in a way that has a settled legal meaning? Here, "certifying" was not shown to carry a settled meaning that includes a signature requirement. The court looked for Louisiana authority and found none.

2. **Examine existing caselaw for the specific term in context.** The court surveyed Louisiana decisions interpreting this statute and found that none imposed a signature requirement. *Benitez* came closest, but its treatment of the signature was descriptive rather than prescriptive.

3. **Identify who the statute charges with the duty.** Because the statute charges the *company* with certifying, not an individual, the court saw no basis to imply an individual-act requirement like a signature.

4. **Resist supplementing an enumerated statutory scheme.** When a statute lays out specific procedural steps, courts generally will not add unstated ones, even under a strict-adherence standard. Strict adherence governs compliance with what is there, not what a court thinks should also be there.

### Bottom line

Under this opinion, "certifying" does not carry an implicit signature requirement in this statutory context. The court's conclusion rests on the silence of the statutory text, the absence of Louisiana authority imposing such a requirement, the corporate identity of the certifying party, and the principle that strict adherence to a detailed statutory scheme is not a license to augment it. *Benitez* acknowledged a missing signature but did not make it a standalone disqualifier, and the Fifth Circuit was careful to limit its reach accordingly.

### Can a computer system's automated receipt satisfy a statutory 'receipt' requirement, or must a human take possession?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the court held that GoAuto's computer system receiving APAC's email constituted 'receipt' under Louisiana's insurance statute, even though no human at GoAuto saw or handled the notice until after the policy was automatically cancelled. The plaintiffs argued that 'receipt' requires human possession. The court used Black's Law Dictionary definitions of 'receipt' and 'receive' to support its conclusion. I'm confused about how courts handle statutory requirements when technology has changed the practical meaning of terms like 'receipt.' Does it matter that the statute was presumably written with human actors in mind? Are there other areas of law where courts have had to decide whether electronic or automated receipt satisfies statutory language?
Answer:
## Answer

The short answer is yes—a computer system's automated receipt can satisfy a statutory "receipt" requirement, at least under the reasoning in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*. The Fifth Circuit held that GoAuto's computer system receiving APAC's email constituted "receipt" under Louisiana's insurance-premium finance statute, even though no human at GoAuto saw or handled the notice until after the policy was automatically cancelled.

### The court's analytical framework

The court began with the text. The Louisiana statute provided that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested." La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the statute itself defined "receipt." Faced with that gap, the court turned to Black's Law Dictionary, which defines "receipt" as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession" and "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." The court accepted those definitions and concluded that GoAuto could cancel "once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation."

The plaintiffs argued that the legislature's use of "receipt"—as opposed to simply requiring the request be "sent"—required a human at GoAuto to take possession before cancellation. The court rejected this. It found that once APAC's computer system sent the email and GoAuto's web service received it, GoAuto had "receipt" for statutory purposes. The court described the GoAuto web service as "like a listener" that is "always listening, waiting to be called," and when the APAC system calls it, the GoAuto system "performs the activity"—that is, cancels the policy.

### Does it matter that the statute was written with human actors in mind?

The court's answer, in substance, is no. The opinion characterizes the statute as "a modern statute set in current times" and states plainly that "company computers do much that individuals once did." This reasoning signals that when a statute is silent about who or what must "receive" a notice, courts will not import a human-actor requirement that the text does not impose.

Notably, the court did not engage in any analysis of legislative intent or drafting history. It did not ask whether the Louisiana legislature contemplated automated receipt when it enacted or amended the statute. Instead, the court relied on the dictionary definitions of "receipt" and "receive" and applied those meanings to the technological reality before it.

### Strict adherence did not mean adding requirements

An important feature of the court's reasoning was its treatment of Louisiana's "strict adherence" standard. Louisiana courts require strict adherence to the statutory cancellation procedures; any defect renders the cancellation ineffective. But the court was careful to distinguish strict adherence from judicial supplementation: "'Strict adherence' to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." The court thus refused to read a human-receipt requirement into a statute that the legislature did not include—even under a strict adherence standard.

This is a significant move. The plaintiffs had a plausible argument that "strict adherence" should mean that every statutory term is given its most demanding reading. The court rejected that framing: strict adherence means following what the statute actually requires, not augmenting the statute with additional procedural hurdles.

### The broader pattern

The opinion does not survey other areas of law, so it does not directly address whether courts have reached similar conclusions in other statutory contexts. What it does provide is a clear method for handling statutory terms in light of technological change:

1. **Identify the statutory text** and determine whether the term is defined.
2. **If the term is undefined, consult dictionary definitions** to establish ordinary meaning.
3. **Apply the ordinary meaning to the current technological context** without importing requirements the text does not impose.
4. **Decline to add procedural requirements** under the guise of strict adherence.

The court's statement that "company computers do much that individuals once did" signals a willingness to treat automated systems as adequate statutory actors when the statutory text does not specify human action.

One caveat: the court emphasized that this was "a modern statute set in current times." The opinion leaves open whether a court would apply the same reasoning to a statute enacted long before automated systems existed, particularly if legislative history or statutory context suggested the legislature specifically contemplated human action. The opinion's reasoning appears anchored in part to the statute's modern context—a factor worth keeping in mind when analogizing to other statutes.

### Is it legally problematic that the policy was cancelled before a human signed the certification documents?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the timeline seems important: APAC's computer automatically sends the cancellation request, GoAuto's computer automatically cancels the policy, and only later in the morning does an APAC employee stamp a signature onto the printed documents. So the policy was effectively cancelled before any human signed anything. The court didn't seem troubled by this temporal sequence. But if the statute requires a 'certifying' statement, and certification typically implies some human verification, how can the cancellation be valid before the certification is signed? Is there a legal doctrine that addresses retroactive validation of automated actions? Does it matter that the signature was purely ministerial rather than substantive?
Answer:
The short answer is that the court didn't need a retroactive validation doctrine because it held that **no signature was required in the first place**. The temporal gap between the automated cancellation and the later signature stamp was legally irrelevant because the statute, as interpreted by the court, was already satisfied at the time of cancellation.

## The statute requires a "statement certifying," not a signature

The key move in the court's reasoning is textual. Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires the premium finance company to send the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four enumerated items. The court observed that "[t]he statute makes no explicit reference to a signature." The plaintiffs argued that the word "certifying" implicitly requires one, but the court rejected that reading, noting that the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity that effects cancellation by certifying—not a named individual employee.

The court supported this with the observation that Louisiana case law has never held a signature to be an independent statutory requirement. The closest case, *Benitez v. Elsayed*, mentioned the absence of a signature but only as one of several shortcomings, and the notice in that case also failed to "purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." Here, by contrast, the e-mailed notice contained the full certifying language. The court distinguished the two cases on exactly that basis: "Unlike in *Benitez*, each notice 'certifies' the relevant information."

So the certification was in the **content of the statement sent**, not in a human's act of signing it.

## The statutory requirements were met at the moment of cancellation

Once the court determined that no signature was required, the temporal sequence becomes straightforward. The statute requires two things before the insurer may cancel: (1) the insurer's "receipt" of the notice, and (2) that the notice contain a "statement certifying" the four enumerated items. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b)(i).

Both were satisfied when APAC's computer sent the e-mail containing the certifying language and GoAuto's computer received it. The court found receipt occurred at that point: "once the e-mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto's computer system, that computer system cancels the policy." The court explicitly embraced the automated process: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

The later signature stamp added nothing that the statute required. It was, as you suggest, ministerial—but the court's reasoning goes further than calling it ministerial. The court held it was **superfluous to statutory compliance**.

## No retroactive validation doctrine was needed

Because the statute was satisfied at the time of cancellation, there is nothing to retroactively validate. A retroactive validation doctrine would only be necessary if the cancellation were defective when performed and needed to be cured by a later act. The court's holding forecloses that premise: the cancellation was valid when it occurred because the e-mail containing the certifying statement had been received. The signature stamp that followed was legally irrelevant to the question of statutory compliance.

In sum, the court's analysis rests on a straightforward textual interpretation: the statute requires a certifying *statement* from the *company*, not a signature from a *person*. Because the certifying statement was received before the cancellation was effected, the statutory prerequisites were met, and the later human signature neither added to nor cured anything.

### When should a federal court certify a question of state law to the state supreme court?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify the statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing they were important to Louisiana's insurance industry. The court declined, quoting Jefferson v. Lead Industry Association that they are 'chary about certifying' and that the mere fact a term hasn't been defined by the state supreme court 'is not sufficient to warrant certification.' I'm confused about the certification mechanism: When is it appropriate for a federal appellate court to certify a question to a state supreme court? What factors do courts consider? Does the importance of the issue to an entire industry matter, or is that specifically not enough? How does certification work procedurally?
Answer:
## Answer

The opinion gives you a fairly compact statement of the Fifth Circuit's certification standard, though it doesn't spell out a multi-factor test. Here's what we can extract.

### The Basic Standard

The Fifth Circuit's governing principle comes from *Jefferson v. Lead Industry Association*, which the court quotes directly: the court is "chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so." The court treats certification as a valuable but finite resource — it says it "will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often." So certification is available, but the court exercises it sparingly and only when there is a *compelling* reason.

### What Is Not Enough

The opinion identifies at least two things that, by themselves, do not justify certification:

1. **An undefined term in the state supreme court's case law.** The court explicitly holds that the mere fact a statutory term "has not yet been defined by a state supreme court 'is not sufficient to warrant certification.'" In this case, the Louisiana Supreme Court had not defined "receipt" as used in the cancellation statute, yet the Fifth Circuit resolved the issue itself using dictionary definitions and statutory context.

2. **Importance to an industry.** The plaintiffs argued that the proper interpretation of the statutory terms was "important to Louisiana's insurance industry and statutory scheme." The court declined to certify without engaging substantively with that argument. The court's silence here is significant — it suggests that industry-wide importance, standing alone, does not constitute the kind of "compelling reason" the standard requires.

### Why the Court Declined Here

Reading between the lines, the court evidently concluded it could resolve the questions presented using existing tools: the statutory text, dictionary definitions, and Louisiana intermediate appellate decisions. The court noted that Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to the cancellation statute but emphasized that "strict adherence" is not "an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." Because the statute made no explicit reference to a signature requirement and because GoAuto's electronic receipt process satisfied the court's understanding of "receipt," there was no genuine ambiguity that the court felt unable to resolve.

### Procedural Mechanics

The opinion is sparse on the procedural details, but it reveals the basic framework. Certification is **requested by a party** — here, the plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify the statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. The federal appellate court then exercises its **discretion** to grant or deny the request. When certification is granted, the federal court formally submits a question (or questions) to the state supreme court, which then answers them, and the federal court applies those answers to resolve the case. When, as here, the court declines, it simply proceeds to decide the state-law question itself.

### The Takeaway

The Fifth Circuit's approach, as reflected in this opinion, is that a federal court should certify only when it has a *compelling reason* — something beyond the mere absence of state supreme court authority or the commercial significance of the issue. The court appears to reserve certification for cases where existing state-law sources (statutory text, intermediate appellate decisions, dictionaries, canons of construction) are genuinely insufficient to predict how the state supreme court would rule. Where the court can resolve the question through ordinary tools of statutory interpretation, it will do so rather than defer to the state court.

### What is a 'private carrier' in the context of delivering legal notices, and why did the court avoid this issue?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the district court held that the APAC employee who delivered cancellation notices qualified as a 'private carrier' under the Louisiana statute, which allows notice to be sent by 'depositing in the mail or with a private carrier.' The district court analogized to a Fifth Circuit case about railroads. The Fifth Circuit called this definition 'imaginative and unconventional' but declined to evaluate it because it found the email satisfied the statute independently. I'm confused: What exactly is a 'private carrier' in the context of delivering legal documents? Why would an employee of the same company delivering documents to a shared filing cabinet potentially qualify? And why did the appellate court seem skeptical of this reasoning but decline to address it?
Answer:
## What "private carrier" means here

The Louisiana statute at issue—La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)—permits a premium finance company to request cancellation of an insurance policy by sending the required notice to the insurer "by depositing in the mail **or with a private carrier**, or via electronic mail." The statute does not define "private carrier," and as the Fifth Circuit noted, there is no Louisiana case law interpreting that term in this context.

In general legal usage, a "private carrier" is a party that transports goods or documents under a specific agreement for a particular customer, as distinguished from a "common carrier," which holds itself out to carry goods for the general public. The district court attempted to import a definition from a Fifth Circuit case involving a railroad—*Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C.*, 452 F. App'x 453 (5th Cir. 2011)—and applied it by analogy to the APAC employee who physically carried cancellation documents to a shared filing cabinet (or, post-2017, to a GoAuto employee who stamped them "received"). The district court concluded that this employee "neatly fits the definition of private carrier."

## Why the Fifth Circuit was skeptical

The Fifth Circuit called the district court's definition "imaginative and unconventional," which is about as close as an appellate court gets to saying "we think this is probably wrong" without explicitly reversing. The skepticism is well-founded: stretching a doctrine developed in the context of railroads to cover a salaried employee of the same corporate family physically walking documents to a shared filing cabinet is a significant interpretive leap. The statute envisions "depositing" notice "with a private carrier," which naturally suggests an independent third-party delivery service—not an intra-office handoff between affiliated entities.

## Why the court declined to address it

The court declined because it found an independent and sufficient basis to uphold the statutory compliance: **the email**. The court held that once APAC's computer system emailed the cancellation notice to GoAuto's computer system and GoAuto's system received it and effected cancellation, the statutory requirement was satisfied. As the court explained:

> "Because we find the e-mail satisfies the statute, we will not consider the validity of the definition."

This reflects a standard principle of appellate review: when a lower court's decision can be affirmed on one ground, the appellate court need not—and generally should not—address alternative rationales that may be questionable. The physical delivery by the APAC employee was, in practical terms, a secondary or backstop procedure; the actual cancellation was triggered electronically. Since the email independently satisfied the statute, the "private carrier" question was effectively moot, and resolving it would have produced an advisory opinion on a novel issue of state law.

Notably, the court also declined the plaintiffs' request to certify the statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, citing the Fifth Circuit's general reluctance to overuse certification. This means the precise meaning of "private carrier" under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) remains an open question in Louisiana law.

### Why was summary judgment appropriate for determining whether GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with the statute?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the parties filed cross-motions for summary judgment 'related solely to whether GoAuto's procedure for cancelling the financed insurance policies satisfied Louisiana law.' The district court granted GoAuto's motion and dismissed all claims. I'm trying to understand the procedural posture: How can a court grant summary judgment when there seem to be factual questions about the timing of signatures, who received what when, and whether the computer systems interacted as described? Is statutory compliance always a question of law suitable for summary judgment, or are there situations where factual disputes about compliance procedures would preclude summary judgment? What standard did the court apply?
Answer:
## Summary judgment was appropriate because the material facts were undisputed; only the legal meaning of the statute was at issue.

Your question seems to assume there were genuine factual disputes about how GoAuto's cancellation process worked. The opinion doesn't support that assumption. The parties filed **cross-motions** for summary judgment on the narrow question of whether GoAuto's procedure "satisfied Louisiana law." Cross-motions for summary judgment typically signal that both sides agree on what happened—the facts are established through discovery—and disagree only on the legal conclusion those facts support.

Here, the facts about GoAuto's procedures were established and not in genuine dispute: APAC's computer system automatically e-mails GoAuto a cancellation request with the required certifications; GoAuto's computer system cancels the policy upon receipt; an APAC employee later applies a signature stamp to printed documents; and since 2017 a GoAuto employee stamps the document as "received." Both sides agreed this is how the process worked. The dispute was about **what the statute requires** given those undisputed facts—specifically, whether the statute demands a signature before cancellation and whether electronic receipt by a computer system counts as "receipt" by the insurer.

### The legal standard

Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56, summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The Fifth Circuit reviews a grant of summary judgment **de novo**, applying the same standard as the district court.

The court framed the question as one of statutory interpretation: what does "certifying" require, and what does "receipt" mean under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)? These are questions of law, not fact. As the court explained, the statute "makes no explicit reference to a signature," and no Louisiana Supreme Court opinion has defined "receipt" in this context. Resolving those questions required the court to interpret the statute and apply it to the undisputed procedures—an exercise in legal reasoning suitable for summary judgment.

### When factual disputes *would* preclude summary judgment

Statutory compliance is not always resolvable on summary judgment. If, for example, there were a genuine dispute about whether APAC actually sent the notice, whether the notice contained the required certifications, whether the computer system actually received and processed the request, or whether payments had in fact been made—those would be factual disputes requiring trial. Summary judgment would be inappropriate because the court could not determine statutory compliance without first resolving contested facts.

But that was not this case. The Plaintiffs did not dispute that the e-mails were sent and received or that the certifications were included. They argued that the statute, properly interpreted, requires more than what GoAuto did—a **pre-cancellation signature** and **physical possession by a human employee**. Those are legal arguments about what the statute demands, not factual disputes about what occurred.

### How the court resolved the legal questions

On the signature issue, the court held that the statute requires the premium finance company to "certif[y]" certain information but does not require a signature. The court noted the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity doing the certifying, and declined to add a procedural requirement the legislature did not include: "Strict adherence to the statute is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures."

On the receipt issue, the court adopted the Black's Law Dictionary definition of "receipt" as the act of receiving, especially by taking physical possession. It then concluded that GoAuto's computer system receiving the e-mail satisfied the statute: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

### In short

Summary judgment was appropriate because the parties agreed on the facts of GoAuto's cancellation process and disagreed only on whether that process satisfied the statute. When the material facts are undisputed and the case turns on statutory interpretation, summary judgment is the proper vehicle for resolving the dispute. The factual details you mention—the timing of signatures, the computer system interaction—were not in genuine dispute; they were the agreed-upon facts to which the court applied its legal interpretation of the statute.
