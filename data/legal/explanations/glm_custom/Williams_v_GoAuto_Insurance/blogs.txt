Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Strict Adherence Meets Automated Cancellation: How Far Can Courts Modernize Statutory Procedures Without Rewriting Them?

When a statute says that a company must "certify" certain information before an insurance policy is cancelled, and must do so in a way that the insurer "receives," does it matter that no human being reviews, signs, or even sees the certification before the cancellation takes effect? That is the question the Fifth Circuit answered in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, No. 24-30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), and the answer—no, it does not matter—deserves close scrutiny, not because the court's reasoning is implausible, but because it may quietly drain a consumer-protective statute of much of its force.

## Why This Case Matters

Louisiana's insurance-premium finance statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G), governs how a premium finance company may cancel an insurance policy when an insured defaults on premium payments. The statute sets out a specific sequence: the finance company sends the insured a notice of cancellation, allows a cure period, and then sends the insurer a notice with a statement "certifying" that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the default remains uncured, that the insured was properly notified, and that interested third parties received copies. "Upon receipt" of that notice, the insurer may cancel the policy.

Louisiana courts have long demanded "strict adherence" to these requirements. The doctrine, as articulated in cases like *Britten v. Reavis* and *Eaglin v. Champion Insurance Co.*, serves two functions: it fixes the exact time and date of cancellation, and it guarantees the insured a minimum window to cure the default. Any defect in the process renders the cancellation ineffective. This is formalist, intentionally so—the formalities protect consumers who may be living paycheck to paycheck and whose insurance coverage is a legal or practical necessity.

The question in *Williams* is what happens to that formalism when the process is fully automated. GoAuto's procedure works as follows: when an insured misses a payment, APAC (the premium finance company) sends a ten-day notice. If payment is not received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically emails GoAuto's computer system a copy of the cancellation notice along with a certification statement on APAC letterhead. GoAuto's computer system—described at deposition as a "web service" that is "always listening"—receives the email and cancels the policy. No human reviews anything before cancellation occurs. Later that morning, an APAC employee affixes a signature stamp to a printed copy of the cancellation documents and delivers them to a GoAuto employee, who stamps them "received" and files them.

The plaintiffs argued that this process failed strict adherence in two principal ways: first, because no one signed the certification before the policy was cancelled, and second, because no one at GoAuto actually "received" the cancellation request before it was acted upon. The Fifth Circuit rejected both arguments.

## The Certification Question: What Does "Certifying" Mean Without a Signature?

The court's analysis of the signature requirement begins with a textual observation: the statute says "the insurance premium finance company" certifies certain information, but it never explicitly requires a signature. The court then surveyed the limited Louisiana case law and found no decision holding that a signature is required. The closest analogue, *Benitez v. Elsayed*, mentioned the absence of a signature among several notice defects, but did not hold that the lack of a signature alone invalidated the cancellation. And critically, unlike in *Benitez*, the notices in this case did contain certification language—they just were not signed by a named individual until after cancellation.

From this, the court concluded that "strict adherence" to the statute is not "an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." The statute requires the company to certify; the company certified; no signature is mentioned; therefore no signature is required.

This reasoning is defensible as a matter of textualism, but it raises a deeper question that the opinion does not fully engage with. The word "certify" carries an inherent connotation of attestation—an assertion by a responsible party that something is true. When a company sends a document on its letterhead with certification language, the court is willing to treat the company itself as the certifying entity, with the signature stamp applied later serving as a sort of retroactive authentication. But the protective purpose of strict adherence, as articulated in the Louisiana cases the court itself cited, is to ensure that the process is reliable and that the insured's rights are protected at every step. A process in which no human verifies the accuracy of the certification before the policy is cancelled—where the "certifying" act is performed by a computer transmitting a pre-written template—arguably dilutes the concept of certification to the point of formality without substance.

To be fair to the court, the plaintiffs did not point to Louisiana authority requiring a signature, and the statute's reference to "the insurance premium finance company" rather than an individual employee supports the idea that the company, not a person, is the certifying entity. But the court's framing of the issue as one of not "adding to" the statute slightly sidesteps the harder question: what does "certifying" meaningfully require if not some form of human attestation before the certified statement takes legal effect? The court's answer—essentially that the certification language itself, transmitted by the company's system, is sufficient—is pragmatic but arguably reduces "certifying" to "transmitting a document that uses the word 'certifies.'"

## The Receipt Question: Can a Computer "Receive"?

The court's analysis of "receipt" is more straightforward and, in my view, more persuasive. The statute provides that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested." The plaintiffs argued that "receipt" requires a human at GoAuto to take possession of the notice before cancellation. The court, relying on Black's Law Dictionary definitions of "receipt" and "receive," concluded that GoAuto satisfied the statute when its computer system received and processed the email. The court noted that "this is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did."

This conclusion is reasonable. The statute does not specify that receipt must be by a natural person, and the dictionary definitions the court cited—taking possession, coming into possession of—can comfortably encompass electronic receipt. Moreover, it would be odd to require a human at the insurer to physically process a document that the statute permits to be sent by email. The court's reading avoids the absurd result of requiring a purely manual step in an otherwise electronic process.

But here too, there is a tension with strict adherence's protective function. The doctrine exists in part to fix the exact time of cancellation and to ensure a minimum cure period. An automated process does fix the time of cancellation precisely—but it does so by eliminating any opportunity for human review of whether the certification is accurate. If, for example, an insured had actually cured the default but APAC's system failed to register the payment, the policy would be cancelled instantly, with no check. The court's framework assumes that the automated process is reliable, but the strict adherence doctrine was designed precisely for cases where processes fail.

## The Certification Decline: Why Not Ask Louisiana?

One of the more notable aspects of the opinion is the court's refusal to certify the statutory questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. The plaintiffs argued that the proper interpretation of "certifying" and "receipt" under section 9:3550(G) is important to Louisiana's insurance industry and statutory scheme, and that the Louisiana Supreme Court has not addressed these specific questions. The court declined, citing its general reluctance to certify absent a "compelling reason" and noting that the mere absence of state supreme court guidance is not sufficient.

This is consistent with Fifth Circuit practice, but it is worth questioning whether the circumstances here warranted an exception. The case presents a novel intersection of a consumer-protective statutory scheme and fully automated processing—a question that affects not just these plaintiffs but potentially every insured in Louisiana whose policy is premium-financed. The Louisiana Supreme Court, which is the authoritative interpreter of Louisiana statutes, might well have a different view of whether "strict adherence" tolerates a process in which no human reviews the certification before cancellation. By resolving the question itself, the Fifth Circuit has effectively made a state-law determination that could shape industry practice across Louisiana without input from the state's highest court.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The most immediate implication of *Williams* is practical: insurers and premium finance companies in Louisiana now have clear federal appellate court authority that fully automated cancellation processes satisfy section 9:3550(G), so long as the certification language is present in the transmitted document and the insurer's computer system receives it. This will likely accelerate the trend toward automation in insurance cancellation, with other companies adopting or maintaining similar procedures.

The deeper doctrinal concern is what *Williams* signals for strict adherence doctrines more broadly. Louisiana's strict adherence rule is one of the more formalist consumer-protection mechanisms in insurance law. Its power lies in the fact that procedural defects—missing signatures, incorrect notices, incomplete certifications—render cancellations ineffective, regardless of whether the insured was actually prejudiced. By interpreting "certifying" to require no human attestation and "receipt" to require no human possession, the Fifth Circuit has preserved the form of strict adherence while permitting a process that arguably lacks the substance the doctrine was designed to ensure.

This is not to say the court was wrong as a matter of textual interpretation. The statute does not explicitly require a signature, and the dictionary definitions of "receipt" can encompass electronic delivery. But there is a difference between reading a statute to accommodate modern technology and reading it to eliminate the human safeguards that the statutory scheme appears to contemplate. The court's opinion walks that line, and it is not obvious that it lands on the right side.

The court was also careful to note that it was not reaching the district court's "imaginative and unconventional" interpretation of "private carrier"—the lower court had found that the APAC employee who delivered printed documents to GoAuto qualified as a "private carrier" under the statute. The Fifth Circuit's decision to rest its holding on the email-receipt theory alone, and its pointed characterization of the district court's reasoning, suggests some awareness that the case was pushing the statutory text in uncomfortable directions. But the alternative path the court chose, while more conventional, may have its own uncomfortable implications for the consumers that strict adherence was meant to protect.



# What Does It Mean to 'Certify'? The Fifth Circuit's Textualist Reading of Louisiana's Cancellation Statute

When does an automated email from one company's computer system to another's constitute a legal "certification"? The Fifth Circuit's decision in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* offers a textualist answer that should give practitioners pause—not because the court got the text wrong, but because the implications of getting it right are broader than the court acknowledged.

The case arises from a common arrangement in the auto-insurance world. Insureds who cannot afford a lump-sum premium payment finance their policies through premium-finance companies. Here, American Premium Assistance Company ("APAC") paid GoAuto the full premium upfront and collected monthly installments from the insureds. When an insured defaulted, APAC—exercising a power of attorney granted in the financing agreement—cancelled the policy. Louisiana's Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) governs that cancellation process and requires the finance company to send the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four enumerated facts: that the financing agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the agreement is in uncured default, that notice of cancellation was sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to all interested persons.

The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's cancellation procedures failed strict statutory compliance in two critical respects. First, no human signature appeared on APAC's certification at the time the policy was cancelled. Second, GoAuto never meaningfully "received" the cancellation request because the entire process was automated. The Fifth Circuit rejected both arguments and affirmed summary judgment for GoAuto. The reasoning on the certification issue is the more analytically interesting of the two holdings and deserves close attention.

## The Signature Question: Text, Silence, and Corporate Actors

The plaintiffs' argument had intuitive force. Louisiana courts demand "strict adherence" to the cancellation statute—any defect renders the cancellation ineffective. A "certification" without a signature, the plaintiffs contended, is no certification at all. A signature authenticates the statement, identifies the person taking responsibility for it, and supplies a basis for evidentiary and accountability mechanisms. Without one, the certification is a naked assertion.

Judge Southwick, writing for the panel, acknowledged that no Louisiana opinion had squarely addressed whether § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires a signature. The court surveyed the relevant case law and found only one decision—*Benitez v. Elsayed*—that had even mentioned the absence of a signature among several noticed shortcomings, and that court had not held a signature to be independently necessary. The plaintiffs thus asked the court to imply a signature requirement from the word "certifying."

The Fifth Circuit declined. The opinion's reasoning rests on two textualist moves. First, the statute says nothing about a signature. The word "certifying" must bear the entire weight of any such requirement, and the court was unwilling to read into that word a formal authentication requirement the legislature did not articulate. Second—and more importantly—the statute identifies the entity that certifies as "the insurance premium finance company," not a named individual. As the court observed, the statute "states it is 'the insurance premium finance company' who effects cancellation by 'certifying' certain information in a notice." Because the statutory actor is the corporation, not a human agent, a corporate certification without an identified employee suffices.

This is a clean textualist holding, and it is probably correct as a matter of statutory reading. The legislature said "the insurance premium finance company" certifies; it did not say "an authorized employee of the insurance premium finance company." If the legislature wanted a signature, it knew how to require one. The court's refusal to add a procedural step the statute does not mention is consistent with its observation that "strict adherence" to the statute "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures."

But the holding raises a question the opinion does not fully engage with: what is the legal function of "certifying" if not to attach an identifiable actor's name and reputation to the truth of the statements being made?

## The Accountability Function of Certifications

Traditionally, certifications serve at least three purposes. They authenticate—confirming that the statement comes from the entity or person it purports to come from. They create accountability—identifying a person whose veracity can be challenged and who may bear consequences for false statements. And they provide evidentiary structure—giving courts and opposing parties a clear record of who said what and when.

The Fifth Circuit's holding preserves the first function adequately. An automated email from APAC's computer system to GoAuto's, on APAC letterhead, with the required substantive statements, is sufficiently authenticated as coming from APAC. The corporation has "certified."

But the accountability and evidentiary functions are attenuated. When APAC's computer sends the certification email at, say, 2:00 a.m., and a human employee applies a signature stamp to a printed copy later that morning—after the policy has already been cancelled—no individual has vouched for the truth of the certified statements at the time they mattered. The signature that eventually appears is retrospective and ceremonial. If one of the four certified facts turns out to be false, who is responsible? The corporation, certainly. But the traditional legal mechanism for certifications—tying a named human to the statement at the time it is made—has been bypassed.

This is not necessarily a problem the Fifth Circuit was obliged to solve. The plaintiffs asked the court to read a signature requirement into a statute that does not contain one, and the court correctly declined. But the opinion's casual acceptance that a fully automated certification satisfies the statute should prompt legislators and practitioners to consider whether the statutory framework's evidentiary and accountability assumptions have been quietly outmoded.

## "Receipt" in the Age of Automated Cancellation

The court's second holding—that GoAuto "received" the cancellation request when its computer system processed APAC's email—follows similar logic. The statute provides that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract." The plaintiffs argued that "receipt" requires human possession, pointing out that the employee who stamps the cancellation documents as "received" is an APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee.

The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument with a nod to modern commercial reality. Citing Black's Law Dictionary for the proposition that "receipt" means taking possession or custody, the court concluded that GoAuto's computer system—described in the record as a "web service" that is "always listening, waiting to be called"—can receive the notice. "This is a modern statute set in current times," the court wrote, "and company computers do much that individuals once did." The cancellation effected by the computer system satisfies the statute.

This holding is sensible and likely unremarkable standing alone. Courts have broadly accepted that computer systems can send and receive legal notices. But combined with the certification holding, it produces a result in which the entire statutory cancellation process—from the finance company's certification to the insurer's receipt to the cancellation itself—unfolds without any human involvement. The human acts that follow—the signature stamp, the physical filing—are post hoc administrative steps that play no role in the legal effectiveness of the cancellation.

## Navigating the Absence of Precedent

One notable feature of the opinion is how the court handled the near-total absence of Louisiana authority on both the signature and receipt questions. The plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify these issues to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the proper interpretation of the statutory terms was important to Louisiana's insurance industry.

The court declined. Quoting *Jefferson v. Lead Industry Association*, the panel reiterated its general reluctance to certify questions absent a compelling reason, noting that the mere absence of a state supreme court definition is not sufficient to warrant certification. This is a defensible application of Fifth Circuit certification doctrine, though practitioners in Louisiana's insurance bar may reasonably question whether questions of this practical importance—ones that affect the validity of countless policy cancellations across the state—deserved state-court attention. The Louisiana Supreme Court remains free to address the issue if and when it chooses, but for now, the Fifth Circuit's reading governs in federal court.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The holding's most significant downstream effect may be its tacit endorsement of fully automated statutory compliance. Louisiana's cancellation statute was written with an implicit assumption that humans would perform the relevant acts—certifying, sending, receiving, cancelling. The Fifth Circuit's opinion does not rewrite the statute but reads it in light of current commercial practices, accepting that automated systems can perform each statutory function. That reading is probably correct under the text. But it shifts the analytical frame: the question is no longer whether a human performed the required act, but whether the system that performed it is attributable to the statutory actor.

For premium-finance companies and insurers, the decision provides helpful clarity. GoAuto's procedures—or procedures substantially similar—are now validated under federal diversity jurisdiction. For insureds, the decision narrows the set of available challenges to automated cancellations. The most plausible remaining avenue for challenge would be the substantive accuracy of the four certified facts—whether the financing agreement actually contained a valid power of attorney, whether the default was real and uncured, whether notice was actually sent to the insured, and whether interested parties were notified. These are the facts the statute requires be "certified," and they remain the substantive heart of the cancellation process. But the procedural formalities—the signature, the human receipt—are no longer independent grounds for invalidating a cancellation, at least not in the Fifth Circuit.

The broader lesson is one that legislative drafters should heed. When a statute requires a "certification" without specifying the form it must take, courts will look to the statutory actor the legislature identified. If the legislature says the corporation certifies, the corporation can certify—through whatever means corporations now use to communicate. If legislators intended certifications to carry the evidentiary and accountability weight of a human signature, that requirement needs to be stated. The Fifth Circuit's textualism is sound, but it reveals a gap between what the statute assumes and what it actually says.



# 'Receipt' in the Age of APIs: Statutory Interpretation When Computers Talk to Computers

When a Louisiana statute says an insurer must "receive" a cancellation notice before terminating a policy, does it matter that no human being ever saw the notice? That is the question the Fifth Circuit answered in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, and the answer—no, it does not matter—has implications that extend well beyond Louisiana insurance law. As more regulatory and commercial processes become fully automated, courts will increasingly confront statutes that use language rooted in a world of paper, mail, and human hands. *Williams* offers an early test case for how courts should handle that translation, and the Fifth Circuit's approach is both revealing and, in some respects, unsatisfying.

## The Case and Its Stakes

The plaintiffs in *Williams* were insureds whose GoAuto automobile policies had been canceled after they defaulted on premium-financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company (APAC). Under Louisiana law, a premium finance company may request cancellation of a policy by sending the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a certified statement confirming the existence of a valid power of attorney, the uncured default, and that proper notice was sent to the insured and interested parties. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The insurer may then cancel the policy "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company." La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to these statutory requirements; "[a]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy." The stakes are significant: if cancellation is ineffective, the policy remains in force, meaning the insurer may be liable for claims arising after the purported cancellation date. Given that premium financing is common among policyholders who cannot afford to pay an entire annual premium upfront—and that such policyholders may be less likely to default carefully on their installment obligations—the volume of potential disputes over cancellation procedures is substantial.

GoAuto's cancellation process was almost entirely automated. When an insured failed to make a timely payment, APAC sent a ten-day notice. If payment was not submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically sent GoAuto an email containing the cancellation notice and the required certification. GoAuto's computer system—a "web service" described by a deponent as "always listening, waiting to be called"—received the email and automatically canceled the policy. No human being was involved until later that morning, when an APAC employee affixed a signature stamp to printed cancellation documents and delivered them to a GoAuto employee who stamped them as "received."

The plaintiffs argued that this process failed to satisfy the statute in two principal ways. First, the certification lacked a signature at the time of cancellation. Second, GoAuto never actually "received" the cancellation request because no human at GoAuto took possession of it before the automated system acted.

## The Signature Question—and Why It Matters Less

The Fifth Circuit's rejection of the signature requirement was straightforward and defensible. The statute requires that the premium finance company certify certain information, but it says nothing about a signature. The court acknowledged one Louisiana appellate decision, *Benitez v. Elsayed*, that noted the absence of a signature among a notice's shortcomings, but correctly observed that *Benitez* did not hold that a missing signature alone invalidated an otherwise proper certification. The statute attributes the certification to "the insurance premium finance company"—an entity, not an individual—and the court reasonably concluded that an entity can certify information without identifying a human signatory.

This holding is unlikely to generate much controversy. Corporate certifications are routinely executed without individual signatures in electronic contexts, and the statute's silence on the point makes the plaintiffs' position difficult to sustain. The more interesting and consequential question is the second one.

## "Receipt" and the Problem of Automated Processing

The plaintiffs' argument on receipt was more textured. The statute uses the word "receipt," which the plaintiffs contended requires the insurer to take possession of the cancellation request before canceling the policy. In GoAuto's process, the cancellation occurred automatically upon the computer system's receipt of the email—before any human at GoAuto was aware of it. The plaintiffs argued that the legislature's use of "receipt," rather than a simple sending requirement, implied that a human at the insurer needed to take possession.

The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, but its reasoning deserves close examination. The court began by noting that the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined "receipt" as used in the statute and that the statute itself contains no definition. The court then turned to Black's Law Dictionary, which defines "receipt" as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession" and "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." From these definitions, the court concluded that "GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation."

The court then applied this conclusion to the automated process. It described the GoAuto web service as a "listener" that responds when "called" by APAC's system, and concluded: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

There are two moves here, and they rest on different analytical foundations. The first move—defining "receipt" as taking possession, delivery, or custody—is conventional dictionary-based interpretation. The second move—concluding that a computer system's automated receipt satisfies this definition—is where the court does something more interesting, and more contestable.

## The Conceptual Gap

The difficulty is that Black's Law Dictionary's definitions, upon which the court relied, are anchored in physical possession. "Taking physical possession" is the paradigmatic example the dictionary gives for "receipt." "To come into possession of" implies an entity capable of possessing something. When a computer system automatically processes an email and executes a cancellation, it is not obvious that anyone—or anything—has "taken possession" in any meaningful sense. The email has been received by a server, parsed by an application, and acted upon by a web service. No human has come into possession of anything. The computer system itself does not "possess" the email in any anthropomorphic sense; it processes data.

One could argue that the court's reliance on physical-possession definitions actually cuts against its conclusion. If "receipt" means taking physical possession, and if only natural persons (or perhaps corporate entities acting through human agents) can take physical possession, then an automated computer process does not satisfy the statutory requirement. The court's logical bridge—that "company computers do much that individuals once did"—is not interpretation so much as adaptation. It is a policy judgment that the statute should accommodate modern commercial practices, not a conclusion that flows from the statutory text or the dictionary definitions the court cited.

This is not to say the court reached the wrong result. The Louisiana statute expressly permits transmission "via electronic mail," which strongly suggests the legislature contemplated digital communication between the finance company and the insurer. It would be odd to allow email transmission but require human possession of the transmitted document before the insurer could act on it. And the statute's reference to the insurer proceeding to cancel "upon receipt" is most naturally read as identifying the trigger for cancellation, not prescribing a human-in-the-loop requirement. But the court could have reached this conclusion more directly by reasoning from the statute's express authorization of email transmission, rather than by invoking dictionary definitions rooted in physical possession and then silently extending them to cover automated processing.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The most significant implication of *Williams* is its tacit endorsement of the principle that when a statute authorizes electronic communication, automated processing of that communication can satisfy statutory prerequisites even without human involvement. This principle will matter well beyond insurance cancellation. Consider the many contexts in which statutes require that an entity "receive," "accept," or "acknowledge" something: regulatory filings, consumer notices, contract formations, and government benefit determinations, to name a few. If automated receipt suffices in the insurance-cancellation context, it is hard to see why it would not suffice in these other contexts as well.

This raises a deeper question that *Williams* does not fully address: should courts distinguish between statutes that expressly contemplate electronic communication and those that do not? The Louisiana statute at issue in *Williams* was drafted or amended to permit email transmission, making it relatively easy to conclude that the legislature contemplated digital processes. But many statutes use "receipt" language without any reference to electronic communication. For those statutes, the argument for automated receipt is weaker. A court applying *Williams*'s logic to a statute drafted in 1965 that requires "receipt" of a notice would be making a much larger interpretive leap, effectively updating the statute to accommodate technology the legislature never considered.

The court's decision also has practical implications for the insurance industry. By approving GoAuto's fully automated cancellation process, the Fifth Circuit has effectively blessed a model in which premium finance companies and insurers can coordinate cancellation through direct system-to-system integration, without human review. This is efficient, and efficiency is generally good for consumers to the extent it reduces administrative costs. But it also eliminates a human checkpoint that might catch errors—incorrect default determinations, misidentified insureds, or improper certifications. The Louisiana statute's "strict adherence" requirement was designed to ensure that cancellation occurs only when the statutory prerequisites are met. Automated processing may satisfy the letter of the statute while reducing the practical protection the strict-adherence doctrine was meant to provide.

The court's refusal to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court is also worth noting. The Fifth Circuit acknowledged that the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined "receipt" under the statute, and the interpretation of that term has significant implications for the state's insurance industry. The court's reason for declining certification—that "a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court 'is not sufficient to warrant certification'"—is technically correct as a statement of Fifth Circuit policy, but it understates the significance of the question. The Louisiana Supreme Court might well disagree with the Fifth Circuit's conclusion that automated computer receipt satisfies the statute, particularly given the strict-adherence doctrine's protective purpose. By resolving the question itself, the Fifth Circuit has effectively made state law on an important question of insurance regulation without input from the state's highest court.

## A Framework for the Digital Statutory Interpretation Problem

*Williams* illustrates a problem that courts will face with increasing frequency: how to apply statutory language rooted in analog-era concepts to digital-era practices. There are at least three approaches courts might take, each with different implications.

The first is what we might call strict textualism: if the statute says "receipt" and the dictionary defines receipt as physical possession, then automated computer processing does not satisfy the requirement unless a human takes possession. This approach respects the original meaning of the statutory text but may produce absurd results when applied to modern commercial practices that the legislature presumably intended to accommodate.

The second is the approach the Fifth Circuit took in *Williams*: invoke dictionary definitions rooted in physical possession, then extend them to cover automated processing through the observation that "company computers do much that individuals once did." This approach reaches sensible practical results but does so through a logical move that is not fully articulated in the opinion. The gap between the dictionary definition and its application is bridged by an implicit policy judgment rather than by textual analysis.

The third approach—and the one I think courts should adopt—is what we might call contextual technological adaptation: examine the statute as a whole to determine whether the legislature contemplated or intended to accommodate electronic communication, and if so, interpret the statute's operative terms in light of that intent. Under this approach, the Louisiana statute's express authorization of email transmission would be the key analytical move. Having permitted email, the legislature presumably intended that email could effectuate the statutory process, including receipt. The dictionary definitions would be secondary, used to confirm rather than to drive the analysis. This approach is more transparent than the one the Fifth Circuit took, and it provides a more principled basis for distinguishing between statutes that contemplate electronic communication and those that do not.

## Conclusion

*Williams* is, in one sense, a narrow case about insurance cancellation procedures in Louisiana. But it raises a question that will recur across many areas of law: when a statute says an entity must "receive" something, and the entity's computer receives it without any human awareness, has "receipt" occurred? The Fifth Circuit answered yes, and that answer is probably correct as applied to this statute. But the court's reasoning—relying on dictionary definitions rooted in physical possession and then silently extending them to automated processing—leaves something to be desired. Courts facing similar questions in the future would do well to ground their analysis more directly in the statutory context, and to be more candid about the policy judgments that necessarily accompany the application of analog-era statutory language to digital-era practices.



# The Fifth Circuit's Reluctance to Certify Novel State-Law Questions: A Pattern or a Problem?

When a federal appellate court interprets a state statute that no state court has ever construed—and the interpretation affects an entire state's insurance industry—one might expect the court to pause and ask the state's supreme court for guidance. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit did not. The result is a decision that resolves genuinely novel questions of Louisiana insurance law through the Erie guessing game, with implications that extend well beyond the parties before the court.

## The Statutory Puzzle at the Heart of the Case

The case turned on Louisiana's premium-financing statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G), which governs how a premium finance company may request cancellation of an insurance policy for nonpayment. Louisiana courts have long required "strict adherence" to these statutory procedures, and any defect renders cancellation ineffective. The plaintiffs challenged GoAuto's automated cancellation process on two grounds: that the certification sent by the finance company lacked a required signature, and that GoAuto never "received" the cancellation request as the statute requires because the entire process was executed by computers in the early morning hours before any human reviewed the documents.

Both arguments required the Fifth Circuit to interpret statutory terms that Louisiana courts have not squarely addressed. The statute says the finance company must send the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" certain information—but says nothing about a signature. It says that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation... has been requested"—but does not define "receipt." Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor any Louisiana appellate court has determined whether "certifying" implies a signature requirement, or whether "receipt" demands human possession rather than electronic ingestion by a computer system.

The Fifth Circuit resolved both questions itself. On certification, the court reasoned that the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity that certifies, not any individual employee, and that strict adherence "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." On receipt, the court turned to Black's Law Dictionary, adopted its definitions of "receipt" and "receive," and concluded that GoAuto's computer system's electronic acceptance of the emailed cancellation request satisfied the statutory requirement. "This is a modern statute set in current times," the court explained, "and company computers do much that individuals once did."

## The Certification Denial

What makes these holdings noteworthy is not necessarily their substance but the court's refusal to certify the questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. The plaintiffs requested certification, arguing that proper interpretation of these statutory terms was important to Louisiana's insurance industry and statutory scheme. The Fifth Circuit declined, quoting its own precedent: "As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often." The court then applied what amounts to a bright-line rule: that a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court "is not sufficient to warrant certification."

This reasoning deserves scrutiny. The court's stated concern is about overusing the certification mechanism—presumably irritating the Louisiana Supreme Court by flooding it with questions the Fifth Circuit could answer itself. That concern has some force; certification should not become a substitute for the federal court's own analytical work. But the standard the court articulates—that the mere absence of a state supreme court definition is insufficient—sets the threshold so high as to be nearly meaningless. Virtually every certification request involves a question the state supreme court has not answered. If that fact alone is insufficient, what *would* suffice?

## Erie and the Certification Function

The certification mechanism exists precisely to address the tension at the heart of Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins: when a federal court sits in diversity and confronts a genuinely unsettled question of state law, it must either guess at the state court's likely answer or ask the state court directly. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that federal courts should be "chary" of expansive interpretations of state statutes, and that certification serves as a valuable tool for maintaining the federal-state balance. In *Lehman Brothers v. Schein*, the Court noted that certification helps "build cooperative judicial federalism" and avoids "disrupting the harmony of [state] law."

The Fifth Circuit's restrictive approach risks inverting this logic. By declining to certify questions that are genuinely novel and have significant commercial implications, the court effectively positions the federal judiciary as the primary interpreter of Louisiana's insurance code. The *Williams* opinion now stands as the leading construction of § 9:3550(G) on both the signature and receipt questions. Louisiana state courts, confronting the same statute in future cases, will look to the Fifth Circuit's reasoning—reasoning developed without the benefit of adversarial briefing by the Louisiana Attorney General, the Louisiana Department of Insurance, or other stakeholders who might participate in a certified-question proceeding.

This is not a hypothetical concern. Louisiana's insurance industry operates under this statute every day. The question of whether automated cancellation systems satisfy the statute's certification and receipt requirements is not an academic exercise; it affects thousands of policy cancellations and potentially the coverage available to accident victims. The Louisiana Supreme Court, had it been asked, might have reached the same conclusions the Fifth Circuit reached. But it also might have emphasized different considerations—the legislature's apparent intent in using the word "certifying," the role of human oversight in a strict-compliance regime, the policy implications of permitting fully automated cancellations without human verification.

## A Pattern of Reluctance

The Fifth Circuit's approach in *Williams* fits a broader pattern. The court's certification jurisprudence, as developed through cases like *Jefferson v. Lead Industry Association* and its progeny, treats certification as a resource to be conserved rather than a tool to be deployed. The "well" metaphor is telling: certification is something to be drawn from sparingly, lest it run dry. But certification is not a finite natural resource. The Louisiana Supreme Court retains complete discretion to accept or decline certified questions, and the mechanism functions as a valve controlled by the state court itself. If the Louisiana Supreme Court finds a question unworthy of its attention, it can say so.

The more honest concern may be docket management. Certification takes time, and the Fifth Circuit's docket is heavy. But that practical consideration, however real, is not one the court acknowledges in its opinions. Instead, the court frames its reluctance in institutional terms—as if asking the Louisiana Supreme Court to weigh in on the meaning of its own statutes would impose an unfair burden.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The substantive holdings in *Williams* may well prove consequential. The court's conclusion that "certifying" does not require a signature removes one potential check on automated cancellation systems. Its conclusion that electronic receipt by a computer system satisfies the statute blesses a process in which no human reviews the cancellation request before the policy terminates. For an industry that processes cancellations at scale, this is a green light. For insureds who might have argued that strict compliance demands human verification, it is a significant loss.

More broadly, the decision illustrates how federal courts, through the accumulation of Erie predictions, can effectively freeze state statutory interpretation in place. Once the Fifth Circuit has spoken, the Louisiana Supreme Court may find it difficult to adopt a different interpretation without appearing to overrule a federal court—a dynamic that, however legally incorrect, exerts real practical pressure. The certification mechanism exists to prevent precisely this dynamic. When the Fifth Circuit declines to use it, the Erie prediction becomes, for practical purposes, the state law.

None of this is to say the Fifth Circuit's statutory analysis was wrong. The court's reading of "certifying" and "receipt" is plausible and in some respects quite sensible. The problem is that the court's analysis is the *only* authoritative interpretation, developed through a process designed for dispute resolution rather than statutory elaboration. Certification would have given the Louisiana Supreme Court the first word. Instead, the Fifth Circuit ensured that it would have the last.
