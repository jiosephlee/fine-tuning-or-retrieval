Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Does the TTAB need to analyze all 13 DuPont factors in a likelihood-of-confusion determination?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the court quotes M2 Software for the proposition that the Board need not consider every DuPont factor, only those 'that are relevant and of record.' But the opinion also says likelihood of confusion is a question of law based on underlying factual findings under the DuPont factors. If the Board can skip factors, how does an appellant know which factors will be considered? And does the Board have an obligation to explain why certain factors are irrelevant, or can it just silently omit them? In this case, the court only discusses factors 1, 2, and 6—does that mean the Board only considered those three, or did it consider others that weren't challenged on appeal?
Answer:
The short answer is no—the Board does not need to analyze all thirteen DuPont factors. But the fact that the Federal Circuit's opinion only discusses three factors tells you more about the scope of appellate review than about what the Board did below.

**The Board's obligation: only relevant factors of record**

The opinion quotes the established standard from *M2 Software*: "The Board need not consider every DuPont factor, only those 'that are relevant and of record.'" This is not a license to silently skip factors at will. The standard implicitly requires the Board to make a judgment about which factors are relevant to the particular case before it and which have evidence in the record supporting them. A factor with no record evidence—say, simultaneous use of a mark (factor 12) where no such use is alleged—need not be belabored. But the Board's job is to conduct the likelihood-of-confusion analysis using the factors that matter given the facts and arguments presented.

**Why the opinion only discusses three factors**

The opinion tells you directly why only factors 1, 2, and 6 appear: "On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred in its likelihood-of-confusion analysis, specifically with respect to its analysis of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors." The Federal Circuit's review is responsive to the issues the parties raise. As the court states, quoting *Bose*, "Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence **when called into question on appeal**."

So the court discusses only those three because those are the ones Apex challenged. The court's silence on the other factors does not mean the Board never considered them—only that they were not "called into question." The Board's actual decision appears at J.A. 24–63, and the opinion references the Board's analysis of factors 1, 2, and 6 with specific record citations, suggesting the Board did address at least those three. Whether the Board addressed additional factors below is not something the opinion reveals, because the court had no reason to discuss unchallenged findings.

**Does the Board need to explain why it skips a factor?**

The opinion doesn't directly address a duty to explain omissions. But the *M2 Software* standard—"relevant and of record"—gives you the practical answer. If a party puts a factor in dispute by arguing it below or introducing evidence on it, the Board must address it. If neither party raises a factor and no evidence in the record bears on it, the Board's failure to discuss it is generally not reversible error. The risk for the Board is in being too narrow in what it treats as relevant, which is exactly what happened here: the court found the Board erred in limiting its sixth-factor analysis to credit card services only, when the Board had already found the parties' services "highly similar" under factor 2. The lesson is that the Board's relevance determination is itself subject to review, and an unduly narrow conception of what's relevant can be a legal error.

**Practical takeaway for an appellant**

You cannot know with certainty before the Board's decision which factors it will find relevant, but you can predict based on the nature of the opposition and the evidence you put in. The more strategic concern this case illustrates is internal consistency: the Board's relevance determinations across factors must be coherent. The Federal Circuit vacated factors 6 and 1 precisely because the Board's narrow sixth-factor analysis was inconsistent with its broad second-factor finding. If you are the appellant, your best approach is to argue the factors the Board actually addressed and, where applicable, challenge the Board's implicit relevance determinations—arguing that a factor it ignored should have been considered given its own findings on the factors it did analyze.
