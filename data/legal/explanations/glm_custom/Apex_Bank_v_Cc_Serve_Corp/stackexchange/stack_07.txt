Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What is a 'letter of protest' in trademark prosecution and how does it differ from a formal opposition?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., CC Serve submitted a 'letter of protest' to the USPTO during prosecution of Apex's applications, but the examining attorney still approved the marks for publication. CC Serve then filed a formal opposition. I'm confused about the procedural distinction. What is a letter of protest—who can submit one, what is the examining attorney's obligation to consider it, and what happens if it's ignored? How does it differ from an opposition proceeding before the TTAB? Is a letter of protest a prerequisite to filing an opposition, or could CC Serve have skipped it?
Answer:
In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit reviews a procedural timeline that perfectly illustrates the distinction between a letter of protest and a formal opposition. 

Here is the breakdown of the two mechanisms:

### What is a Letter of Protest and Who Can Submit One?
A letter of protest is an informal procedure that allows a third party to bring evidence to the attention of the USPTO examining attorney during the prosecution of a trademark application. Any third party who believes they would be damaged by the registration of a pending mark can submit one. In this case, CC Serve, as the owner of the existing ASPIRE mark, submitted a letter of protest to assert that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks were confusingly similar to its own.

### The Examining Attorney's Obligation and Consequences of Ignoring It
A letter of protest is advisory. The examining attorney has the discretion to consider the submitted evidence, but is not a formal party to a dispute and is not obligated to refuse the application based on the letter. As the opinion notes, CC Serve submitted its letter of protest, but "Nonetheless, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication." 

If the examining attorney ignores the letter or finds it unpersuasive, the application simply proceeds to the next stage of prosecution: publication for opposition. The third party's recourse is then to file a formal opposition.

### How Does it Differ from a Formal Opposition?
The primary differences lie in timing, forum, and procedural formality:

1. **Timing**: A letter of protest is submitted *during prosecution* (before a mark is approved). An opposition is filed *after* the examining attorney has approved the mark for publication. In *Apex Bank*, the marks published on December 17, 2019, and CC Serve initiated its formal opposition in February 2020.
2. **Forum**: A letter of protest is reviewed by the examining attorney assigned to the application. A formal opposition is an adversarial proceeding before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB).
3. **Procedure**: A letter of protest is an *ex parte* submission; the third party simply provides evidence to the USPTO and does not participate further in the examination. An opposition is an *inter partes* proceeding where both sides litigate the issue. In this case, the TTAB conducted a full likelihood-of-confusion analysis applying the thirteen *DuPont* factors, resulting in a formal Board decision that was then appealed to the Federal Circuit. 

### Is a Letter of Protest a Prerequisite?
No. A letter of protest is an optional, early-step tactic, not a prerequisite to filing an opposition. 

A third party can choose to skip the letter of protest entirely, wait for the examining attorney to approve the mark for publication, and then file an opposition with the TTAB. Here, CC Serve attempted to halt the prosecution early via the letter of protest. When that informal attempt failed, CC Serve pursued its formal statutory right to oppose the mark before the TTAB. The opinion treats the formal opposition as the operative proceeding that resulted in the Board's refusal, entirely independent of the earlier letter of protest.
