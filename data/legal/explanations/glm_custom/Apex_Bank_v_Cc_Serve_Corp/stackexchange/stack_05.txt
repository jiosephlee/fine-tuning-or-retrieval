Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How does the sixth DuPont factor (third-party marks) affect the first DuPont factor (commercial impression)?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the court remanded the first DuPont factor analysis solely because reconsideration of the sixth factor 'may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.' I don't understand the doctrinal link between these two factors. The first factor is about similarity of the marks themselves (appearance, sound, connotation), while the sixth is about how many similar marks exist in the marketplace. How does the number of third-party marks change the 'commercial impression' of CC Serve's ASPIRE mark? Is the court saying that if there are many ASPIRE marks in financial services, consumers will perceive CC Serve's mark as weaker, and that weaker impression affects the first factor analysis? Is commercial strength part of the first factor, or is it exclusively a sixth factor consideration?
Answer:
---

Your understanding is essentially correct. The court is describing an interdependency between the two factors: the sixth factor's assessment of commercial strength (or weakness) feeds into the first factor's "commercial impression" element.

**The doctrinal link is "commercial strength."**

The first DuPont factor asks whether the marks are similar "as to appearance, sound, connotation and **commercial impression**." The sixth factor examines "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods," which serves as evidence of whether a mark is commercially strong or weak. As the court explains, evidence of third-party use "is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection," and when a field is crowded with similar marks, "customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused."

The connection is this: a mark's commercial strength—as determined under the sixth factor—shapes its **overall commercial impression** under the first factor. A mark that consumers encounter frequently across multiple sources in the same industry leaves a different (weaker) impression than one that stands alone. Consumers who have been "educated to distinguish between marks" will perceive any single ASPIRE mark differently if there are forty-two similar marks in the financial services industry versus nine.

So the court's logic on remand is straightforward:

1. The Board erred by too narrowly limiting the universe of third-party marks under the sixth factor (only credit card services, excluding broader banking/financing).
2. On remand, the Board must reconsider the sixth factor with a broader set of similar marks.
3. That reconsideration "may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness."
4. Because commercial strength affects "the overall commercial impression," and commercial impression is an element of the first factor, the first factor must also be reconsidered.

**Is commercial strength part of the first factor or exclusively a sixth factor consideration?**

It is assessed primarily under the sixth factor, but it is not *exclusively* a sixth factor consideration. The court's remand makes clear that commercial strength is an input into the first factor's commercial impression analysis. The first factor does not ask about commercial strength in isolation—it asks about the "overall commercial impression" of the marks, which is informed by the marketplace reality that the sixth factor captures. The two factors are analytically distinct but not hermetically sealed from one another.

The Board here had found that CC Serve's mark was neither particularly strong nor weak and was entitled to the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." If, on remand, the broader universe of third-party ASPIRE marks shows the mark to be commercially weak, that determination would narrow the mark's commercial impression and, correspondingly, the scope of protection under the first factor. That is why the court could not simply affirm the first factor while vacating the sixth—the first factor's conclusion may depend on the outcome of the sixth.
