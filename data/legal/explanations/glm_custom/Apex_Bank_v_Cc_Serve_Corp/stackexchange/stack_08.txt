Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What is the practical effect of 'affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand' on the trademark application?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the court affirmed the second DuPont factor finding but vacated the first and sixth factor findings, remanding for the Board to reconsider. I'm trying to understand what happens procedurally. Does Apex's application remain suspended during remand? If the Board reconsiders factors 1 and 6 but reaches the same ultimate conclusion (likelihood of confusion exists), can Apex appeal again? Could the Board's reconsideration of the sixth factor lead to a finding that CC Serve's mark is weak enough that there's no likelihood of confusion, thus allowing Apex's registration? Also, the court awarded costs to appellant—does that mean Apex 'won' even though the Board's ultimate ruling was mostly affirmed?
Answer:
Your question touches on several important aspects of administrative review and TTAB procedure following a partial remand from the Federal Circuit.

### Does the application remain suspended during remand?

Procedurally, yes, the application cannot proceed to registration while the remand is pending. The Board's original decision sustaining the opposition and refusing registration has been vacated in part. The Federal Circuit instructed the Board to "remand for the Board to consider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods and the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks." Because the Board must issue a new decision after reconsidering DuPont factors 1 and 6, the opposition remains unresolved. The application is effectively held in abeyance until the Board completes its reconsideration and issues a new final order.

### Can Apex appeal again if the Board reaches the same ultimate conclusion?

Yes. The remand requires the Board to conduct a new analysis of the first and sixth DuPont factors. If, after reconsideration, the Board issues a new decision sustaining the opposition and refusing registration, that constitutes a new final decision of the Board. The opinion notes that the Federal Circuit has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which grants the court jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of the Board. If Apex is dissatisfied with the Board's new findings on remand—whether regarding the strength of the mark or the ultimate likelihood of confusion—it may appeal that new final decision.

### Could reconsideration of the sixth factor lead to a finding of no likelihood of confusion?

Yes, that is a possible outcome. The sixth DuPont factor considers "the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." As the court explained, "Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection." The court vacated the Board's sixth factor finding because the Board improperly limited its review to only marks related to credit card services, excluding 42 other Aspire-formative marks used in the broader financial services industry.

The court noted that "when a field is crowded with similar marks... customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused." By expanding the universe of third-party marks on remand, the Board could find that CC Serve's ASPIRE mark is commercially weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. Furthermore, the court explicitly linked the sixth factor to the first, stating that reconsideration of the mark's strength "may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression." If the Board finds the mark is weak and the commercial impression of the parties' marks is different, it could conclude there is no likelihood of confusion, which would result in dismissal of the opposition and allow Apex's registration to proceed.

### Did Apex "win" if it was awarded costs?

Yes, in the context of appellate litigation, obtaining a vacatur and remand constitutes a meaningful "win" for purposes of costs, even though the Board's ultimate ruling on the second factor was affirmed. The court affirmed the Board's finding on the second DuPont factor (similarity of the services) against Apex, but Apex successfully challenged the Board's analysis on two of the three factors it appealed, resulting in a remand for further proceedings.

Under Federal Circuit rules, costs are generally awarded to the prevailing party. In mixed results like this—affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand—the court has discretion to award costs. By awarding costs to Apex as the appellant, the court acknowledged that Apex prevailed on the most significant issues raised on appeal, specifically by demonstrating that the Board committed legal error in its analysis of the first and sixth DuPont factors. While Apex did not secure a complete reversal of the refusal, it succeeded in forcing the Board to reconsider its ruling, which is a significant procedural victory.
