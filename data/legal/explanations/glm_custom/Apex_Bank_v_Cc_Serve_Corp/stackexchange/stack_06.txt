Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How many third-party uses are needed to show a mark is 'weak' under the sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the Board found that nine third-party ASPIRE-formative marks for credit card services did not rise to the level of 'considerable' or 'ubiquitous' use needed to demonstrate weakness. The court cited Jack Wolfskin (14 'notable examples' with 'voluminous evidence') and Juice Generation (26 marks as 'a considerable number'). Is there a minimum threshold number of third-party marks needed to establish weakness? Does the quality or notoriety of the third-party uses matter, or is it purely a numbers game? And if Apex introduces the additional 42 marks on remand, is there a number that would be dispositive, or does the Board still have discretion to weigh the evidence?
Answer:
## Answer

**There is no fixed numerical threshold, and the inquiry is not purely a numbers game.** The sixth DuPont factor expressly considers both "the number *and nature* of similar marks in use on similar goods," meaning the Board evaluates the quality and prominence of the third-party uses alongside the raw count. *DuPont*, 476 F.2d at 1361. The cases the Board cited are illustrative, not prescriptive—*Jack Wolfskin* referenced fourteen "notable examples" supported by "voluminous evidence," and *Juice Generation* described twenty-six marks as "a considerable number"—but neither case establishes a minimum count that must be exceeded. The Federal Circuit has not set such a line, and the *Apex* opinion does not purport to either.

The court's language throughout points to a qualitative assessment. Terms like "considerable" and "ubiquitous" describe the *overall evidentiary picture*, not a bright-line rule. The court's discussion of *Jack Wolfskin* emphasizes not just the number of examples but that they were "notable" and backed by "voluminous evidence," suggesting that the prominence and documentation of the third-party uses matter. The underlying theory of the sixth factor supports this: third-party use is relevant because it shows that "consumers have been educated to distinguish between marks," *Jack Wolfskin*, 797 F.3d at 1374, and that education effect depends on whether consumers actually encounter the third-party marks in the marketplace—not merely on whether the marks exist on a list.

**The Board retains discretion to weigh the evidence, and no number is automatically dispositive.** The Federal Circuit in *Apex* did not say that if Apex introduces enough marks, the Board must find weakness. The court vacated the sixth-factor analysis not because nine marks were too few as a matter of law, but because the Board applied an unduly narrow standard of similarity when deciding *which* marks to count. The Board had already found the parties' services "highly similar" and "partially legally identical" under the second DuPont factor, but then restricted its sixth-factor analysis to marks used only for credit card services—excluding marks for other banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit held this was legally inconsistent: the sixth factor requires only "similar" goods, not identical ones, and the Board "should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

So on remand, the Board must broaden the universe of eligible third-party marks to include those used on services similar to the parties' services—not just credit card services narrowly defined. The additional forty-two marks Apex identified may enter the analysis if they meet this similarity standard. But the Board still must assess their "number *and nature*" and determine whether they collectively rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use demonstrating that the mark is weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The Board retains its role as fact-finder in weighing this evidence, subject to substantial-evidence review on appeal.

In short: there is no magic number, quality and notoriety matter, and the Board exercises informed discretion—constrained by the requirement that it apply a consistent standard of similarity across the DuPont factors.
