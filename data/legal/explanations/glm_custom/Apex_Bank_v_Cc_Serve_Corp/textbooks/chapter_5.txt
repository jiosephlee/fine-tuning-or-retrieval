Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 5: # The First DuPont Factor and Overall Disposition

## The First DuPont Factor: Similarity of Marks in Their Entireties

The first DuPont factor directs the factfinder to consider the "similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." The opinion quotes this formulation directly from the foundational DuPont decision, 476 F.2d at 1361, and it is the standard the Board applies when evaluating whether two marks are sufficiently similar to give rise to a likelihood of confusion. The inquiry is holistic: the court does not dissect the marks into their constituent elements and compare them in isolation. Instead, the analysis focuses on the overall commercial impression each mark creates—the net mental image that a consumer retains upon encountering the mark in the marketplace. As the Federal Circuit explained, citing *In re I.A.M.Symbolic, LLC*, 866 F.3d at 1323, the question is whether confusion as to the source of the services offered under the respective marks is likely to result.

In this case, the Board had concluded that the first DuPont factor weighed in favor of CC Serve, finding that Apex's ASPIRE BANK word and design marks were sufficiently similar to CC Serve's ASPIRE mark. Apex challenged this conclusion on appeal. However, the Federal Circuit did not reach the merits of whether the Board's comparison of the marks was correct. Instead, the court identified a structural problem in the Board's analysis that required remand before the first factor could be properly evaluated.

## The Relationship Between Commercial Strength and Commercial Impression

The key to understanding the court's disposition of the first factor lies in the connection between two DuPont factors: the first (similarity of the marks) and the sixth (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods). The sixth factor bears directly on the commercial strength of the senior user's mark. When a mark exists in a field crowded with similar marks used on similar goods, consumers become more adept at distinguishing among them, and the senior mark is entitled to only a narrower scope of protection. Conversely, when few similar marks exist, the senior mark may be entitled to a broader scope. The Board's sixth-factor analysis thus produces a finding about the mark's commercial strength or weakness.

That finding, in turn, has implications for the first factor. The first factor's focus on commercial impression is not conducted in a vacuum. The overall commercial impression of a mark, and the scope of protection it commands, is informed by how strong or weak the mark has been shown to be. A mark that has been determined to be commercially weak because of extensive third-party use will receive a narrower scope of protection, meaning that a junior user's similar mark is less likely to cause confusion. A mark that is commercially strong will receive a broader scope, making confusion more likely even with marks that are somewhat more distant.

## Why Reconsideration of the Sixth Factor Affects the First Factor

The Federal Circuit had already determined that the Board's sixth-factor analysis was legally flawed because the Board improperly narrowed the universe of third-party marks it considered, restricting its review to marks used in connection with credit card services while excluding marks used for other banking and financing services. The court held that this was inconsistent with the Board's own factual finding under the second factor—that the parties' services were highly similar, even partially legally identical. The Board could not adopt a broad definition of similarity for one factor and then impose a narrow one for another.

This error mattered for the first factor because the sixth factor's reconsideration might produce a different result. If, on remand, the Board considers the broader universe of third-party ASPIRE-formative marks—including those used in the broader banking and financing services industry—it might find that the field is more crowded than the Board originally concluded. That finding could lead the Board to determine that CC Serve's ASPIRE mark is commercially weaker than it previously found, entitled to only a narrower scope of protection. Such a determination would, in turn, affect the overall commercial impression analysis under the first factor, because the scope of protection accorded to the senior mark shapes the question of whether a similar mark is likely to cause confusion. As the court stated, "reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."

The court therefore concluded that it could not meaningfully review the Board's first-factor analysis while the sixth-factor analysis remained infected with legal error. The prudent course was to remand the first factor as well, so that the Board could reconsider the similarity of the marks in light of whatever conclusion it reaches about the mark's commercial strength upon a proper sixth-factor analysis.

## The Court's Disposition

The Federal Circuit's ultimate disposition reflects a precise calibration of what it affirmed, what it vacated, and what it remanded. As to the second DuPont factor—the similarity of the parties' goods and services—the court affirmed the Board's finding. The court concluded that the Board's legal analysis was sound and that substantial evidence supported the Board's finding that credit card services and banking and financing services are highly similar. Apex's argument that the services were not directly competitive was rejected; the court noted that the services need not be identical, only related in a manner that could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.

As to the sixth DuPont factor—the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods—the court vacated the Board's finding. The Board had erred by applying an unduly narrow definition of similarity, limited to credit card services alone, inconsistent with its own broader finding of service similarity under the second factor. The court remanded for the Board to reconsider the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration, applying the same standard of similarity it had already found under the second factor.

As to the first DuPont factor—the similarity of the marks in their entireties—the court likewise vacated and remanded. The court did not hold that the Board's analysis of the marks' appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression was necessarily wrong. Rather, the court held that the analysis was dependent on a proper resolution of the sixth factor. Because the sixth-factor analysis was being remanded for reconsideration, the first-factor analysis had to be remanded as well to ensure that the commercial impression inquiry was informed by a correct determination of the mark's commercial strength.

The court thus affirmed-in-part, vacated-in-part, and remanded. The disposition is notable for its restraint: the court did not substitute its own judgment on the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion question. Instead, it identified specific legal errors in the Board's factor-by-factor analysis and returned the case to the Board for further proceedings consistent with the correct legal standards.

## Costs and the Practical Significance of the Decision

The court awarded costs to Apex Bank, the appellant. This award reflects the fact that Apex prevailed on appeal, at least in part: the court vacated two of the three Board findings Apex challenged and remanded for reconsideration. Although Apex did not win outright—the Board's finding under the second factor was affirmed, and the opposition was not overturned—the court's decision required further Board action favorable to Apex's position, and the costs award acknowledges Apex's partial success.

The broader practical significance of the decision lies in its insistence on consistency across DuPont factor analyses. The Federal Circuit's central holding is that the Board cannot apply one definition of similarity under the second factor—finding the parties' services highly similar, even partially legally identical—and then apply a narrower, more stringent definition of similarity under the sixth factor, one limited only to identical services. The court saw "no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor" than the Board had already applied under the second. This principle—that the Board must maintain a consistent conception of similarity across related DuPont factors—ensures that the likelihood-of-confusion analysis remains coherent and that each factor is evaluated against a common understanding of the relationship between the parties' goods and services.
