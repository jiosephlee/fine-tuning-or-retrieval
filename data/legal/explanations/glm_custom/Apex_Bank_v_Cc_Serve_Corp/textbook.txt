Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## CC Serve's Business Model and Its Registered ASPIRE Word Mark for Credit Card Services

CC Serve Corporation is a company that offers credit card services to customers. It is the owner of U.S. Trademark Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, used in connection with credit card services. This registration was issued in 1998 and carries an effective priority date of October 17, 1996, meaning that CC Serve's rights in the ASPIRE mark are treated as having begun on that date for purposes of priority against later users.

CC Serve's business model is notable for how it structures its credit card offerings. Rather than issuing credit cards directly to consumers, CC Serve partners with a bank. The bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers, while CC Serve and its affiliates service those accounts. This arrangement—where a non-bank entity collaborates with a chartered bank to offer financial products—is a common feature of the credit card industry, but it takes on significance in this case because Apex Bank argues that CC Serve is merely managing credit card programs rather than providing banking services itself. The Federal Circuit, as we will see, ultimately found this argument unpersuasive in the context of the similarity-of-services analysis.

## Apex Bank's Business Operations and Its Intent-to-Use Applications for ASPIRE BANK Word and Design Marks

Apex Bank is a retail bank chartered in Tennessee. It operates 18 branch locations and offers a range of traditional banking products, including personal checking accounts, personal savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, and consumer and business loans. Notably, Apex Bank does not offer credit cards—a fact that becomes relevant when assessing the similarity of the parties' services.

Apex Bank plans to launch an internet bank under a separate brand from its existing retail operations. This new online brand would use the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) to register both the ASPIRE BANK word mark and a corresponding design mark for services described as "[b]anking and financing services."

An intent-to-use application is a procedural device under the Lanham Act that allows an applicant to reserve a trademark before actually using it in commerce, provided the applicant has a bona fide intent to use the mark. The applicant must later demonstrate actual use before the registration will issue. This mechanism allows businesses to secure trademark rights prospectively while still in the planning stages of a new product or service line.

## The Letter of Protest, Publication for Opposition, and CC Serve's Opposition Proceeding Before the Board

During the prosecution of Apex's applications—that is, while the examining attorney was reviewing the applications—the trademark examination process was interrupted by a submission from CC Serve. CC Serve submitted a letter of protest to the USPTO, asserting that Apex's proposed ASPIRE BANK marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve's existing ASPIRE mark. A letter of protest is a mechanism by which a third party can bring potentially adverse information to the attention of the examining attorney during prosecution, without formally initiating an opposition proceeding.

Despite CC Serve's letter of protest, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication. The marks were published for opposition on December 17, 2019. Publication for opposition is a critical step in the trademark registration process: once a mark is published, any party who believes it would be damaged by the registration has 30 days to file an opposition with the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (the Board), an administrative tribunal within the USPTO.

CC Serve took advantage of this opportunity. In February 2020, CC Serve initiated an opposition proceeding before the Board, alleging a likelihood of confusion between Apex's proposed ASPIRE BANK marks and CC Serve's existing ASPIRE word mark. An opposition proceeding is a formal, adversarial administrative trial before the Board, governed by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and the Board's own rules, in which the opposer (here, CC Serve) seeks to prevent the registration of an applicant's mark.

## The Board's Decision Sustaining the Opposition Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act

The Board sustained CC Serve's opposition, ruling that Apex's ASPIRE BANK marks should be refused registration. The Board's decision was grounded in Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). Section 2(d) prohibits the registration of a mark that, when applied to the goods or services for which it is sought to be registered, so resembles a mark registered in the Patent and Trademark Office, or a mark or trade name previously used in the United States by another and not abandoned, as to be likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive.

The core legal standard at issue is "likelihood of confusion." Under the framework established by the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals in *In re E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973), the Board evaluates likelihood of confusion by considering a set of thirteen factors—known as the DuPont factors—that are relevant to the facts of a particular case. The Board need not consider every factor, only those that are relevant and supported by record evidence. The Board concluded that, weighing the relevant DuPont factors, consumer confusion between Apex's ASPIRE BANK marks and CC Serve's ASPIRE mark was likely, and therefore sustained the opposition.

## Apex's Appeal to the Federal Circuit and the Court's Jurisdiction Under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)

Apex Bank appealed the Board's decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from decisions of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which grants the Federal Circuit jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of the Board in cases where the parties meet certain residency or entity requirements. This jurisdictional grant is part of the broader framework establishing the Federal Circuit as the appellate court for certain specialized areas of federal law, including patent and trademark matters arising from the USPTO.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit applies two distinct standards of review to the Board's decision. The court reviews the Board's legal conclusions de novo, meaning it considers them anew without deferring to the Board's reasoning. The court reviews the Board's findings of fact for substantial evidence, meaning it asks whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate to support the Board's factual conclusions. Substantial evidence is defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion," a standard derived from *Consolidated Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938).

Likelihood of confusion itself is a question of law, but it is based on findings of relevant underlying facts—namely, the factual findings made under each of the DuPont factors. Each DuPont factor presents a question of fact, and the Federal Circuit tests the Board's findings under each factor for substantial evidence when those findings are challenged on appeal. This layered structure—legal conclusions reviewed de novo, factual findings reviewed for substantial evidence, and the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion determination treated as a legal conclusion resting on factual underpinnings—will be important to keep in mind as we turn to the Federal Circuit's analysis of the specific DuPont factors at issue in this appeal.

# Legal Framework: Likelihood of Confusion and the DuPont Factors

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act and the Prohibition on Registration When Confusion Is Likely from Concurrent Use of Marks

The central statutory provision at issue in this case is Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). Section 2(d) prohibits the registration of a trademark when confusion is likely because of the concurrent use of the applicant's mark and a prior user's mark on their respective goods or services. The purpose of this prohibition is to protect consumers from mistaken beliefs about the source of goods or services and to protect prior users from having the distinctiveness of their marks diluted by registrations that would create confusion in the marketplace.

In this case, CC Serve initiated an opposition to Apex Bank's intent-to-use applications for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. CC Serve claimed that Apex's proposed marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve's previously registered ASPIRE mark, which CC Serve used in connection with credit card services. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board sustained the opposition under Section 2(d), concluding that consumer confusion between the marks was likely. Apex appealed that decision to the Federal Circuit, which has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

The opposition procedure used here is one mechanism by which a party can invoke Section 2(d). After an examining attorney approves a mark for publication, a party who believes it would be damaged by the registration may file an opposition with the Board. CC Serve took this path after the examining attorney approved Apex's marks for publication despite CC Serve's earlier letter of protest. The Board's role in an opposition is to determine whether the statutory grounds for refusal, such as likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d), have been established. If they have, the Board refuses registration.

## The Thirteen DuPont Factors and the Board's Discretion to Consider Only Those That Are Relevant and of Record

To evaluate whether confusion is likely under Section 2(d), courts and the Board apply the framework articulated in In re E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The DuPont decision set forth thirteen factors that may be relevant to the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry. These factors are designed to capture the full range of considerations that might bear on whether consumers would be confused by the concurrent use of similar marks on related goods or services.

The thirteen DuPont factors encompass a broad array of considerations, including the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks, the similarity or dissimilarity of the goods or services, the similarity of trade channels, the conditions under which purchasers encounter the goods, the fame of the prior mark, the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods, and others. The factors are not a rigid checklist; rather, they are tools for organizing the factual inquiry that underlies the ultimate legal question of whether confusion is likely.

Critically, the Board need not consider every DuPont factor in every case. As the Federal Circuit has explained, the Board is required to consider only those factors that are "relevant and of record." M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Communications, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006). This principle reflects the practical reality that not every factor will have bearing on every case. For example, if there is no evidence in the record regarding the nature of third-party uses of similar marks, the sixth DuPont factor may have little or nothing to contribute to the analysis. The Board has discretion to focus its attention on the factors that are supported by evidence and that meaningfully bear on the likelihood of confusion.

In this case, the Board's analysis centered on three DuPont factors: the first (similarity of the marks), the second (similarity of the goods or services), and the sixth (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods). Apex challenged the Board's analysis of all three on appeal. The Federal Circuit's opinion accordingly addressed each of these three factors in turn, while affirming the Board's analysis of the second factor and vacating its analysis of the first and sixth.

## Likelihood of Confusion as a Question of Law Based on Underlying Factual Findings Under the DuPont Factors

A central principle in trademark law, and one that is essential to understanding how the Federal Circuit reviewed the Board's decision, is that likelihood of confusion is a question of law based on underlying factual findings. The Federal Circuit has explained that "likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors." M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. This formulation has important implications for how the Board's work is structured and how it is reviewed on appeal.

The Board's task is first to develop a factual record and make factual findings under the relevant DuPont factors. Each DuPont factor presents a question of fact, and the Board's findings under each factor are tested for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Products, Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). After making these factual findings, the Board must then determine, as a matter of law, whether the totality of those findings supports a conclusion that confusion is likely. The ultimate determination of likelihood of confusion is thus a legal conclusion drawn from the factual predicate established by the DuPont factor findings.

This distinction between the factual findings under each factor and the ultimate legal conclusion is not merely academic. It determines the standard of appellate review applied to each part of the Board's analysis. Factual findings under each DuPont factor are reviewed for substantial evidence, while the Board's legal conclusions are reviewed de novo. In this case, for example, the Federal Circuit reviewed the Board's factual finding that the parties' services were highly similar for substantial evidence, while examining de novo the Board's legal methodology in limiting its sixth-factor analysis to credit card services only.

The interplay among DuPont factors is also significant. As this case demonstrates, a factual finding under one factor can have consequences for the analysis under another. The Board found under the second factor that the parties' services were highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical. The Federal Circuit held that this factual finding should have informed the scope of the Board's analysis under the sixth factor, which asks about similar marks used on similar goods. The court reasoned that the Board could not adopt an expansive view of similarity for the second factor and then narrow that view for the sixth factor. Similarly, the court noted that the sixth-factor analysis of the mark's commercial strength or weakness could affect the first-factor analysis of commercial impression, warranting remand of the first factor as well.

## De Novo Review of the Board's Legal Conclusions and Substantial Evidence Review of Its Factual Findings

The Federal Circuit's standard of review reflects the law/fact distinction described above. As the court stated at the outset of its analysis: "We review the Board's legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence." M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382. Each prong of this standard serves a distinct function.

De novo review of legal conclusions means that the Federal Circuit examines the Board's legal methodology and legal rulings afresh, without deferring to the Board's judgment. When the Board makes an error in its legal analysis—for example, by applying an unduly narrow definition of similarity under the sixth DuPont factor—the Federal Circuit does not ask whether a reasonable Board could have reached the same conclusion. Instead, the court independently determines whether the Board's legal approach was correct. In this case, the Federal Circuit conducted de novo review of the Board's legal methodology under the sixth factor and concluded that the Board erred by restricting its universe of third-party marks to only those related to credit card services, excluding marks related to other banking and financing services, when the Board had already found those services to be highly similar under the second factor.

Substantial evidence review of factual findings is a more deferential standard. Substantial evidence is defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." Consolidated Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under this standard, the Federal Circuit does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the Board. Instead, the court asks whether the record contains evidence sufficient to support the Board's factual findings. If a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate, the finding stands, even if the court might have reached a different conclusion on the same record.

In this case, the Federal Circuit applied the substantial evidence standard to the Board's finding under the second DuPont factor that the parties' services were highly similar. Apex argued that the services were "not the same" and "not directly competitive," emphasizing that CC Serve partnered with banks to manage credit card programs rather than providing banking services itself. The court rejected this argument, finding that the Board had "carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services" and that substantial evidence supported the Board's finding of high similarity. The court therefore affirmed the Board's second-factor finding.

The interplay between these two standards of review is particularly evident in the court's disposition. The court affirmed the second-factor finding because it was supported by substantial evidence and involved no legal error. The court vacated the sixth-factor finding because the Board committed a legal error in its methodology, not because the Board's factual findings were unsupported by evidence. And the court vacated the first-factor finding not because of any identified error in the Board's specific analysis of the marks' appearance, sound, and connotation, but because the reconsideration of the sixth factor on remand could alter the assessment of the mark's commercial strength, which in turn could affect the first-factor analysis of commercial impression.

## The Scope of Apex's Appeal: Challenges to the Board's Analysis of the First, Second, and Sixth DuPont Factors

Apex's appeal focused on three of the thirteen DuPont factors: the first, the second, and the sixth. The Board had concluded that the first and second factors weighed in favor of CC Serve and that the sixth factor did not weigh in favor of Apex. The Federal Circuit addressed each factor in turn.

The second DuPont factor assesses the similarity of the parties' goods or services. The services need not be identical; the relevant inquiry is whether the goods or services are "related in some manner" or whether "the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." Coach Servs., Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012). The Board compared CC Serve's credit card services to Apex's banking and financing services. Because the Trademark ID Manual entry for "credit card services" had been deleted after CC Serve's registration, the Board first determined the scope of that term, concluding that it encompassed issuing credit cards, managing credit card accounts, processing transactions, and related activities. The Board then found that dictionary definitions of "banking" and "finance" encompass extending credit through credit cards, and it relied on third-party registrations covering both credit card and banking services to support its conclusion that the services could emanate from a single source. The Board concluded that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of finding likelihood of confusion. On appeal, the Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the Board's finding and affirmed.

The sixth DuPont factor considers the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant because it can show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). The theory is that when a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers become more adept at distinguishing among them and are less likely to be confused. Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex submitted evidence of third-party uses of marks containing the word "Aspire" in connection with credit card services and the broader financial services industry. The Board, however, limited its consideration to marks used specifically for credit card services, finding only nine such marks and concluding that this number was insufficient to demonstrate that CC Serve's mark was weak. The Federal Circuit held that this was legal error. The court explained that the sixth factor requires consideration of similar marks for similar goods, not identical goods, and that the Board's definition of similarity was too narrow given its own finding under the second factor that the parties' services were highly similar. The court vacated the sixth-factor finding and remanded for reconsideration using a consistent standard of similarity.

The first DuPont factor considers the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. The analysis focuses on the overall commercial impression of the marks and whether confusion as to source is likely. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). The Federal Circuit vacated the Board's first-factor analysis as well, reasoning that the reconsideration of the sixth factor on remand could result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness, which could in turn affect the overall commercial impression analysis under the first factor. This aspect of the court's disposition illustrates the interconnectedness of the DuPont factors: an error in the analysis of one factor can necessitate reconsideration of another, even when the second factor's analysis was not itself independently flawed.

The Federal Circuit's ultimate disposition was to affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand. The court affirmed the Board's analysis of the second DuPont factor, finding no legal error and substantial evidentiary support. The court vacated the Board's findings on the sixth and first factors, directing the Board on remand to reconsider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods using a standard of similarity consistent with its second-factor finding, and to reconsider the marks' appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression in light of that reconsideration.

# The Second DuPont Factor: Similarity of the Parties' Goods and Services

## The Legal Standard for the Second DuPont Factor

The second DuPont factor assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and/or services. In this case, the Board was tasked with comparing CC Serve's credit card services with Apex's banking and financing services. The legal standard does not require that the services be identical. Rather, as the Federal Circuit explained, quoting *Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, the evidence need only establish that "the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." This is a relatively broad standard: the inquiry is not whether the parties offer the same services in the same way, but whether a consumer encountering both services might believe they come from a single source.

This standard reflects the underlying purpose of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. The question is whether concurrent use of similar marks on related goods or services would cause consumer confusion as to the source of those goods or services. The second factor addresses one dimension of that question by focusing on the relationship between the services themselves, independent of the similarity of the marks (which is addressed by the first DuPont factor) or the sophistication of the consumers (addressed by other factors).

## The Board's Determination of the Scope of "Credit Card Services"

Before the Board could compare the parties' services, it had to address a preliminary issue: the meaning of "credit card services." The entry for "credit card services" had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve's registration was issued. The Trademark ID Manual, more formally known as the Acceptable Identification of Goods and Services Manual, is a resource used by the United States Patent and Trademark Office that provides acceptable identifications of goods and services for trademark applications. The deletion of the entry meant that the Board could not simply rely on a current, standardized definition of the term.

The Board therefore undertook its own determination of the scope of "credit card services." It concluded that the term encompasses "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores." This is a broad definition that covers not only the issuance of credit cards but also a range of related account management and financial services. The Board's definition would prove important to its comparison of the parties' services, because it framed the scope of CC Serve's services expansively rather than narrowly.

## The Board's Finding of "Legally Identical, in Part" Based on Dictionary Definitions and Third-Party Registrations

Having determined the scope of "credit card services," the Board compared CC Serve's services with Apex's banking and financing services. The Board relied on two categories of evidence in reaching its conclusion.

First, the Board examined dictionary definitions for the terms "banking," "bank," and "finance." It determined that these definitions encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. In other words, the ordinary meaning of "banking" and "finance" includes activities that fall within the Board's definition of "credit card services." Based on this definitional overlap, the Board concluded that the parties' services are "legally identical, in part." The phrase "legally identical" is a term of art in trademark law indicating that the identified services overlap to such a degree that they are treated as the same services for purposes of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The qualifier "in part" acknowledges that the overlap is not complete—Apex offers services such as personal checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and business loans that may not all fall within "credit card services"—but that the overlap is sufficient to treat a portion of the services as identical.

Second, the Board considered third-party registrations that cover both credit card services and banking and financing services. The existence of registrations in which a single mark covers both categories of services supported the Board's finding that "the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark." This evidentiary approach is well established in trademark practice: third-party registrations that cover both parties' services tend to show that consumers may associate those services with a single source, thereby increasing the likelihood of confusion.

Based on the high degree of similarity between the parties' services, the Board concluded that the second DuPont factor weighed heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion.

## Apex's Argument: CC Serve Merely Partners with Banks

On appeal, Apex challenged the Board's finding regarding the second DuPont factor. Apex argued that the parties' services are "not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other." Apex's argument centered on the nature of CC Serve's business model. As described in the opinion, CC Serve offers credit card services by joining with a bank, which issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers. CC Serve and its affiliates then service those accounts. Apex contended that because CC Serve partners with banks to manage credit card programs, CC Serve is "not providing banking services" itself.

This argument attempts to narrow the characterization of CC Serve's services by emphasizing the structural arrangement through which CC Serve operates. The implication is that because CC Serve does not directly perform banking functions—such as holding deposits or making loans—but rather manages credit card programs in partnership with banks that do, its services should be distinguished from Apex's banking and financing services.

## The Federal Circuit's Affirmance: Substantial Evidence Supports the Board's Finding

The Federal Circuit rejected Apex's argument, finding it "unavailing." The court affirmed the Board's finding as to the second DuPont factor on two grounds.

First, the court noted that the Board "carefully considered the descriptions of each party's services." This observation emphasizes that the Board did not rely on a superficial comparison but examined the specific services as identified by the parties. The Board's methodology—determining the scope of "credit card services," comparing dictionary definitions, and examining third-party registrations—demonstrated a thorough analysis of the services' relationship.

Second, the court held that "substantial evidence supports the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar." Under the standard of review articulated earlier in the opinion, the Board's findings of fact are reviewed for substantial evidence, which the court defined as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." The court found that the dictionary definitions showing that "banking" and "finance" encompass credit card issuance, together with the third-party registrations covering both categories of services, constituted substantial evidence supporting the Board's finding.

The Federal Circuit's affirmance of the second DuPont factor is significant for the remainder of the opinion. The Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar—and partially legally identical—becomes a point of reference in the court's analysis of the sixth DuPont factor, where the court faults the Board for applying an inconsistent and narrower standard of similarity. Thus, the second factor serves as both a substantive holding affirmed on appeal and a benchmark against which the Board's analysis of another factor is measured.

# The Sixth DuPont Factor: Number and Nature of Similar Marks in Use on Similar Goods

## The Legal Purpose of the Sixth DuPont Factor

The sixth DuPont factor considers "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor serves a specific and important function within the likelihood-of-confusion analysis: it helps determine the strength of the senior user's mark. As the Federal Circuit explained, quoting its prior precedent in *Palm Bay Imports, Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin*, "Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection." The logic is straightforward. When a marketplace is crowded with similar marks used on similar goods, consumers become accustomed to encountering those marks and learn to distinguish among them. As the court noted, citing *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enterprises LLC*, "customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused by similar marks." This consumer education effect, in turn, tends to indicate that the senior user's mark lacks commercial strength. See *Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U.* The practical consequence is significant: a weak mark is entitled to a narrower scope of protection, making it less likely that a junior user's similar mark would cause consumer confusion. The sixth factor thus operates as a tool that a junior user like Apex Bank can deploy to argue that the senior user's mark deserves only limited protection.

## Apex's Evidence of Third-Party ASPIRE Marks

Before the Board, Apex submitted several exhibits demonstrating third-party uses of marks that included the word "Aspire." These marks were used in connection with credit card-related services and, more broadly, across the financial services industry. The breadth of this evidence mattered because the scope of third-party usage bears directly on whether the senior mark is weak. Apex's strategy was to show that the ASPIRE mark was not unique to CC Serve but was instead one of many similar marks in the financial services marketplace. If Apex could demonstrate that consumers of financial services already encountered numerous ASPIRE-formative marks from different sources, it could argue that consumers had been educated to distinguish among them and would not likely confuse Apex Bank's ASPIRE BANK marks with CC Serve's ASPIRE mark. The number and nature of these third-party marks thus formed the evidentiary foundation of Apex's argument that the sixth DuPont factor should weigh against finding a likelihood of confusion.

## The Board's Restriction to Credit Card Services

The Board's analysis of the sixth factor began with a critical threshold decision: defining the relevant public and the universe of similar marks it would consider. The Board concluded that, because of the overlap between Apex and CC Serve's services, the properly defined relevant public was "consumers of 'credit card services.'" This definitional choice had dramatic consequences. The Board then focused solely on marks identified for credit card services and deemed marks using ASPIRE for other services to be "essentially irrelevant." In effect, the Board excluded from consideration the broader category of banking and financing marks—precisely the category of services that Apex sought to register its marks for. On appeal, Apex argued that the Board erred in limiting the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and that the Board should have considered the 42 other third-party marks that used ASPIRE and ASPIRE-formative marks across the financial services industry. This exclusion became the central legal issue on appeal.

## The Board's Finding on "Considerable" or "Ubiquitous" Use

Even within its narrowed universe of credit card services, the Board found only nine ASPIRE-formative marks. The Board then determined that this number did not rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use sufficient to demonstrate that CC Serve's mark was weak. The court's opinion provides context for this finding by reference to prior cases. In *Jack Wolfskin*, the court had highlighted fourteen "notable examples of third-party registration and use" of paw print design elements, referring to "voluminous evidence." In *Juice Generation*, the court had referred to 26 third-party marks as "a considerable number." Against those benchmarks, nine marks did not satisfy the Board's standard. The Board ultimately concluded that while CC Serve had not shown its mark to have any particular commercial strength, Apex had also not shown CC Serve's mark to be commercially or conceptually weak such that it would be entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The Board determined that CC Serve's mark was entitled to the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." This finding meant that the sixth DuPont factor did not weigh in Apex's favor.

## The Federal Circuit's Legal Error Analysis

The Federal Circuit agreed with Apex that the Board's analysis was legally flawed. The court's reasoning centered on a critical inconsistency in how the Board defined "similarity" across different DuPont factors. The sixth DuPont factor requires the Board to consider similar marks for *similar* goods and services—not identical ones. The court cited *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy's, Inc.* and *Juice Generation* for the established principle that the sixth factor does not require identical goods, only similar ones. The problem was that the Board had already made a factual finding, in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, that the parties' services were highly similar—in fact, partially "legally identical." The Board reached this conclusion by determining that dictionary definitions for "banking," "bank," and "finance" encompass extending credit through the issuance of credit cards, and by pointing to third-party registrations covering both credit card and banking and financing services. Yet when the Board turned to the sixth factor, it abruptly narrowed the universe of relevant marks to only those for credit card services, excluding marks for other banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit found this inconsistency to be a legal error. The court stated plainly: "We find the Board's definition of similarity to be too narrow."

## The Consistency Principle Across DuPont Factors

The Federal Circuit's ruling articulates an important principle for likelihood-of-confusion analysis: the Board must apply a consistent standard of similarity across DuPont factors. The court framed the issue with precision: "When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors." The court saw "no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor." This principle flows from the structure of the DuPont analysis itself. The factors are interrelated and together inform the ultimate question of likelihood of confusion. If the Board determines that credit card services and banking and financing services are highly similar for purposes of the second factor—weighing that factor heavily in favor of finding confusion—it cannot then adopt a more restrictive definition of similarity under the sixth factor that excludes banking and financing marks from the universe of relevant third-party uses. Such an approach would artificially insulate the senior mark from evidence of weakness. The consistency principle ensures that the Board's analysis of mark strength under the sixth factor reflects the same understanding of marketplace realities that informed its analysis of service similarity under the second factor.

## Vacation and Remand

Having identified the legal error, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board's finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remanded for reconsideration. The court directed the Board to reconsider "the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board's factual finding that the parties' services are highly similar." This remand did not pre-judge the outcome; the court did not hold that the broader universe of marks would necessarily demonstrate that CC Serve's mark is weak. Rather, the court required only that the Board apply the correct legal standard—considering similar marks on similar services, using the same definition of similarity it had already applied under the second factor. The court also vacated the Board's analysis of the first DuPont factor, which addresses the similarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. Because commercial impression is informed by the mark's commercial strength, the court reasoned that reconsideration of the sixth factor "may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression." The interdependence of the factors thus required a fresh analysis of the first factor as well. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's analysis of the second DuPont factor, where it found no legal error and concluded that substantial evidence supported the Board's finding of high service similarity. But the inconsistency in the Board's treatment of similarity across factors led the court to vacate and remand on factors one and six.

# The First DuPont Factor and Overall Disposition

## The First DuPont Factor: Similarity of Marks in Their Entireties

The first DuPont factor directs the factfinder to consider the "similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." The opinion quotes this formulation directly from the foundational DuPont decision, 476 F.2d at 1361, and it is the standard the Board applies when evaluating whether two marks are sufficiently similar to give rise to a likelihood of confusion. The inquiry is holistic: the court does not dissect the marks into their constituent elements and compare them in isolation. Instead, the analysis focuses on the overall commercial impression each mark creates—the net mental image that a consumer retains upon encountering the mark in the marketplace. As the Federal Circuit explained, citing *In re I.A.M.Symbolic, LLC*, 866 F.3d at 1323, the question is whether confusion as to the source of the services offered under the respective marks is likely to result.

In this case, the Board had concluded that the first DuPont factor weighed in favor of CC Serve, finding that Apex's ASPIRE BANK word and design marks were sufficiently similar to CC Serve's ASPIRE mark. Apex challenged this conclusion on appeal. However, the Federal Circuit did not reach the merits of whether the Board's comparison of the marks was correct. Instead, the court identified a structural problem in the Board's analysis that required remand before the first factor could be properly evaluated.

## The Relationship Between Commercial Strength and Commercial Impression

The key to understanding the court's disposition of the first factor lies in the connection between two DuPont factors: the first (similarity of the marks) and the sixth (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods). The sixth factor bears directly on the commercial strength of the senior user's mark. When a mark exists in a field crowded with similar marks used on similar goods, consumers become more adept at distinguishing among them, and the senior mark is entitled to only a narrower scope of protection. Conversely, when few similar marks exist, the senior mark may be entitled to a broader scope. The Board's sixth-factor analysis thus produces a finding about the mark's commercial strength or weakness.

That finding, in turn, has implications for the first factor. The first factor's focus on commercial impression is not conducted in a vacuum. The overall commercial impression of a mark, and the scope of protection it commands, is informed by how strong or weak the mark has been shown to be. A mark that has been determined to be commercially weak because of extensive third-party use will receive a narrower scope of protection, meaning that a junior user's similar mark is less likely to cause confusion. A mark that is commercially strong will receive a broader scope, making confusion more likely even with marks that are somewhat more distant.

## Why Reconsideration of the Sixth Factor Affects the First Factor

The Federal Circuit had already determined that the Board's sixth-factor analysis was legally flawed because the Board improperly narrowed the universe of third-party marks it considered, restricting its review to marks used in connection with credit card services while excluding marks used for other banking and financing services. The court held that this was inconsistent with the Board's own factual finding under the second factor—that the parties' services were highly similar, even partially legally identical. The Board could not adopt a broad definition of similarity for one factor and then impose a narrow one for another.

This error mattered for the first factor because the sixth factor's reconsideration might produce a different result. If, on remand, the Board considers the broader universe of third-party ASPIRE-formative marks—including those used in the broader banking and financing services industry—it might find that the field is more crowded than the Board originally concluded. That finding could lead the Board to determine that CC Serve's ASPIRE mark is commercially weaker than it previously found, entitled to only a narrower scope of protection. Such a determination would, in turn, affect the overall commercial impression analysis under the first factor, because the scope of protection accorded to the senior mark shapes the question of whether a similar mark is likely to cause confusion. As the court stated, "reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."

The court therefore concluded that it could not meaningfully review the Board's first-factor analysis while the sixth-factor analysis remained infected with legal error. The prudent course was to remand the first factor as well, so that the Board could reconsider the similarity of the marks in light of whatever conclusion it reaches about the mark's commercial strength upon a proper sixth-factor analysis.

## The Court's Disposition

The Federal Circuit's ultimate disposition reflects a precise calibration of what it affirmed, what it vacated, and what it remanded. As to the second DuPont factor—the similarity of the parties' goods and services—the court affirmed the Board's finding. The court concluded that the Board's legal analysis was sound and that substantial evidence supported the Board's finding that credit card services and banking and financing services are highly similar. Apex's argument that the services were not directly competitive was rejected; the court noted that the services need not be identical, only related in a manner that could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.

As to the sixth DuPont factor—the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods—the court vacated the Board's finding. The Board had erred by applying an unduly narrow definition of similarity, limited to credit card services alone, inconsistent with its own broader finding of service similarity under the second factor. The court remanded for the Board to reconsider the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration, applying the same standard of similarity it had already found under the second factor.

As to the first DuPont factor—the similarity of the marks in their entireties—the court likewise vacated and remanded. The court did not hold that the Board's analysis of the marks' appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression was necessarily wrong. Rather, the court held that the analysis was dependent on a proper resolution of the sixth factor. Because the sixth-factor analysis was being remanded for reconsideration, the first-factor analysis had to be remanded as well to ensure that the commercial impression inquiry was informed by a correct determination of the mark's commercial strength.

The court thus affirmed-in-part, vacated-in-part, and remanded. The disposition is notable for its restraint: the court did not substitute its own judgment on the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion question. Instead, it identified specific legal errors in the Board's factor-by-factor analysis and returned the case to the Board for further proceedings consistent with the correct legal standards.

## Costs and the Practical Significance of the Decision

The court awarded costs to Apex Bank, the appellant. This award reflects the fact that Apex prevailed on appeal, at least in part: the court vacated two of the three Board findings Apex challenged and remanded for reconsideration. Although Apex did not win outright—the Board's finding under the second factor was affirmed, and the opposition was not overturned—the court's decision required further Board action favorable to Apex's position, and the costs award acknowledges Apex's partial success.

The broader practical significance of the decision lies in its insistence on consistency across DuPont factor analyses. The Federal Circuit's central holding is that the Board cannot apply one definition of similarity under the second factor—finding the parties' services highly similar, even partially legally identical—and then apply a narrower, more stringent definition of similarity under the sixth factor, one limited only to identical services. The court saw "no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor" than the Board had already applied under the second. This principle—that the Board must maintain a consistent conception of similarity across related DuPont factors—ensures that the likelihood-of-confusion analysis remains coherent and that each factor is evaluated against a common understanding of the relationship between the parties' goods and services.
