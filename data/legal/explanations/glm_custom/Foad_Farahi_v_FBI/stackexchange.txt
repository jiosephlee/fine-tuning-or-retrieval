Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What counts as "enforcement proceedings" under FOIA Exemption 7(A)—do ongoing criminal investigations qualify by themselves?
Question:
In Farahi v. FBI, the court says that "ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings'" under Exemption 7(A), citing CREW v. DOJ. I'm confused because I thought "proceedings" implied something more formal—like a filed case or a formal adjudication. How can a criminal investigation that hasn't resulted in charges be a "proceeding"? What's the threshold for when an investigation becomes a "proceeding" under Exemption 7(A)? Does the government just need to say the investigation is gathering evidence for a possible future case, as the court suggests quoting Juarez?
Answer:
## Answer

Your intuition that "proceedings" ordinarily implies something formal is understandable, but Exemption 7(A) uses a broader, specialized definition. The D.C. Circuit has expressly held that **ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings"** under the exemption, even before any charges are filed or any formal case is opened.

### The governing standard

The court in *Farahi* states the rule clearly:

> "Ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' for these purposes, and the government may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their 'existence.'" (citing *CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d at 1097–98, 1099)

It then quotes *Juarez v. DOJ* for the operative threshold:

> "So long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." (518 F.3d at 59)

So the court does not require a filed indictment, a formal adjudication, or any specific charging decision. An investigation that is **actively gathering evidence toward a potential future prosecution** suffices.

### But the government cannot be conclusory

The opinion does set some limits. The proceeding must be a "**concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding**," not a vague or hypothetical one (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1097). And *Farahi* explicitly acknowledges that "the 'vague nature' of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support the invocation of Exemption 7(A)" (quoting *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1098).

That concern was actually at issue in *Farahi* itself. The FBI's updated Bender declaration—which simply stated that investigations "remain ongoing"—was "surprisingly terse" standing alone. The court sustained it only because it was not meant to be read in isolation: the earlier Hardy declarations had already established, in detail, the existence and scope of the investigation, and Bender's submission was merely confirming that nothing had changed in the interim. The court also relied on the breadth and seriousness of the underlying investigation—documented ties to designated terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists—to find the government's representation plausible.

### So what is the threshold?

Drawing the opinion together, the government meets its burden under Exemption 7(A) when it shows, **with reasonable specificity**, that:

1. **Records were compiled for law enforcement purposes**—here, the FBI's national-security and counterterrorism investigation (*Farahi* § IV.A);
2. **An investigation is ongoing** and continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59); and
3. **Disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere** with that investigation—for example, by tipping off targets about the scope of the inquiry (*Farahi* § IV.B, citing *CNSS*, 331 F.3d at 929).

The government need not point to a filed case or formal charges. But a bare, conclusory assertion that an investigation is "ongoing" may be insufficient, particularly without supporting detail or context. The court will look at the totality of the agency's submissions—public and ex parte—to determine whether the government has carried its burden.

### Practical takeaway

Exemption 7(A)'s "enforcement proceedings" requirement is meaningfully easier to satisfy than the formal-sounding term "proceedings" might suggest. In the D.C. Circuit, an active criminal investigation is enough, provided the government explains with reasonable specificity what the investigation involves and how disclosure would interfere with it. The real analytical focus in these cases is usually not whether a "proceeding" exists, but whether the government's showing is sufficiently concrete and specific to satisfy its burden of proof.

### How does deference to the FBI in FOIA cases work, and why is it heightened in national security contexts?
Question:
The Farahi opinion says the FBI's invocation of Exemption 7 is entitled to deference because it's a law-enforcement agency, and that such deference is "particularly appropriate" for records implicating national security, citing CNSS. But I'm not sure I understand what "deference" means here practically. Is the court saying it takes the FBI's word for it that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes? Or does it still independently verify the "nexus" requirement from Campbell v. DOJ? And what makes national security cases different—is it a constitutional separation-of-powers deference, or just practical institutional competence?
Answer:
## What "Deference" Means Here

Deference in this context is not a rubber stamp. The court makes clear that the government always "bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies" and that summary judgment is reviewed de novo. What deference means is that the court gives weight to the FBI's characterization of its own records and purposes, recognizing that the agency has institutional expertise in identifying whether its investigations serve law enforcement objectives and whether disclosure would interfere with them.

The practical effect is that the court does not require the FBI to prove these things from scratch in a way that would effectively duplicate the agency's investigative judgment. But it still requires the agency to submit declarations that meet substantive legal standards, and it evaluates those declarations in context.

## The Nexus Requirement Survives Deference

The opinion directly addresses your question about Campbell. It states that "because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its determination on this point is entitled to a fair measure of deference," but then immediately adds: "Still, its declarations must establish a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties,' as well as a 'connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'"

That word "still" is doing important work. It signals that deference does not displace the Campbell nexus test—it modifies how the court assesses the evidence supporting it. The court then independently evaluates whether the nexus was established, looking at the public Hardy declaration, the ex parte Hardy declaration, and "broader context," including Farahi's own concessions in immigration proceedings about his connections to designated foreign terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists. The court concluded it had "no doubt" the FBI carried its burden, but that conclusion followed an independent review of the record, not blind acceptance.

The same pattern holds for the second prong of Exemption 7(A)—whether disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court did not simply accept the FBI's word. It evaluated whether the declarations were too conclusory, considered Farahi's argument about the passage of time, and examined whether the terse Bender declaration could be read in isolation or had to be understood as an update to the more detailed Hardy declarations. The court's analysis was deferential—it accepted that the government may show pending proceedings "simply by attesting to their existence"—but it still engaged with the specific content of the declarations and the opposing arguments.

## Why National Security Cases Are Different

The opinion suggests both institutional competence and separation-of-powers considerations, though it does not explicitly ground the heightened deference in constitutional doctrine.

The general FBI deference is tied to institutional specialization: "Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference." That is a practical institutional-competence rationale—the FBI knows what its investigations are for and what disclosure would risk.

The heightened deference in national security cases draws on CNSS, which the opinion quotes for the principle that courts have "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated," including specifically in Exemption 7(A) counterterrorism cases. The phrase "deference to the executive" carries separation-of-powers overtones, suggesting the judiciary should be especially cautious about second-guessing the political branches' national security determinations. But the opinion does not cite a constitutional source for this principle or frame it in expressly constitutional terms.

The practical manifestations of heightened deference in this case are visible in two respects. First, the court accepted ex parte declarations, recognizing that this "handicaps FOIA plaintiffs" but is "inherent in those FOIA cases where an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." Second, the court was willing to treat the surprisingly terse Bender declaration as adequate—not because it was sufficient standing alone, but because it could be understood as a confirmation that nothing had changed since the more detailed Hardy declarations, and because "the breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation" made plausible its representation that the investigation remained ongoing.

## Summary

Deference here means the court gives the FBI's submissions real weight given its institutional expertise, but the court still independently verifies that the statutory requirements are met. The Campbell nexus requirement remains in force. National security heightens the deference through both institutional-competence and separation-of-powers logic, which practically manifests in greater willingness to rely on ex parte submissions and to accept relatively concise confirmations when the broader record and context support them.

### When are ex parte declarations appropriate in FOIA litigation, and what can a plaintiff do to challenge them?
Question:
In Farahi, the FBI submitted ex parte declarations under seal, and the court relied on them for both the Exemption 7(A) analysis and the segregability determination. The court says Farahi "did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them." But how could Farahi meaningfully oppose something he couldn't see? And the court acknowledges that ex parte declarations "handicap FOIA plaintiffs." What procedural options does a FOIA plaintiff have to push back on ex parte submissions when the subject matter is classified or sensitive? Is there any recourse, or is the plaintiff just stuck once the court allows them?
Answer:
## When Ex Parte Declarations Are Appropriate

The opinion articulates the governing standard through its citation to *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep't of Veterans Affairs*, 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C. Cir. 2023): ex parte declarations are appropriate when they are **"the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."** The court acknowledges that ex parte submissions "handicap FOIA plaintiffs," but treats this as an inherent and unavoidable feature of FOIA litigation involving classified or sensitive material—not as a defect that renders the practice improper. As the court puts it, that problem "is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

So the threshold question is necessity: if the agency can justify its withholding through public declarations alone, ex parte submission is not warranted. Only when public explanations would themselves reveal the protected information does the court treat ex parte submission as justified.

## What a Plaintiff Can Do

The opinion's treatment of Farahi's challenge reveals several procedural options—and one critical forfeiture.

**1. Oppose the motion to file ex parte when it is made.** This is the most important takeaway. The court's statement that Farahi "did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them" makes clear that a plaintiff *can* oppose the acceptance of exparte declarations at the district court level—and that failing to do so forfeits the argument on appeal. The FBI's ex parte submission was initiated by motion (here, a motion to file the supplemental Hardy declaration "under seal and ex parte"), and Farahi had the opportunity to contest that motion. A plaintiff opposing such a motion could argue that the agency has not demonstrated that ex parte treatment is truly necessary under the *Perioperative Services* standard—for example, by contending that the agency could provide a more detailed public declaration with redactions, or that the information in question is not so sensitive as to require complete secrecy from the opposing party.

**2. Challenge the adequacy of the public declarations and demand greater specificity before ex parte submission.** The opinion shows the district court itself doing something like this: it initially denied summary judgment without prejudice because the Hardy declarations were potentially stale and because the court "needed more information to address segregability, for reasons it could not state on the public record." A plaintiff can press the court to require the agency to supplement its public filings with greater detail before resorting to sealed submissions, narrowing the scope of whatever ultimately must be submitted ex parte.

**3. Challenge staleness and seek updates.** Farahi successfully persuaded the district court that the passage of time undermined the FBI's initial showing, resulting in an order for updated declarations. While not a direct challenge to ex parte submission, this is a mechanism to test whether the government's justifications—public or sealed—remain current and adequately supported.

**4. Raise specific, developed arguments about overbreadth and categorization.** The opinion notes that Farahi objected to the FBI's document categories but "provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad," leading to forfeiture. A plaintiff who develops specific, concrete challenges to an agency's withholding categories forces the court and the agency to justify their approach with greater precision, which may reduce reliance on ex parte submissions.

## The Hard Reality

The opinion is candid that once ex parte declarations are accepted, the plaintiff faces structural limitations. The court states that "nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration," meaning the court can rely on sealed material without explaining its reasoning in a way the plaintiff can meaningfully review or rebut. This is the "handicap" the court acknowledges.

The key practical lesson from *Farahi* is therefore timing. The critical moment to push back is **when the agency moves to file ex parte**, not after. A plaintiff who fails to oppose at that stage—as Farahi did—loses the ability to challenge the court's reliance on those declarations on appeal. The opposition should be specific: argue that the agency has not met the *Perioperative Services* standard of showing ex parte submission is the *only* way to decide the dispute without disclosure, and propose alternatives (more detailed redacted declarations, in camera document review with a public explanation of methodology, narrower scope of sealed material). Once the court accepts the ex parte submission without opposition, the plaintiff is largely stuck with the consequences.

### How does the "reasonably segregable" requirement work under FOIA, and when can an agency withhold entire documents?
Question:
The Farahi court says the FBI can withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt portions. It also says that individual words or phrases that could be disclosed without harm are "neither here nor there" because "the focus of the FOIA is information, not simply words." I'm confused about the line between what is and isn't "reasonably segregable." If you can redact individual words or lines without revealing protected information, why isn't that segregable? And how does the court assess segregability when it can't publicly explain its reasoning—does it just take the agency's word for it based on the ex parte declaration?
Answer:
## The Segregability Standard: Information, Not Words

The "reasonably segregable" requirement does not require agencies to perform word-by-word redactions. It requires them to release portions of records that convey *meaningful information* without revealing exempt material. The distinction matters.

### Why Individual Words or Phrases Aren't Necessarily Segregable

The court's reasoning rests on a distinction between "words" and "information" drawn from *Mead Data Central*:

> "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." *Mead*, 566 F.2d at 261 n.55.

The idea is that if non-exempt material is "randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout" a document, pulling out individual words or phrases may produce nothing coherent. Redacting around scattered non-exempt words yields fragments that don't convey meaningful information to the requester. That doesn't advance FOIA's purpose, so it isn't what the statute requires.

The court applies this logic to Farahi's argument directly:

> "while Farahi is correct that individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI's interests, that is neither here nor there."

So even if you *could* technically carve out individual words without causing harm, that doesn't mean those words are "reasonably segregable" in the statutory sense. The question is whether there is a coherent, meaningful portion of the record that can be released without revealing protected information—not whether stray words can survive redaction.

### When Entire Documents Can Be Withheld

An agency may withhold a document in its entirety when the non-exempt portions are "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61 (quoting *Mead*, 566 F.2d at 260). The court also cited *Mead* for the proposition that when non-exempt material is interspersed line-by-line throughout a document, it is not likely to be reasonably segregable. 566 F.2d at 261.

In this case, the district court concluded—based on its review of Bender's supplemental ex parte declaration—that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit deferred to that conclusion.

### How the Court Assesses Segregability Through Ex Parte Declarations

The court does not simply take the agency's word for it. The standard is that the agency's submissions must show "with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated." *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61. The court reviews the declaration to determine whether it meets that standard.

But when the explanation itself would reveal the protected information, the court cannot put its reasoning on the public record. The court acknowledged this tension:

> "while ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs, that problem 'is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.'" (quoting *Perioperative Services*, 57 F.4th at 1067).

The court also noted two additional points relevant to Farahi's challenge:

1. **Farahi did not oppose the acceptance of ex parte declarations**, so he could not challenge the district court's reliance on them.
2. **Nothing required the court to publicly explain its analysis** of the ex parte declaration, because doing so would risk disclosing the very information the exemption protects.

In practice, the court's review is substantive but non-public. The judge reads the ex parte declaration, assesses whether the agency has shown with reasonable specificity that non-exempt material is not reasonably segregable, and rules accordingly—without publishing an opinion explaining the specifics of that reasoning.

### Summary

- **Segregability is about meaningful information, not individual words.** Redacting to produce scattered non-exempt fragments doesn't satisfy the statute.
- **Entire documents can be withheld** when non-exempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt material, including when it is interspersed line-by-line.
- **Ex parte declarations are the mechanism** for courts to review segregability claims when public explanation would itself cause the harm the exemption protects against. The court still applies the "reasonable specificity" standard, but its analysis is necessarily conducted off the public record.

### What happens when a FOIA declaration becomes stale due to the passage of time during litigation?
Question:
In Farahi, the district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice because of the "significant passage of time" since the FBI's original declaration, ordering the agency to update its assessment. The FBI then submitted a new declaration confirming that investigations were still ongoing. I'm confused about what triggers the need for an updated declaration. Is there a specific time threshold? And if the government's investigation drags on for years, can the plaintiff keep forcing the agency to submit updates? What if the investigation never formally closes but also never progresses—does Exemption 7(A) protect the records indefinitely?
Answer:
## What Triggers the Need for an Updated Declaration

There is no fixed time threshold in this opinion. The district court acted based on what it described as the "significant passage of time" since the FBI's original declaration. The Hardy declaration was submitted in October 2019, and the district court did not rule on the summary judgment motion until November 2022—roughly three years later. The court acknowledged that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden to show pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, but it was not willing to grant summary judgment on a stale record. It therefore denied the motion without prejudice and ordered the FBI to "update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated."

The D.C. Circuit affirmed this approach without articulating a bright-line rule. The takeaway is that staleness is a case-by-case, common-sense assessment by the district court: if enough time has passed between the submission of a declaration and the court's ruling that the factual basis for the exemption might have changed, the court can require a contemporaneous confirmation.

## Whether a Plaintiff Can Repeatedly Force Updates

The opinion suggests not, or at least not indefinitely. The key analytical move the D.C. Circuit made was to treat the Bender declaration as **an update, not a standalone submission**. The court emphasized that "the government submitted the Bender declaration not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending." The court also noted that the district court had already concluded the FBI "seemingly" met its burden in the first round—so the update was a confirmation step, not a do-over.

This framing matters for your concern about repetitive demands. Once the government has established the predicate for Exemption 7(A) with sufficient detail (as the FBI did through the public and ex parte Hardy declarations), a subsequent update need only confirm the continued pendency of proceedings. The court will evaluate that update in the context of the existing record, not in isolation. A plaintiff who disagrees with the update cannot simply demand another one by re-raising the same staleness argument—the burden is on the plaintiff to identify something concrete that undermines the agency's updated representation.

## Whether Exemption 7(A) Can Protect Records Indefinitely

The opinion does not directly address a scenario in which an investigation never formally closes but also never progresses, but it provides the analytical framework that would govern that situation.

The court reiterated the governing standard: "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." The government may show pending proceedings "simply by attesting to their 'existence,'" and the FBI's invocation of Exemption 7(A) is entitled to deference—particularly in cases implicating national security and counterterrorism.

Farahi argued, relying on *CREW*, that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may be insufficient. The D.C. Circuit agreed that the Bender declaration, "considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse." But the court declined to consider it in isolation. It looked to the detailed ex parte Hardy declaration and the broader public record—including the serious nature of the allegations (ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations, Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, Jose Padilla, and terrorism-financing charities)—to conclude that the FBI had met its burden to show a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" that "continues to exist."

So the answer to your hypothetical depends on whether the agency can provide more than a bare, conclusory assertion that an investigation is ongoing. Here, the terseness of the update was cured by the detailed prior submissions and the seriousness and breadth of the underlying investigation. In a different case—where the government's only submission is a vague, standalone attestation with no detail and no indication that the investigation is actively progressing—the *CREW* concern about vagueness would have more force. The opinion does not hold that Exemption 7(A) protects records in perpetuity, but it does signal that courts will give substantial weight to agency representations about ongoing investigations, especially in the national security context, and will evaluate those representations against the totality of the record rather than demanding that every update independently re-establish the entire predicate for the exemption.

### Can a terse supplemental FOIA declaration cure an earlier detailed one, or must each declaration stand alone?
Question:
In Farahi, the court acknowledges that the Bender declaration, "considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse"—it just says investigations "remain ongoing." But the court says context matters: the original Hardy declaration had detailed the investigation, and Bender's declaration was just an update. Farahi argued this was too conclusory under CREW. My question is: in FOIA Exemption 7(A) cases, does each declaration need to independently satisfy the government's burden, or can the court read a later declaration as a supplement to an earlier one? What if the earlier declaration is years old—can a one-line update really keep the burden satisfied?
Answer:
## Answer

**Short answer:** In the D.C. Circuit, FOIA declarations can be read together in context; a later supplemental declaration does not need to independently re-establish what an earlier, detailed declaration already established. But the court's willingness to accept a terse update depends on several contextual factors present in *Farahi*.

---

### The Governing Standard

Under Exemption 7(A), the government must show that withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." *CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014). The government may meet this burden by attesting to the "existence" of ongoing proceedings, and the FBI's invocation of Exemption 7 is entitled to deference, particularly in national-security contexts. *Id.* at 1099; *Ctr. for Nat'l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003).

However, *CREW* also cautioned that a "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not always suffice. 746 F.3d at 1098. That is the principle Farahi invoked against the Bender declaration.

### How *Farahi* Resolves the Question

The court explicitly held that the Bender declaration should **not** be considered in isolation. Writing for the court, Judge Katsas acknowledged that "the Bender declaration, considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse"—it stated only that investigations "remain ongoing." But the court explained that "context counsels against considering the Bender declaration in isolation" for three reasons:

1. **The earlier Hardy declarations had already established the predicate.** The public Hardy declaration attested to the existence of pending enforcement proceedings, and the ex parte Hardy declaration supplied "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation." The Bender declaration was not submitted to establish pendency "from scratch."

2. **The purpose of the supplemental declaration was narrow.** The government submitted the Bender declaration "only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending." The district court had already concluded the FBI "seemingly" met its burden; it simply ordered an update due to the passage of time.

3. **The breadth of the underlying investigation lent plausibility.** The court noted the seriousness and scope of the FBI's public allegations against Farahi—which included connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, Jose Padilla, the Holy Land Foundation, and others—as corroborating context that made the representation of ongoing investigation plausible.

Thus, the court concluded that the Bender declaration "can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document."

### What This Means for the "Years-Old Declaration" Concern

Your concern about staleness is legitimate, and the district court shared it—that is precisely why it denied the first summary-judgment motion without prejudice and ordered the FBI to update its assessment. But *Farahi* establishes that the cure for staleness does not require the government to start over with a fully detailed new declaration. Rather:

- The **original** declaration must have established the Exemption 7(A) predicate with reasonable specificity.
- The **supplemental** declaration need only confirm that circumstances have not changed—provided the court can review the two together in context.
- The **plausibility** of the "still ongoing" representation may be bolstered by the nature and breadth of the underlying investigation, as reflected in the record.

That said, the court left room for the opposite result in different circumstances. If there had been no detailed prior declaration, or if the original declaration had been conclusory in the first instance, a terse one-line update likely would not suffice under *CREW*. The court's holding is best read as: **a supplemental declaration may be terse when it supplements, rather than replaces, an adequate prior submission.**

### What are the requirements for category-based withholding in FOIA, and what happens if a plaintiff fails to specifically challenge the categories?
Question:
The Farahi opinion mentions that agencies using category-based withholding must use categories that are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each category, citing CREW. Farahi objected that the FBI's categories were overbroad, but the court said he "provided no specific explanation" and therefore forfeited his argument. What does a plaintiff need to do to properly challenge category overbreadth? Does the plaintiff need to have seen the documents to challenge the categories, or can they challenge the categories based on the agency's description alone? How specific does the objection need to be?
Answer:
## Answer

The opinion addresses category-based withholding in Section IV.C, though it does so briefly. Here is what can be drawn from it.

### The Legal Standard

Under *CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1088 (D.C. Cir. 2014), when an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, it must use categories that are **"sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied"** to each category (quoting *Gallant v. NLRB*, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). This standard is about the *agency's* burden: the categories must be defined clearly enough that the court can evaluate whether the claimed exemption actually fits each category.

### What a Plaintiff Must Do to Challenge Overbreadth

The opinion tells us what Farahi did *wrong* rather than what a plaintiff must do *right*. The court states that Farahi "briefly objects" to the categories but **"provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad."** The consequence was forfeiture under *Khine v. DHS*, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

From this, the clear takeaway is that a plaintiff must do more than assert that categories are overbroad. The plaintiff must provide a **specific explanation** of *why* the categories are overbroad—that is, why they are not "sufficiently distinct" to allow the court to evaluate whether the claimed exemptions properly apply to each category. A bare assertion of overbreadth, without more, is insufficient.

### Can a Plaintiff Challenge Categories Without Seeing the Documents?

The opinion does not explicitly address this question. However, the legal standard the court applies suggests that the challenge operates on the **agency's description of the categories**, not on the contents of the withheld documents. The test is whether the categories are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied." This is an assessment of whether the agency's categorical descriptions are adequate; it does not require the plaintiff to have seen the underlying documents. The plaintiff can presumably challenge the categories based on the agency's own declarations and descriptions.

### How Specific Must the Objection Be?

Again, the opinion does not provide a detailed roadmap, but it signals the floor. The court notes that Farahi "briefly" objects and provides "no specific explanation." This suggests two failures: the objection was too cursory in form, and it lacked substantive content. At minimum, a plaintiff should **identify the specific categories at issue and explain why each fails the distinctness standard**—for example, by explaining why a given category sweeps together documents to which different exemptions might apply, making it impossible for the court to assess whether the claimed exemption fits.

### Summary

The opinion establishes a clear allocation of burdens: the agency must define sufficiently distinct categories, and the plaintiff must provide a specific explanation if it wishes to challenge them. A plaintiff who merely asserts overbreadth without explaining *why* the categories are inadequate forfeits the argument. While the opinion does not directly address whether the plaintiff must have seen the documents, the nature of the standard—evaluating the adequacy of the agency's categorical descriptions—suggests that the challenge can be mounted based on the agency's own submissions.

### How does the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard for Exemption 7(A) differ from other FOIA exemptions that require showing concrete harm?
Question:
In Farahi, the court says the government can show pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings simply by "attesting to their existence." This seems like a very low bar compared to other FOIA exemptions that seem to require more specific showings of harm. Is Exemption 7(A) unique in this regard? How does the "could reasonably be expected to interfere" standard compare to the harm showing required under, say, Exemption 7(E (which protects techniques and procedures) or Exemption 1 (national security)? And does the deference the court gives to the FBI in national security cases effectively lower the government's burden even further than the statutory text suggests?
Answer:
## Exemption 7(A) Is Indeed a Low-Bar Exemption—By Design

You've identified a real structural feature of FOIA's Exemption 7(A). The opinion in *Farahi* illustrates how 7(A) operates on a fundamentally different axis than exemptions like 7(E) or Exemption 1. The short answer is that 7(A) asks a *prospective, existence-based* question—"are there proceedings that could be interfered with?"—rather than requiring the government to demonstrate *what specific harm* would flow from disclosure of the particular records at issue.

### The 7(A) Standard as Explained in *Farahi*

The court lays out the two-part test for 7(A): records must be (1) "compiled for law enforcement purposes" and (2) whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The key passage is the court's citation to *CREW* for the proposition that "the government may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their 'existence.'" The court then quotes *Juarez*: "[S]o long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

This is a low bar because the government doesn't need to show *what* specific damage disclosure would cause to the proceedings. It needs to show that proceedings exist (or are reasonably anticipated) and that releasing law enforcement records compiled in connection with them would generally "interfere"—with "tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation" being, as the court notes, "a widely accepted justification."

### How This Differs from 7(E) and Exemption 1

The opinion doesn't directly analyze 7(E) or Exemption 1, but the contrast is apparent from how the court frames 7(A).

**Exemption 7(E** protects techniques and procedures "if such disclosure could reasonably be expected to risk circumvention of the law." That standard is harm-specific: the agency must identify *what* techniques or procedures would be revealed and explain *how* their disclosure would risk circumvention. The showing is about the content of the records and the specific harm from revealing that content.

**Exemption 1** requires that records be "in fact properly classified" under executive order criteria. That involves a detailed classification analysis—connecting the information to specific classification categories and demonstrating that the information fits those criteria. Again, the showing is content-specific.

**Exemption 7(A**, by contrast, is *proceeding-specific*. Once the government establishes that enforcement proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated, the interference concern is essentially built into the analysis: releasing records compiled for those proceedings could tip off targets, reveal the scope of the investigation, or otherwise undermine the case. The government doesn't have to itemize what's in each document and explain how each item would cause interference—it just has to show the proceedings exist and that the records were compiled for them.

### Deference Further Lowers the Burden—Especially for National Security

You asked whether deference effectively lowers the government's burden beyond what the statutory text suggests. The opinion strongly suggests it does, in at least two ways.

First, the court states broadly that "the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference," citing *Campbell*. The court then escalates this for national security: "Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security," citing *CNSS* for the principle that courts "consistently reiterated" deference "in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated—including specifically in Exemption 7(A) cases seeking records related to counterterrorism investigations."

Second, the court's actual application of the standard shows how deference operates in practice. The Bender declaration, which the court itself acknowledged "appears surprisingly terse," was accepted because it was understood as "an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document." The court also used the "breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation" to find it "plausible" that the investigation remained ongoing—pointing to the FBI's public allegations in Farahi's immigration proceedings about ties to terrorist organizations. Notably, the court relied on ex parte declarations for the detail it couldn't put on the public record, and Farahi couldn't challenge that reliance because he hadn't opposed the acceptance of ex parte submissions.

So the deference operates at multiple levels: deference to the agency's characterization of its records as compiled for law enforcement, deference to its attestation that proceedings are ongoing, and deference that is heightened when national security is implicated. The practical result is that a terse "the investigation remains ongoing" can suffice when it builds on prior, more detailed submissions and when the subject matter involves counterterrorism.

### A Few Checks Remain

The opinion does show that the standard isn't *no* bar at all. The *CREW* court noted that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing "may not suffice." Here, the district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice because of the "significant passage of time" since the Hardy declarations, requiring the FBI to update its assessment. And the D.C. Circuit was careful to note that it wasn't evaluating the Bender declaration "in isolation"—it relied on the prior public and ex parte Hardy declarations to provide the substance that the Bender declaration lacked. So while 7(A)'s burden is low, it isn't purely formal; courts can require updates and can push back when submissions are too vague to evaluate. But the opinion makes clear that in national security cases, the combination of 7(A)'s existence-based standard and deference to the executive results in a burden that is significantly lighter than what other exemptions require.
