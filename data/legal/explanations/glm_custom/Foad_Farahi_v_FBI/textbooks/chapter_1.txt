Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # The FOIA Framework and Exemption 7(A)

## FOIA's General Disclosure Mandate Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3)

The Freedom of Information Act rests on a simple but powerful premise: the federal government works for the public, and the public is entitled to see what the government is doing. As the court explains at the outset of *Farahi v. FBI*, FOIA "requires federal agencies to release records to members of the public upon request." This general disclosure mandate is codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), which gives any person the right to request records from federal agencies and obligates those agencies to produce responsive records.

The statute's default setting is openness. When an individual submits a FOIA request, the agency must search for records responsive to the request and produce them. If the agency fails to act within the statutory deadline, the requester may sue in federal court to compel compliance—which is exactly what Foad Farahi did here when the FBI missed its deadline for responding to his request for his own file.

This case illustrates the scale at which FOIA operates in practice. Farahi's request led the FBI to locate approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive information. The agency then undertook a years-long process of reviewing those records, disclosing some, and withholding most. This illustrates that FOIA's disclosure mandate is not self-executing; it requires agencies to make sometimes resource-intensive judgments about what must be released and what may be withheld.

## The Role of Statutory Exemptions Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)

FOIA's disclosure mandate is not absolute. The statute enumerates a series of exemptions that permit agencies to withhold records in specified circumstances. These exemptions are codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552(b). As the court notes, the general rule is that agencies must release records "unless an enumerated statutory exemption applies."

The exemptions reflect a policy judgment that certain government interests—such as national security, personal privacy, and law enforcement—sometimes outweigh the public's interest in disclosure. The burden of demonstrating that an exemption applies falls on the government. In FOIA litigation, the agency must justify its withholding decisions, typically through declarations submitted to the court explaining why particular records fall within one or more exemptions.

In this case, the FBI invoked one specific exemption: Exemption 7(A). The litigation thus turned on whether the FBI could carry its burden of showing that the withheld records satisfied the requirements of that exemption. Understanding the court's analysis requires a careful examination of the statutory text and the governing case law interpreting it.

## Text and Elements of Exemption 7(A)

Exemption 7(A) is one of several law-enforcement-related exemptions within FOIA. It covers records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The exemption thus has two core elements: first, that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, and second, that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.

The first element—compilation for law enforcement purposes—reflects the exemption's focus on records connected to the government's law enforcement function. Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, courts give its determination on this point "a fair measure of deference." Still, the agency must "establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties," as well as a "connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law." In *Farahi*, this element was satisfied by the FBI's declarations explaining that the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national security and international terrorism violations, combined with Farahi's own concession that the FBI had investigated him for ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations.

The second element—reasonable interference with enforcement proceedings—is the heart of the dispute in this case. The government must show that disclosure of the withheld records could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court accepted the FBI's explanation that disclosure could tip off targets about the scope of the ongoing investigation—a justification the D.C. Circuit has recognized as a valid basis for invoking Exemption 7(A).

## The 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated' Standard for Enforcement Proceedings

The court's analysis of the second element of Exemption 7(A) is governed by the D.C. Circuit's decision in *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* ("CREW"), 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014), which holds that enforcement proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated" for the exemption to apply. This requirement prevents agencies from invoking Exemption 7(A) based on speculative or hypothetical proceedings that have no concrete basis.

Ongoing criminal investigations count as "proceedings" under this standard. The government need not show that charges have been filed or that a prosecution is imminent. Rather, it must demonstrate the existence of a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding." The court in *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008), explained that "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." The government may satisfy its burden simply by attesting to the existence of such proceedings, though in some cases a "vague" assertion may be insufficient.

The *Farahi* case presents a wrinkle on this standard: what happens when significant time passes between the agency's initial showing and the court's resolution of the dispute? Farahi argued that the passage of time cast doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending. The district court itself recognized this concern when it initially denied the FBI's first summary-judgment motion without prejudice, ordering the agency to update its assessment. The D.C. Circuit ultimately held that the government's updated declaration, read in context alongside the earlier and more detailed declarations, was sufficient to confirm that the proceedings remained ongoing. The court also found that the breadth of the underlying investigation—implicating connections to designated foreign terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists—made the government's representation plausible. This analysis illustrates that the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard is not a one-time showing but may require updating when material time has elapsed during litigation.

## The Segregability Requirement Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b)

Even when records fall within a FOIA exemption, the agency's obligation to disclose is not entirely extinguished. The statute imposes a segregability requirement: agencies must take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). Section 552(b) reinforces this obligation by providing that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt."

The segregability requirement serves an important function: it ensures that exemptions do not become a license for blanket withholding. If a record contains both exempt and nonexempt material, the agency must release the nonexempt portions if they can be reasonably segregated from the exempt material. However, the requirement has limits. The government may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions "are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." In such cases, extracting the nonexempt information would be impracticable or would result in the disclosure of protected material.

The court's analysis in *Farahi* illustrates an important principle about the segregability requirement: it focuses on meaningful information, not isolated words. Farahi argued that given the vast volume of responsive records—thousands of pages and dozens of CDs—some information must be segregable and releasable. The court rejected this argument, drawing on *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977), for the principle that if a small amount of nonexempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it is not "reasonably segregable." As the court explained, "the focus of the FOIA is information, not simply words which the Government has written down." Individual words or phrases might technically be disclosed without harming law enforcement interests, but if they do not convey meaningful information independent of the exempt material, they are not reasonably segregable.

The segregability analysis also intersected with the procedural question of ex parte submissions. The district court relied on an ex parte declaration from the FBI to evaluate whether exempt material was reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit upheld this approach, noting that while ex parte declarations "handicap FOIA plaintiffs," this problem is "inherent in those FOIA cases where an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." Because Farahi had not opposed the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, he could not later challenge the court's reliance on them.
