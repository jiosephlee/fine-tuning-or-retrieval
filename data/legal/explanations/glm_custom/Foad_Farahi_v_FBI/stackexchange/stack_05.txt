Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What happens when a FOIA declaration becomes stale due to the passage of time during litigation?
Question:
In Farahi, the district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice because of the "significant passage of time" since the FBI's original declaration, ordering the agency to update its assessment. The FBI then submitted a new declaration confirming that investigations were still ongoing. I'm confused about what triggers the need for an updated declaration. Is there a specific time threshold? And if the government's investigation drags on for years, can the plaintiff keep forcing the agency to submit updates? What if the investigation never formally closes but also never progresses—does Exemption 7(A) protect the records indefinitely?
Answer:
## What Triggers the Need for an Updated Declaration

There is no fixed time threshold in this opinion. The district court acted based on what it described as the "significant passage of time" since the FBI's original declaration. The Hardy declaration was submitted in October 2019, and the district court did not rule on the summary judgment motion until November 2022—roughly three years later. The court acknowledged that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden to show pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, but it was not willing to grant summary judgment on a stale record. It therefore denied the motion without prejudice and ordered the FBI to "update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated."

The D.C. Circuit affirmed this approach without articulating a bright-line rule. The takeaway is that staleness is a case-by-case, common-sense assessment by the district court: if enough time has passed between the submission of a declaration and the court's ruling that the factual basis for the exemption might have changed, the court can require a contemporaneous confirmation.

## Whether a Plaintiff Can Repeatedly Force Updates

The opinion suggests not, or at least not indefinitely. The key analytical move the D.C. Circuit made was to treat the Bender declaration as **an update, not a standalone submission**. The court emphasized that "the government submitted the Bender declaration not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending." The court also noted that the district court had already concluded the FBI "seemingly" met its burden in the first round—so the update was a confirmation step, not a do-over.

This framing matters for your concern about repetitive demands. Once the government has established the predicate for Exemption 7(A) with sufficient detail (as the FBI did through the public and ex parte Hardy declarations), a subsequent update need only confirm the continued pendency of proceedings. The court will evaluate that update in the context of the existing record, not in isolation. A plaintiff who disagrees with the update cannot simply demand another one by re-raising the same staleness argument—the burden is on the plaintiff to identify something concrete that undermines the agency's updated representation.

## Whether Exemption 7(A) Can Protect Records Indefinitely

The opinion does not directly address a scenario in which an investigation never formally closes but also never progresses, but it provides the analytical framework that would govern that situation.

The court reiterated the governing standard: "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." The government may show pending proceedings "simply by attesting to their 'existence,'" and the FBI's invocation of Exemption 7(A) is entitled to deference—particularly in cases implicating national security and counterterrorism.

Farahi argued, relying on *CREW*, that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may be insufficient. The D.C. Circuit agreed that the Bender declaration, "considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse." But the court declined to consider it in isolation. It looked to the detailed ex parte Hardy declaration and the broader public record—including the serious nature of the allegations (ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations, Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, Jose Padilla, and terrorism-financing charities)—to conclude that the FBI had met its burden to show a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" that "continues to exist."

So the answer to your hypothetical depends on whether the agency can provide more than a bare, conclusory assertion that an investigation is ongoing. Here, the terseness of the update was cured by the detailed prior submissions and the seriousness and breadth of the underlying investigation. In a different case—where the government's only submission is a vague, standalone attestation with no detail and no indication that the investigation is actively progressing—the *CREW* concern about vagueness would have more force. The opinion does not hold that Exemption 7(A) protects records in perpetuity, but it does signal that courts will give substantial weight to agency representations about ongoing investigations, especially in the national security context, and will evaluate those representations against the totality of the record rather than demanding that every update independently re-establish the entire predicate for the exemption.
