Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard for Exemption 7(A) differ from other FOIA exemptions that require showing concrete harm?
Question:
In Farahi, the court says the government can show pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings simply by "attesting to their existence." This seems like a very low bar compared to other FOIA exemptions that seem to require more specific showings of harm. Is Exemption 7(A) unique in this regard? How does the "could reasonably be expected to interfere" standard compare to the harm showing required under, say, Exemption 7(E (which protects techniques and procedures) or Exemption 1 (national security)? And does the deference the court gives to the FBI in national security cases effectively lower the government's burden even further than the statutory text suggests?
Answer:
## Exemption 7(A) Is Indeed a Low-Bar Exemption—By Design

You've identified a real structural feature of FOIA's Exemption 7(A). The opinion in *Farahi* illustrates how 7(A) operates on a fundamentally different axis than exemptions like 7(E) or Exemption 1. The short answer is that 7(A) asks a *prospective, existence-based* question—"are there proceedings that could be interfered with?"—rather than requiring the government to demonstrate *what specific harm* would flow from disclosure of the particular records at issue.

### The 7(A) Standard as Explained in *Farahi*

The court lays out the two-part test for 7(A): records must be (1) "compiled for law enforcement purposes" and (2) whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The key passage is the court's citation to *CREW* for the proposition that "the government may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their 'existence.'" The court then quotes *Juarez*: "[S]o long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

This is a low bar because the government doesn't need to show *what* specific damage disclosure would cause to the proceedings. It needs to show that proceedings exist (or are reasonably anticipated) and that releasing law enforcement records compiled in connection with them would generally "interfere"—with "tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation" being, as the court notes, "a widely accepted justification."

### How This Differs from 7(E) and Exemption 1

The opinion doesn't directly analyze 7(E) or Exemption 1, but the contrast is apparent from how the court frames 7(A).

**Exemption 7(E** protects techniques and procedures "if such disclosure could reasonably be expected to risk circumvention of the law." That standard is harm-specific: the agency must identify *what* techniques or procedures would be revealed and explain *how* their disclosure would risk circumvention. The showing is about the content of the records and the specific harm from revealing that content.

**Exemption 1** requires that records be "in fact properly classified" under executive order criteria. That involves a detailed classification analysis—connecting the information to specific classification categories and demonstrating that the information fits those criteria. Again, the showing is content-specific.

**Exemption 7(A**, by contrast, is *proceeding-specific*. Once the government establishes that enforcement proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated, the interference concern is essentially built into the analysis: releasing records compiled for those proceedings could tip off targets, reveal the scope of the investigation, or otherwise undermine the case. The government doesn't have to itemize what's in each document and explain how each item would cause interference—it just has to show the proceedings exist and that the records were compiled for them.

### Deference Further Lowers the Burden—Especially for National Security

You asked whether deference effectively lowers the government's burden beyond what the statutory text suggests. The opinion strongly suggests it does, in at least two ways.

First, the court states broadly that "the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference," citing *Campbell*. The court then escalates this for national security: "Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security," citing *CNSS* for the principle that courts "consistently reiterated" deference "in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated—including specifically in Exemption 7(A) cases seeking records related to counterterrorism investigations."

Second, the court's actual application of the standard shows how deference operates in practice. The Bender declaration, which the court itself acknowledged "appears surprisingly terse," was accepted because it was understood as "an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document." The court also used the "breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation" to find it "plausible" that the investigation remained ongoing—pointing to the FBI's public allegations in Farahi's immigration proceedings about ties to terrorist organizations. Notably, the court relied on ex parte declarations for the detail it couldn't put on the public record, and Farahi couldn't challenge that reliance because he hadn't opposed the acceptance of ex parte submissions.

So the deference operates at multiple levels: deference to the agency's characterization of its records as compiled for law enforcement, deference to its attestation that proceedings are ongoing, and deference that is heightened when national security is implicated. The practical result is that a terse "the investigation remains ongoing" can suffice when it builds on prior, more detailed submissions and when the subject matter involves counterterrorism.

### A Few Checks Remain

The opinion does show that the standard isn't *no* bar at all. The *CREW* court noted that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing "may not suffice." Here, the district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice because of the "significant passage of time" since the Hardy declarations, requiring the FBI to update its assessment. And the D.C. Circuit was careful to note that it wasn't evaluating the Bender declaration "in isolation"—it relied on the prior public and ex parte Hardy declarations to provide the substance that the Bender declaration lacked. So while 7(A)'s burden is low, it isn't purely formal; courts can require updates and can push back when submissions are too vague to evaluate. But the opinion makes clear that in national security cases, the combination of 7(A)'s existence-based standard and deference to the executive results in a burden that is significantly lighter than what other exemptions require.
