Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does deference to the FBI in FOIA cases work, and why is it heightened in national security contexts?
Question:
The Farahi opinion says the FBI's invocation of Exemption 7 is entitled to deference because it's a law-enforcement agency, and that such deference is "particularly appropriate" for records implicating national security, citing CNSS. But I'm not sure I understand what "deference" means here practically. Is the court saying it takes the FBI's word for it that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes? Or does it still independently verify the "nexus" requirement from Campbell v. DOJ? And what makes national security cases different—is it a constitutional separation-of-powers deference, or just practical institutional competence?
Answer:
## What "Deference" Means Here

Deference in this context is not a rubber stamp. The court makes clear that the government always "bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies" and that summary judgment is reviewed de novo. What deference means is that the court gives weight to the FBI's characterization of its own records and purposes, recognizing that the agency has institutional expertise in identifying whether its investigations serve law enforcement objectives and whether disclosure would interfere with them.

The practical effect is that the court does not require the FBI to prove these things from scratch in a way that would effectively duplicate the agency's investigative judgment. But it still requires the agency to submit declarations that meet substantive legal standards, and it evaluates those declarations in context.

## The Nexus Requirement Survives Deference

The opinion directly addresses your question about Campbell. It states that "because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its determination on this point is entitled to a fair measure of deference," but then immediately adds: "Still, its declarations must establish a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties,' as well as a 'connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'"

That word "still" is doing important work. It signals that deference does not displace the Campbell nexus test—it modifies how the court assesses the evidence supporting it. The court then independently evaluates whether the nexus was established, looking at the public Hardy declaration, the ex parte Hardy declaration, and "broader context," including Farahi's own concessions in immigration proceedings about his connections to designated foreign terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists. The court concluded it had "no doubt" the FBI carried its burden, but that conclusion followed an independent review of the record, not blind acceptance.

The same pattern holds for the second prong of Exemption 7(A)—whether disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court did not simply accept the FBI's word. It evaluated whether the declarations were too conclusory, considered Farahi's argument about the passage of time, and examined whether the terse Bender declaration could be read in isolation or had to be understood as an update to the more detailed Hardy declarations. The court's analysis was deferential—it accepted that the government may show pending proceedings "simply by attesting to their existence"—but it still engaged with the specific content of the declarations and the opposing arguments.

## Why National Security Cases Are Different

The opinion suggests both institutional competence and separation-of-powers considerations, though it does not explicitly ground the heightened deference in constitutional doctrine.

The general FBI deference is tied to institutional specialization: "Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference." That is a practical institutional-competence rationale—the FBI knows what its investigations are for and what disclosure would risk.

The heightened deference in national security cases draws on CNSS, which the opinion quotes for the principle that courts have "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated," including specifically in Exemption 7(A) counterterrorism cases. The phrase "deference to the executive" carries separation-of-powers overtones, suggesting the judiciary should be especially cautious about second-guessing the political branches' national security determinations. But the opinion does not cite a constitutional source for this principle or frame it in expressly constitutional terms.

The practical manifestations of heightened deference in this case are visible in two respects. First, the court accepted ex parte declarations, recognizing that this "handicaps FOIA plaintiffs" but is "inherent in those FOIA cases where an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." Second, the court was willing to treat the surprisingly terse Bender declaration as adequate—not because it was sufficient standing alone, but because it could be understood as a confirmation that nothing had changed since the more detailed Hardy declarations, and because "the breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation" made plausible its representation that the investigation remained ongoing.

## Summary

Deference here means the court gives the FBI's submissions real weight given its institutional expertise, but the court still independently verifies that the statutory requirements are met. The Campbell nexus requirement remains in force. National security heightens the deference through both institutional-competence and separation-of-powers logic, which practically manifests in greater willingness to rely on ex parte submissions and to accept relatively concise confirmations when the broader record and context support them.
