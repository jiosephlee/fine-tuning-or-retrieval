Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does "to the extent of available appropriations" make statutory obligations discretionary?
Question:
Section 1522(a)(1)(A) says the Director shall provide certain services "to the extent of available appropriations." The majority seems to read this as: the duty is mandatory, but the scope is limited by what Congress has appropriated. Judge Lee reads the whole section as discretionary. But Congress did appropriate over $3 billion for migration and refugee assistance (later reduced by $800 million). If money has been appropriated, doesn't that mean the "extent of available appropriations" has been satisfied and the duty to provide services is triggered? Or can the executive branch still decide not to spend appropriated funds? I'm confused about how appropriations interact with mandatory spending statutes.
Answer:
## Answer

Your confusion is understandable because the opinion (which is a stay order, not a full merits opinion) presents two competing readings of the same statutory language without fully resolving them. Here is how to think about the issue.

### "To the extent of available appropriations" is a scope limitation, not a discretion grant

The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" is a standard fiscal qualifier. It does not, by itself, convert a mandatory duty into a discretionary one. Rather, it limits the *scope* of an otherwise mandatory obligation: the agency must act, but only up to the amount Congress has actually funded. If Congress appropriates zero dollars, the duty may be unenforceable for lack of funding. If Congress appropriates funds, the duty is triggered and the agency must provide the services described, up to the amount available.

That is how the majority reads § 1522(a)(1)(A). The majority focuses on the word "shall" and treats "to the extent of available appropriations" as capping the government's obligation, not giving it a veto. The opinion states directly: "Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government *must* provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission into the United States." It then notes that Congress appropriated over $3 billion for migration and refugee assistance (later reduced by $800 million), and concludes that the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services. In the majority's framework, once appropriations exist, the mandatory duty is operative and the executive cannot simply decline to carry it out.

### Judge Lee's competing structural argument

Judge Lee does not dispute that funds have been appropriated. His argument is that the *statute itself* never created a mandatory duty in the first place—so the availability of appropriations is irrelevant. He makes three structural points:

1. **The section title says "Authorization."** Section 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee reads "authorization" as permissive, citing Black's Law Dictionary for the proposition that authorization means "permission to do something."

2. **The funding subsections use "authorized."** The specific provisions in §§ 1522(b)–(e) that actually provide money for services say the "Director *is authorized* to provide assistance." Judge Lee reads these as discretionary spending grants.

3. **The "shall" in § 1522(a) is conditional.** Section 1522(a) is titled "Conditions and consideration" and begins with the phrase "In providing assistance under this section." Judge Lee argues that this means the "shall" language in subsection (a) imposes mandatory *conditions* on the government's spending choices, but only *if* the government decides to spend. In other words, if the executive chooses to fund refugee programs, it must do so in a way that satisfies the conditions in subsection (a)—but nothing requires the executive to fund those programs at all.

Judge Lee also addresses the majority's reliance on §§ 1522(d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which use "shall" but also contain qualifying language—"if necessary" and "as needed"—that he reads as preserving executive discretion.

### Answering your specific question

Your question is: if money has been appropriated, doesn't the duty trigger?

Under the **majority's reading**, yes. The "to the extent of available appropriations" language means the duty is mandatory but bounded by the appropriation amount. Congress appropriated funds, so the duty is operative and the executive must provide the statutorily required services. The executive cannot decline to spend appropriated funds that Congress has designated for this mandatory purpose.

Under **Judge Lee's reading**, no. The appropriation is beside the point because § 1522 is an authorization statute, not a mandate. Congress gave the executive permission to spend money on refugee services, but did not require it to do so. The existence of an appropriation means the executive *may* spend the funds, but the executive retains discretion not to.

The disagreement, then, is not really about whether the executive can refuse to spend appropriated funds in the abstract. It is about whether § 1522 ever imposed a mandatory spending duty in the first place. The majority says the "shall" in § 1522(a), combined with the existence of appropriations, creates an enforceable obligation. Judge Lee says the overall structure of § 1522—title, funding subsections, and the conditional framing of subsection (a)—shows that Congress authorized but did not compel the executive to act.

### A note on what the opinion does not address

This is a stay order, not a full opinion. The majority explicitly says "an opinion on the merits will issue in due course" and that its reasoning is explained "in full" in that forthcoming opinion. The order resolves the stay question—which requires only a likelihood of success on the merits, not a final determination—and directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements in the interim. The full opinion will need to address the statutory interpretation question more completely, including the tension between the "shall" language in § 1522(a) and the "authorized" language in the funding subsections.

In summary: "to the extent of available appropriations" does not inherently make a statutory obligation discretionary. It limits the scope of a mandatory duty by the amount of funds Congress has provided. Whether § 1522 creates such a duty in the first place is the real dispute between the majority and Judge Lee, and it turns on the broader statutory structure, not on the appropriations clause alone.
