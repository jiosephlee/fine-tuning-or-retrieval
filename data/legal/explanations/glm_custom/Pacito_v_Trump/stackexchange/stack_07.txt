Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Can the government unilaterally terminate cooperative agreements entered into under statutory authority?
Question:
The State Department terminated all cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations in February 2025, and the court now orders them reinstated to the extent necessary to provide § 1522 services. But what are the legal terms of a "cooperative agreement"? Is it a contract that the government can terminate at will, or are there legal constraints on termination? If Congress appropriated money for refugee assistance and the government entered into cooperative agreements to fulfill that purpose, does the government need APA-compliant reasoning to terminate those agreements, or can it simply walk away? I'm not clear on the legal status of cooperative agreements versus contracts or grants.
Answer:
## Cooperative Agreements Are Statutory Funding Mechanisms Subject to APA Review

The opinion doesn't provide a detailed exposition of the legal nature of cooperative agreements, but it tells us enough to answer your core question. Here's what the court's reasoning establishes:

### What the Statute Authorizes

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), the government may provide reception and placement services "through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The opinion treats cooperative agreements as one of three interchangeable funding mechanisms Congress authorized for delivering statutorily required services. The court does not distinguish cooperative agreements from contracts or grants for purposes of its analysis—it focuses instead on whether the underlying statutory obligation to provide services constrains the government's ability to terminate the agreements.

### The Constraint on Termination Is Statutory, Not Contractual

The key move in the majority's reasoning is this: the government's ability to walk away from these cooperative agreements is constrained by whether § 1522 creates a mandatory obligation to provide the services those agreements were funding. The court frames the issue as an APA challenge, stating that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA."

The majority reads § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations. It highlights that § 1522(a)(1)(A) uses "shall" and requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, "to the extent of available appropriations," to provide specific services: employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and equal access for women. The court also cites § 1522(d)(2)(B), which provides that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minor children when necessary, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), which states that the Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for cash and medical support. Because Congress appropriated funds for FY 2025 (reduced but not eliminated by the Rescissions Act of 2025), the "extent of available appropriations" condition was satisfied.

Given these statutory mandates, the court reasons that the government cannot simply terminate the cooperative agreements through which those services are delivered. The termination is agency action reviewable under the APA, and if § 1522 requires the services, then terminating the agreements that provide them would be "not in accordance with law" under 5 U.S.C. § 706. That is the APA-compliant reasoning problem you're asking about: the government needs a lawful basis for the termination, and the court finds it likely lacks one.

### Judge Lee's Contrary Reading

Judge Lee's partial dissent directly contests the majority's characterization of § 1522 as mandatory. He argues that the statute's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—signals permission, not obligation. He reads the word "shall" in § 1522(a) as imposing conditions *only if* the government chooses to fund programs under the discretionary provisions of §§ 1522(b)–(e), which use "authorized to" language. On this view, the government has discretion over whether to operate the programs at all, and the APA imposes no constraint on termination because no statutory duty is being violated.

### The Bottom Line

The majority treats the termination of cooperative agreements as agency action subject to APA review. The legal constraint on termination is not the terms of the cooperative agreements themselves but the underlying statutory obligations in § 1522. If those obligations are mandatory (as the majority holds they likely are), the government cannot terminate the agreements without violating the APA because doing it would unlawfully frustrate Congress's statutory mandate. The court therefore orders reinstatement of the cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

Whether this reasoning survives the merits depends on which reading of § 1522—"authorization" or "mandate"—the full opinion ultimately adopts. That is the central legal dispute this case turns on.
