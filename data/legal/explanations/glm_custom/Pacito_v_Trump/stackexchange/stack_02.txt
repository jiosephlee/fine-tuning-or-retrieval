Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How does the Nken stay factors analysis work when the court barely discusses irreparable harm?
Question:
The opinion cites Nken v. Holder for the four-factor stay test and says the first two factors (likelihood of success and irreparable injury) are "most critical." But after spending several paragraphs on likelihood of success regarding the EO, the court doesn't really analyze irreparable harm to either side—it just says the remaining factors "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests" favor the United States because of the President's "broad discretion." Is this a proper application of the Nken test? Can the court essentially collapse factors 2-4 into a single cursory statement about deference to the executive?
Answer:
The short answer is that the court's treatment is terse and arguably inconsistent with *Nken*'s own guidance, but it is not necessarily reversible error given the interim nature of the order and the immigration context. Here is why.

## What *Nken* Requires

Under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009), a stay applicant must satisfy a four-factor test: (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) irreparable injury to the applicant absent a stay, (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties, and (4) the public interest. The Supreme Court described the first two factors as "the most critical." But "most critical" is not the same as "exclusive." *Nken* presents the four factors as a holistic inquiry, and the Court did not hold that the remaining factors can be ignored or absorbed into the first.

## What the Court Did Here

The court's stay analysis proceeds in two steps.

First, it devotes several paragraphs to factor 1, concluding that the government is likely to succeed on the plaintiffs' challenge to EO 14163's suspension of refugee admissions. It relies heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

Second, it disposes of factors 2–4 in a single sentence: "The remaining factors, touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President, *Hawaii*, 585 U.S. at 683–84, favor the United States as well."

## Why This Raises Concerns

**The court does not separately analyze irreparable harm, despite calling it "critical."** *Nken* says irreparable injury to the applicant is one of the two most critical factors. The court here does not identify what irreparable harm the government suffers from living with the injunction pending appeal. It does not explain why the inability to implement the President's policy during the appeal constitutes irreparable (as opposed to merely delayable) injury. If factor 2 is "most critical," one would expect more than its subsumption into a sentence about "domestic and foreign interests."

**The court uses the same reasoning for all four factors.** The likelihood-of-success analysis turns on the breadth of § 1182(f) and deference to the President's finding. The remaining-factors analysis turns on the same "broad discretion." This risks collapsing the four-factor test into a single inquiry: if executive deference resolves every factor, the test becomes redundant. The four factors exist precisely to force courts to consider distinct questions—what happens to the applicant, what happens to the opposing parties, and what serves the public—rather than treating one rationale as dispositive of everything.

**Factors 2, 3, and 4 are analytically distinct.** Irreparable harm to the government (factor 2) asks what injury the executive branch suffers from the injunction remaining in place. Substantial injury to the plaintiffs (factor 3) asks what harm refugees and resettlement organizations suffer from having the injunction stayed—e.g., refugees denied entry, organizations losing funding, services interrupted. The public interest (factor 4) asks about broader societal concerns. The court's single sentence about "domestic and foreign interests" does not clearly address all three, and notably does not grapple with the plaintiffs' harm at all.

## Why It May Still Be Defensible

Several contextual factors mitigate these concerns.

**This is an interim order, not a final disposition.** The court explicitly states at the outset: "An opinion will issue in due course." Interim stay orders commonly provide condensed reasoning, with fuller analysis reserved for the merits opinion. The court is making a preliminary assessment to maintain the status quo during the appeal, not issuing a final ruling on the stay factors.

**The court does make a finding on factors 2–4; it just does so briefly.** The court does not ignore the remaining factors entirely—it explicitly concludes they "favor the United States." The deficiency is one of explanation, not of omission.

**In immigration cases, the public interest often aligns with executive discretion.** *Trump v. Hawaii* itself reflects the principle that the President's assessment of domestic and foreign interests carries substantial weight in defining the public interest in immigration matters. The court's reliance on *Hawaii* for the proposition that the President exercises "broad discretion" over these interests is consistent with that precedent, even if the application is cursory.

**Strong showings on factor 1 often reduce the burden on the other factors.** While *Nken* does not explicitly adopt a "sliding scale," courts in practice often abbreviate their analysis of the remaining factors when the applicant makes a strong showing on likelihood of success. The court's extensive analysis of the government's likelihood of success on the EO challenge may explain its brevity on the other factors.

## Bottom Line

The court's treatment is arguably deficient under *Nken*'s own statement that irreparable injury is one of the two "most critical" factors. A more careful application would have separately addressed what irreparable harm the government faces absent a stay, what harm the plaintiffs face from a stay, and how the public interest balances—rather than disposing of all three in a single sentence about presidential discretion. But because this is an interim order in a context of significant executive deference, the approach—while terse—is not necessarily reversible error. The fuller opinion that the court says will follow may provide the more detailed analysis that *Nken* contemplates.
