Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 1: # Background and Procedural Posture

## Executive Order No. 14163: Suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program

The dispute at the center of *Pacito v. Trump* began on January 20, 2025, when President Trump signed Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The order invoked two provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) as its statutory authority: 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a). Section 1182(f) is the broader of the two provisions, and it occupies a central role throughout this litigation. It provides that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." In other words, § 1182(f) delegates to the President sweeping authority to suspend the entry of noncitizens whenever the President makes a "finding" that their entry would be "detrimental" to the nation's interests. The Supreme Court has described this statute as one that "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [INA]." *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018).

Relying on this authority, Executive Order No. 14163 stated that the President "determine[d] that entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended" pending further findings. The order thus operated as a complete moratorium on the admission of refugees under USRAP. This suspension of admissions is one of the two core governmental actions that the plaintiffs challenged.

## Executive Order No. 14169: Suspension of USRAP Funding

Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for USRAP. The State Department purported to act pursuant to a separate executive order—"Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," Executive Order No. 14169—rather than under Executive Order No. 14163 itself. The funding suspension was broad: it encompassed not only overseas processing of refugee applications but also domestic resettlement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. These domestic services—often called "reception and placement" services—include employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, medical assistance, and specialized support for unaccompanied refugee minors. Congress has authorized these services through 8 U.S.C. § 1522, a provision of the Refugee Act of 1980, and has historically funded them through cooperative agreements between the State Department and private nonprofit resettlement organizations.

This distinction between the two executive orders and the two categories of governmental action—suspension of admissions versus suspension of funding—is critical for understanding the Ninth Circuit's eventual ruling. While the court would find that the President likely possessed authority under § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, it reached a different conclusion regarding the termination of funding for resettlement services, which it analyzed under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

## The Plaintiffs and Their Claims

The plaintiffs in this case fall into two categories. The first group consists of individual refugees—some already admitted to the United States, others approved for resettlement but still outside the country. The second group comprises three nonprofit organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide overseas processing for refugee applicants and domestic resettlement services for admitted refugees. These cooperative agreements are the primary mechanism through which the federal government has historically delivered reception and placement services to refugees, as Congress authorized in 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii).

In February 2025, the plaintiffs filed suit in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington. They advanced two principal legal claims. First, they argued that Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102, which is codified in various provisions of the INA at 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq. The plaintiffs characterized the Refugee Act as establishing "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons," quoting *Negusie v. Holder*, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009). The essence of this claim was that the Refugee Act, as a more specific statute, constrained the broad authority the President claimed under § 1182(f).

Second, the plaintiffs argued that the defunding of USRAP violated various provisions of the APA, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. APA claims typically challenge agency actions as arbitrary and capricious, contrary to law, or in excess of statutory authority. Here, the plaintiffs contended that the termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of resettlement funding violated the statutory mandates Congress imposed in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the plaintiffs read as requiring the government to provide certain reception and placement services to admitted refugees. This APA-based challenge to the funding suspension would prove to be the more successful of the two claims before the Ninth Circuit.

## The District Court's Preliminary Injunctions

The district court moved quickly to provide relief. On February 25, 2025, it orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. A preliminary injunction is a court order that maintains the status quo pending the resolution of the case on the merits. It does not represent a final decision on the legal claims; rather, it provides interim relief to prevent irreparable harm while the litigation proceeds. To obtain a preliminary injunction, a plaintiff typically must demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm absent the injunction, that the balance of equities favors the plaintiff, and that the injunction serves the public interest.

The government's response to the oral injunction was swift. On February 26—the day after the oral injunction—the government terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States and all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad. This action effectively cut off the funding and infrastructure through which resettlement services were delivered, prompting the plaintiffs to return to the district court.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order to define the scope of its February 25 oral injunction. The court specified that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding, thereby bringing the funding terminations within the protective scope of the preliminary injunction. This February 28 written order would become one of the two injunctions at issue in the appeal.

On March 24, 2025, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction. This order directed the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. The purpose of this second injunction was to ensure that the resettlement infrastructure—comprising the nonprofit organizations that actually delivered services to refugees—remained functional while the case proceeded. The March 24 injunction would become the second of the two injunctions at issue in the appeal.

The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, challenging both injunctions. These appeals brought the case to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit.

## The March 25 Stay and the Limited Carveout

Once the appeals were filed, the government sought a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A stay pending appeal is an order by the appellate court that suspends the effect of the lower court's order while the appeal is resolved. The Supreme Court has identified four factors that courts consider when deciding whether to grant a stay: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The first two factors—the likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are considered "the most critical." *Id.*

A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part on March 25, 2025. The panel quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with broad authority to impose entry restrictions. The panel concluded that the government was likely to succeed on the merits of the challenge to Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions, and it stayed the district court's injunction insofar as it prohibited that suspension.

However, the panel denied the stay "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the panel called a "limited carveout from the stay": the stay went into effect for the broad injunction, but the injunction remained in force for one specific category of refugees—those who had received conditional approval before the executive order was issued. The rationale for this carveout, as the panel saw it, was that these individuals had progressed far enough in the refugee admission process that their equitable interests warranted continued protection pending appeal.

The motions panel directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar, setting the case on a path toward oral argument.

## Clarifying Orders, the Enforcement Framework, and the Administrative Stay

The "limited carveout" proved difficult to administer in practice, leading to a series of further proceedings. The Ninth Circuit issued two clarifying orders—on April 21 and May 9, 2025—to define the scope of the carveout more precisely. These orders illustrate a common feature of complex injunctions: when courts create exceptions or carveouts, the parties often need judicial clarification to determine exactly who is covered and what obligations the government retains.

Meanwhile, the district court remained involved in enforcing its own orders. On July 14, 2025, the district court issued an order establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee the framework. This order reflected the district court's effort to ensure that refugees within the carveout actually received the benefits the injunction preserved for them. The appointment of a magistrate judge to oversee compliance is a procedural tool sometimes used when ongoing supervision of a complex injunction is needed.

The government responded to the July 14 enforcement order by filing an emergency motion with the Ninth Circuit on July 17, 2025. The motion asked the court to enforce, clarify, or amend its stay order—essentially arguing that the district court's enforcement framework exceeded the scope of what the Ninth Circuit had permitted under the carveout. The plaintiffs opposed the motion.

Before the Ninth Circuit could resolve the emergency motion, the court issued an administrative stay on July 18, 2025. An administrative stay is a temporary measure that preserves the status quo while the court considers the pending motion. It is typically issued without full briefing or argument and is intended to provide short-term stability until the court can rule. The July 18 administrative stay temporarily halted the district court's July 14 enforcement order while the panel considered the government's emergency motion.

## Expedited Oral Argument and the Procedural Context for the Interim Order

The Ninth Circuit scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis, which the panel conducted on September 3, 2025. Expedited treatment is commonly used in cases involving time-sensitive government policies or where ongoing harm to the parties requires prompt resolution. At oral argument, the panel heard from both sides on the merits of the stay questions, including the government's likelihood of success on the plaintiffs' claims under both the Refugee Act and the APA.

A notable moment came during oral argument when the government revealed that it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services." This admission proved significant. The government had previously represented to the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency." The gap between that representation and the uncertainty expressed at oral argument would factor into the panel's decision to require reinstatement of cooperative agreements for services to already-admitted refugees.

Following oral argument, the case was "submitted for decision," meaning that the panel had received all briefing and argument necessary to decide the case. However, rather than issuing a full opinion immediately, the panel issued the September 12, 2025 order that is the subject of this casebook chapter. The order was explicitly described as an interim measure: "An opinion on the merits will issue in due course." The panel vacated both its prior administrative stay of July 18 and its prior stay of March 25 (as amended on April 21 and May 9), and issued a new stay pending final resolution of the appeal. This procedural posture—an interim order that resolves the stay questions while a full merits opinion is pending—is important because it means the September 12 order does not represent a final decision on the merits. It resolves only the question of what relief should be in place while the appeal proceeds, though the panel's assessment of the parties' likelihood of success offers a strong signal of how the court is likely to rule when it issues its full opinion.
