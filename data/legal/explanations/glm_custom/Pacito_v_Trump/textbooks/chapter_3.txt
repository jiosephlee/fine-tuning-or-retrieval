Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 3: # The Standard for a Stay Pending Appeal and Its Application to the Refugee Admission Suspension

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test for Stays Pending Appeal

When a party seeks to pause the effect of a lower court's order while an appeal is pending, the party is seeking what is known as a "stay pending appeal." A stay is an interim form of relief: it does not decide the merits of the appeal but instead preserves the status quo (or a particular version of it) while the appellate court prepares its decision. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the government asked the Ninth Circuit to stay the district court's preliminary injunctions, which had blocked enforcement of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions and required reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugee resettlement services.

The governing standard for evaluating a stay motion comes from the Supreme Court's decision in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009). Under *Nken*, a court evaluates four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits of the appeal; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. These factors are not rigid mathematical formulas but rather interrelated considerations that the court weighs together in the exercise of its discretion. The court's task is to assess the balance of equities and the likelihood that the applicant's legal position will ultimately prevail.

The *Pacito* panel applied this framework to determine whether to continue or modify the stays it had previously issued. The procedural history is important here. A prior motions panel of the Ninth Circuit had granted a partial stay of the district court's February 28 preliminary injunction on March 25, 2025, but had carved out protection for individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. The panel had issued clarifying orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025. Then, after the district court issued a July 14 enforcement order, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of that order on July 18, 2025. The September 12 Order at issue here vacates both the administrative stay and the prior March 25 stay (as amended), and replaces them with a new stay pending final resolution of the appeal.

## The Primacy of the First Two Factors: Likelihood of Success on the Merits and Irreparable Injury

Although *Nken* lists four factors, the Supreme Court has emphasized that the first two—"the showing of success on the merits and irreparable injury"—are "the most critical." This means that a court evaluating a stay motion will focus most of its analytical energy on whether the applicant is likely to win on appeal and whether the applicant faces irreparable harm if the stay is denied. The remaining two factors—harm to other parties and the public interest—serve as important but secondary considerations that can tip the balance in closer cases.

This hierarchy of factors has practical significance. An applicant that cannot make a strong showing of likely success on the merits will generally not obtain a stay, regardless of how compelling the other factors might be. Conversely, a strong showing on the merits can sometimes compensate for a less compelling showing of irreparable injury. The *Pacito* panel's analysis reflects this structure: the bulk of its reasoning addresses the government's likelihood of success on the merits, with the remaining factors treated more summarily.

## Application of the Likelihood-of-Success Factor to the Challenge to EO 14163's Suspension of Refugee Admissions

The central legal question on the merits of the admission suspension challenge is whether Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) is lawful. The plaintiffs argued that the suspension violated the Refugee Act of 1980, which was enacted to establish "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons," as the Supreme Court described it in *Negusie v. Holder*, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009). The plaintiffs' theory was that Congress, through the Refugee Act, had set up a system for refugee admissions that the President could not simply override by executive order.

The panel majority concluded that the government was likely to prevail on this challenge. Its reasoning rested on the breadth of the President's statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), as interpreted by the Supreme Court in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). The panel explained that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." This quotation from *Hawaii* signals that the panel viewed the President's authority under § 1182(f) as sweeping and largely insulated from searching judicial review.

The practical effect of this analysis is significant. If the President's power under § 1182(f) is as broad as *Hawaii* suggests, then the Refugee Act's framework for refugee admissions does not constrain the President's ability to suspend the entry of refugees as a class of aliens. The plaintiffs' argument that the Refugee Act created an entitlement to refugee admission that the President could not override was thus likely to fail. The panel's conclusion on this point was sufficiently firm that it stated, without qualification, that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions."

## Why § 1182(f)'s Text Is "Clear" and the Executive Order "Does Not Exceed Any Textual Limit on the President's Authority"

Section 1182(f) provides, in relevant part, that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The panel's analysis of this provision focused on its text and structure.

The panel, drawing directly on *Hawaii*, described the language of § 1182(f) as "clear." This characterization is legally significant. When a court says that a statute's text is "clear," it means that the statute unambiguously confers the authority in question, and there is no need to consult legislative history, statutory purpose, or other interpretive tools to resolve ambiguity. The clarity of the text forecloses arguments that the statute should be read more narrowly.

The panel then applied *Hawaii*'s holding that the executive order at issue in that case (and, by extension, Executive Order No. 14163 here) "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." This phrasing is crucial. It acknowledges that the President's power under § 1182(f) is not unlimited—it must operate within the "textual limits" of the statute—but holds that the statute's text imposes only a minimal constraint. The statute authorizes the President to suspend the entry of "any class of aliens," and refugees admitted under USRAP constitute such a class. The statute permits the President to act "for such period as he shall deem necessary," meaning the duration of the suspension is within the President's discretion. And the statute allows the President to impose "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate," giving the President broad latitude in shaping the scope of the suspension.

The plaintiffs' counterargument—that the Refugee Act of 1980 imposed additional, more specific constraints on the President's authority over refugee admissions—was effectively subordinated to the broader authority conferred by § 1182(f). The panel's reasoning suggests that § 1182(f) operates as a general grant of authority that is not displaced by more specific provisions relating to refugee admissions, at least insofar as the President is exercising the power to suspend entry.

## Why the President's Finding of Detrimentality Satisfies the Sole Statutory Prerequisite

One of the most striking aspects of the panel's analysis is its treatment of the statutory prerequisite for exercising authority under § 1182(f). The panel, again quoting *Hawaii*, stated that "[t]he sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" The word "sole" is doing significant analytical work here. It means that the statute requires only one thing: a presidential finding of detrimentality. No other procedural or substantive precondition must be met.

The panel then concluded that "[t]he President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here." Executive Order No. 14163, § 3(a) contains the President's determination that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [USRAP] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." This express finding in the text of the executive order satisfied the statutory prerequisite.

Equally important is what the panel said about the nature of judicial review of this finding. The panel stated that courts "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" This quotation from *Hawaii* establishes a highly deferential standard of review. The court will not second-guess the President's assessment of whether refugee admissions are detrimental to the national interest. The court will not evaluate the quality of the evidence supporting the finding. The court will not weigh the finding against contrary evidence or alternative assessments. The court's role is limited to verifying that the President made the finding; the substance of the finding is beyond judicial scrutiny.

This deferential posture has profound implications for the plaintiffs' case. Any argument that the President's finding of detrimentality was poorly reasoned, unsupported by evidence, or motivated by improper purposes would face a nearly insurmountable barrier under the panel's reading of *Hawaii*. The court's inquiry is essentially formal: Did the President make the requisite finding? If yes, the statutory prerequisite is satisfied.

## How the Remaining Factors Favor the Government

Having concluded that the government made a strong showing of likely success on the merits of the admission suspension issue, the panel turned to the remaining stay factors. Its treatment of these factors was brief but instructive.

With respect to irreparable injury, the panel did not engage in an extended analysis of the specific harms the government might suffer absent a stay. Instead, it referenced the "broad discretion" conferred on the President in matters touching upon "both domestic and foreign interests of the United States," as determined by the President himself under the authority of § 1182(f). This framing effectively treats the government's inability to implement its chosen policy as a form of irreparable injury: when the President has determined that a particular immigration policy is necessary to protect the national interest, being prevented from implementing that policy presumptively causes irreparable harm to the government.

The third and fourth factors—harm to other parties and the public interest—were similarly analyzed through the lens of presidential discretion. The panel stated that the remaining factors "favor the United States as well," citing the broad discretion conferred on the President over domestic and foreign affairs as articulated in *Hawaii*. This approach reflects a broader principle in stay analysis involving the federal government: when the government is acting pursuant to statutory authority that confers broad discretion on the executive branch, the balance of equities and the public interest tend to favor allowing the government to implement its chosen policy, at least where the government has shown a strong likelihood of success on the merits.

Notably, the panel did not separately analyze the harms to the plaintiffs—the refugees whose admissions were suspended and the organizations whose cooperative agreements were terminated—in the context of the stay factors as applied to the admission suspension. This reflects the panel's view that the President's authority under § 1182(f) is so broad that the balance of equities necessarily tips in the government's favor when the government is exercising that authority.

## The Panel's Decision to Stay the District Court's Injunctions on the Admission Suspension in Their Entirety

The cumulative effect of the panel's analysis of the four *Nken* factors was a decision to stay the district court's preliminary injunctions in their entirety with respect to the admission suspension issue. This means that Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions could go into effect while the appeal was pending. The district court's February 28 order, which had prohibited enforcement of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of the executive order, was stayed. The district court's March 24 order, which had required reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements, was also stayed.

This stay replaced the prior partial stay issued on March 25, which had included a "limited carveout" protecting individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. By vacating the March 25 stay (as amended on April 21 and May 9) and the July 18 administrative stay, and by issuing a new stay that did not include the carveout, the panel effectively allowed the government to suspend refugee admissions for all categories of refugees, including those who had been conditionally approved before the executive order was issued.

It is important to understand what the panel did and did not decide. The panel did not decide the merits of the plaintiffs' challenge to the executive order. It did not hold that the executive order is lawful. It did not hold that the Refugee Act imposes no constraints on the President's authority. It held only that the government was likely to succeed on the merits—an assessment sufficient for stay purposes but not a final determination. The panel expressly stated that "[a]n opinion on the merits will issue in due course." The stay is thus an interim measure, designed to maintain a particular status quo while the court prepares its substantive ruling.

The one exception to the complete stay concerned the APA challenge to the defunding of resettlement services. The panel concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' APA claims, based on the mandatory service provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Accordingly, the panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This partial ruling reflects the panel's separation of two distinct legal issues: the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions (where the government likely prevails) and the statutory obligation to provide resettlement services to already-admitted refugees (where the plaintiffs likely prevail on at least one claim).

Judge Lee's partial dissent underscores the contested nature of this second issue. Judge Lee read § 1522 as permissive rather than mandatory, emphasizing the word "authorization" in the section's title and the repeated use of "authorized" in the funding provisions. This interpretive disagreement highlights the complexity of the statutory framework and foreshadows the detailed analysis that will appear in the court's merits opinion.
