Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) give the President unlimited power to suspend refugee admissions despite the Refugee Act of 1980?
Question:
The opinion cites Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and allows him to suspend entry of any class of aliens. But I thought the Refugee Act of 1980 was meant to create a comprehensive framework for refugee admissions, including a statutory target number of refugees each year. How can the President simply override that entire statutory scheme by making a "finding" of detrimentality? Is there no limit on how long the President can suspend admissions, or does the Refugee Act impose any constraints that § 1182(f) cannot override? I'm confused about how these two statutes interact.
Answer:
## How § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act Interact

You've identified a genuine tension, but the opinion resolves it by drawing a critical distinction between **the power to suspend entry** and **the obligation to serve those already admitted**. Let me walk through the opinion's reasoning.

### The President's Authority to Suspend Admissions Under § 1182(f)

On the suspension of refugee admissions itself, the panel concludes that § 1182(f) gives the President broad authority that is **not constrained by the Refugee Act's framework**. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court emphasizes that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." The court treats the Refugee Act's admissions framework—annual targets, processing procedures, and the like—as operating alongside § 1182(f), not as cabin it.

The panel makes clear that the **sole statutory prerequisite** for exercising § 1182(f) authority is a presidential "finding" that entry of the relevant class of aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." Once that finding is made—and the court says the President "has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here"—the President may suspend entry "for such period as he shall deem necessary." The opinion does not identify any temporal limit on the suspension, nor does it suggest the Refugee Act's annual admission targets constrain the President's § 1182(f) power. The court explicitly states that it "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'"

So to answer your question directly: **as a matter of this panel's reasoning, yes—§ 1182(f) permits the President to override the Refugee Act's admissions framework by making a finding of detrimentality, and the opinion identifies no limit on the duration of such a suspension.**

### Where the Refugee Act Still Binds: Post-Admission Services

The opinion does, however, find that the Refugee Act constrains executive action in a different domain: **services to refugees already admitted to the United States.** This is the key nuance.

The panel concludes that the government is **not likely to prevail** on the plaintiffs' APA challenge regarding the defunding of reception and placement services mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The court reads § 1522 as requiring the government to provide certain services—employment training, English language training, cash assistance, and care for unaccompanied minor refugees—to refugees who have already been admitted. The court notes that these services "may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies," and orders the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide them.

In other words, the opinion draws a line:

- **§ 1182(f) governs entry.** The President's authority to suspend the entry of new refugees is broad, requires only a detrimentality finding, and is not constrained by the Refugee Act's admissions targets or procedures.
- **§ 1522 governs post-admission services.** Once refugees are admitted, the government has statutory obligations to provide reception and placement services that it cannot simply abandon.

### The Dissent's Counter

Judge Lee's partial dissent argues that even this line is too generous to the plaintiffs. He reads § 1522 as **permissive, not mandatory**, emphasizing that the statute's title uses the word "Authorization" and that the operative provisions state the Director "is authorized" to provide services. In his view, the word "shall" in § 1522(a) merely imposes conditions on the government *if* it chooses to fund programs—it does not require that those programs operate at all. This is the interpretive dispute to watch as the merits opinion develops.

### Summary

The opinion treats § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act as operating in different spheres. Section 1182(f) gives the President broad, largely unreviewable authority to suspend the **entry** of any class of aliens, including refugees, for any period he deems necessary. The Refugee Act's framework for admissions does not constrain that power. But the Refugee Act's service provisions in § 1522 may impose **post-admission obligations** that survive the President's suspension of new entries—though whether those provisions are truly mandatory is contested by the dissent. The panel's stay reflects this split: it allows the suspension of admissions to proceed while requiring the government to maintain services for refugees already admitted.

### How does the Nken stay factors analysis work when the court barely discusses irreparable harm?
Question:
The opinion cites Nken v. Holder for the four-factor stay test and says the first two factors (likelihood of success and irreparable injury) are "most critical." But after spending several paragraphs on likelihood of success regarding the EO, the court doesn't really analyze irreparable harm to either side—it just says the remaining factors "touching upon both domestic and foreign interests" favor the United States because of the President's "broad discretion." Is this a proper application of the Nken test? Can the court essentially collapse factors 2-4 into a single cursory statement about deference to the executive?
Answer:
The short answer is that the court's treatment is terse and arguably inconsistent with *Nken*'s own guidance, but it is not necessarily reversible error given the interim nature of the order and the immigration context. Here is why.

## What *Nken* Requires

Under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009), a stay applicant must satisfy a four-factor test: (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) irreparable injury to the applicant absent a stay, (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties, and (4) the public interest. The Supreme Court described the first two factors as "the most critical." But "most critical" is not the same as "exclusive." *Nken* presents the four factors as a holistic inquiry, and the Court did not hold that the remaining factors can be ignored or absorbed into the first.

## What the Court Did Here

The court's stay analysis proceeds in two steps.

First, it devotes several paragraphs to factor 1, concluding that the government is likely to succeed on the plaintiffs' challenge to EO 14163's suspension of refugee admissions. It relies heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and that courts cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

Second, it disposes of factors 2–4 in a single sentence: "The remaining factors, touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, as determined by the 'broad discretion' conferred on the President, *Hawaii*, 585 U.S. at 683–84, favor the United States as well."

## Why This Raises Concerns

**The court does not separately analyze irreparable harm, despite calling it "critical."** *Nken* says irreparable injury to the applicant is one of the two most critical factors. The court here does not identify what irreparable harm the government suffers from living with the injunction pending appeal. It does not explain why the inability to implement the President's policy during the appeal constitutes irreparable (as opposed to merely delayable) injury. If factor 2 is "most critical," one would expect more than its subsumption into a sentence about "domestic and foreign interests."

**The court uses the same reasoning for all four factors.** The likelihood-of-success analysis turns on the breadth of § 1182(f) and deference to the President's finding. The remaining-factors analysis turns on the same "broad discretion." This risks collapsing the four-factor test into a single inquiry: if executive deference resolves every factor, the test becomes redundant. The four factors exist precisely to force courts to consider distinct questions—what happens to the applicant, what happens to the opposing parties, and what serves the public—rather than treating one rationale as dispositive of everything.

**Factors 2, 3, and 4 are analytically distinct.** Irreparable harm to the government (factor 2) asks what injury the executive branch suffers from the injunction remaining in place. Substantial injury to the plaintiffs (factor 3) asks what harm refugees and resettlement organizations suffer from having the injunction stayed—e.g., refugees denied entry, organizations losing funding, services interrupted. The public interest (factor 4) asks about broader societal concerns. The court's single sentence about "domestic and foreign interests" does not clearly address all three, and notably does not grapple with the plaintiffs' harm at all.

## Why It May Still Be Defensible

Several contextual factors mitigate these concerns.

**This is an interim order, not a final disposition.** The court explicitly states at the outset: "An opinion will issue in due course." Interim stay orders commonly provide condensed reasoning, with fuller analysis reserved for the merits opinion. The court is making a preliminary assessment to maintain the status quo during the appeal, not issuing a final ruling on the stay factors.

**The court does make a finding on factors 2–4; it just does so briefly.** The court does not ignore the remaining factors entirely—it explicitly concludes they "favor the United States." The deficiency is one of explanation, not of omission.

**In immigration cases, the public interest often aligns with executive discretion.** *Trump v. Hawaii* itself reflects the principle that the President's assessment of domestic and foreign interests carries substantial weight in defining the public interest in immigration matters. The court's reliance on *Hawaii* for the proposition that the President exercises "broad discretion" over these interests is consistent with that precedent, even if the application is cursory.

**Strong showings on factor 1 often reduce the burden on the other factors.** While *Nken* does not explicitly adopt a "sliding scale," courts in practice often abbreviate their analysis of the remaining factors when the applicant makes a strong showing on likelihood of success. The court's extensive analysis of the government's likelihood of success on the EO challenge may explain its brevity on the other factors.

## Bottom Line

The court's treatment is arguably deficient under *Nken*'s own statement that irreparable injury is one of the two "most critical" factors. A more careful application would have separately addressed what irreparable harm the government faces absent a stay, what harm the plaintiffs face from a stay, and how the public interest balances—rather than disposing of all three in a single sentence about presidential discretion. But because this is an interim order in a context of significant executive deference, the approach—while terse—is not necessarily reversible error. The fuller opinion that the court says will follow may provide the more detailed analysis that *Nken* contemplates.

### When does "shall" create a mandatory duty versus a discretionary authorization in federal statutes?
Question:
I'm struggling with the disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee's dissent on 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The statute is titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement" but uses the word "shall" in subsection (a) and elsewhere. Judge Lee says the title and the word "authorized" in subsections (b)-(e) make the whole thing discretionary, while the majority reads "shall" as creating mandatory obligations. How do courts typically resolve this tension? Does the statute's title control over the operative text? And what about the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations"—does that make the duty contingent on funding being available, or does it just acknowledge the Appropriations Clause limitation?
Answer:
## Answer

The short answer: courts treat "shall" as presumptively mandatory, and statutory titles do not override operative text. The majority's approach in *Pacito* follows well-established interpretive principles; Judge Lee's reliance on the title and the "authorized" language in subsections (b)–(e) represents the weaker position under standard canons of statutory construction.

### Titles and headings are not controlling

Judge Lee places significant weight on § 1522's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—arguing that "authorization" signals permission rather than command. But the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that statutory titles and section headings are not part of the operative text and cannot control over the actual language Congress used. *Bhd. of R.R. Trainmen v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 331 U.S. 519, 529 (1947) ("The heading of a section is no part of the text"); *Almendarez-Torres v. United States*, 523 U.S. 224, 234 (1998). Titles are organizational tools; they help orient the reader but do not supply substantive commands or restrict operative text. So the title's use of "authorization" cannot neutralize the word "shall" in subsection (a).

### "Shall" is presumptively mandatory; "authorized" describes mechanism, not discretion

The word "shall" carries a presumption of mandatory obligation. *Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26, 35 (1998); *Gutierrez de Martinez v. Lamagno*, 515 U.S. 417, 432 n.9 (1995) ("'Shall' generally connotes a mandate"). The burden falls on the party arguing that "shall" is merely precatory or directory to show that surrounding context displaces the ordinary presumption.

The majority reads § 1522(a)(1)(A) as imposing mandatory conditions: the Director "shall" ensure that certain services—employment training, English language training, cash assistance, and equal opportunity for women—are made available. Judge Lee counters that this "shall" language is qualified by the introductory phrase "In providing assistance under this section," which he reads as making the conditions contingent on the government first choosing to act under the discretionary provisions in subsections (b)–(e).

The majority's reading is the stronger one. Subsections (b)–(e) use "authorized" to describe the *mechanisms* by which services are delivered—contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. The word "authorized" tells us *how* the government may provide assistance; it does not say *whether* the government must ensure that assistance is provided. In other words, "authorized" grants the government latitude in choosing delivery methods, while the "shall" in subsection (a) and in specific provisions like (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) imposes the underlying duty to ensure the services exist. Reading "authorized" as making the entire program optional would render the "shall" language in subsection (a) and elsewhere superfluary—a result courts generally avoid. *See TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001) ("It is a cardinal principle of statutory construction that a statute ought, upon the whole, to be so construed that, if it can be prevented, no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant.").

Judge Lee does have a reasonable point that subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) contain qualifying language—"if necessary" and "as needed"—that injects executive discretion into the *scope* of particular obligations. The majority acknowledges this by ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements only for refugees "who have been admitted to the United States" and only for "the usual and customary services" previously provided—not for every conceivable service the statute mentions. But the existence of discretion over *how much* or *in what form* assistance is provided does not eliminate the duty to provide assistance *at all*.

### "To the extent of available appropriations" is a funding limitation, not a discretion grant

The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" does not convert a mandatory duty into a discretionary one. It is a standard fiscal qualification that acknowledges the Appropriations Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 9, cl. 7, which prohibits government spending absent a congressional appropriation. The phrase operates as a ceiling on the *amount* of the obligation, not an on-off switch for the *existence* of the obligation.

This distinction matters because it determines what happens when Congress has actually appropriated funds. Here, the majority notes that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025 (later reduced by $800 million). Because appropriated funds exist, the duty is operative to that extent. The government cannot invoke "to the extent of available appropriations" as a blanket excuse to provide zero services when Congress has funded the program. The phrase would only excuse non-performance if Congress had appropriated nothing—and even then, the better reading is that the duty is suspended for lack of funding, not extinguished.

Contrast this with language like "may, subject to the availability of appropriations," which would more naturally create discretionary authority contingent on funding. § 1522(a) uses "shall" qualified by available appropriations, which signals that the government *must* spend available funds on the specified services, not that it *may* choose to do so.

### How the disagreement resolves

The majority applies the standard interpretive hierarchy: operative "shall" language controls over a statutory title, "authorized" provisions describe permissible mechanisms rather than discretionary triggers, and "to the extent of available appropriations" limits the fiscal scope of a mandatory duty without eliminating it. Judge Lee's dissent inverts this hierarchy by treating the title and the "authorized" language in subsections (b)–(e) as overriding the "shall" in subsection (a). Under ordinary canons, the majority has the better of the argument.

### What APA violation supports the court's refusal to stay the injunction on resettlement services?
Question:
The opinion mentions the plaintiffs brought claims "under various provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act" regarding the defunding of USRAP, and the court says the government is "not likely to prevail on at least one" APA challenge. But the opinion doesn't specify which APA section is at issue or what the alleged violation is. Is this an arbitrary and capricious challenge under § 706(2)(A) to the termination of cooperative agreements? Or could it be something else, like agency action "not in accordance with law" under § 706(2)(C) because the government failed to fulfill a statutory mandate under § 1522? What's the most likely APA theory here?
Answer:
# The most likely APA theory is § 706(2)(C): agency action "not in accordance with law"

The opinion's reasoning points to a challenge under **5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(C)**—agency action "not in accordance with law"—rather than an arbitrary and capricious challenge under § 706(2)(A). Here's why:

## The court's reasoning is statutory, not procedural

The panel's analysis is structured around interpreting 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and whether it imposes a **mandatory duty** on the government to provide reception and placement services. The court emphasizes the mandatory language throughout the statute:

- § 1522(a)(1)(A) "requires the Director" to provide certain services
- § 1522(d)(2)(B) states "the Director **shall** assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minors
- § 1522(e)(7)(A) states "the Secretary **shall** develop and implement" support projects
- Services "may be provided through" cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)

The court then notes that the government terminated the cooperative agreements—the statutory mechanism for providing these services—and, critically, that the government "was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies." The court's concern is not that the termination decision was poorly reasoned (which would be the hallmark of arbitrary and capricious review), but that the government **cannot demonstrate it is fulfilling its statutory obligation**.

## The dissent confirms this framing

Judge Lee's partial dissent is telling. She does not argue that the government provided an inadequate explanation for its decision (an arbitrary-and-capricious-type objection). Instead, she disputes the **statutory interpretation** itself, arguing that § 1522 is permissive ("Authorization for programs"), not mandatory. Her entire dissent is a debate about whether the statute creates a duty the government can violate—which is the core question in a § 706(2)(C) "not in accordance with law" challenge.

If the operative theory were arbitrary and capricious review under § 706(2)(A), we would expect both the majority and the dissent to discuss whether the agency examined the relevant data, articulated a satisfactory explanation, or considered alternatives. Neither does. The disagreement is purely about **what the statute requires**.

## Why not § 706(2)(A)?

An arbitrary and capricious challenge would focus on the *process* of the termination decision—whether the State Department offered a reasoned explanation, considered reliance interests of the cooperative agreement holders, or engaged in reasoned decisionmaking. Nothing in the opinion discusses the adequacy of the agency's explanation. The court doesn't quote any agency reasoning, doesn't discuss the administrative record, and doesn't apply the "reasoned decisionmaking" framework from *State Farm* or *Fox Television*.

Instead, the opinion treats the termination of cooperative agreements as problematic **because it leaves the government without a mechanism to fulfill a statutory mandate**, not because the decision to terminate was itself unreasoned.

## Bottom line

The court's holding is that the government is "not likely to prevail" because its actions conflict with a statutory mandate under § 1522. That is a textbook § 706(2)(C) claim: agency action "not in accordance with law." The opinion signals that the full merits opinion will explain this in detail, but the structure of the analysis—statutory interpretation of mandatory language, the government's inability to show compliance, and the dissent's counter-argument about the statute being merely permissive—strongly indicates the APA theory is "not in accordance with law," not arbitrary and capricious.

### Why did the Ninth Circuit vacate its prior stays rather than simply modify them?
Question:
The court vacates its administrative stay of July 18, its prior stay of March 25 (as amended), and then issues a new stay pending appeal. I'm confused about the procedural significance of "vacating" a stay versus "modifying" one. Does vacating the prior stays change the legal posture in a meaningful way, or is this just housekeeping? And what's the difference between the "administrative stay" of July 18 and the substantive stay of March 25—are they treated differently procedurally?
Answer:
## Why the Court Vacated Rather Than Modified

The short answer is that the new stay order has materially different terms from the prior stays, and vacating the old stays eliminates any ambiguity about which provisions remain operative.

### The new stay replaces the old carveout with a different one

The March 25 stay (as amended on April 21 and May 9) created a "limited carveout" preserving the district court's injunction "to the extent [it] applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by [USCIS] before January 20, 2025." That carveout concerned **admissions**—specific refugees who had cleared a threshold approval step before the executive order issued.

The September 12 order eliminates that carveout. It stays the district court's injunctions "in their entirety, except as noted below"—and the new exception is categorically different. It concerns not admissions but **services**: the court directs the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been **admitted** to the United States." So the protected class shifts from conditionally-approved-but-not-yet-admitted refugees to already-admitted refugees, and the subject matter shifts from entry to post-entry resettlement funding.

If the court had simply "modified" the March 25 stay, a litigant could argue that the old carveout (conditionally approved individuals) survived alongside the new one (services for admitted refugees). By vacating the prior stays wholesale and issuing a single new stay with its own self-contained exceptions, the panel forecloses that argument. The only surviving protections are those expressly stated in the September 12 order.

### The procedural posture has changed

The March 25 stay was issued by a motions panel before full briefing and argument. The September 12 order issues after expedited oral argument, with the panel now in a position to assess the merits more concretely. The order reflects a bifurcated merits assessment that the prior stays did not: the government is likely to prevail on the § 1182(f) challenge to the executive order's suspension of admissions, but is **not** likely to prevail on the APA challenge to defunding reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. A fresh stay order captures that refined analysis more cleanly than serial modifications to a pre-argument order would.

### Administrative stay vs. stay pending appeal

The July 18 administrative stay was a temporary measure preserving the status quo while the panel considered the government's emergency motion regarding the district court's July 14 enforcement order. An administrative stay is issued without full analysis of the *Nken* factors; its purpose is simply to hold things in place pending consideration of whether a more durable stay is warranted.

The March 25 stay, by contrast, was a stay pending appeal—an order assessing (at least implicitly) the four *Nken* factors and intended to remain in effect through the appeal. The September 12 order is likewise a stay pending appeal, but one issued after full briefing and argument, with explicit *Nken* analysis.

Procedurally, vacating the administrative stay is straightforward: it was always meant to be temporary, and it is superseded by the new stay pending appeal. Vacating the March 25 stay serves the different function described above—replacing an interim stay with a considered one on different terms.

### Practical effect of the vacatur

The vacatur also moots the government's July 17 emergency motion, which the court denies as moot. That motion sought to enforce, clarify, or amend the March 25 stay in light of the district court's July 14 enforcement order. Once the panel vacates both the March 25 stay and the July 18 administrative stay and replaces them with a new comprehensive stay, there is no longer any live dispute about the scope or enforcement of the prior stays.

### Summary

The vacatur is not mere housekeeping. It resets the terms of the stay to reflect the panel's post-argument assessment of the merits, replaces the prior admissions-related carveout with a new services-related carveout, and eliminates potential confusion about which of the accumulated stay orders and amendments remain in force. The new order is the single operative stay, and its exceptions are the only exceptions.

### Why does the court distinguish between refugee admissions and services for already-admitted refugees?
Question:
The court stays the injunction on the refugee admission suspension (favoring the government) but refuses to stay the injunction requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for reception and placement services to refugees already admitted (favoring the plaintiffs). I understand the practical distinction—entry vs. post-entry services—but I'm confused about the legal basis for treating them differently. If § 1182(f) gives the President broad discretion over immigration, why doesn't that same deference extend to decisions about funding services for refugees who are already here? Is there a statutory or constitutional principle that limits executive discretion once a refugee has been admitted?
Answer:
The short answer is that **different statutes govern different stages of the refugee process**, and the broad deference the President enjoys under § 1182(f) does not automatically carry over to a separate statutory scheme that Congress enacted specifically to govern post-admission services.

**Entry is governed by § 1182(f).** That statute gives the President authority to suspend the *entry* of any class of aliens he finds detrimental to the United States. As the Ninth Circuit notes, quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, this provision "exudes deference" to the executive and sets only one prerequisite: that the President make the requisite finding. 585 U.S. at 684–85. The court applies that standard straightforwardly to the admission moratorium, concluding the government is likely to succeed on the merits.

**Post-entry services are governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1522**, part of the Refugee Act of 1980. This statute addresses what the government owes refugees *after* admission—employment training, English language instruction, cash and medical assistance, and care for unaccompanied minors. The court reads several provisions of § 1522 as mandatory: subsection (a)(1)(A) states the Director "shall" make available specified resources; (d)(2)(B) provides the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minors "if necessary"; and (e)(7)(A) states the Secretary "shall develop and implement" alternative support projects. The court also notes that § 1522 expressly envisions services being delivered through cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies—exactly the agreements the government terminated.

The legal principle at work is straightforward statutory interpretation: **when Congress enacts a specific statute imposing obligations on the executive, the executive cannot avoid those obligations by invoking a different, broader statute that grants discretionary authority over a different subject matter.** Section 1182(f) speaks to *entry*; § 1522 speaks to *treatment after entry*. The deference owed under the former does not dissolve the duties imposed by the latter.

This is also why the court finds the government's practical position problematic. At oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement service providers. Given that § 1522 appears to mandate these services and Congress has appropriated funds for them, the court declines to stay the injunction requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for already-admitted refugees.

Judge Lee's partial dissent is worth noting because it highlights the interpretive dispute at the heart of the case. He argues § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory, pointing to the statute's title ("Authorization for programs…"), the "is authorized" language in subsections (b)–(e), and the qualifying phrase in subsection (a) that conditions its "shall" language on the government having first chosen to fund programs under those subsections. On his reading, the mandatory-sounding language in (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A) only triggers *if* the government opts to provide services—imposing conditions on voluntary action rather than compelling the action itself.

So the split boils down to this: the majority reads § 1522 as creating enforceable statutory obligations for already-admitted refugees, distinct from the President's § 1182(f) admission authority. Judge Lee reads § 1522 as a grant of discretionary authority no different in kind from § 1182(f), and therefore concludes the court has no basis to mandate services the statute does not require.

### Can the government unilaterally terminate cooperative agreements entered into under statutory authority?
Question:
The State Department terminated all cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations in February 2025, and the court now orders them reinstated to the extent necessary to provide § 1522 services. But what are the legal terms of a "cooperative agreement"? Is it a contract that the government can terminate at will, or are there legal constraints on termination? If Congress appropriated money for refugee assistance and the government entered into cooperative agreements to fulfill that purpose, does the government need APA-compliant reasoning to terminate those agreements, or can it simply walk away? I'm not clear on the legal status of cooperative agreements versus contracts or grants.
Answer:
## Cooperative Agreements Are Statutory Funding Mechanisms Subject to APA Review

The opinion doesn't provide a detailed exposition of the legal nature of cooperative agreements, but it tells us enough to answer your core question. Here's what the court's reasoning establishes:

### What the Statute Authorizes

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), the government may provide reception and placement services "through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The opinion treats cooperative agreements as one of three interchangeable funding mechanisms Congress authorized for delivering statutorily required services. The court does not distinguish cooperative agreements from contracts or grants for purposes of its analysis—it focuses instead on whether the underlying statutory obligation to provide services constrains the government's ability to terminate the agreements.

### The Constraint on Termination Is Statutory, Not Contractual

The key move in the majority's reasoning is this: the government's ability to walk away from these cooperative agreements is constrained by whether § 1522 creates a mandatory obligation to provide the services those agreements were funding. The court frames the issue as an APA challenge, stating that "the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA."

The majority reads § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations. It highlights that § 1522(a)(1)(A) uses "shall" and requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, "to the extent of available appropriations," to provide specific services: employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and equal access for women. The court also cites § 1522(d)(2)(B), which provides that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minor children when necessary, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), which states that the Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for cash and medical support. Because Congress appropriated funds for FY 2025 (reduced but not eliminated by the Rescissions Act of 2025), the "extent of available appropriations" condition was satisfied.

Given these statutory mandates, the court reasons that the government cannot simply terminate the cooperative agreements through which those services are delivered. The termination is agency action reviewable under the APA, and if § 1522 requires the services, then terminating the agreements that provide them would be "not in accordance with law" under 5 U.S.C. § 706. That is the APA-compliant reasoning problem you're asking about: the government needs a lawful basis for the termination, and the court finds it likely lacks one.

### Judge Lee's Contrary Reading

Judge Lee's partial dissent directly contests the majority's characterization of § 1522 as mandatory. He argues that the statute's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—signals permission, not obligation. He reads the word "shall" in § 1522(a) as imposing conditions *only if* the government chooses to fund programs under the discretionary provisions of §§ 1522(b)–(e), which use "authorized to" language. On this view, the government has discretion over whether to operate the programs at all, and the APA imposes no constraint on termination because no statutory duty is being violated.

### The Bottom Line

The majority treats the termination of cooperative agreements as agency action subject to APA review. The legal constraint on termination is not the terms of the cooperative agreements themselves but the underlying statutory obligations in § 1522. If those obligations are mandatory (as the majority holds they likely are), the government cannot terminate the agreements without violating the APA because doing it would unlawfully frustrate Congress's statutory mandate. The court therefore orders reinstatement of the cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

Whether this reasoning survives the merits depends on which reading of § 1522—"authorization" or "mandate"—the full opinion ultimately adopts. That is the central legal dispute this case turns on.

### Does "to the extent of available appropriations" make statutory obligations discretionary?
Question:
Section 1522(a)(1)(A) says the Director shall provide certain services "to the extent of available appropriations." The majority seems to read this as: the duty is mandatory, but the scope is limited by what Congress has appropriated. Judge Lee reads the whole section as discretionary. But Congress did appropriate over $3 billion for migration and refugee assistance (later reduced by $800 million). If money has been appropriated, doesn't that mean the "extent of available appropriations" has been satisfied and the duty to provide services is triggered? Or can the executive branch still decide not to spend appropriated funds? I'm confused about how appropriations interact with mandatory spending statutes.
Answer:
## Answer

Your confusion is understandable because the opinion (which is a stay order, not a full merits opinion) presents two competing readings of the same statutory language without fully resolving them. Here is how to think about the issue.

### "To the extent of available appropriations" is a scope limitation, not a discretion grant

The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" is a standard fiscal qualifier. It does not, by itself, convert a mandatory duty into a discretionary one. Rather, it limits the *scope* of an otherwise mandatory obligation: the agency must act, but only up to the amount Congress has actually funded. If Congress appropriates zero dollars, the duty may be unenforceable for lack of funding. If Congress appropriates funds, the duty is triggered and the agency must provide the services described, up to the amount available.

That is how the majority reads § 1522(a)(1)(A). The majority focuses on the word "shall" and treats "to the extent of available appropriations" as capping the government's obligation, not giving it a veto. The opinion states directly: "Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government *must* provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission into the United States." It then notes that Congress appropriated over $3 billion for migration and refugee assistance (later reduced by $800 million), and concludes that the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services. In the majority's framework, once appropriations exist, the mandatory duty is operative and the executive cannot simply decline to carry it out.

### Judge Lee's competing structural argument

Judge Lee does not dispute that funds have been appropriated. His argument is that the *statute itself* never created a mandatory duty in the first place—so the availability of appropriations is irrelevant. He makes three structural points:

1. **The section title says "Authorization."** Section 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Judge Lee reads "authorization" as permissive, citing Black's Law Dictionary for the proposition that authorization means "permission to do something."

2. **The funding subsections use "authorized."** The specific provisions in §§ 1522(b)–(e) that actually provide money for services say the "Director *is authorized* to provide assistance." Judge Lee reads these as discretionary spending grants.

3. **The "shall" in § 1522(a) is conditional.** Section 1522(a) is titled "Conditions and consideration" and begins with the phrase "In providing assistance under this section." Judge Lee argues that this means the "shall" language in subsection (a) imposes mandatory *conditions* on the government's spending choices, but only *if* the government decides to spend. In other words, if the executive chooses to fund refugee programs, it must do so in a way that satisfies the conditions in subsection (a)—but nothing requires the executive to fund those programs at all.

Judge Lee also addresses the majority's reliance on §§ 1522(d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which use "shall" but also contain qualifying language—"if necessary" and "as needed"—that he reads as preserving executive discretion.

### Answering your specific question

Your question is: if money has been appropriated, doesn't the duty trigger?

Under the **majority's reading**, yes. The "to the extent of available appropriations" language means the duty is mandatory but bounded by the appropriation amount. Congress appropriated funds, so the duty is operative and the executive must provide the statutorily required services. The executive cannot decline to spend appropriated funds that Congress has designated for this mandatory purpose.

Under **Judge Lee's reading**, no. The appropriation is beside the point because § 1522 is an authorization statute, not a mandate. Congress gave the executive permission to spend money on refugee services, but did not require it to do so. The existence of an appropriation means the executive *may* spend the funds, but the executive retains discretion not to.

The disagreement, then, is not really about whether the executive can refuse to spend appropriated funds in the abstract. It is about whether § 1522 ever imposed a mandatory spending duty in the first place. The majority says the "shall" in § 1522(a), combined with the existence of appropriations, creates an enforceable obligation. Judge Lee says the overall structure of § 1522—title, funding subsections, and the conditional framing of subsection (a)—shows that Congress authorized but did not compel the executive to act.

### A note on what the opinion does not address

This is a stay order, not a full opinion. The majority explicitly says "an opinion on the merits will issue in due course" and that its reasoning is explained "in full" in that forthcoming opinion. The order resolves the stay question—which requires only a likelihood of success on the merits, not a final determination—and directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements in the interim. The full opinion will need to address the statutory interpretation question more completely, including the tension between the "shall" language in § 1522(a) and the "authorized" language in the funding subsections.

In summary: "to the extent of available appropriations" does not inherently make a statutory obligation discretionary. It limits the scope of a mandatory duty by the amount of funds Congress has provided. Whether § 1522 creates such a duty in the first place is the real dispute between the majority and Judge Lee, and it turns on the broader statutory structure, not on the appropriations clause alone.
