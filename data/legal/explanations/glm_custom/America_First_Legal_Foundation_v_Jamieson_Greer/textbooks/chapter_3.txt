Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # Article III Standing Doctrine and the Procedural Injury Framework

## Article III's Case-or-Controversy Requirement and the Three Elements of Standing

Article III of the United States Constitution limits the federal judicial power to resolving certain "Cases" and "Controversies." This seemingly simple phrase has given rise to the entire doctrine of standing—a judicially created set of requirements that determine who may properly bring suit in federal court. As the D.C. Circuit explains in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, standing doctrine flows directly from this case-or-controversy requirement. The doctrine serves as a constitutional threshold: before a federal court may reach the merits of a dispute, the plaintiff must demonstrate that the dispute is of the kind that Article III permits the judiciary to resolve.

The standing inquiry has three core elements. To have Article III standing, a plaintiff must show that it has suffered an injury that is caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. These three elements—injury in fact, causation, and redressability—operate as a unified test. A plaintiff must satisfy all three to establish standing. Failure on any one element is fatal to the court's subject-matter jurisdiction. This is why the court in *Greer* addresses standing before reaching the merits of AFL's claims, even though neither party initially raised the issue. Standing is not a waivable defense; it is a constitutional prerequisite that courts must address sua sponte when in doubt.

The three elements serve distinct functions. The injury requirement ensures that the plaintiff has a real stake in the dispute, rather than a generalized grievance shared by the public at large. The causation requirement ensures that the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct, so that the court is not being asked to remedy harm caused by someone who is not before it. And the redressability requirement ensures that a favorable judicial decision would actually alleviate the plaintiff's injury, so that the court is not issuing an advisory opinion with no practical effect on the parties. In *Greer*, the court's analysis focuses heavily on causation and redressability, because AFL's asserted injuries depend on a long and speculative chain of events involving actors not before the court.

## Injury in Fact: Concrete, Particularized, and Actual or Imminent

The first element of standing—injury in fact—has its own internal set of requirements. The injury must be "an invasion of a legally protected interest" that is both "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical." Each of these terms carries specific doctrinal weight.

A concrete injury is one that is real and tangible, as opposed to abstract. The concreteness inquiry asks whether the plaintiff has suffered a harm that is sufficiently material in a way that the law has historically recognized. A particularized injury is one that affects the plaintiff in a personal and individual way, as opposed to a harm shared equally by the public at large. A generalized grievance—a harm suffered by every citizen in the same degree—does not satisfy the particularization requirement. The actual-or-imminent requirement ensures that the plaintiff is not seeking relief for a harm that has already occurred and passed, nor for a harm that is merely speculative and may never come to pass. The injury must be either presently existing or so likely to occur that it presents a real and immediate threat.

In *Greer*, AFL advances two theories of injury, both of which the court rejects. AFL's first theory is that OSC's failure to investigate has prevented AFL from receiving the records it requested under FOIA from the Department of Justice. AFL's second theory is that OSC's refusal to investigate has deprived AFL of the notices and information that would have been available had OSC conducted an investigation under section 1214. The court analyzes both theories under the framework for procedural injuries, to which we now turn.

## The Standard for Standing at the Motion-to-Dismiss Stage

The point in the litigation at which standing is evaluated matters. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court takes the plaintiff's factual allegations as true and asks whether those allegations plausibly support each element of standing. As the D.C. Circuit states in *Greer*, citing *Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack*, "a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of its standing." This standard mirrors the familiar plausibility standard from *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* and *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, but applied specifically to the constitutional standing inquiry.

This means that a plaintiff cannot simply recite the elements of standing in conclusory fashion. The complaint must contain factual allegations that, if accepted as true, make it plausible that the plaintiff has suffered a concrete and particularized injury, that the defendant caused that injury, and that a favorable judicial decision would likely redress it. At later stages of litigation—summary judgment and trial—the plaintiff must support each element of standing with evidence, not merely allegations. But in *Greer*, the case was decided at the motion-to-dismiss stage, so the court evaluated whether AFL's complaint plausibly alleged standing.

## The Distinction Between Procedural and Substantive Rights for Standing Purposes

Not all statutory rights create the same kind of standing analysis. A substantive right gives a plaintiff a direct entitlement to some benefit or outcome—such as the right to receive a government benefit, the right to be free from discrimination, or the right to access certain information. Violation of a substantive right can, in appropriate circumstances, itself constitute a concrete injury for Article III purposes.

A procedural right, by contrast, is a right to have the government follow certain procedures before taking action. Examples include the right to notice and comment in administrative rulemaking, the right to an environmental impact statement, or—as AFL argues in *Greer*—the right to have an agency conduct an investigation. A procedural right does not entitle the plaintiff to any particular outcome; it merely entitles the plaintiff to a particular process. This distinction matters because the Supreme Court has held that the deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone, does not always constitute a cognizable Article III injury.

In *Greer*, AFL argues that OSC violated its procedural right to an investigation under section 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC "shall conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. The court treats this as an asserted procedural right. The court notes that it need not decide whether AFL's asserted injuries are procedural or substantive, because AFL fails to satisfy even the relaxed standing requirements that apply to procedural injuries. This analytical move—considering AFL's claims under the more lenient procedural-injury framework and finding them insufficient—is significant. It means that the court gives AFL the benefit of the more relaxed standard and still finds standing lacking.

## The Prohibition on Procedural Rights in Vacuo

The central principle governing procedural injuries is this: a procedural right in vacuo is insufficient to confer Article III standing. The Supreme Court made this clear in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, where it held that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer standing. In other words, a plaintiff cannot sue solely because the government failed to follow a required procedure. The plaintiff must also show that the procedural failure harmed, or threatens to harm, some concrete, particularized interest of the plaintiff.

This principle prevents plaintiffs from suing over procedural violations that cause them no real harm. It ensures that courts are not transformed into general-purpose overseers of government compliance with procedural rules. A plaintiff who complains that an agency skipped a required step must also explain what concrete harm the plaintiff suffers because the step was skipped.

In *Greer*, AFL's assertion that OSC violated its procedural right to an investigation under section 1216 falls squarely within this prohibition. The court quotes *Summers* directly and cites the D.C. Circuit's own decision in *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau* for the same proposition. The problem for AFL is that section 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, does not by itself create an entitlement to any information or outcome. AFL has a procedural right—if it has any right at all—to an investigation. But it cannot identify a concrete interest affected by the denial of that investigation, at least not one that satisfies causation and redressability.

## The Relaxed Standing Framework for Procedural Injuries

Although a procedural right alone is insufficient for standing, the law recognizes that requiring plaintiffs alleging procedural injuries to satisfy the full rigor of the ordinary standing test would often be impossible. This is because procedural violations frequently make it difficult to predict what would have happened if the procedure had been followed, which in turn makes it difficult to show imminence of harm and redressability through a judicial order.

To address this difficulty, the D.C. Circuit applies a relaxed standing framework for cases involving alleged procedural injury. As the court explains, quoting *Center for Law and Education v. Department of Education*: "Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation." This framework thus relaxes two of the three standing elements but leaves the other two intact.

The relaxation of imminence means that a plaintiff alleging a procedural violation need not show that the threatened harm is as immediately impending as would otherwise be required. The relaxation of redressability means that the plaintiff need not show that a favorable judicial decision would as certainly or directly redress the injury as would be required in an ordinary case. But the plaintiff must still show a concrete and particularized injury in fact, and must still show that the defendant's procedural violation caused injury to that concrete interest.

The rationale for this partial relaxation is straightforward. When a statute gives a plaintiff a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest, and the government violates that procedural right, the plaintiff should not be denied access to court simply because the procedural violation makes the chain of causation and the prospect of redress somewhat uncertain. The procedural violation itself may have prevented the plaintiff from learning whether and how the concrete interest was harmed. But the relaxation is not unlimited. The plaintiff must still point to a concrete interest that the procedural right was designed to protect, and must still show that the violation of the procedural right caused injury to that interest.

## The Requirement That Plaintiffs Show the Procedural Violation Resulted in Injury to a Concrete, Particularized Interest

The final and crucial step in the procedural-injury standing analysis is the requirement that the plaintiff show that the procedural violation "resulted in injury" to some "concrete, particularized interest." This requirement bridges the gap between the abstract procedural violation and the concrete injury that Article III demands. Without this bridge, the procedural violation remains a right in vacuo, insufficient for standing.

In *Greer*, AFL attempts to build this bridge with two theories, both of which the court rejects. AFL's first theory connects the OSC's failure to investigate to AFL's interest in obtaining the records it requested from DOJ under FOIA. But the court finds that this theory suffers from fatal causation and redressability problems. The injury—failure to receive FOIA records—is traceable to DOJ, not to OSC. And the causal chain connecting OSC's inaction to DOJ's production of records is too speculative: it requires speculation that OSC would have pursued corrective or disciplinary action, that the Merit Systems Protection Board would have sanctioned DOJ employees, and that DOJ would then have produced the records. This chain involves the choices of multiple actors not before the court, and the court cannot rely on such speculation to establish causation or redressability.

AFL's second theory connects OSC's failure to investigate to AFL's interest in receiving the notices and information that section 1214 requires during investigations of prohibited personnel practices. The theory is that if OSC had conducted the section 1216 investigation, it might have applied the section 1214 notice requirements and provided AFL with information about the investigation. But the court rejects this theory as well. Even if section 1216 requires an investigation, it creates no entitlement to receive information. And AFL does not contend that OSC must apply section 1214's notice requirements to section 1216 investigations; indeed, AFL acknowledges authority holding that OSC's decision whether to follow section 1214 procedures in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without an entitlement to receive information, AFL has no concrete injury. And without any requirement that OSC provide such information, a judicial order compelling the investigation would not be likely to redress the asserted informational injury.

The court's analysis thus illustrates the practical operation of the procedural-injury framework. AFL receives the benefit of the relaxed standards for imminence and redressability. But it still must show a concrete, particularized interest that the procedural right was designed to protect, and it still must show causation. On both theories, AFL cannot make these showings. Its first theory fails because the causal chain is too attenuated and speculative, involving independent choices by actors not before the court. Its second theory fails because the statute creates no entitlement to the information AFL claims it was denied. The result is that AFL's procedural right—assuming one exists—remains a right in vacuo, unconnected to any concrete interest that Article III recognizes as a cognizable injury.

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, the court does not reach the merits of the dispute. The court vacates the district court's judgment and remands with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This disposition underscores a fundamental principle of federal courts: standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite. Without it, the court has no constitutional authority to decide the case, no matter how meritorious the plaintiff's claims might otherwise be.
