Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights in Vacuo: AFL v. Greer and the Limits of Procedural Standing

When does a statutory right to have the government investigate your complaint become a judicially cognizable injury? The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* offers a sharp reminder that the answer is not always—and perhaps not often—when the statute creates only a procedural entitlement to agency action. The case, decided October 3, 2025, by Judge Katsas, vacates a district court judgment on Article III standing grounds and, in doing so, tightens the doctrinal screws on plaintiffs who would leverage statutory oversight mechanisms into federal lawsuits.

## Why This Case Matters

The case matters because it tests the boundary between statutory entitlements and Article III injury in the context of agency oversight—a boundary that has absorbed considerable judicial attention in recent years. AFL sought to compel the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged DOJ policy of arbitrarily withholding FOIA records. The underlying dispute was real enough: AFL had requested DOJ emails as part of an investigation into judicial recusals, and DOJ's Civil Division reportedly refused to search employee emails without the employees' consent. But AFL's chosen path to redress—suing OSC for failing to investigate that policy under § 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act—required the court to confront a threshold question that neither party had initially raised: Does a plaintiff who demands an agency investigation have standing to compel it?

The D.C. Circuit's answer, grounded in the "procedural right in vacuo" doctrine from *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), is that a statutory procedural right, standing alone, cannot supply Article III standing. Something more is needed—a concrete interest that the procedural right is designed to protect. This principle, though well established, has proven difficult to apply at the margins, and *AFL v. Greer* illustrates its operational force in a setting that might have seemed promising for the plaintiff.

## The Statutory Framework and AFL's Theory

The statutory architecture here is layered. Section 1216(a)(3) directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, under FOIA. Section 1216(c) adds that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Separately, FOIA's own enforcement provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), requires OSC to initiate disciplinary proceedings when a court has ordered production of records, awarded fees, and specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously.

OSC declined to investigate, taking the position that the three predicates under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been satisfied. AFL countered that § 1216 independently required an investigation regardless of whether the FOIA-specific triggers were met. The district court agreed with AFL on the legal question—holding that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC's § 1216 authority—but nonetheless declined to order an investigation, reading § 1216(c) as committing the investigative decision to OSC's discretion. After a remand on which OSC again declined to act, the district court entered final judgment against AFL.

## The Standing Analysis

The D.C. Circuit never reached the merits. Judge Katsas, writing for the panel, framed the standing question around the well-established principle that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient to confer Article III standing." *Summers*, 555 U.S. at 496. This is the doctrinal hinge on which the case turns.

AFL advanced two theories of concrete informational injury, each attempting to tether its § 1216 procedural right to a concrete interest. The court found both wanting, but for instructively different reasons.

The first theory posited that OSC's failure to investigate caused AFL to be denied the FOIA records it had requested from DOJ. The court rejected this on causation and redressability grounds. Any informational injury from DOJ's withholding was "plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." The causal chain AFL proposed—OSC investigates, OSC pursues corrective or disciplinary action, the Merit Systems Protection Board sanctions offending DOJ employees, and then DOJ produces the records—required speculation about the choices of two entities not before the court, plus speculation about how OSC would exercise its enforcement discretion. Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024), the court observed that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." And citing *Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023), the court reminded that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might influence non-parties.

The second theory was more creative. AFL argued that if OSC conducted the § 1216 investigation, AFL might receive the notices and information that § 1214 requires when OSC investigates a "prohibited personnel practice." Because § 1216(c) incorporates § 1214 procedures, AFL reasoned, an OSC investigation would carry procedural entitlements to information. The court dispatched this theory by noting that AFL itself did not contend that OSC *must* apply § 1214 notice requirements to § 1216 investigations. AFL acknowledged—and the Sixth Circuit had held in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)—that OSC's decision to follow § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to receive information, there is no concrete informational injury. And if OSC need not provide § 1214 notices, ordering an investigation would not be likely to redress the asserted information deficit.

## The Procedural Standing Framework in Context

The decision sits comfortably within the D.C. Circuit's evolving procedural standing jurisprudence. The governing framework, drawn from *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education*, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), relaxes—though does not eliminate—the imminence and redressability requirements when a plaintiff alleges the violation of a procedural right designed to protect a threatened concrete interest. But the relaxation is conditional: the plaintiff must still demonstrate that the procedural violation caused injury to a "concrete, particularized interest." The court in *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau*, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), applied this same framework to reject standing where plaintiffs sought to compel the Census Bureau to perform a statutory quality-review procedure, finding no concrete interest affected by the procedural deprivation.

*AFL v. Greer* extends this logic in a significant way. The plaintiff here was not seeking information directly from the defendant agency, as in *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity*. Rather, AFL sought to compel an oversight agency to investigate a *different* agency, with the downstream hope that the oversight process would eventually yield either the original records or procedural information about the investigation itself. The attenuated, multi-link causal chain—OSC to MSPB to DOJ, or OSC investigation to discretionary § 1214 notices—proved too long and too speculative to satisfy even the relaxed requirements.

One notable feature of the opinion is the court's explicit choice not to decide whether AFL's alleged injuries were procedural or substantive. Because AFL could not satisfy even the relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries, the court saw no need to classify the injuries. This is doctrinally tidy—it avoids an unnecessary constitutional question—but it leaves some ambiguity about how courts should characterize statutory rights to agency oversight. If § 1216 creates a mandatory duty to investigate, is the denial of that duty a procedural injury (because it involves a process right) or a substantive injury (because the duty is mandatory and non-discretionary)? The court's avoidance of this question means that a future plaintiff with a stronger concrete interest might still press the classification issue, and the D.C. Circuit has left the door open.

## Downstream Implications

The practical implications for plaintiffs seeking to compel agency oversight are considerable. The decision erects a significant barrier for any litigant who hopes to use statutory oversight provisions as a vehicle for judicial intervention in agency behavior. The key problem is not that such provisions lack legal force—it may well be, as the district court concluded, that § 1216 creates a mandatory duty independent of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—but that the *judicial remedy* for violating that duty is constrained by Article III in ways the statute does not address.

This creates something of an enforcement gap. If OSC has a statutory duty to investigate FOIA violations under § 1216, but no plaintiff has standing to compel that investigation, the duty is, as a practical matter, unenforceable in federal court. Congress may need to build standing into such provisions more deliberately—either by creating explicit information-disclosure entitlements for complainants or by designating specific persons or entities with recognized concrete interests. The D.C. Circuit's approach suggests that without such legislative care, oversight duties will remain largely self-executing, subject only to whatever internal or political accountability mechanisms exist.

The case also illustrates the continuing force of *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), in the oversight context. Even where a statute uses the word "shall," the prospect of compelling an agency's exercise of enforcement discretion through judicial process remains doctrinally fraught. Judge Katsas's citation to *Heckler* signals that the speculative nature of agency enforcement decisions remains a core redressability problem, even when the plaintiff's claim is framed not as a direct request to enforce but as a request to investigate.

Finally, the opinion underscores a structural point about Article III that the Supreme Court has emphasized with increasing clarity: federal courts are not oversight bodies. They resolve concrete disputes between parties with concrete, particularized, and redressable injuries. A statute that creates an oversight duty does not, without more, create a justiciable controversy when that duty goes unfulfilled. Whether that is a feature or a defect of the constitutional design depends on one's perspective, but *AFL v. Greer* confirms that the D.C. Circuit will enforce that design with care.



# The Speculative Chain: Redressability and Multi-Agency Causation in Standing Analysis

When a plaintiff asks a federal court to compel one agency to investigate another, the question of standing often does more work than the merits. The D.C. Circuit's decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a case in point. The court never reached whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) misread its statutory mandate to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Instead, Judge Katsas's opinion for the panel held that AFL lacked Article III standing to demand such an investigation in the first place—and did so by deploying a causal-chain analysis that may reshape how courts evaluate attempts to compel oversight agencies to act.

## The Statutory Framework and AFL's Theory

The case arose from AFL's FOIA request to the Department of Justice for emails between an outside attorney and DOJ's Civil Division. DOJ denied the request on the ground—dubious, AFL maintains—that it does not search employee emails without the employee's consent. AFL sued DOJ under FOIA and simultaneously asked OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged policy of arbitrary withholding under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation" of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. When OSC declined—relying on the narrower enforcement triggers in FOIA's own disciplinary provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—AFL sued to compel the investigation.

The district court agreed with AFL that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC's authority under § 1216, meaning OSC's refusal rested on legal error. But the court declined to order OSC to investigate, reading § 1216(c) as committing that decision to OSC's discretion, and remanded. After OSC again declined, AFL appealed. The D.C. Circuit, raising standing sua sponte, stopped the analysis at the threshold.

## The Causal Chain and the Redressability Problem

AFL's primary standing theory was that OSC's failure to investigate had caused it a concrete informational injury: it still had not received the records it requested from DOJ. The court rejected this theory on both causation and redressability grounds, and the reasoning is worth tracing carefully.

The court framed AFL's theory as resting on the following chain: if OSC conducted the § 1216 investigation, it *might* pursue corrective or disciplinary action before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB); the MSPB *might* then sanction DOJ employees; and DOJ *might* then produce the requested records. The opinion treats each link as a speculative choice by an independent actor not before the court, invoking *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*'s admonition that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." The court further cited *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the principle that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale "might persuade actors who are not before the court."

This is a demanding application of redressability doctrine. The court is not merely saying that AFL's theory is unlikely to succeed; it is saying that the theory depends on a series of discretionary decisions by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ—each of which could break the causal chain at any point. The investigation might produce no findings. OSC might exercise its enforcement discretion not to pursue corrective action. The MSPB might decline to sanction. And DOJ might resist regardless. Under this framework, a plaintiff seeking to compel an investigative agency to act can almost never establish redressability if the hoped-for remedy depends on downstream enforcement by other bodies.

## Heckler v. Chaney in the Standing Inquiry

Perhaps the most analytically interesting move in the opinion is the court's invocation of *Heckler v. Chaney* within the standing analysis. *Heckler* holds that an agency's decision not to initiate enforcement proceedings is presumptively unreviewable under the APA—a merits doctrine about the availability of judicial review. Yet the D.C. Circuit deploys it here not to determine whether AFL's claim is reviewable, but to establish that AFL cannot satisfy the redressability element of standing because "it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel."

This importation of *Heckler* into the jurisdictional inquiry deserves scrutiny. On one view, the move is defensible: if courts generally cannot compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion in particular ways, then a plaintiff's requested remedy—compelling OSC to investigate and potentially prosecute—cannot be said to be "likely" to redress the underlying injury. The logic of *Heckler* informs the redressability calculus because the unavailability of compulsion makes the causal chain speculative.

On another view, however, this conflation of *Heckler* with standing is analytically problematic. Standing is a jurisdictional threshold; *Heckler* is a merits doctrine with a presumption that Congress can override. By importing *Heckler*'s enforcement-discretion principle into Article III, the court effectively transforms a waivable, statute-dependent merits limit into a constitutional jurisdictional bar. A plaintiff who might have argued that § 1216's mandatory "shall" language overrides the *Heckler* presumption on the merits now faces the argument that the same discretion dooms standing before the merits are even reached. The opinion does not fully grapple with this tension. It acknowledges that AFL argues § 1216 *mandates* investigation, but treats OSC's subsequent enforcement choices—whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action—as discretionary in a way that defeats redressability regardless of whether the initial investigation is legally required.

The result is a kind of doctrinal bootstrapping: the very discretion that *Heckler* protects on the merits is used to defeat jurisdiction, which in turn prevents the plaintiff from ever reaching the merits to argue that the statute overrides *Heckler*. Whether one finds this circular or coherent likely depends on whether one views redressability as asking whether a favorable judgment *can* produce the remedy or whether it *likely will*. The court clearly adopts the latter, more demanding formulation.

## The Second Theory and Informational Standing

AFL's fallback theory—that it had an informational interest in the fruits of the OSC investigation itself—failed for a cleaner reason. The court held that even if § 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information. AFL argued that if OSC investigated, it *might* provide AFL with the notices and information required under § 1214's procedures for prohibited personnel practices. But AFL conceded that OSC's decision whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, citing the Sixth Circuit's decision in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*. Without a statutory entitlement to information, there is no concrete informational injury, and without a requirement that OSC provide § 1214 notices during a § 1216 investigation, a court order compelling investigation would not redress the asserted informational harm.

This portion of the opinion is doctrinally straightforward but practically significant. It underscores that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to information, not merely a possibility that an investigation might produce relevant facts. And it closes off a creative workaround by which plaintiffs might manufacture standing by claiming an interest in the procedural outputs of an investigation they seek to compel.

## Broader Implications

The decision fits into a recognizable pattern in recent standing jurisprudence: courts increasingly demand that plaintiffs demonstrate a tight, non-speculative causal connection between the defendant's conduct and the plaintiff's injury, particularly when the requested relief depends on the actions of non-party government actors. *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Brackeen*—both cited here—reflect the Supreme Court's mounting insistence that standing theories cannot rest on assumptions about how independent actors will respond to judicial rulings.

What makes *AFL v. Greer* notable is its application of this framework to the oversight-agency context. Plaintiffs who seek to compel watchdog agencies—OSC, agency Inspectors General, or similar bodies—to investigate other federal actors face a structural redressability problem. The investigating agency's conduct is a necessary but rarely sufficient step toward the plaintiff's ultimate relief. The D.C. Circuit's opinion effectively holds that this multi-link causal chain is too attenuated to satisfy Article III, at least where each link involves the exercise of prosecutorial or enforcement discretion.

The downstream effect is predictable: suits seeking to compel oversight agencies to act will face standing hurdles that may be effectively insurmountable. Litigants who believe an agency has unlawfully withheld FOIA records will be limited to FOIA's own judicial enforcement mechanism—and cannot use the OSC as an intermediary pathway to the same relief. Whether this is a sound structural principle or an unduly cramped reading of Article III depends on one's view of the role of standing doctrine. But the D.C. Circuit has clearly signaled that when a plaintiff's theory requires a court to imagine a sequence of discretionary decisions by multiple federal actors, the Article III door is closed.



## Informational Standing Without an Entitlement: What AFL v. Greer Tells Us About the Boundaries of Informational Injury

Informational injury has become one of the more elastic doctrines in Article III jurisprudence—a doctrine that, depending on one's perspective, either usefully adapts standing principles to statutes that create rights of access to government information, or risks eroding the case-or-controversy requirement by recognizing injuries that are not really injuries at all. The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* offers a useful corrective to the more expansive readings of informational standing, drawing a line that, while narrow, clarifies an important doctrinal boundary: a statutory mandate that an agency investigate something does not, by itself, create an entitlement in the complainant to receive information from that investigation.

The case matters because it addresses a recurring dynamic in administrative law: disappointed FOIA requesters turning to alternative oversight mechanisms when direct judicial enforcement proves unsatisfying. AFL wanted the Office of Special Counsel to investigate what it alleged was an arbitrary DOJ policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent. When OSC declined, AFL sued to compel the investigation. The D.C. Circuit never reached whether OSC's refusal was legally correct. Instead, Judge Katsas, writing for the panel, held that AFL lacked standing to ask the federal courts to weigh in at all.

## The Doctrinal Background: Informational Standing and the Entitlement Requirement

Informational standing, as the Supreme Court has developed it, requires that a plaintiff be denied information that a statute entitles him to receive. The canonical cases are *FEC v. Akins*, where the Federal Election Campaign Act entitled certain persons to receive information about political committees, and *Public Citizen v. DOJ*, where the Federal Advisory Committee Act entitled the public to attend certain meetings. In both cases, the statute created a right to receive information; the denial of that right was itself the injury. The D.C. Circuit has applied this framework in numerous cases, most prominently in *ASPCA v. Feld Entertainment*, which held that the denial of information a statute entitled a plaintiff to receive constitutes a concrete informational injury.

The requirement of a statutory entitlement is doing significant work in this doctrine. It ensures that the plaintiff is not simply a concerned citizen who would like to know more, but rather the holder of a legal right to information that the government has withheld. Without that entitlement, the plaintiff's injury is aesthetic or ideological—not the kind of concrete invasion of a legally protected interest that Article III demands.

## The Court's Reasoning: A Mandate to Investigate Is Not an Entitlement to Inform

AFL's second standing theory pressed hard on this entitlement requirement. AFL argued that section 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act requires OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, and that this statutory mandate translated into an informational injury when OSC refused to investigate. If OSC conducted the investigation, AFL reasoned, it would owe AFL the notices and information that section 1214 requires during investigations of prohibited personnel practices—since section 1216(c) provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214" for FOIA violations.

The court rejected this theory at its foundational step. Even assuming section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate, the statute creates no entitlement for the complainant to receive information. Section 1216 is written as a mandate directed to the agency—it says OSC "shall conduct an investigation"—not as a right conferred on the requester. This is a critical distinction. A statutory command that an agency do something is not the same as a statutory right that a person receive something. The former creates a government obligation; the latter creates a private entitlement. Only the latter can support informational standing.

AFL attempted to bridge this gap by arguing that section 1214's notice requirements would attach to any section 1216 investigation, thereby giving AFL an entitlement to receive information during the investigative process. But AFL effectively conceded the point against itself. It acknowledged that under *Carson v. Office of Special Counsel* (6th Cir. 2011), OSC's decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. If the application of section 1214's notice requirements is discretionary, then there is no entitlement—and without an entitlement, there is no informational injury.

This concession was dispositive in two respects. First, it eliminated the injury element: AFL could not point to any information that a statute entitled it to receive. Second, it eliminated redressability: even if the court ordered OSC to conduct a section 1216 investigation, OSC would retain discretion over whether to provide section 1214 notices, so AFL still might not receive the information it sought.

## Analytical Perspective: The Line Between Process and Substance

The court's reasoning is sound, and it draws a line that the case law has needed for some time. The distinction between a mandate to investigate and an entitlement to information is not merely formalistic. It tracks the underlying logic of the informational standing doctrine, which is rooted in the idea that the plaintiff has suffered the denial of a right that the political branches have chosen to confer. When Congress says an agency "shall investigate," it is establishing a government function, not a private right. When Congress says a person "is entitled to receive" information, it is creating a private right. The standing inquiry must respect that distinction, or else virtually any statutory mandate directed at an agency could be converted into a private right to sue by a plaintiff who claims an interest in the agency's action.

AFL's theory would have substantially expanded informational standing by making any statutory mandate to investigate potentially actionable by any person who triggered the mandate. That is a significant doctrinal shift, and the court was right to resist it. The fact that AFL was the party that prompted the investigation does not give it a judicially cognizable interest in the investigation's fruits unless Congress has said so.

One might argue that the court could have taken a more generous approach to standing while still reaching the same result on the merits. After all, the district court had already concluded that OSC erred in reading section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as limiting its section 1216 authority. A more permissive standing doctrine would have allowed the D.C. Circuit to address that legal question, which has some practical importance for the division of authority between FOIA's own enforcement mechanisms and the Civil Service Reform Act's oversight framework. But the court's choice to resolve the case on standing grounds reflects a deliberate judicial posture: courts should not decide the scope of agency investigative authority at the behest of plaintiffs who have not suffered a judicially cognizable injury, even if the underlying legal question is interesting.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The decision has several implications worth noting.

First, it reinforces the principle that procedural rights do not create standing in a vacuum. The court cited *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* for the proposition that the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete affected interest is insufficient. This is well-established, but *AFL v. Greer* applies it in a context—statutory oversight mechanisms—where the temptation to conflate process with substance is particularly strong. The decision clarifies that a complainant who triggers an agency's investigative process does not, by virtue of that trigger alone, acquire a concrete interest in the investigation's output.

Second, the case has practical implications for FOIA requesters who seek to use OSC as an alternative enforcement tool. The Civil Service Reform Act's section 1216 provides a mechanism for addressing arbitrary FOIA withholdings that is distinct from FOIA's own judicial enforcement provisions. But the decision effectively insulates OSC's exercise of that authority from judicial review at the instance of the requesting party, at least where the requester cannot point to a statutory entitlement to information from the investigation. This does not mean OSC's decisions are wholly unreviewable—other plaintiffs, such as agency employees facing disciplinary action, might have standing in different circumstances. But it does mean that FOIA requesters cannot use section 1216 as a lever to compel additional oversight of agencies that have denied their requests.

Third, the decision may influence how courts treat similar oversight mechanisms in other statutory schemes. Many statutes create agency obligations to investigate, audit, or review government action without conferring rights on the persons who prompt those inquiries. The D.C. Circuit's reasoning suggests that such provisions generally will not support informational standing unless they include an entitlement for the complainant to receive information. This is a meaningful constraint on the use of oversight statutes as vehicles for litigation, and it may channel future challenges toward the direct enforcement mechanisms that Congress has provided—such as FOIA's own judicial review provisions—rather than toward collateral oversight procedures.

Fourth, the court's treatment of the section 1214 notice requirements highlights an asymmetry in the statutory framework that Congress may wish to address. Section 1216(c) allows OSC to investigate FOIA violations "in the same way as" prohibited personnel practices, which might suggest that section 1214's protections for complainants should apply. But the Sixth Circuit in *Carson* and now the D.C. Circuit have treated the application of section 1214 procedures to section 1216 investigations as discretionary. If Congress intends for FOIA complainants to receive notice and information during section 1216 investigations, it could clarify that section 1214's notice requirements apply mandatorily. Until then, the absence of a mandatory notice requirement means that section 1216 investigations do not generate the kind of informational entitlement that standing doctrine requires.

## Conclusion

*AFL v. Greer* is a standing decision that quietly does important doctrinal work. It confirms that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement, not merely a statutory mandate, and it applies that principle to a statutory scheme that creates an oversight mechanism without conferring rights on the persons who trigger it. The decision narrows the path for plaintiffs seeking to convert agency oversight obligations into private causes of action, and it reinforces the D.C. Circuit's insistence that procedural mechanisms, however important, cannot substitute for concrete injury in the Article III inquiry. For FOIA requesters, the message is clear: the primary enforcement mechanism for FOIA violations remains FOIA's own judicial review provision, and collateral oversight statutes are not a straightforward substitute.



# The Merits Question Left Unanswered: § 1216, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), and the Architecture of FOIA Oversight

When a federal appellate court vacates a district court's statutory interpretation ruling on standing grounds, it sometimes leaves behind a legal holding that continues to shape agency behavior—even though no appellate court has endorsed it. The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a textbook example, and the doctrinal consequences may be significant for the future of FOIA oversight.

At the center of the case is a largely overlooked statutory provision—5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)—that requires the Office of Special Counsel to investigate allegations of "arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," that is, under FOIA. The provision sits within the Civil Service Reform Act's framework for addressing federal employee misconduct, and it authorizes OSC to pursue corrective and disciplinary action against employees responsible for improper withholdings. But FOIA itself contains a separate enforcement mechanism: § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides that when a court orders production of improperly withheld records, awards attorneys' fees, and "specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted." That provision establishes three predicates—a court order, a fee award, and a judicial expression of concern—before OSC's disciplinary obligation kicks in.

The question AFL posed was whether OSC can decline to investigate an alleged arbitrary FOIA withholding under § 1216 simply because the three predicates of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) have not been satisfied. AFL argued that § 1216 independently requires OSC to investigate such allegations, regardless of whether a court has already adjudicated a FOIA violation and triggered the disciplinary provisions of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). OSC countered that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) supplies the exclusive enforcement pathway.

The district court agreed with AFL on the statutory interpretation question. In a 2023 decision, Judge Rudolph Contreras held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate under § 1216. The court reasoned that the two provisions operate in different statutory frameworks, address different stages of the enforcement process, and serve different purposes. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) governs post-litigation discipline; § 1216 governs OSC's independent investigative authority. Having reached that conclusion, however, the district court then held that § 1216(c)'s reference to seeking corrective action "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved" committed the decision whether to investigate to OSC's discretion—and declined to order any investigation. After OSC reconsidered and again declined to act, the district court dismissed AFL's remaining claims, and AFL appealed.

The D.C. Circuit never reached the merits. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Gregory Katsas held that AFL lacked Article III standing on both of the theories it pressed.

AFL's first theory was that OSC's refusal to investigate had caused it a concrete informational injury by preventing it from obtaining the records it had requested from DOJ under FOIA. The court rejected this on causation and redressability grounds. The causal chain AFL advanced was jaw-droppingly attenuated: if OSC had conducted a § 1216 investigation, it *might* have pursued corrective or disciplinary action before the Merit Systems Protection Board; the MSPB *might* have sanctioned the responsible DOJ employees; and DOJ *might* then have produced the requested records. Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Heckler v. Chaney*, Judge Katsas observed that causation cannot rest on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors not before the court—here, the MSPB and DOJ—as well as speculation about how OSC might exercise its enforcement discretion. Redressability, the court emphasized, turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not on whether its rationale might persuade non-parties.

AFL's second theory was more creative. It argued that, even if it could not show that an OSC investigation would lead to disclosure of the requested records, it was independently entitled to the notices and information that § 1214 requires OSC to provide to complaining parties during investigations of prohibited personnel practices. Because § 1216(c) permits OSC to investigate FOIA withholdings "under section 1214," AFL contended that the § 1214 notice provisions should apply, giving AFL a right to information about the investigation's progress. The D.C. Circuit rejected this theory as well. The court noted that § 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates no entitlement to receive information. And AFL itself acknowledged—citing the Sixth Circuit's decision in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011)—that OSC's decision whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without any alleged entitlement to information, AFL could not establish a concrete informational injury. And because OSC need not provide § 1214 notices during a § 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL's claimed informational harm.

The court thus vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The merits question—whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits § 1216—went unanswered.

This is where the doctrinal picture gets complicated. The district court's statutory interpretation ruling remains on the books. It was never reversed, because the D.C. Circuit concluded it lacked jurisdiction to reach it. Under the standard rule that a jurisdictional dismissal leaves the lower court's substantive reasoning without precedential effect, the district court's analysis of § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is technically non-binding. But it is the only judicial decision to address the question, and it may well influence how OSC, agencies, and future litigants think about the scope of OSC's FOIA oversight authority.

The textual and structural arguments on the merits are genuinely interesting. The case for treating the provisions as independent rests on several features of the statutory scheme. Section 1216 was enacted as part of the Civil Service Reform Act and uses mandatory language: OSC "shall" investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), by contrast, was enacted as an amendment to FOIA itself and uses a different trigger—judicial findings—to activate a disciplinary proceeding. The two provisions address different ends of the enforcement spectrum: § 1216 authorizes investigation and corrective action; § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) requires discipline after a court has already adjudicated a violation. One can read them as complementary rather than conflicting, with § 1216 providing OSC an independent investigative pathway and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creating a distinct, post-litigation disciplinary mandate.

The case for treating § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as a limitation on § 1216 is less straightforward but not frivolous. Congress specifically legislated in the FOIA context about when and how OSC should respond to arbitrary withholdings, and it chose to do so through a judicially triggered mechanism. One might argue that by specifying the predicates for OSC action in the FOIA statute itself, Congress intended to occupy the field—making the three predicates not merely triggers for a disciplinary proceeding but limits on OSC's authority to act on FOIA-related misconduct at all. Under this reading, § 1216's general mandate to investigate arbitrary withholdings cannot be deployed in the FOIA context without satisfying FOIA's own enforcement conditions.

The district court rejected this reading, and the textual evidence arguably supports that conclusion. Nothing in § 1216 suggests it is subject to FOIA's enforcement provision, and nothing in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) purports to be exclusive. The provisions use different operative verbs, address different enforcement stages, and arise from different statutory frameworks. The district court's reading also finds some support in *Carson*, where the Sixth Circuit held that § 1216 investigations are governed by OSC's discretion as to whether to follow § 1214 procedures—a conclusion that presupposes § 1216 establishes an investigative authority operating independently of the FOIA judicial-trigger mechanism.

But *Carson* is a mixed signal. While it assumed § 1216's applicability to FOIA violations, it also treated OSC's investigative decisions under § 1216 as committed to agency discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*—a holding that, if adopted by the D.C. Circuit, would render § 1216's "shall" directive largely unenforceable. The D.C. Circuit did not reach that question either, and the district court's discretion-based dismissal remains unreviewed on appeal.

This leaves the architecture of FOIA oversight in a genuinely uncertain state. OSC maintains that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s three predicates must be satisfied before it can investigate FOIA violations. The only district court to address the question held otherwise, but did so in a ruling that has been vacated for lack of jurisdiction. The D.C. Circuit's standing analysis, meanwhile, may have quietly closed the courthouse door to litigants seeking to challenge OSC's position. Any future plaintiff trying to compel a § 1216 investigation will face the same formidable standing barriers that defeated AFL: the causal chain from OSC investigation to document disclosure is too attenuated, and § 1214's notice provisions appear to be discretionary in the § 1216 context. It is difficult to construct a plaintiff who could clear both hurdles.

The practical implications are considerable. If OSC's reading is correct—that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) governs OSC's FOIA enforcement role—then OSC's disciplinary authority is effectively limited to cases where a court has already adjudicated a FOIA violation, awarded fees, and expressed concern about arbitrary withholding. That is a narrow category. If the district court's reading is correct—that § 1216 provides independent authority—then OSC has broader latitude to investigate agency FOIA practices, but the D.C. Circuit's standing analysis makes it difficult for any outside party to compel such investigations. Either way, the result is that OSC retains substantial control over when and whether to investigate FOIA-related misconduct, and private litigants have limited tools to force the issue.

There is a broader institutional question lurking here. When a court identifies a plausible statutory interpretation question of genuine public importance—whether a federal oversight agency has broader or narrower authority to investigate government misconduct—and then declines to resolve it on standing grounds, the legal question persists in a state of unresolved uncertainty. Agencies are left to make their own judgments, litigants lack clear guidance, and the district court's reasoning—however thoughtful—carries only persuasive weight. This is not a criticism of the D.C. Circuit's standing analysis, which is careful and doctrinally sound. But it is worth noting that the standing doctrine that properly restrains judicial power can also, in cases like this one, leave important questions of administrative oversight authority in limbo.

The question whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits § 1216 remains open in the D.C. Circuit. Given the standing barriers the court has erected, it may take a differently situated plaintiff—or congressional action—to force its resolution. In the meantime, the district court's reading lives on, not as binding precedent but as the only judicial word on a question that matters for the future of FOIA accountability.
