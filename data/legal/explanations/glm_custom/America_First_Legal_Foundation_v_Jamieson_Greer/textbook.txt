Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The Office of Special Counsel and the Statutory Framework for Investigating FOIA Violations

## The Role and Mandate of the Office of Special Counsel Under the Civil Service Reform Act

The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is an investigative and prosecutorial agency whose mission, as the D.C. Circuit has explained, is to seek "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The Civil Service Reform Act vests OSC with authority to investigate misconduct by federal employees and, when warranted, to bring enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). The Act creates two categories of misconduct that OSC may pursue. The first is "prohibited personnel practices"—a defined set of improper actions in federal employment. The second, relevant to this case, encompasses certain other forms of misconduct by government employees, including violations of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). For both categories, OSC serves as a gatekeeper: it investigates allegations, decides whether to pursue formal proceedings, and, if so, prosecutes those proceedings before the MSPB, which can order corrective or disciplinary action.

This institutional design matters for understanding the dispute in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*. AFL asked OSC to investigate the Department of Justice's alleged policy of arbitrarily withholding records in response to FOIA requests. OSC declined, reasoning that the statutory triggers for investigating FOIA violations had not been met. The question whether OSC's refusal was proper requires understanding the several statutes that govern its authority—sections 1214, 1215, and 1216 of Title 5, as well as FOIA's own enforcement provision in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i). These provisions interact in ways that create both broad mandates and specific limitations, and the tension among them lies at the heart of the case.

## Section 1214: Procedures for Corrective Action, Including Notification Requirements

Section 1214 of Title 5 sets forth the procedures governing corrective action for prohibited personnel practices. When OSC investigates an allegation of a prohibited personnel practice and finds it warranted, it may file a complaint with the MSPB seeking corrective action—such as reinstatement, back pay, or other remedies for the affected employee. The statute also includes provisions designed to keep the complaining party informed about the progress of an investigation. Specifically, section 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D) requires OSC to provide certain notices and information to the complainant during the investigative process.

These notification requirements are significant for two reasons in this case. First, AFL later argued that if OSC had conducted the investigation it demanded under section 1216, AFL would have been entitled to receive the notices and information required by section 1214. AFL invoked these notification provisions as the basis for its claim that it suffered a concrete informational injury when OSC refused to investigate. Second, as the court notes, section 1216(c) authorizes OSC to investigate FOIA violations "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved," which raises the question whether section 1214's notification requirements apply to section 1216 investigations. The Sixth Circuit has held that OSC's decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary, and AFL did not contest that point. The absence of a mandatory entitlement to such notices would later prove fatal to AFL's standing argument.

## Section 1215: Disciplinary Action Procedures

Section 1215 of Title 5 governs disciplinary action against federal employees who commit prohibited personnel practices or other misconduct within OSC's jurisdiction. Disciplinary action is distinct from corrective action: corrective action remedies the harm to the affected party, while disciplinary action punishes the responsible employee. Under section 1215(a)(1), OSC may initiate disciplinary proceedings by filing a written complaint with the MSPB. Under section 1215(a)(3)(A), the MSPB may then impose sanctions, which can include removal, suspension, or other disciplinary measures against the offending employee.

In this case, section 1215 is relevant to AFL's causation theory. AFL argued that if OSC had investigated under section 1216, OSC might have pursued disciplinary action under section 1215 against DOJ employees responsible for arbitrary FOIA withholdings, and the MSPB might then have sanctioned those employees. The court found this causal chain too speculative to support standing, in part because it required speculation about whether the MSPB—an agency not before the court—would decide to impose sanctions. Understanding the role of section 1215 thus helps illuminate why the court found AFL's theory of redressability deficient.

## Section 1216(a)(3): OSC's Duty to Investigate Arbitrary or Capricious FOIA Withholdings

Section 1216 of Title 5 empowers OSC to investigate and seek corrective action for certain forms of misconduct beyond prohibited personnel practices. The provision central to this case is section 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, withholdings prohibited by FOIA. The use of the word "shall" is critical: AFL read this language as imposing a mandatory duty on OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholding, regardless of the circumstances.

This broad investigative mandate is what AFL invoked when it asked OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged policy of not searching employee emails without consent. AFL argued that section 1216(a)(3) required OSC to investigate, full stop. OSC disagreed, reasoning that the mandatory investigation provision of section 1216 should be read in light of FOIA's own enforcement provision in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which sets out specific predicates that must be satisfied before OSC initiates disciplinary proceedings. The district court sided with AFL on this interpretive question, concluding that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate under section 1216. But the D.C. Circuit did not reach this question, because it dismissed the case for lack of standing. The interpretive tension between these provisions, however, remains essential to understanding the parties' positions.

## Section 1216(c): Authorization to Pursue Corrective and Disciplinary Action for FOIA Violations

Section 1216(c) provides that when OSC investigates alleged FOIA violations under section 1216(a)(3), it "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." This provision bridges the two statutory regimes: it allows OSC to use the same enforcement tools for FOIA violations that it uses for prohibited personnel practices.

Two features of section 1216(c) are particularly important. First, the word "may" suggests that the decision to pursue corrective or disciplinary action is committed to OSC's discretion. The district court relied on this reasoning when it declined to order OSC to investigate, concluding that section 1216(c) committed the decision to OSC's discretion even if section 1216(a)(3) mandated an investigation. Second, the phrase "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved" raises the question whether all of the procedural requirements of sections 1214 and 1215—including the notification requirements in section 1214—apply to section 1216 investigations. As noted above, the Sixth Circuit has held that OSC's decision to follow section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. The D.C. Circuit cited this holding in rejecting AFL's argument that it had a concrete informational interest in receiving notices and information from OSC.

## FOIA's Separate Enforcement Provision Under Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i): The Three Predicates

FOIA contains its own enforcement provision directed at OSC. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) of Title 5 states that if a court (1) orders production of agency records improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) awards attorneys' fees against the agency, and (3) specifically questions whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," then OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding."

This provision creates a mandatory trigger for OSC disciplinary proceedings, but only when all three predicates are satisfied. The first two predicates—a court order compelling production and an award of attorneys' fees—are typical outcomes of successful FOIA litigation. The third predicate—a judicial finding questioning whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously—requires more: the court must go beyond merely ordering disclosure and must specifically question the agency's conduct. This third predicate thus screens out ordinary FOIA disputes and reserves OSC disciplinary proceedings for cases involving potentially culpable behavior.

In this case, the three predicates of section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not satisfied. AFL had sued DOJ under FOIA and was receiving records through that process, but there was no court order triggering all three predicates. OSC therefore reasoned that it had no authority—or at least no obligation—to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy. This refusal is what prompted AFL to sue OSC, arguing that section 1216 imposed an independent investigative duty not conditioned on the section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates.

## The Tension Between Section 1216's Broad Investigative Mandate and Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s Narrower Predicates

The central statutory tension in this case is between section 1216(a)(3)'s broad language—which states that OSC "shall" investigate "any allegation" of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings—and section 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s narrower set of three judicial predicates, which trigger OSC disciplinary proceedings only after a court has ordered production, awarded fees, and questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. OSC read these provisions as working together: the section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates limit the circumstances under which OSC must investigate FOIA violations, even though section 1216(a)(3) uses the word "shall." AFL read them as independent: section 1216 imposes a free-standing duty to investigate regardless of whether the section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates are met.

The district court agreed with AFL's reading, holding that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate under section 1216. But the district court still declined to order OSC to investigate, reasoning that section 1216(c) committed the decision to pursue corrective or disciplinary action to OSC's discretion. The court remanded the case to OSC for further consideration, but OSC again declined to investigate, leading to the dismissal that AFL appealed.

The D.C. Circuit did not resolve the interpretive question. Because the court concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing, it vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The statutory tension between sections 1216 and 552(a)(4)(F)(i) thus remains unresolved by this opinion. Understanding that tension, however, is essential to grasping what AFL demanded of OSC, why OSC declined, and why the district court ruled as it did before the circuit court ultimately dismissed the case on jurisdictional grounds.

# Factual Background and Procedural History

## AFL's FOIA Request to DOJ and the Alleged Email Policy

This case arises from a dispute over access to federal agency records, but it reaches the court of appeals through an unusual procedural path—one that does not involve a direct challenge to the withholding of records, but rather a challenge to an agency's refusal to investigate that withholding. Understanding the posture requires tracing the parallel tracks of litigation that the plaintiff, America First Legal Foundation ("AFL"), pursued simultaneously.

As part of its effort to investigate judicial recusals, AFL submitted a Freedom of Information Act ("FOIA") request to the Department of Justice ("DOJ"). AFL sought certain emails between an outside attorney and DOJ's Civil Division. FOIA, codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552, generally requires federal agencies to disclose agency records upon request, subject to enumerated exemptions. The request at issue here was apparently straightforward in scope, but DOJ's response was not.

According to AFL, DOJ's Civil Division denied the request on a dubious ground: it does not search employee emails for responsive records without the employee's consent. This alleged policy—declining to search employee emails absent consent—became the focal point of AFL's subsequent legal challenges. AFL characterized this practice as an arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under FOIA, a characterization that would later carry legal significance under the Civil Service Reform Act.

## AFL's Parallel FOIA Lawsuit Against DOJ

AFL pursued two separate but related legal strategies in response to DOJ's alleged policy. First, AFL sued DOJ directly under FOIA, seeking to compel production of the requested emails. This was a conventional FOIA enforcement action, the type of suit expressly authorized by 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), which permits district courts to order the production of agency records improperly withheld.

In that FOIA lawsuit against DOJ, DOJ produced—and was continuing to produce—records responsive to AFL's request. The opinion notes that the sufficiency of this production is not at issue in the present case. This detail is significant because it means the case before the D.C. Circuit is not a FOIA production dispute at all. The direct FOIA action against DOJ was proceeding on its own track, with documents being produced. The present case instead concerns AFL's second, parallel strategy: seeking to compel a different federal agency—the Office of Special Counsel—to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy.

## AFL's Request to OSC Under Section 1216(a)(3)

AFL's second strategy involved the Office of Special Counsel ("OSC"), an investigative and prosecutorial agency established to seek "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The Civil Service Reform Act empowers OSC to investigate "prohibited personnel practices" and, when warranted, to bring enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board ("MSPB"). Under 5 U.S.C. § 1214, OSC may seek corrective action for prohibited personnel practices, and under 5 U.S.C. § 1215, it may seek disciplinary action. Section 1214 also includes provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of an investigation.

Section 1216 extends OSC's authority to investigate and seek corrective action for certain other forms of misconduct by government employees. Critically for this case, section 1216(a)(3) provides that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, withholdings prohibited by FOIA. For such alleged FOIA violations, section 1216(c) provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved."

Relying on this statutory scheme, AFL asked OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent. AFL invoked section 1216(a)(3), emphasizing its mandatory language: OSC "shall" conduct an investigation of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. In AFL's view, this language required OSC to investigate DOJ's policy, regardless of any other statutory prerequisites.

## OSC's Refusal to Investigate and the Statutory Dispute

OSC refused to investigate AFL's allegation. It disclaimed authority to do so because the three predicates for triggering an investigation under a different statutory provision—5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—were not satisfied. That provision, which is part of FOIA itself rather than the Civil Service Reform Act, states that if a court (1) orders production of agency records improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) awards attorneys' fees against the agency, and (3) specifically questions whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," then OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding."

The dispute between AFL and OSC thus turned on the relationship between two statutory provisions. Section 1216(a)(3) states that OSC "shall" investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, using mandatory language with no apparent preconditions. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), by contrast, requires OSC to initiate a proceeding only after a court has ordered production of records, awarded attorneys' fees, and specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. OSC took the position that the three predicates in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were prerequisites for any OSC investigation of FOIA withholdings, including under section 1216. AFL disagreed, arguing that section 1216 mandated an investigation even if the section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates were not satisfied.

## AFL's Lawsuit Against OSC and the District Court's Mixed Ruling

AFL sued OSC in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia. The district court issued a mixed ruling. On the key statutory question, the court sided with AFL: it concluded that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. The court declared that OSC's decision not to investigate was therefore based on a legal error, because OSC had refused to investigate solely on the ground that the section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates were not met.

However, the district court did not simply order OSC to investigate. Instead, it concluded that section 1216(c) committed the decision whether to investigate to OSC's discretion. Recall that section 1216(c) provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." The district court read the word "may" in this provision as conferring discretion on OSC rather than imposing a mandatory duty. Having so concluded, the court declined to order OSC to investigate any FOIA policy. Instead, it remanded the case to OSC for further consideration of whether to exercise its discretion to investigate.

## The Remand, OSC's Second Refusal, and the District Court's Dismissal

On remand, OSC again declined to investigate. The opinion does not detail OSC's reasoning on remand, but the result was clear: OSC persisted in its refusal to investigate AFL's allegation. AFL then moved for entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed its remaining claims. AFL appealed.

## The Appeal and the Standard of Review

The case arrived at the D.C. Circuit as a dismissal for failure to state a claim. The D.C. Circuit reviews such dismissals de novo, as the court notes, citing *North American Butterfly Association v. Wolf*, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020). De novo review means the appellate court considers the legal questions anew, without deferring to the district court's conclusions.

However, before reaching the merits of the dismissal, the D.C. Circuit confronted a threshold jurisdictional question that neither party had initially addressed: whether AFL had Article III standing to pursue its claims. The court ordered supplemental briefing on this question. This is a critical feature of the procedural posture. Under *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998), federal courts must satisfy themselves of their subject-matter jurisdiction before reaching the merits of a case. Standing is a core component of Article III jurisdiction, and the obligation to verify standing exists regardless of whether the parties raise the issue.

The standing question would dominate the D.C. Circuit's analysis. As the court explains, Article III of the Constitution restricts the federal "judicial Power" to resolving certain "Cases" and "Controversies." Standing doctrine follows from this case-or-controversy requirement. To have standing, a plaintiff must have suffered an injury caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of standing. The court's analysis of whether AFL could satisfy these requirements—particularly given that AFL was seeking to compel an investigation by one agency based on the conduct of another—would prove decisive.

# Article III Standing Doctrine and the Procedural Injury Framework

## Article III's Case-or-Controversy Requirement and the Three Elements of Standing

Article III of the United States Constitution limits the federal judicial power to resolving certain "Cases" and "Controversies." This seemingly simple phrase has given rise to the entire doctrine of standing—a judicially created set of requirements that determine who may properly bring suit in federal court. As the D.C. Circuit explains in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, standing doctrine flows directly from this case-or-controversy requirement. The doctrine serves as a constitutional threshold: before a federal court may reach the merits of a dispute, the plaintiff must demonstrate that the dispute is of the kind that Article III permits the judiciary to resolve.

The standing inquiry has three core elements. To have Article III standing, a plaintiff must show that it has suffered an injury that is caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. These three elements—injury in fact, causation, and redressability—operate as a unified test. A plaintiff must satisfy all three to establish standing. Failure on any one element is fatal to the court's subject-matter jurisdiction. This is why the court in *Greer* addresses standing before reaching the merits of AFL's claims, even though neither party initially raised the issue. Standing is not a waivable defense; it is a constitutional prerequisite that courts must address sua sponte when in doubt.

The three elements serve distinct functions. The injury requirement ensures that the plaintiff has a real stake in the dispute, rather than a generalized grievance shared by the public at large. The causation requirement ensures that the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct, so that the court is not being asked to remedy harm caused by someone who is not before it. And the redressability requirement ensures that a favorable judicial decision would actually alleviate the plaintiff's injury, so that the court is not issuing an advisory opinion with no practical effect on the parties. In *Greer*, the court's analysis focuses heavily on causation and redressability, because AFL's asserted injuries depend on a long and speculative chain of events involving actors not before the court.

## Injury in Fact: Concrete, Particularized, and Actual or Imminent

The first element of standing—injury in fact—has its own internal set of requirements. The injury must be "an invasion of a legally protected interest" that is both "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical." Each of these terms carries specific doctrinal weight.

A concrete injury is one that is real and tangible, as opposed to abstract. The concreteness inquiry asks whether the plaintiff has suffered a harm that is sufficiently material in a way that the law has historically recognized. A particularized injury is one that affects the plaintiff in a personal and individual way, as opposed to a harm shared equally by the public at large. A generalized grievance—a harm suffered by every citizen in the same degree—does not satisfy the particularization requirement. The actual-or-imminent requirement ensures that the plaintiff is not seeking relief for a harm that has already occurred and passed, nor for a harm that is merely speculative and may never come to pass. The injury must be either presently existing or so likely to occur that it presents a real and immediate threat.

In *Greer*, AFL advances two theories of injury, both of which the court rejects. AFL's first theory is that OSC's failure to investigate has prevented AFL from receiving the records it requested under FOIA from the Department of Justice. AFL's second theory is that OSC's refusal to investigate has deprived AFL of the notices and information that would have been available had OSC conducted an investigation under section 1214. The court analyzes both theories under the framework for procedural injuries, to which we now turn.

## The Standard for Standing at the Motion-to-Dismiss Stage

The point in the litigation at which standing is evaluated matters. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court takes the plaintiff's factual allegations as true and asks whether those allegations plausibly support each element of standing. As the D.C. Circuit states in *Greer*, citing *Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack*, "a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of its standing." This standard mirrors the familiar plausibility standard from *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* and *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, but applied specifically to the constitutional standing inquiry.

This means that a plaintiff cannot simply recite the elements of standing in conclusory fashion. The complaint must contain factual allegations that, if accepted as true, make it plausible that the plaintiff has suffered a concrete and particularized injury, that the defendant caused that injury, and that a favorable judicial decision would likely redress it. At later stages of litigation—summary judgment and trial—the plaintiff must support each element of standing with evidence, not merely allegations. But in *Greer*, the case was decided at the motion-to-dismiss stage, so the court evaluated whether AFL's complaint plausibly alleged standing.

## The Distinction Between Procedural and Substantive Rights for Standing Purposes

Not all statutory rights create the same kind of standing analysis. A substantive right gives a plaintiff a direct entitlement to some benefit or outcome—such as the right to receive a government benefit, the right to be free from discrimination, or the right to access certain information. Violation of a substantive right can, in appropriate circumstances, itself constitute a concrete injury for Article III purposes.

A procedural right, by contrast, is a right to have the government follow certain procedures before taking action. Examples include the right to notice and comment in administrative rulemaking, the right to an environmental impact statement, or—as AFL argues in *Greer*—the right to have an agency conduct an investigation. A procedural right does not entitle the plaintiff to any particular outcome; it merely entitles the plaintiff to a particular process. This distinction matters because the Supreme Court has held that the deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone, does not always constitute a cognizable Article III injury.

In *Greer*, AFL argues that OSC violated its procedural right to an investigation under section 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC "shall conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. The court treats this as an asserted procedural right. The court notes that it need not decide whether AFL's asserted injuries are procedural or substantive, because AFL fails to satisfy even the relaxed standing requirements that apply to procedural injuries. This analytical move—considering AFL's claims under the more lenient procedural-injury framework and finding them insufficient—is significant. It means that the court gives AFL the benefit of the more relaxed standard and still finds standing lacking.

## The Prohibition on Procedural Rights in Vacuo

The central principle governing procedural injuries is this: a procedural right in vacuo is insufficient to confer Article III standing. The Supreme Court made this clear in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, where it held that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer standing. In other words, a plaintiff cannot sue solely because the government failed to follow a required procedure. The plaintiff must also show that the procedural failure harmed, or threatens to harm, some concrete, particularized interest of the plaintiff.

This principle prevents plaintiffs from suing over procedural violations that cause them no real harm. It ensures that courts are not transformed into general-purpose overseers of government compliance with procedural rules. A plaintiff who complains that an agency skipped a required step must also explain what concrete harm the plaintiff suffers because the step was skipped.

In *Greer*, AFL's assertion that OSC violated its procedural right to an investigation under section 1216 falls squarely within this prohibition. The court quotes *Summers* directly and cites the D.C. Circuit's own decision in *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau* for the same proposition. The problem for AFL is that section 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, does not by itself create an entitlement to any information or outcome. AFL has a procedural right—if it has any right at all—to an investigation. But it cannot identify a concrete interest affected by the denial of that investigation, at least not one that satisfies causation and redressability.

## The Relaxed Standing Framework for Procedural Injuries

Although a procedural right alone is insufficient for standing, the law recognizes that requiring plaintiffs alleging procedural injuries to satisfy the full rigor of the ordinary standing test would often be impossible. This is because procedural violations frequently make it difficult to predict what would have happened if the procedure had been followed, which in turn makes it difficult to show imminence of harm and redressability through a judicial order.

To address this difficulty, the D.C. Circuit applies a relaxed standing framework for cases involving alleged procedural injury. As the court explains, quoting *Center for Law and Education v. Department of Education*: "Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded to them by statute and designed to protect their threatened concrete interest, the courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation." This framework thus relaxes two of the three standing elements but leaves the other two intact.

The relaxation of imminence means that a plaintiff alleging a procedural violation need not show that the threatened harm is as immediately impending as would otherwise be required. The relaxation of redressability means that the plaintiff need not show that a favorable judicial decision would as certainly or directly redress the injury as would be required in an ordinary case. But the plaintiff must still show a concrete and particularized injury in fact, and must still show that the defendant's procedural violation caused injury to that concrete interest.

The rationale for this partial relaxation is straightforward. When a statute gives a plaintiff a procedural right designed to protect a concrete interest, and the government violates that procedural right, the plaintiff should not be denied access to court simply because the procedural violation makes the chain of causation and the prospect of redress somewhat uncertain. The procedural violation itself may have prevented the plaintiff from learning whether and how the concrete interest was harmed. But the relaxation is not unlimited. The plaintiff must still point to a concrete interest that the procedural right was designed to protect, and must still show that the violation of the procedural right caused injury to that interest.

## The Requirement That Plaintiffs Show the Procedural Violation Resulted in Injury to a Concrete, Particularized Interest

The final and crucial step in the procedural-injury standing analysis is the requirement that the plaintiff show that the procedural violation "resulted in injury" to some "concrete, particularized interest." This requirement bridges the gap between the abstract procedural violation and the concrete injury that Article III demands. Without this bridge, the procedural violation remains a right in vacuo, insufficient for standing.

In *Greer*, AFL attempts to build this bridge with two theories, both of which the court rejects. AFL's first theory connects the OSC's failure to investigate to AFL's interest in obtaining the records it requested from DOJ under FOIA. But the court finds that this theory suffers from fatal causation and redressability problems. The injury—failure to receive FOIA records—is traceable to DOJ, not to OSC. And the causal chain connecting OSC's inaction to DOJ's production of records is too speculative: it requires speculation that OSC would have pursued corrective or disciplinary action, that the Merit Systems Protection Board would have sanctioned DOJ employees, and that DOJ would then have produced the records. This chain involves the choices of multiple actors not before the court, and the court cannot rely on such speculation to establish causation or redressability.

AFL's second theory connects OSC's failure to investigate to AFL's interest in receiving the notices and information that section 1214 requires during investigations of prohibited personnel practices. The theory is that if OSC had conducted the section 1216 investigation, it might have applied the section 1214 notice requirements and provided AFL with information about the investigation. But the court rejects this theory as well. Even if section 1216 requires an investigation, it creates no entitlement to receive information. And AFL does not contend that OSC must apply section 1214's notice requirements to section 1216 investigations; indeed, AFL acknowledges authority holding that OSC's decision whether to follow section 1214 procedures in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without an entitlement to receive information, AFL has no concrete injury. And without any requirement that OSC provide such information, a judicial order compelling the investigation would not be likely to redress the asserted informational injury.

The court's analysis thus illustrates the practical operation of the procedural-injury framework. AFL receives the benefit of the relaxed standards for imminence and redressability. But it still must show a concrete, particularized interest that the procedural right was designed to protect, and it still must show causation. On both theories, AFL cannot make these showings. Its first theory fails because the causal chain is too attenuated and speculative, involving independent choices by actors not before the court. Its second theory fails because the statute creates no entitlement to the information AFL claims it was denied. The result is that AFL's procedural right—assuming one exists—remains a right in vacuo, unconnected to any concrete interest that Article III recognizes as a cognizable injury.

Because AFL lacks Article III standing, the court does not reach the merits of the dispute. The court vacates the district court's judgment and remands with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This disposition underscores a fundamental principle of federal courts: standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite. Without it, the court has no constitutional authority to decide the case, no matter how meritorious the plaintiff's claims might otherwise be.

# The Court's Standing Analysis and Disposition

## AFL's Framing of Its Injury as a Procedural Right to an Investigation Under Section 1216 and Its Attempt to Identify Concrete Informational Injuries

Before reaching the merits of AFL's claims, the court addressed whether AFL satisfied the requirements of Article III standing—a threshold question that bears on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction. Standing doctrine derives from Article III's limitation of the federal judicial power to resolving "Cases" and "Controversies." To establish standing, a plaintiff must show that it has suffered an injury that is caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. The injury must be "an invasion of a legally protected interest" that is "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical." Because the case was at the motion-to-dismiss stage, AFL needed only to allege facts plausibly supporting each element of standing.

In its supplemental brief, AFL framed its injury as the deprivation of a procedural right to an investigation under section 1216. The court recognized a critical limitation on this theory: the deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone and disconnected from any concrete interest affected by the deprivation, is insufficient to confer Article III standing. A procedural right in vacuo will not do. This principle follows from *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, where the Supreme Court held that plaintiffs must connect their procedural injury to some concrete interest that the procedural right was designed to protect.

To overcome this problem, AFL identified two concrete, informational injuries that it said resulted from OSC's refusal to investigate. First, AFL argued that DOJ had not provided the records it requested through FOIA. Second, AFL argued that OSC had not provided the notices and information required for an investigation under section 1214. Through this framing, AFL sought to invoke the relaxed standing requirements that apply in cases involving alleged procedural injury. Under this relaxed standard, courts relax—but do not eliminate—the requirements of imminence and redressability, while still requiring plaintiffs to show that the violation of a procedural right resulted in injury to some "concrete, particularized interest." The court ultimately concluded that AFL satisfied neither the requirements for the relaxed standard nor even the more relaxed redressability prong. Because AFL could not satisfy even the relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries, the court found it unnecessary to decide whether the alleged injuries were procedural or substantive.

## First Standing Theory: OSC's Failure to Investigate Prevented AFL from Receiving Requested FOIA Records

AFL's first standing theory was that OSC's failure to investigate DOJ under section 1216 had prevented AFL from receiving the records it requested under FOIA. Under this theory, the concrete interest that AFL's procedural right to an investigation was designed to protect was AFL's interest in obtaining the requested records from DOJ. The theory was thus an attempt to link the procedural violation—OSC's failure to conduct a mandatory investigation under section 1216—to a concrete informational harm—the nonproduction of responsive FOIA records by DOJ.

## Causation Problems with the First Theory: Any Informational Injury Is Traceable to DOJ, Not OSC

The court rejected AFL's first theory, identifying what it called "fatal causation and redressability problems." The court began with causation. The injury that AFL identified—the failure to receive requested FOIA records—was plainly traceable to DOJ, not OSC. AFL had sued OSC, not DOJ. DOJ was not a party to the lawsuit. Any causal connection between OSC's failure to conduct a section 1216 investigation and DOJ's failure to comply with the FOIA request was, in the court's view, "too speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing."

## The Speculative Causal Chain: OSC Investigation → Possible Corrective or Disciplinary Action → Possible MSPB Sanctions → Possible DOJ Production

To illustrate the attenuated nature of the causal connection, the court traced the chain of events that would have to unfold for OSC's investigation to lead to production of the requested records. First, if OSC had conducted the assertedly mandatory section 1216 investigation, it *might* have decided to pursue a corrective action under section 1214 or a disciplinary action under section 1215 against offending DOJ employees. Second, the Merit Systems Protection Board *might* have decided to sanction those employees for arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Third, DOJ *might* have decided to produce the requested records in response to such sanctions. Each link in this chain involved a discretionary choice by a separate actor, and the court found the accumulation of these contingencies too speculative to support standing.

## The Principle That Causation Cannot Rely on Speculation About Unfettered Choices of Independent Actors Not Before the Court

The court grounded its rejection of this causal chain in established doctrine. Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the court reiterated that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Here, the causal chain required speculation about choices by two government agencies that were not parties to the lawsuit—the MSPB and DOJ. The court also noted that the causal chain required speculation about how OSC itself might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which courts generally may not compel under *Heckler v. Chaney*. The court thus concluded that AFL had not shown that a favorable judgment in this case "would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" its informational injury, quoting its recent decision in *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC*.

## Redressability Problems with the First Theory: A Judgment Binds the Parties, Not Non-Parties Who Might Be Persuaded by Its Rationale

The court further identified redressability problems with AFL's first theory. Redressability asks whether a favorable decision is likely to redress the plaintiff's injury. The court explained that redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties to the lawsuit—not whether the judgment's rationale "might persuade actors who are not before the court." This principle, drawn from *Haaland v. Brackeen*, was directly applicable: even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, that order would bind only OSC. It would not bind DOJ, which was not a party, and DOJ's decision to produce records would depend on its own independent assessment of its legal obligations. Because the alleged injury was caused by DOJ's nonproduction, and because DOJ was not before the court, a judgment against OSC could not redress that injury. AFL could not establish standing based on its interest in the information it requested from DOJ through FOIA.

## Second Standing Theory: AFL's Informational Interest in the Notices and Information from the OSC Investigation Itself

AFL's second standing theory focused on a different informational interest. Rather than tying its injury to the FOIA records held by DOJ, AFL claimed an informational interest in the fruits of the OSC investigation itself. Specifically, AFL argued that if OSC conducted the assertedly mandatory investigation under section 1216, OSC might choose to give AFL the notices and information that would be required if OSC were investigating an alleged "prohibited personnel practice" under section 1214. AFL thus sought to characterize its injury as the denial of information that it was entitled to receive as part of the OSC investigation process.

## Rejection of the Second Theory: Section 1216 Creates a Duty to Investigate but No Entitlement to Receive Information

The court rejected AFL's second theory as well. The court explained that a plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury only if denied information that a "statute entitled him to receive." The court assumed, without deciding, that section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations. But even so, the court held that section 1216 creates no entitlement to receive information. The statute imposes a duty on OSC to investigate, but it does not confer on the complainant any right to receive information generated by or disclosed during that investigation. Without a statutory entitlement to receive information, AFL lacked the concrete injury necessary to support its second standing theory.

## AFL's Concession That OSC's Decision to Follow Section 1214 Notice Requirements in Section 1216 Investigations Is Discretionary

AFL attempted to rescue its second theory by pointing to the notice and information provisions in section 1214, which govern investigations of prohibited personnel practices. AFL argued that if OSC conducted a section 1216 investigation, it might choose to provide AFL with the notices and information required under section 1214. But AFL did not contend that OSC was required to engraft section 1214's notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations. To the contrary, AFL acknowledged existing case law, including *Carson v. United States Office of Special Counsel*, holding that OSC's "decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements" in a section 1216 investigation is "discretionary." Because OSC's decision to provide section 1214 notices and information during a section 1216 investigation was discretionary rather than mandatory, AFL had no statutory entitlement to receive such information. This concession was fatal to AFL's claim that it had suffered a concrete informational injury. Likewise, because OSC need not provide section 1214 notice requirements in a section 1216 investigation, a judicial order mandating such an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL's second alleged injury.

## Disposition: Vacatur and Remand for Lack of Subject-Matter Jurisdiction Due to Absence of Article III Standing

Having concluded that AFL failed to establish Article III standing under either theory, the court vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This disposition reflects an important procedural consequence of the standing determination: because standing is a component of subject-matter jurisdiction, its absence means the district court never had authority to reach the merits of AFL's claims. The court's vacatur of the district court's merits dismissal—rather than simple affirmance—ensures that the prior judgment carries no precedential weight on the substantive questions the district court addressed, including its conclusion that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's investigative authority under section 1216. On remand, the district court was directed to dismiss the case entirely for lack of jurisdiction, bringing the litigation to a close unless AFL could identify some basis for standing that the court had not considered.
