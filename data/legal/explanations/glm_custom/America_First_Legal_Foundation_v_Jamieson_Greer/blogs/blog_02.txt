Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The Speculative Chain: Redressability and Multi-Agency Causation in Standing Analysis

When a plaintiff asks a federal court to compel one agency to investigate another, the question of standing often does more work than the merits. The D.C. Circuit's decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a case in point. The court never reached whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) misread its statutory mandate to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Instead, Judge Katsas's opinion for the panel held that AFL lacked Article III standing to demand such an investigation in the first place—and did so by deploying a causal-chain analysis that may reshape how courts evaluate attempts to compel oversight agencies to act.

## The Statutory Framework and AFL's Theory

The case arose from AFL's FOIA request to the Department of Justice for emails between an outside attorney and DOJ's Civil Division. DOJ denied the request on the ground—dubious, AFL maintains—that it does not search employee emails without the employee's consent. AFL sued DOJ under FOIA and simultaneously asked OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged policy of arbitrary withholding under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs OSC to "conduct an investigation" of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. When OSC declined—relying on the narrower enforcement triggers in FOIA's own disciplinary provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—AFL sued to compel the investigation.

The district court agreed with AFL that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC's authority under § 1216, meaning OSC's refusal rested on legal error. But the court declined to order OSC to investigate, reading § 1216(c) as committing that decision to OSC's discretion, and remanded. After OSC again declined, AFL appealed. The D.C. Circuit, raising standing sua sponte, stopped the analysis at the threshold.

## The Causal Chain and the Redressability Problem

AFL's primary standing theory was that OSC's failure to investigate had caused it a concrete informational injury: it still had not received the records it requested from DOJ. The court rejected this theory on both causation and redressability grounds, and the reasoning is worth tracing carefully.

The court framed AFL's theory as resting on the following chain: if OSC conducted the § 1216 investigation, it *might* pursue corrective or disciplinary action before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB); the MSPB *might* then sanction DOJ employees; and DOJ *might* then produce the requested records. The opinion treats each link as a speculative choice by an independent actor not before the court, invoking *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*'s admonition that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." The court further cited *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the principle that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale "might persuade actors who are not before the court."

This is a demanding application of redressability doctrine. The court is not merely saying that AFL's theory is unlikely to succeed; it is saying that the theory depends on a series of discretionary decisions by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ—each of which could break the causal chain at any point. The investigation might produce no findings. OSC might exercise its enforcement discretion not to pursue corrective action. The MSPB might decline to sanction. And DOJ might resist regardless. Under this framework, a plaintiff seeking to compel an investigative agency to act can almost never establish redressability if the hoped-for remedy depends on downstream enforcement by other bodies.

## Heckler v. Chaney in the Standing Inquiry

Perhaps the most analytically interesting move in the opinion is the court's invocation of *Heckler v. Chaney* within the standing analysis. *Heckler* holds that an agency's decision not to initiate enforcement proceedings is presumptively unreviewable under the APA—a merits doctrine about the availability of judicial review. Yet the D.C. Circuit deploys it here not to determine whether AFL's claim is reviewable, but to establish that AFL cannot satisfy the redressability element of standing because "it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel."

This importation of *Heckler* into the jurisdictional inquiry deserves scrutiny. On one view, the move is defensible: if courts generally cannot compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion in particular ways, then a plaintiff's requested remedy—compelling OSC to investigate and potentially prosecute—cannot be said to be "likely" to redress the underlying injury. The logic of *Heckler* informs the redressability calculus because the unavailability of compulsion makes the causal chain speculative.

On another view, however, this conflation of *Heckler* with standing is analytically problematic. Standing is a jurisdictional threshold; *Heckler* is a merits doctrine with a presumption that Congress can override. By importing *Heckler*'s enforcement-discretion principle into Article III, the court effectively transforms a waivable, statute-dependent merits limit into a constitutional jurisdictional bar. A plaintiff who might have argued that § 1216's mandatory "shall" language overrides the *Heckler* presumption on the merits now faces the argument that the same discretion dooms standing before the merits are even reached. The opinion does not fully grapple with this tension. It acknowledges that AFL argues § 1216 *mandates* investigation, but treats OSC's subsequent enforcement choices—whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action—as discretionary in a way that defeats redressability regardless of whether the initial investigation is legally required.

The result is a kind of doctrinal bootstrapping: the very discretion that *Heckler* protects on the merits is used to defeat jurisdiction, which in turn prevents the plaintiff from ever reaching the merits to argue that the statute overrides *Heckler*. Whether one finds this circular or coherent likely depends on whether one views redressability as asking whether a favorable judgment *can* produce the remedy or whether it *likely will*. The court clearly adopts the latter, more demanding formulation.

## The Second Theory and Informational Standing

AFL's fallback theory—that it had an informational interest in the fruits of the OSC investigation itself—failed for a cleaner reason. The court held that even if § 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information. AFL argued that if OSC investigated, it *might* provide AFL with the notices and information required under § 1214's procedures for prohibited personnel practices. But AFL conceded that OSC's decision whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, citing the Sixth Circuit's decision in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*. Without a statutory entitlement to information, there is no concrete informational injury, and without a requirement that OSC provide § 1214 notices during a § 1216 investigation, a court order compelling investigation would not redress the asserted informational harm.

This portion of the opinion is doctrinally straightforward but practically significant. It underscores that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to information, not merely a possibility that an investigation might produce relevant facts. And it closes off a creative workaround by which plaintiffs might manufacture standing by claiming an interest in the procedural outputs of an investigation they seek to compel.

## Broader Implications

The decision fits into a recognizable pattern in recent standing jurisprudence: courts increasingly demand that plaintiffs demonstrate a tight, non-speculative causal connection between the defendant's conduct and the plaintiff's injury, particularly when the requested relief depends on the actions of non-party government actors. *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Brackeen*—both cited here—reflect the Supreme Court's mounting insistence that standing theories cannot rest on assumptions about how independent actors will respond to judicial rulings.

What makes *AFL v. Greer* notable is its application of this framework to the oversight-agency context. Plaintiffs who seek to compel watchdog agencies—OSC, agency Inspectors General, or similar bodies—to investigate other federal actors face a structural redressability problem. The investigating agency's conduct is a necessary but rarely sufficient step toward the plaintiff's ultimate relief. The D.C. Circuit's opinion effectively holds that this multi-link causal chain is too attenuated to satisfy Article III, at least where each link involves the exercise of prosecutorial or enforcement discretion.

The downstream effect is predictable: suits seeking to compel oversight agencies to act will face standing hurdles that may be effectively insurmountable. Litigants who believe an agency has unlawfully withheld FOIA records will be limited to FOIA's own judicial enforcement mechanism—and cannot use the OSC as an intermediary pathway to the same relief. Whether this is a sound structural principle or an unduly cramped reading of Article III depends on one's view of the role of standing doctrine. But the D.C. Circuit has clearly signaled that when a plaintiff's theory requires a court to imagine a sequence of discretionary decisions by multiple federal actors, the Article III door is closed.
