Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The Merits Question Left Unanswered: § 1216, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), and the Architecture of FOIA Oversight

When a federal appellate court vacates a district court's statutory interpretation ruling on standing grounds, it sometimes leaves behind a legal holding that continues to shape agency behavior—even though no appellate court has endorsed it. The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* is a textbook example, and the doctrinal consequences may be significant for the future of FOIA oversight.

At the center of the case is a largely overlooked statutory provision—5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)—that requires the Office of Special Counsel to investigate allegations of "arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552," that is, under FOIA. The provision sits within the Civil Service Reform Act's framework for addressing federal employee misconduct, and it authorizes OSC to pursue corrective and disciplinary action against employees responsible for improper withholdings. But FOIA itself contains a separate enforcement mechanism: § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides that when a court orders production of improperly withheld records, awards attorneys' fees, and "specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted." That provision establishes three predicates—a court order, a fee award, and a judicial expression of concern—before OSC's disciplinary obligation kicks in.

The question AFL posed was whether OSC can decline to investigate an alleged arbitrary FOIA withholding under § 1216 simply because the three predicates of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) have not been satisfied. AFL argued that § 1216 independently requires OSC to investigate such allegations, regardless of whether a court has already adjudicated a FOIA violation and triggered the disciplinary provisions of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). OSC countered that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) supplies the exclusive enforcement pathway.

The district court agreed with AFL on the statutory interpretation question. In a 2023 decision, Judge Rudolph Contreras held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate under § 1216. The court reasoned that the two provisions operate in different statutory frameworks, address different stages of the enforcement process, and serve different purposes. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) governs post-litigation discipline; § 1216 governs OSC's independent investigative authority. Having reached that conclusion, however, the district court then held that § 1216(c)'s reference to seeking corrective action "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved" committed the decision whether to investigate to OSC's discretion—and declined to order any investigation. After OSC reconsidered and again declined to act, the district court dismissed AFL's remaining claims, and AFL appealed.

The D.C. Circuit never reached the merits. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Gregory Katsas held that AFL lacked Article III standing on both of the theories it pressed.

AFL's first theory was that OSC's refusal to investigate had caused it a concrete informational injury by preventing it from obtaining the records it had requested from DOJ under FOIA. The court rejected this on causation and redressability grounds. The causal chain AFL advanced was jaw-droppingly attenuated: if OSC had conducted a § 1216 investigation, it *might* have pursued corrective or disciplinary action before the Merit Systems Protection Board; the MSPB *might* have sanctioned the responsible DOJ employees; and DOJ *might* then have produced the requested records. Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Heckler v. Chaney*, Judge Katsas observed that causation cannot rest on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors not before the court—here, the MSPB and DOJ—as well as speculation about how OSC might exercise its enforcement discretion. Redressability, the court emphasized, turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not on whether its rationale might persuade non-parties.

AFL's second theory was more creative. It argued that, even if it could not show that an OSC investigation would lead to disclosure of the requested records, it was independently entitled to the notices and information that § 1214 requires OSC to provide to complaining parties during investigations of prohibited personnel practices. Because § 1216(c) permits OSC to investigate FOIA withholdings "under section 1214," AFL contended that the § 1214 notice provisions should apply, giving AFL a right to information about the investigation's progress. The D.C. Circuit rejected this theory as well. The court noted that § 1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates no entitlement to receive information. And AFL itself acknowledged—citing the Sixth Circuit's decision in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011)—that OSC's decision whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without any alleged entitlement to information, AFL could not establish a concrete informational injury. And because OSC need not provide § 1214 notices during a § 1216 investigation, a court order compelling such an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL's claimed informational harm.

The court thus vacated the district court's judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The merits question—whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits § 1216—went unanswered.

This is where the doctrinal picture gets complicated. The district court's statutory interpretation ruling remains on the books. It was never reversed, because the D.C. Circuit concluded it lacked jurisdiction to reach it. Under the standard rule that a jurisdictional dismissal leaves the lower court's substantive reasoning without precedential effect, the district court's analysis of § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is technically non-binding. But it is the only judicial decision to address the question, and it may well influence how OSC, agencies, and future litigants think about the scope of OSC's FOIA oversight authority.

The textual and structural arguments on the merits are genuinely interesting. The case for treating the provisions as independent rests on several features of the statutory scheme. Section 1216 was enacted as part of the Civil Service Reform Act and uses mandatory language: OSC "shall" investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), by contrast, was enacted as an amendment to FOIA itself and uses a different trigger—judicial findings—to activate a disciplinary proceeding. The two provisions address different ends of the enforcement spectrum: § 1216 authorizes investigation and corrective action; § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) requires discipline after a court has already adjudicated a violation. One can read them as complementary rather than conflicting, with § 1216 providing OSC an independent investigative pathway and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creating a distinct, post-litigation disciplinary mandate.

The case for treating § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as a limitation on § 1216 is less straightforward but not frivolous. Congress specifically legislated in the FOIA context about when and how OSC should respond to arbitrary withholdings, and it chose to do so through a judicially triggered mechanism. One might argue that by specifying the predicates for OSC action in the FOIA statute itself, Congress intended to occupy the field—making the three predicates not merely triggers for a disciplinary proceeding but limits on OSC's authority to act on FOIA-related misconduct at all. Under this reading, § 1216's general mandate to investigate arbitrary withholdings cannot be deployed in the FOIA context without satisfying FOIA's own enforcement conditions.

The district court rejected this reading, and the textual evidence arguably supports that conclusion. Nothing in § 1216 suggests it is subject to FOIA's enforcement provision, and nothing in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) purports to be exclusive. The provisions use different operative verbs, address different enforcement stages, and arise from different statutory frameworks. The district court's reading also finds some support in *Carson*, where the Sixth Circuit held that § 1216 investigations are governed by OSC's discretion as to whether to follow § 1214 procedures—a conclusion that presupposes § 1216 establishes an investigative authority operating independently of the FOIA judicial-trigger mechanism.

But *Carson* is a mixed signal. While it assumed § 1216's applicability to FOIA violations, it also treated OSC's investigative decisions under § 1216 as committed to agency discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*—a holding that, if adopted by the D.C. Circuit, would render § 1216's "shall" directive largely unenforceable. The D.C. Circuit did not reach that question either, and the district court's discretion-based dismissal remains unreviewed on appeal.

This leaves the architecture of FOIA oversight in a genuinely uncertain state. OSC maintains that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)'s three predicates must be satisfied before it can investigate FOIA violations. The only district court to address the question held otherwise, but did so in a ruling that has been vacated for lack of jurisdiction. The D.C. Circuit's standing analysis, meanwhile, may have quietly closed the courthouse door to litigants seeking to challenge OSC's position. Any future plaintiff trying to compel a § 1216 investigation will face the same formidable standing barriers that defeated AFL: the causal chain from OSC investigation to document disclosure is too attenuated, and § 1214's notice provisions appear to be discretionary in the § 1216 context. It is difficult to construct a plaintiff who could clear both hurdles.

The practical implications are considerable. If OSC's reading is correct—that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) governs OSC's FOIA enforcement role—then OSC's disciplinary authority is effectively limited to cases where a court has already adjudicated a FOIA violation, awarded fees, and expressed concern about arbitrary withholding. That is a narrow category. If the district court's reading is correct—that § 1216 provides independent authority—then OSC has broader latitude to investigate agency FOIA practices, but the D.C. Circuit's standing analysis makes it difficult for any outside party to compel such investigations. Either way, the result is that OSC retains substantial control over when and whether to investigate FOIA-related misconduct, and private litigants have limited tools to force the issue.

There is a broader institutional question lurking here. When a court identifies a plausible statutory interpretation question of genuine public importance—whether a federal oversight agency has broader or narrower authority to investigate government misconduct—and then declines to resolve it on standing grounds, the legal question persists in a state of unresolved uncertainty. Agencies are left to make their own judgments, litigants lack clear guidance, and the district court's reasoning—however thoughtful—carries only persuasive weight. This is not a criticism of the D.C. Circuit's standing analysis, which is careful and doctrinally sound. But it is worth noting that the standing doctrine that properly restrains judicial power can also, in cases like this one, leave important questions of administrative oversight authority in limbo.

The question whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits § 1216 remains open in the D.C. Circuit. Given the standing barriers the court has erected, it may take a differently situated plaintiff—or congressional action—to force its resolution. In the meantime, the district court's reading lives on, not as binding precedent but as the only judicial word on a question that matters for the future of FOIA accountability.
