Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## Informational Standing Without an Entitlement: What AFL v. Greer Tells Us About the Boundaries of Informational Injury

Informational injury has become one of the more elastic doctrines in Article III jurisprudence—a doctrine that, depending on one's perspective, either usefully adapts standing principles to statutes that create rights of access to government information, or risks eroding the case-or-controversy requirement by recognizing injuries that are not really injuries at all. The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* offers a useful corrective to the more expansive readings of informational standing, drawing a line that, while narrow, clarifies an important doctrinal boundary: a statutory mandate that an agency investigate something does not, by itself, create an entitlement in the complainant to receive information from that investigation.

The case matters because it addresses a recurring dynamic in administrative law: disappointed FOIA requesters turning to alternative oversight mechanisms when direct judicial enforcement proves unsatisfying. AFL wanted the Office of Special Counsel to investigate what it alleged was an arbitrary DOJ policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent. When OSC declined, AFL sued to compel the investigation. The D.C. Circuit never reached whether OSC's refusal was legally correct. Instead, Judge Katsas, writing for the panel, held that AFL lacked standing to ask the federal courts to weigh in at all.

## The Doctrinal Background: Informational Standing and the Entitlement Requirement

Informational standing, as the Supreme Court has developed it, requires that a plaintiff be denied information that a statute entitles him to receive. The canonical cases are *FEC v. Akins*, where the Federal Election Campaign Act entitled certain persons to receive information about political committees, and *Public Citizen v. DOJ*, where the Federal Advisory Committee Act entitled the public to attend certain meetings. In both cases, the statute created a right to receive information; the denial of that right was itself the injury. The D.C. Circuit has applied this framework in numerous cases, most prominently in *ASPCA v. Feld Entertainment*, which held that the denial of information a statute entitled a plaintiff to receive constitutes a concrete informational injury.

The requirement of a statutory entitlement is doing significant work in this doctrine. It ensures that the plaintiff is not simply a concerned citizen who would like to know more, but rather the holder of a legal right to information that the government has withheld. Without that entitlement, the plaintiff's injury is aesthetic or ideological—not the kind of concrete invasion of a legally protected interest that Article III demands.

## The Court's Reasoning: A Mandate to Investigate Is Not an Entitlement to Inform

AFL's second standing theory pressed hard on this entitlement requirement. AFL argued that section 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act requires OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, and that this statutory mandate translated into an informational injury when OSC refused to investigate. If OSC conducted the investigation, AFL reasoned, it would owe AFL the notices and information that section 1214 requires during investigations of prohibited personnel practices—since section 1216(c) provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214" for FOIA violations.

The court rejected this theory at its foundational step. Even assuming section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate, the statute creates no entitlement for the complainant to receive information. Section 1216 is written as a mandate directed to the agency—it says OSC "shall conduct an investigation"—not as a right conferred on the requester. This is a critical distinction. A statutory command that an agency do something is not the same as a statutory right that a person receive something. The former creates a government obligation; the latter creates a private entitlement. Only the latter can support informational standing.

AFL attempted to bridge this gap by arguing that section 1214's notice requirements would attach to any section 1216 investigation, thereby giving AFL an entitlement to receive information during the investigative process. But AFL effectively conceded the point against itself. It acknowledged that under *Carson v. Office of Special Counsel* (6th Cir. 2011), OSC's decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary. If the application of section 1214's notice requirements is discretionary, then there is no entitlement—and without an entitlement, there is no informational injury.

This concession was dispositive in two respects. First, it eliminated the injury element: AFL could not point to any information that a statute entitled it to receive. Second, it eliminated redressability: even if the court ordered OSC to conduct a section 1216 investigation, OSC would retain discretion over whether to provide section 1214 notices, so AFL still might not receive the information it sought.

## Analytical Perspective: The Line Between Process and Substance

The court's reasoning is sound, and it draws a line that the case law has needed for some time. The distinction between a mandate to investigate and an entitlement to information is not merely formalistic. It tracks the underlying logic of the informational standing doctrine, which is rooted in the idea that the plaintiff has suffered the denial of a right that the political branches have chosen to confer. When Congress says an agency "shall investigate," it is establishing a government function, not a private right. When Congress says a person "is entitled to receive" information, it is creating a private right. The standing inquiry must respect that distinction, or else virtually any statutory mandate directed at an agency could be converted into a private right to sue by a plaintiff who claims an interest in the agency's action.

AFL's theory would have substantially expanded informational standing by making any statutory mandate to investigate potentially actionable by any person who triggered the mandate. That is a significant doctrinal shift, and the court was right to resist it. The fact that AFL was the party that prompted the investigation does not give it a judicially cognizable interest in the investigation's fruits unless Congress has said so.

One might argue that the court could have taken a more generous approach to standing while still reaching the same result on the merits. After all, the district court had already concluded that OSC erred in reading section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as limiting its section 1216 authority. A more permissive standing doctrine would have allowed the D.C. Circuit to address that legal question, which has some practical importance for the division of authority between FOIA's own enforcement mechanisms and the Civil Service Reform Act's oversight framework. But the court's choice to resolve the case on standing grounds reflects a deliberate judicial posture: courts should not decide the scope of agency investigative authority at the behest of plaintiffs who have not suffered a judicially cognizable injury, even if the underlying legal question is interesting.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The decision has several implications worth noting.

First, it reinforces the principle that procedural rights do not create standing in a vacuum. The court cited *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* for the proposition that the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete affected interest is insufficient. This is well-established, but *AFL v. Greer* applies it in a context—statutory oversight mechanisms—where the temptation to conflate process with substance is particularly strong. The decision clarifies that a complainant who triggers an agency's investigative process does not, by virtue of that trigger alone, acquire a concrete interest in the investigation's output.

Second, the case has practical implications for FOIA requesters who seek to use OSC as an alternative enforcement tool. The Civil Service Reform Act's section 1216 provides a mechanism for addressing arbitrary FOIA withholdings that is distinct from FOIA's own judicial enforcement provisions. But the decision effectively insulates OSC's exercise of that authority from judicial review at the instance of the requesting party, at least where the requester cannot point to a statutory entitlement to information from the investigation. This does not mean OSC's decisions are wholly unreviewable—other plaintiffs, such as agency employees facing disciplinary action, might have standing in different circumstances. But it does mean that FOIA requesters cannot use section 1216 as a lever to compel additional oversight of agencies that have denied their requests.

Third, the decision may influence how courts treat similar oversight mechanisms in other statutory schemes. Many statutes create agency obligations to investigate, audit, or review government action without conferring rights on the persons who prompt those inquiries. The D.C. Circuit's reasoning suggests that such provisions generally will not support informational standing unless they include an entitlement for the complainant to receive information. This is a meaningful constraint on the use of oversight statutes as vehicles for litigation, and it may channel future challenges toward the direct enforcement mechanisms that Congress has provided—such as FOIA's own judicial review provisions—rather than toward collateral oversight procedures.

Fourth, the court's treatment of the section 1214 notice requirements highlights an asymmetry in the statutory framework that Congress may wish to address. Section 1216(c) allows OSC to investigate FOIA violations "in the same way as" prohibited personnel practices, which might suggest that section 1214's protections for complainants should apply. But the Sixth Circuit in *Carson* and now the D.C. Circuit have treated the application of section 1214 procedures to section 1216 investigations as discretionary. If Congress intends for FOIA complainants to receive notice and information during section 1216 investigations, it could clarify that section 1214's notice requirements apply mandatorily. Until then, the absence of a mandatory notice requirement means that section 1216 investigations do not generate the kind of informational entitlement that standing doctrine requires.

## Conclusion

*AFL v. Greer* is a standing decision that quietly does important doctrinal work. It confirms that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement, not merely a statutory mandate, and it applies that principle to a statutory scheme that creates an oversight mechanism without conferring rights on the persons who trigger it. The decision narrows the path for plaintiffs seeking to convert agency oversight obligations into private causes of action, and it reinforces the D.C. Circuit's insistence that procedural mechanisms, however important, cannot substitute for concrete injury in the Article III inquiry. For FOIA requesters, the message is clear: the primary enforcement mechanism for FOIA violations remains FOIA's own judicial review provision, and collateral oversight statutes are not a straightforward substitute.
