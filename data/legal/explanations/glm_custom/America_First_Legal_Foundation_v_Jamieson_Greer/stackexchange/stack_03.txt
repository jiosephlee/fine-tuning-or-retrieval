Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How attenuated is too attenuated for traceability when an injury requires a chain of actions by multiple non-party agencies?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's first standing theory because the causal chain from OSC's refusal to investigate to AFL not receiving FOIA records is too speculative. The chain requires: (1) OSC investigates, (2) OSC decides to pursue corrective or disciplinary action, (3) MSPB decides to sanction DOJ employees, and (4) DOJ decides to produce records. MSPB and DOJ are not parties to the lawsuit. The court cites FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for the rule that causation can't rely on "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." But many standing cases involve indirect causation through third parties. Where is the line between acceptable indirect causation and impermissibly speculative chains? Does the number of links matter, or is it about the nature of each link—e.g., whether each step is mandatory vs. discretionary?
Answer:
## The line turns on whether each link requires discretionary choices, especially by non-parties

The court's reasoning in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* clarifies that the problem is not simply the *number* of links in the causal chain, but the *nature* of each link—specifically, whether each step requires an independent actor to make a discretionary choice. You're on the right track with the mandatory/discretionary distinction.

### Mandatory links can support standing; discretionary links by non-parties cannot

The court identifies two distinct problems with AFL's causal chain, and both stem from discretion:

1. **OSC's enforcement discretion.** Even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, OSC would still have to *decide* whether to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215. The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney* for the principle that courts generally may not compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion. So the very next step after the ordered investigation is itself discretionary and committed to the defendant's own judgment.

2. **Independent choices by non-party actors.** Beyond OSC's discretionary choice, the chain requires MSPB to *decide* whether to sanction DOJ employees, and then DOJ to *decide* whether to produce records. Both MSPB and DOJ are not parties to the lawsuit. The court quotes *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the rule that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

### Why non-party status matters independently of discretion

The court adds a separate redressability point: a "judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court" (quoting *Haaland v. Brackeen*). This means even if MSPB and DOJ's choices were somehow predictable, a judgment against OSC would not bind them. The chain depends on the rationale of the court's decision *persuading* non-parties to act, which is not how redressability works.

So the problem is compounded: each link requires (a) a discretionary choice, (b) by an actor not bound by the judgment, (c) with no legal mandate compelling the outcome.

### Contrast with cases involving indirect causation that supports standing

The court doesn't say all indirect causation is impermissible. It acknowledges the relaxed standing framework for procedural injuries, under which "courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability." The key is that even relaxed causation and redressability require the plaintiff to show that the procedural violation "resulted in injury" to a "concrete, particularized interest."

Indirect causation can support standing when the intermediate steps are legally mandated rather than discretionary. For example, if a statute required an agency to take action upon receiving a court order, and that action would directly produce the plaintiff's desired information, the causal chain would run through a mandatory link. Here, by contrast, every link after the investigation is discretionary—OSC's decision to pursue enforcement, MSPB's decision to sanction, and DOJ's decision to produce records. No statute compels any of these outcomes.

### The bottom line

The opinion supports this framework: the line between acceptable and impermissible indirect causation turns on whether the causal chain depends on **unfettered discretionary choices by actors not before the court**. A chain with multiple links can support standing if each link is legally mandated. But when the chain requires speculation about how multiple independent actors—especially non-parties—might choose to exercise their discretion, standing fails. It's the combination of discretion and non-party status at *each* link that makes the chain "too speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing."
