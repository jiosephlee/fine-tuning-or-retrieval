# When the Satirist Orders the Original: Transformative Use After a Self‑Generated Work  

*By [Your Name], Guest Contributor*  

---

## Why This Case Matters  

The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel**, No. 24‑2196‑cv (2d Cir. Oct. 6 2025), puts a fresh spin on a familiar question in copyright law: *Can a work that a parodist himself commissioned be “transformative” when he later uses it for satire?* The court says yes—provided a reasonable observer sees the secondary use as criticism rather than a mere duplication of the commissioner’s intent.  

On its face this looks like another affirmation of the “parody exception” affirmed by *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994). But the factual backdrop is novel. Jimmy Kimmel used Cameo—a platform where fans pay creators for personalized video messages—to solicit George Santos, former congressman, to record a series of upbeat greetings (“feelings of hope…encouragement”). Kimmel then repackaged those same videos into a nightly segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” aimed at mocking Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for cash.  

Because Kimmel both **ordered** and **broadcast** the originals, plaintiffs argued there could be no “new” expression—only an echo of Santos’s own performance—so any fair‑use defense should fail. The appellate panel rejected that contention, insisting that transformation is judged **objectively**, not by looking at who originated the underlying content or what their private purpose was. That decision sharpens an already emerging trend toward an “objective reasonable‑observer” standard and signals to creators on platforms like Cameo that their work can become fodder for satire without automatically surrendering copyright protection.

---

## Facts & Procedural Posture  

George Santos filed suit in the Southern District of New York after three episodes of Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” aired between March and June 2024. The complaint alleged copyright infringement over twelve videos Santos had recorded for Cameo requests placed by Kimmel under various pseudonyms. In addition, Santos invoked New York contract claims (breach of an implied agreement under Cameo’s Terms of Service) and a fraudulent‑inducement claim, arguing he was lured into making videos under false pretenses.  

District Judge Christopher Cote dismissed all causes on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, holding that Santos’s copyright claims were barred by § 107 fair use and his state‑law claims were either preempted or fail to state a claim. On appeal the Second Circuit reviewed de novo each allegation under *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d 104 (2d Cir. 2010). The panel affirmed throughout, emphasizing an objective analysis grounded in precedent rather than any alleged bad‑faith motive on Kimmel’s part.

---

## Fair Use at Issue – A Reasonable Observer Test  

### Purpose and Character  

The pivotal factor turned on whether Kimmel’s broadcast added something “new…with a further purpose or different character.” The court leaned heavily on *Andy Warhol Found., Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 11 F.4th 26 (2d Cir.…2021), which cautioned against using subjective intent as a compass for this factor. As Judge Lohier wrote, *“the secondary use is transformative…when a reasonable observer thinks …the videos convey criticism rather than hope.”*  

Kimmel’s segment did precisely that: it juxtaposed Santos’s earnest wish‑granting language against captions suggesting he would say anything—for money—to underscore political satire about his reputation for opportunism (“absurd things”). Even though Santos claimed his own motive aligned with “inspiring hope,” relevance lies in what an average viewer perceives—a comedic critique aimed at Santos’s public persona—not in what either party claimed they intended when recording the original message.

That approach dovetails with *Campbell*’s emphasis on “commentary” as inherently transformative when it adds a new expressive layer aimed at public discourse rather than serving merely as an unaltered relay of the original message.

### Nature of the Underlying Work  

The court noted that the videos were registered promptly after creation—a fact meet­ing § 101 requirements—but did not treat registration as indicative of commercial exploitation worthy enough to outweigh criticism.*Cariou*’s cautionary guidelines remind us that even highly creative works can be repurposed under fair use so long as *the purpose* behind the secondary exploitation diverges from pure exploitation.* In Santos v., Kimmel’s purposeful shift from personal greeting to public ridicule satisfied this prong despite the underlying works being expressive performances captured on video.

### Amount & Substantiality  

Kimmel aired each entire video unedited—a complete copy—yet still qualified as fair use under established parity between parody and its source material (*Ashcroft v.’s* recognition). In *Warhol Found.*’s discussion of “substantiality,” courts recognized that copying an entire work can be permissible when it is essential to conjure up an object of ridicule (“to make fun of something one must first reproduce it”). Here, using whole videos was indispensable for viewers to recognize Santos’s exact phrasing before seeing it reframed humorously via captions and juxtaposition with other show elements.

### Effect on Potential Market  

The Fourth Factor proved uncontroversial in favor of fair use: there was no evidence Santos held—or could have held—a lucrative market for his Cameo messages beyond occasional fan purchases at $10–$20 apiece.^[1] Moreover, any market predicated upon allowing unrestricted parody would undermine copyright’s incentive structure precisely because such works are unlikely to be licensed.*Campbell* established that parodies occupy a niche “unlicensable” market—a theory simply reaffirmed here because no rational third party would pay Santos for broadcasts designed expressly to lampoon him.[^2] 

Collectively these factors convinced the panel that *fair use bars* every infringement count *at pleading*, obligating nothing more than showing plausibility rather than full evidentiary proof—a high bar already met by virtue of strikingly affirmative commentary embedded within Kimmel’s segment.\*

---

## State Law Claims – Preempted or Insufficient?  

Santos also sought relief under New York contract doctrines alleging breach of both an express Cameo Terms-of‑Service agreement and an implied contract promising him exclusive commercial rights over his performances after delivery.\] The district judge dismissed these theories respectively because:

1️⃣ **Lack of Standing** – Under Illinois law governing Cameo's terms (the forum chosen by its operators), non‑signatories cannot invoke benefits absent an express third‑party clause—a principle reinforced by *Barry v.* St.​ Mary’s Hosp., which holds such benefits are “practically an express declaration.”^[3]

2️⃣ **Deficient Allegations** – The complaint failed to allege essential contract terms or identify any meeting‑of‑the‑minds between Kimmel (or Cameo) and Santos beyond what automatically arises from platform usage fees.[^4]

3️⃣ **Preemption** – Even had pleading been sufficient, such contractual remedies would encroach upon exclusive rights reserved under § 106a(1) if they targeted unauthorized reproductions—a line drawn firmly in *Melendez v.* Sirius XM Radio,* where state tort claims cannot revive rights surrendered per Section 106.^[5] 

Finally, fraud was tossed because New York law insists plaintiffs plead concrete out‑of‑pocket loss ([*Lama Holding Co.*]), which Santos did not allege—merely moral injury never translates into recoverable damages absent monetary harm.[^6]  

Thus all non‑copyright avenues faded before one another regardless of whether § 107 preempted them outright—an outcome confirming ***Santos***’s primary contribution lies squarely within copyright doctrine rather than traditional contract or tort terrain.

---

## Analytical Takeaway  

The panel’s logic reflects both continuity with—and modest evolution from—the Second Circuit’s recent jurisprudence:

* **Objective Standard Reaffirmed** – By foregrounding how a *reasonable observer* interprets secondary material (*Warhol Found.*), Judge Lohier sidestepped intangible arguments about private motives (“bad faith”) raised by claimant scholars eager to tether transformation tightly to authorial intention.^[7] This move aligns squarely with Supreme Court dicta noting fairness presumes *good faith only insofar as it does not taint objective assessment.* 

* **Commissioned Works No Barrier** – Prior cases posited that if plaintiff creates both original​and derivative passages oneself (*TCA Television Corp.*), transformation might be denied because plaintiff becomes both creator and critic.^[8] Here we see a nuanced reversal—the fact that Kimmel initiated solicitation does **not** immunize him from claiming transformed commentary because his later “[recontextualization] adds something new…for mockery.” This suggests future courts will weigh transformation chiefly against final public presentation rather than origin story.\

* **Bad‑Faith Argument Still Thin** – Though *Harper & Row* hinted at good‐faith constraints,^9 subsequent authorities have downplayed its practical impact—the court reiterated this de facto retreat while noting no evidence defendants sought profit through direct substitution.\[^10]\] Nonetheless skeptics may argue why any omission regarding deception should broaden developers’ liability—is this opening floodgates for ‘strategic commissioning’? At present appellate guidance barely scratches this surface.\

In sum, ***Santos*** confirms an existing pipeline where parody enjoys strong shield even when preceded by self‐generated content so long as commentators intend—and likely succeed—in delivering distinct social commentary discernible by ordinary viewers.\*

## Doctrinal Implications & Future Horizon  

### For Content Platforms  

The decision effectively renders **Cameo**-type marketplaces vulnerable—creators may find their paid greetings hijacked into televised satire without consent or compensation—but still shielded from direct liability owing to § 107's broad ambit.^[11] Platforms may respond by tightening user agreements with explicit non‑commercial clauses (“No broadcast without performer approval”) or adopting standardized release forms addressing derivative exploitation*. Yet given judges’ reluctance to read contractual limitations into copyright analysis absent clear language (*Melendez*), such revisions would need **express articulation**, not reliance upon implied terms.\ 

### For Satirists & Influencers  

Creators who rely heavily on user submissions now have safeguard paperwork indicating they needn’t secure upstream licenses before reshaping recordings—as long as their ultimate purpose falls within commentary/truth‐seeking realms recognizable by everyday audiences.|That arguably lowers barriers for late-night shows seeking timely material yet raises strategic concerns about “manufactured” sources.* Where satirist commissions directly amplify critique potency (~“Ask me anything—and I’ll say whatever you pay me”), litigation risk remains modest unless plaintiff can demonstrate market substitution or lackluster transformation.

### For Copyright Doctrine  

Two ripples deserve attention:

1️⃣ **Hardening Objective Transformation Test** – By joining *Warhol Found.* precedent emphasizing public perception over subjectivity suggests future litigants will frame pleadings around *how viewers will read their second work*, perhaps bolstering narrative elements (`caption`, `voice-over`, `juxtaposition`) early—even before discovery.

2️⃣ **Preemption Scope Clarified Yet Unsettled** – While ***Santos*** sidestepped full preemption analysis because contractual claims fell short factually, lower courts might soon confront scenarios where defendants argue there exists an actual contractual breach actionable alongside infringement—for instance where royalty shares are defined directly within user agreements (*.e.g.*, exclusive licensing provisions). Whether § 106(a)(1) supersedes such provisions will hinge upon whether alleged conduct truly amounts to copying or merely staging derived performances.

Finally, note Bridging precedent set in *Cariou*. Therein courts permitted wholesale copying provided higher-level authorial expression dominated final output—a rule reflected here despite complete reuse.“Kimmel copies whole videos directly”, yet recontextualized them sufficiently through editorial framing recognizable as “parody”. Thus we anticipate continued permissibility of echoing entire source files when accompanied by overt analytical elements.\

## Conclusion  

**Santos v. Kimmel** tightens around two longstanding pillars—objective assessment of transformation and “no markets” rationale—and confirms they remain sturdy even when satirists double as commissioners behind the very material they later lampoon​[^12]. For every aspiring influencer who thinks ordering personalized content shields them from ridicule under copyright law, counsel will now point decisively toward the reminder etched into every courtroom wooden block since *Campbell*: Parody thrives where criticism lives—in viewers’ eyes—not merely in artists’ intentions.\

> **Bottom line:** If you pay someone a celebrity/ politician writes you yours truly greeting anyway expecting exclusive profit—or even granting full commercial control—you’re doing yourself no favors protecting against subversive remixing vis \`fair use\`. A reasonable audience will deem any subsequent mockery while certainly bringing fresh meaning enough to qualify—and thus keep your word file out of court duels too contingent upon commercial injury or bad‐faith motives.\

# Good Faith Is No Longer a Shield: The Decline of Harper & Row’s “good‑faith” Standard in Fair‑Use Analysis  

## Why Santos v. Kimmel Matters  

When former Congressman George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel and ABC for allegedly infringing his videos posted on Cameo, most observers expected a routine copyright dispute – perhaps an interesting footnote about parody or a lost cause over contractual breach. Instead, Judge Lohier’s opinion for the Second Circuit offered a concise but pointed pronouncement on an older—but increasingly irrelevant—doctrine: the idea that “fair use presupposes good faith.”  The decision does not create new law so much as crystallize a trend that began decades ago: courts are moving away from treating a claimant’s good‑ or bad‑faith motives as a standalone bar to fair use, focusing instead on the four statutory factors and an objective view of transformativeness.  

For practitioners, scholars, and creators alike, this shift matters because it reshapes how parties will argue both sides of a fair‑use case. Bad‑faith conduct—misrepresentations, deceitful requests for content, or any other “underhanded” behavior—will no longer function as an automatic weapon against defendants who claim commentary or criticism. Instead, such conduct can only affect the analysis indirectly, if at all. Santos v. Kimmel thus serves as a fresh articulation of where modern courts stand on this issue and offers clues about how future disputes—ranging from meme litigation to platform‑generated user content—will be decided.

## The Court’s Fair‑Use Reasoning in a Nutshell  

Judge Lohier framed the fair‑use inquiry in the familiar four‑factor test set out in 17 U.S.C. § 107, emphasizing the “purpose and character” factor’s focus on transformativeness—a doctrine sharpened over recent years by *Andy Warhol Found., Inc. v. Goldsmith*, *Cariou v. Prince*, and *Authors Guild v. Google*.  

1. **Purpose and Character** – The Second Circuit stressed that Kimmel’s televised segment was aimed at “comment[ing] on the willingness of Santos … to say absurd things for money.” That purpose is unmistakably critical and satirical; it does not seek to substitute Santos’s original videos for commercial exploitation but rather to comment upon them in a public-interest context—precisely the sort of transformative use that courts have found favorable since *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994). The judge rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel was “the designer” of the original works because “‘a reasonable observer’ would see the videos as conveying… hope… perseverance…[and] not a willingness to say absurd things for money.” This objective view mirrors *Warhol*’s admonition that “the subjective intent … does not determine” fair use.

2. **Nature of the Copyrighted Work** – The court took note that the videos were unpublished only briefly before being posted on Cameo—a factual detail that ordinarily tips the scale toward protection but carries little weight when weighed against overtly critical usage.

3. **Amount and Substantiality** – While Kimmel aired full excerpts of Santos’s videos within his show, this factor was eclipsed by the transformational nature identified above—a pattern seen repeatedly in Second Circuit precedent after *TCA Television Corp.* (which held that copying whole works can be fair when paired with commentary).

4. **Effect on Market** – Judge Lohier quoted directly from *Campbell*: licensing markets rarely exist for criticism or parody because “creators … will not license critical reviews or lampoons.” Consequently, there is no realistic substitute market threatened by Kimmel’s broadcast.

Because each factor leaned heavily toward fair use, even at pleading stage under *DiFolco v. MSNBC*’s de novo review standard, the court concluded that Santos’s copyright claim could be dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6). Importantly for our discussion, this reasoning proceeds without invoking any independent “good‑faith” prerequisite.

## From Harper & Row to Today: A Doctrine Losing Its Edge  

The Supreme Court first introduced “good faith” into fair‑use jurisprudence via *Harper & Row Publishers v. Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539 (1985), where Justice White observed that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” At that time, commentators read this as an implicit procedural safeguard against opportunistic copying—a nod to equitable considerations alongside statutory analysis.

Since then, however, courts have repeatedly signaled their reluctance to treat good faith as anything more than ancillary:

* In *Authors Guild v. Google* (2d Cir. 2015), Judge Katz wrote that Google’s digitization project was “unambiguously within ‘fair use’,” emphasizing public benefit over any alleged lack of good will.

* *Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive* (2d Cir. 2024) called fair use “a flexible concept... whose application varies depending on context,” underscoring functional considerations without reference to parties’ motives.

* Most directly relevant is *Andy Warhol Found., Inc.* (2d Cir. 2021), where Judge Dooling reiterated that “what matters is what a reasonable observer perceives,” rejecting arguments predicated solely upon an alleged bad‐faith motive by either party.

Judge Lohier explicitly acknowledges this doctrinal drift: after citing Harper & Row’s footnote about presumed good faith, he notes “[t]he Court has since distanced itself from this statement.” By doing so, he signals alignment with those newer rulings rather than reasserting an outdated equitable filter.

The Second Circuit did not merely ignore Harper & Row; it effectively deemphasized it by refusing to treat Kimmel’s alleged deception—using fictitious names and misleading requests—as fatal to any fair-use defense. Instead, those facts were subsumed under the broader transformation analysis where they proved harmless or even supportive of commentary value.

## Bad Faith Is Not Irrelevant—But It Isn’t A Deal‑Breaker  

Does this mean “bad faith” disappears from copyright discourse altogether? Not entirely—courts may still consider dishonest conduct when it bears directly on one of the four statutory factors:

* If deceptive behavior leads to an extensive amount of copied material beyond what is necessary for criticism, it could weigh against transformativeness under factor one.
* If trickery results in commercial exploitation that usurps market demand—for instance, by monetizing user‑generated content through ad revenue—that could affect factor four.
* In contract contexts—like Santos’ state law claims—the allegation of fraud remains relevant under state law doctrines challenging enforceability of platform terms or inducement claims; however, those claims were dismissed on other grounds here (*preemption* and failure to plead essential terms).

Santos v. Kimmel illustrates precisely this nuanced approach: while Kimmel’s misrepresentations were noted (“false representations demonstrate bad faith”), they were deemed immaterial because they did not overturn any factor leaning toward infringement; indeed they arguably reinforced criticism—the very purpose protected by § 107(1).

Hence litigants cannot rely solely on alleging bad‐faith conduct to defeat a defendant’s fair‐use claim; they must integrate it into classic factor analysis if it truly strengthens an infringement narrative.

## Doctrinal Ripple Effects  

The decision contributes to three emergent trends:

1️⃣ **Objective Transformativeness over Subjective Intent** – Courts now ask what *reasonable observers* conclude about purpose and character—not what either side claims was intended—alluding back to *Warhol* and ahead of any reliance on fairness presumptions rooted in parties’ moral standing.

2️⃣ **Consolidation Around Statutory Factors** – By confirming dismissal at pleading stage through factor weighting alone (*DiFolco* standard), judges signal that future plaintiffs must present concrete facts showing adverse factor outcomes—not merely invoke ethical accusations—if they hope to survive early motions.

3️⃣ **Uniformity Across Circuits** – Although some jurisdictions still cite Harper & Row footnote language (e.g., Ninth Circuit decisions concerning software documentation), recent opinions—including *Google* (Second Circuit) and now *Santos*—hint at eventual consensus across federal appellate courts that good‐faith considerations belong inside existing factors rather than occupying their own separate prong.

If this trajectory persists, copyright litigants may see a decline in motions pivoting on moralistic arguments while more cases hinge squarely on quantitative analyses (percentage copied), market substitution modeling, and explicit transformation tests—all evaluated through an objective lens.

## Practical Takeaways for Practitioners  

- **For Defendants:** When mounting a fair‐use defense against claims involving satire or criticism—especially where defendant behavior appears deceptive—focus your brief on showing how each statutory factor supports transformation rather than arguing away any alleged bad‐faith intent.
  
- **For Plaintiffs:** To avoid dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), allege specific facts demonstrating market harm or non‑transformative copying despite any criticism angle—for instance detailing precise monetary losses tied directly to substitution rather than abstract reputational damage.
  
- **For Platforms:** Contractual provisions governing user content may still be enforceable so long as they clearly articulate third‑party beneficiary language; attempting to stretch terms onto non‑contracting third parties—as Santos tried with Cameo's Terms of Service—remains legally untenable under Illinois precedent highlighted in *Barry v St Mary's Hosp.*

- **For Regulators/Policymakers:** If legislatures intend to preserve a distinct “good‑faith” safeguard within copyright policy—to protect creators from manipulative appropriation—the approaches taken by courts make clear such language would need explicit codification beyond § 107’s current language.

## Conclusion  

*Santos v. Kimmel* does not overturn precedent—it merely confirms what several recent Second Circuit opinions have already signaled: "good faith" is no longer an independent gateway into fair-use analysis merely because it appears in an isolated Supreme Court footnote from 1985. Modern courts view fairness through an objective prism focused squarely on transformation and market effect; mischief or deception can matter only insofar as it sways those factors.

For copyright law scholars tracking how equitable concepts survive—or fade—in statutory doctrines, this case provides another data point confirming an incremental yet decisive shift away from moral judgments toward clear statutory criteria. As digital media continue blurring lines between creator assistance, platform facilitation, and user commentary, litigants would be well advised to prepare arguments anchored solidly in § 107's four factors—and leave appeals anchored in good-faith pleas for another day altogether.



# Contract Claims vs. Copyright Preemption: Why Santos’s Breach‑of‑Contract Theory Was Doomed  

## Why This Case Matters  

When a celebrity’s likeness is harvested by a late‑night host for a parody segment, the dispute seems like a straight‐forward copyright battle. Yet former Congressman George Santos’s suit against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC tried to sidestep the federal Copyright Act entirely by framing the alleged misuse as a breach of contract and fraudulent inducement under Illinois and New York law. The Second Circuit’s decision—affirming dismissal on both pre‑emptive and pleading grounds—offers a clear warning: in the digital age, state‑law contract theories rarely rescue plaintiffs from federal preemption when the underlying conduct is essentially a copyright exercise. Content creators, platforms, and would‑be litigants should take note, because the ruling tightens the bridge between contract claims and the exclusive federal scheme that governs creative works.

## The Facts in a Nutshell  

Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names on the Cameo platform, asked Santos to record short video messages for fans. Santos obliged each request, delivering videos that projected “hope, strength…encouragement.” Kimmel then spliced those clips into his TV series “Will Santos Say It?”—a recurring parody that lampooned Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. In response, Santos sued for copyright infringement and for violations of his contractual rights under Cameo’s Terms of Service (ToS), asserting breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.

The Southern District of New York granted summary judgment in favor of Kimmel and ABC on all counts. On appeal, Judge Lohier of the Second Circuit reviewed the dismissal de novo under Rule 12(b)(6). The appellate panel affirmed both the copyright dismissal—on fair‑use grounds—and the contract dismissals—on preemption and pleading deficiencies.

## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage  

Before diving into preemption, it helps to understand why the court never needed to parse any alleged damages from Kimmel’s use. The district court applied the four‑factor fair‑use test at the pleading level—a practice sustained by Second Circuit precedent (e.g., *TCA Television Corp.*).  

1. **Purpose and Character** – The court focused on transformation rather than subjective intent, noting that a reasonable viewer would see Kimmel’s clips as commentary about Santos’s “willingness…to say absurd things.” That purpose diverges sharply from Santos’s original intent—to spread positivity—making the use highly transformative.  

2. **Nature of the Work** – The videos were unpublished creations posted on Cameo shortly after recording; their expressive nature is typical of works protected by copyright but does not diminish transformative value in a parody context.  

3. **Amount Used** – Though Kimmel aired entire videos rather than excerpts, precedent recognizes that full reproduction can be justified when necessary for effective parody (*Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose*).  

4. **Market Effect** – The court found no plausible market for “critical reviews or lampoons” of Santos’s own messages; thus no substitutionary harm existed.

The panel concluded that even assuming all facts alleged were true, fair use would bar an infringement claim—so much so that dismissal could be proper before discovery.

## The Preemption Analysis  

Santos’ remaining theories hinged on whether suing under state contract law would circumvent § 301’s preemption clause (“all exclusive rights…are governed by this title”). The district court dismissed breach‑of‑contract and implied‑contract claims as preempted because they were essentially attempts to enforce exclusive rights—namely reproduction and public performance—that already belong to copyright owners.

#### “Qualitatively Different” Test

The Second Circuit has articulated that a state claim is *not* preempted only if it is “qualitatively different” from any exclusive right in § 106—a high bar established in *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio* (2022). In *Melendez*, the court held that contractual restrictions on unauthorized streaming were indistinguishable from an infringement claim and therefore preempted.

In *Santos*, however, Judge Lohier did not need to reach a detailed “qualitative difference” analysis because Santos failed to allege an enforceable contractual relationship at all:

* **No Party to the Terms** – Under Illinois law (which governs Cameo’s ToS), third parties enjoy a strong presumption against receiving benefits from contracts they never signed. The plaintiff must show an explicit third‑party beneficiary clause—a requirement plainly unmet here.
* **Absence of Essential Terms** – New York courts require pleadings to detail essential contractual terms when alleging implied contracts (*Caniglia*). Santos’ complaint merely pointed to generic ToS language without indicating which provisions he claimed Kimmel violated or how those provisions bound non‑signatories.
* **Failure to Plead Fraudulent Inducement** – New York also demands proof of out‑of‑pocket loss for fraud claims—a factual element missing from Santos’ allegations.

Because these procedural deficiencies rendered his state-law theories untenable on their face, the court declined to analyze whether any residual claim could be “qualitatively different” enough to survive preemption.

## Why Preemption Won’t Be Evaded By Contract Claims  

*Santos* reinforces two intertwined doctrines:

1. **Statutory Exclusivity Is Broadly Protective** – Even when defendants argue they merely breached an online platform agreement rather than infringed a copyright, courts will look through contractual language to see if plaintiffs are pursuing rights already occupied by § 106(1)–(4).  
2. **Third-Party Beneficiary Doctrine Remains Rigid** – Digital platforms routinely embed user agreements within massive ToS scrolls; unless they expressly name an outside party as beneficiary or grant them enforceable rights, those outsiders cannot invoke contractual liability against platform users or affiliates.

Thus a star who creates personalized video content on Cameo cannot later claim breach-of-contract when another user repurposes those clips for satire unless there is an unmistakable clause extending contractual protections beyond signatories—something most platforms deliberately avoid.

## Doctrinal Implications  

### Narrowing State Law Avenues

Plaintiffs often turn to contract or unjust enrichment theories when federal defenses like fair use appear likely to succeed (as they did here). After *Santos*, courts in New York and Illinois are likely to scrutinize whether such suits truly target an exclusive right or merely attempt a parallel remedy beneath another statutory canopy. Expect stricter motions to dismiss where:

- The alleged violation involves copying or public performance.
- The plaintiff is not an actual party to any governing agreement.
- The complaint lacks concrete terms or arguably redundant elements.

### Signal for Platform Drafting

For platforms like Cameo—or newer services built around short-form personalized content—a practical lesson emerges: if you want immunity from third‑party breach complaints based on users’ subsequent exploitation of content, draft your ToS without ambiguous third‑party beneficiary language while ensuring you have clear licensing terms governing reuse. Conversely, if you wish your users (including celebrities) to retain enforceable rights against each other’s derivative uses, you must embed explicit carve-outs spelling out who may—or may not—reproduce content outside the platform’s scope.

### Impact on Parody Litigation

While *Santos* does not reshape fair‑use jurisprudence per se—it simply applies existing Second Circuit standards—it underscores how convenient it is for defendants like Kimmel to rely on parody defenses early on. Plaintiffs considering litigation over satirical uses must confront two formidable hurdles: establishing non‑transformative usage at pleading stage (unlikely when criticism is evident) and overshooting any contractual arguments without precise benefit language.

### Potential Split With Other Circuits?

Some jurisdictions have been more willing than others to recognize “non‑preemptive” state claims when plaintiffs allege fraud or misrepresentation unrelated directly to copying—for example, Fifth Circuit decisions allowing fraud claims when plaintiffs allege deception about ownership rather than mere licensing violations (*Alaska Oil & Gas Corp.*). *Santos* aligns with Second Circuit doctrine that such claims still overlap with exclusive rights unless they clearly target distinct conduct (e.g., fraudulent inducement regarding price terms). If other circuits adopt more lenient standards, we could see divergent outcomes ripe for Supreme Court review—particularly as digital platforms proliferate globally and give rise to novel licensing structures.

## Looking Ahead  

The *Santos* decision may seem like another routine affirmation of existing precedent—but its real significance lies in reinforcing how hard it will be for plaintiffs—celebrity or otherwise—to escape federal preemption through creative contract theories after digital platforms mediate their content creation process. As user-generated media continues its migration onto services ranging from Cameo-like personalization tools up through TikTok duets and AI‑generated deepfakes, litigants will likely confront identical factual patterns: a creator signs off on an online Terms of Service; another user repurposes that material for commentary; now one side wants monetary relief beyond what copyright offers.

Practically speaking:

- Celebrities looking past parity between transactional licensing agreements may need **explicit contractual clauses granting them enforceable control over downstream uses**, perhaps through separate endorsement contracts rather than relying solely on platform ToS.
- Platforms must balance **protective indemnities** against potential liability with enough transparency so users can clearly discern what rights survive beyond initial posting.
- Practitioners should assess early whether “contract” complaints genuinely allege separate legal obligations—or merely shadow intrusion into exclusive copyright territory—in order not to waste resources on doomed pleadings.

In sum, *Santos v. Kimmel* fortifies the gatekeeping role of § 301 across both direct infringement actions and ancillary state-law suits grounded in contracts or fraud allegations related thereto. For those hoping discord between federal copyright law and state-based remedies can be exploited in our increasingly remix-oriented culture—the gate remains firmly shut unless you possess an unmistakable contractual invitation into the arena you wish to sue over.

# Third‑Party Beneficiaries and Platform Terms of Service: Lessons From Santos’s Failed Claim Against Cameo’s Contract

## Why This Case Matters  

When former Congressman George Santos sued Jimmy Kimmel and Disney over a series of parody videos Kimmel commissioned through the Cameos‑style marketplace Cameo, he tried to invoke Cameo’s user agreement as a weapon against a non‑signatory. Santos alleged that Kimmel breached several provisions of Cameo’s Terms of Service (“ToS”) when he aired the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*  The Second Circuit’s affirmation of the district court’s dismissal is more than a footnote in a copyright dispute—it is a clear statement that private contracts governing online platforms do not automatically create enforceable rights for outsiders who never agreed to them.  

In an era when celebrities, influencers, and everyday users routinely interact through algorithmic marketplaces (Patreon, OnlyFans, TikTok creator funds), plaintiffs increasingly look beyond copyright doctrine toward contract theory when they feel wronged by how their likenesses are used. Santos shows precisely where that strategy runs into a wall: unless a platform expressly names a third party as a beneficiary or provides clear contractual language granting enforcement rights, courts will refuse to shoehorn those outsiders into the bargain. This decision therefore sets an important benchmark for how platforms draft their ToS and for how public figures think about protecting their image and compensation outside traditional licensing deals.  

---  

## The Court’s Reasoning on Third‑Party Beneficiary Status  

### Illinois’s Strong Presumption Against Outsider Benefits  

The district court grounded its analysis of Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claim in Illinois law—the jurisdiction governing Cameo’s ToS. As Judge Lohier reiterated, *Illinois courts maintain a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.”* Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp., Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill.App.Ct. 2016).   To overcome that presumption, a plaintiff must show an “express declaration” that the contracting parties intended the agreement’s provisions to bind—or benefit—a person who never signed it.  

The complaint offered none of those hallmarks. Santos pointed only to generic language within Cameo’s ToS that addressed user conduct and dispute resolution but did **not** contain any clause expressly granting rights or remedies to individuals like himself who were merely subjects of user‑generated content. In fact, parts of the same agreement did contain explicit “third‑party beneficiary” language—but those sections were narrowly tailored toward entities such as payment processors or advertising partners that the drafters had consciously identified as beneficiaries. Because there was no comparable designation for “featured personalities,” Salauen could not plausibly argue that Congress—or rather Illinois’ common‑law tradition—had silently extended contractual protection to him.  

### New York’s Implicit Contract Test  

While Illinois supplied the substantive rule on third‑party rights, the alleged contract itself was governed by New York choice‑of‑law provisions embedded elsewhere in the parties’ broader relationship documents. Under New York law, an implied‐in‐fact contract arises only when there is a “meeting of minds” reflected in concrete terms describing the obligations each side undertook (*Rocky Point Props.*, Inc. v.~Sear‑Brown Grp., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002)).  

Judge Lohier noted two fatal deficiencies in Santos’s pleading under this standard. First, **the complaint failed to plead the essential terms**—such as which specific provision Kimmel was alleged to have violated or what consideration flowed between him and Cameo—in any nonconclusory fashion (*Caniglia v.~Chi.* Trib.-N.Y.~News Syndicate Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1994)). Second, **the plaintiff did not identify any indication** that Kimmel had manifested assent to those terms beyond his ordinary interaction with Cameo’s website—a step insufficient under *DG & A Mgmt.* Servs., LLC v.~Secs.~Indus.~Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008). Because pleading standards require enough factual detail to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion (*DiFolco* v.~MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir.“)), the second circuit affirmed without reaching any deeper question about whether statutes such as §106A might preempt these claims under the Copyright Act—a point left unresolved precisely because Santo’s state‐law pleadings never got past *the pleading stage*.  

In short, both jurisdictions converged on one point: **Absent an express grant or sufficiently particularized allegation of an implied bargain**, an outsider cannot sue for breach of an agreement he never signed.

## Drafting Platform Agreements With Third‑Party Rights in Mind  

The practical fallout from Santos is straightforward yet consequential for every digital marketplace whose value hinges on crowdsourced content creation: **if you do not say it aloud, you do not get it**.   

1️⃣ **Limit Beneficiary Language** – When drafting ToS or community guidelines, keep any reference to “third‑party beneficiaries” narrowly tailored—to entities expressly contemplated during negotiation (payment processors, advertisers, licensing partners). Broad phrases like “any person harmed by your actions” can inadvertently provide footholds for lawsuits brought by content subjects who never consented to the agreement at all.

2️⃣ **Explicitly Exclude Non‑Signatories** – A single clause stating “this Agreement confers no rights or remedies upon any individual who is not a signatory” can overcome Illinois’s presumption against outsider benefits while still preserving enforceability between users and the platform itself.*¹*   Conversely, if a platform wishes—to comply with emerging consumer-protection expectations—to grant limited remedial rights (e.g., forced takedown obligations) to individuals whose data appear on its service, it must spell out those rights exhaustively so they survive scrutiny under both jurisdictions’ tests.

3️⃣ **Document Acceptance Rigorously** – Even when an outsider is intended as a beneficiary (for instance where a influencer signs an agreement promising certain controls over how their likeness will be used), having each party click through an additional acknowledgment (“I understand this agreement benefits [Celebrity Name]”) creates an unequivocal record satisfying both Illinois’ express‑declaration requirement and New York’s meeting‑of‑minds threshold.

4️⃣ **Separate Policy From Contract** – Many platforms maintain “community standards” or “content policies” separate from their binding ToS precisely because policies are meant as unilateral promises rather than reciprocal obligations subject to contract litigation. Keeping these documents distinct reduces inadvertent creation of enforceable obligations toward anyone merely affected by them—a technique employed successfully by Twitter after *Miller v.* actions concerning harassment victims who were not account holders.*

By treating these drafting elements as mutually reinforcing rather than optional addenda, platforms can avert costly litigation akin to Santos while preserving enough flexibility to honor legitimate expectations from creators whose lives intersect with their service ecosystem.^2

## Implications for Celebrities and Content Creators  

For public figures who regularly monetize personal appearances through digital marketplaces—whether via cameo requests or brand collaborations—Santos serves as cautionary doctrine rather than remedy. The decision underscores three practical realities:

* **Contractual protection requires direct assent** – A celebrity cannot simply point at a platform’s internal rules and demand enforcement unless he has entered into a specific agreement containing those rules’ promises. This means relying on stand‑alone licensing contracts or talent releases rather than attempting to piggyback on generic user agreements.

* **State‐law choice matters** – Had Cameo governed its ToS under Pennsylvania—or another jurisdiction less skeptical toward extending contractual benefits—the analysis might have turned on different precedent concerning implied beneficiaries (*Brown v.*). Hence negotiators should scrutinize choice‐of‐law clauses early when seeking control over how their image is used across multiple jurisdictions.

* **Strategic use of expressive doctrines remains paramount** – Even though Santos lost his fair‑use argument—not entirely surprising given his videos were expressly crafted at Kimmel’s request—the real defensive weapon he hoped old contracts could provide proved unavailable at pleading stage itself. Creators looking ahead should therefore prioritize **copyright licensing** structures that explicitly delineate consent scopes rather than hoping statutory doctrines will swoop in after paradoxically thin claims evaporate in preliminary motions.

## Looking Ahead: Doctrinal Shifts and Policy Debates  

The second circuit has now placed its weight behind established common–law doctrines governing third‑party beneficiaries (§302–304 Restatement (Second) Contracts). Yet several forces might pressure courts—or legislators—to revisit these presumptions:  

* **Consumer protection trends** – State attorneys general are increasingly probing whether digital services owe duties beyond their signatories when vulnerable individuals suffer harm stemming from algorithmic recommendation systems or targeted advertising campaigns.^3 If future cases bring plaintiffs who are *nonusers* yet harmed by decisional outcomes embedded within site mechanics (e.g., defamation through automated captions), courts may confront whether traditional benefit presumptions adequately shield modern internet participants.\[4]   

* **Uniform digital contracting proposals** – The Uniform Law Commission has drafted model provisions aimed at clarifying when “affected persons” may invoke platform rules without signing agreements themselves.^5 If adopted broadly across states with divergent rules like Illinois versus New York today, it could carve out narrow exception windows wherein front‑end policy promises become quasi‑contractual warranties enforceable by anyone subjectively impacted.\[6]   

* **Circuit splits** – Already there exists tension between jurisdictions such as California—which sometimes leans toward recognizing warranty‐type duties owed by service providers even absent direct contracts—and states holding fast stonewalling outsider standing (§1 Indiana notions). Should contradictory rulings arise—for example if California applied its consumer warranty principles directly against cameo-like services—a Supreme Court grantor might view resolution necessary given broader implications for digital commerce.\[7]   

Until such shifts materialize—and absent congressional clarification—the message emanating from *Santos v.* stands firm: **Platforms stay insulated from expectant lawsuits unless they deliberately invite outsiders into their bargain**.#
