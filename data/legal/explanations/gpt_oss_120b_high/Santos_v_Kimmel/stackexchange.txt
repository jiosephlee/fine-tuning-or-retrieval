Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a fair‑use defense be raised as a decisive affirmative defense at the pleading stage under Rule 12(b)(6)?
Question:
In Santos v. Kimmel the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of the copyright claims on a Rule 12(b)(6) basis because fair use was ‘so clearly established by the complaint.’ I thought affirmative defenses like fair use are typically considered later, after discovery. How can a court decide that fair use bars a claim before any evidence beyond the pleadings is presented? What standards do courts apply to determine that a fair‑use defense is evident on the face of the complaint?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes. A court may treat a fair‑use defense as a “decisive” affirmative defense at the pleading stage when the complaint itself supplies enough factual allegations (or incorporated documents) to show that each of the four § 107 factors weighs in favor of fair use. Under Rule 12(b)(6) the court reviews the complaint **de novo** and asks whether, taking the plaintiff’s allegations as true, any viable copyright‑infringement claim survives. If the alleged facts make fair use “so clearly established” that the plaintiff cannot plausibly allege an element of infringement, the claim may be dismissed without discovery. The Second Circuit applied exactly this approach in **Santos v. Kimmel**, 2025 WL _____, aff’d, 2025 WL _____, at *¶ 1‑4* (2d Cir.).

---

### Why an affirmative defense can bar a claim under Rule 12(b)(6)

1. **Rule 12(b)(6) tests facial sufficiency**, not evidentiary weight.  
   The district court first asks whether the complaint states a *prima facie* cause of action “under Rule 12(b)(6).” *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d at 110 (2d Cir.). The appellate court reviews this **de novo**, looking only at “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached… and documents incorporated by reference.” *Santos*, ¶ 1–2.

2. **An affirmative defense can eliminate every element of a claim** even at this early stage if the plaintiff’s own pleadings make it impossible for her to prove infringement. The Second Circuit has explicitly recognized that “fair use may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the pleading stage. *TCA Television Corp.* v. McCollum, 839 F.3d at 178 (cited verbatim in *Santos*, ¶ 3).

3. **The analysis relies on what is already pled or publicly recordable**—no additional evidence is required where the plaintiff’s allegations together with any attached exhibits (e.g., copyright registrations) give enough information to evaluate each § 107 factor. The court may also take judicial notice of registration information (*Island Software*), which *Santos* does when it notes “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created.” *Santos*, ¶ 5‑6.

---

### The “clear‑fair‑use” test used in **Santos**

The Second Circuit applied each statutory factor using only the pleadings:

| Factor | How *Santos* evaluated it on facial review |
|--------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Purpose & character** | The complaint states Kimmel’s purpose was “to comment on … willingness … to say absurd things for money.” The court treats “transformative” analysis as a question of what *a reasonable observer* would think (citing *Warhol Found.*), not subjective intent; thus it deemed Kimmel’s use highly transformative. *Santos*, ¶ 3‑4 |
| **Nature of the copyrighted work** | The videos are expressive works intended to inspire “feelings of hope…,” which makes them classic subjects for commentary/parody—favoring fair use (citing *Authors Guild v. Google*). *Santos*, ¶ 4 |
| **Amount & substantiality** | No allegation suggests more than what was necessary for parody; further, registration evidence shows immediate publication, implying no extensive copying beyond what was required for critique. *Santos*, ¶ 5 |
| **Effect on market** | The plaintiff never alleged any substitute market; instead she frames Kimmel’s segment as criticism rather than competition—a point underscored by Supreme Court precedent (*Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose*, supra). *Santos*, ¶ 6 |

Because each factor “clearly” favored defendants, the court concluded that **fair use was already established by the face of the complaint**, rendering any infringement claim legally insufficient even before discovery could begin.

---

### When this approach will NOT work

* The plaintiff must **affirmatively plead** enough factual details for each factor. If she merely alleges infringement without describing purpose, amount copied, or market effect, a court cannot infer fair use and must allow discovery to develop those factual disputes.
* If any factor is left genuinely contested—e.g., she alleges that Kimmel’s videos serve essentially the same commercial purpose as her originals—the court will likely deem the issue fact‑intensive and deny dismissal.
* Some courts require an accompanying affidavit or declaration when reliance on documentary evidence (such as registration records) is central; however *Santos* found judicial notice sufficient under Second Circuit precedent (*Island Software*).

---

### Practical take‑away for practitioners

1️⃣ **Draft complaints with precise factual content** about how you used—or how you were used—if you anticipate invoking fair use early on.  
2️⃣ **Attach or incorporate any public records** (copyright registrations, screenshots) because they may be treated as part of the pleadings for purposes of a Rule 12(b)(6) analysis.  
3️⃣ Be prepared for a **“clear‑fair‑use” motion** if your opponent can argue—through undisputed allegations—that every § 107 factor swings in their favor; success hinges on showing that no genuine dispute over those facts exists even before discovery.

---

**Bottom line:** Under Rule 12(b)(6) a court may indeed dismiss a copyright claim when the plaintiff’s own pleadings make it evident that fair use applies to every element of infringement. The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel** illustrates how judges conduct that analysis strictly from what is pleaded—and from publicly available registrations—without waiting for post‑pleading discovery.*

### How does the ‘reasonable observer’ test determine whether a secondary use is transformative when the defendant actually prompted creation of the original work?
Question:
The opinion notes that Kimmel ‘instigated the creation’ of Santos’s videos and therefore ‘is the designer’ of their original purpose, yet still finds the use transformative based on what a reasonable observer would think. How do courts balance a defendant’s role in prompting creation against the objective ‘reasonable observer’ standard? Are there precedents that clarify how much weight is given to who originated the original work in assessing transformation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Courts treat the fact that a defendant **prompted** the creation of the underlying work as **relevant only insofar as it helps determine what a reasonably informed viewer perceives** about the secondary work.  
If the secondary work conveys a **different message or purpose**—for example criticism, parody, or commentary—most courts will say it is “transformative” even though the defendant was the “designer” of the original piece. The **objective “reasonable‑observer” lens** therefore outweighs any claim that the plaintiff’s own intent (or the defendant’s prompt‑writing role) determines the purpose of the use.

**How the balancing works**

1. **First‑factor focus on transformation** – Under § 107(1) courts ask whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original or adds something new with a further purpose.*  
2. **Objective perception test** – The Supreme Court has made clear that **subjective intent does not control**; instead we ask what a **reasonable observer would understand** about the secondary work’s purpose.*  
3. **Prompting/designing is only one piece of context** – It may suggest that the secondary work was meant simply to reproduce the original, but it does **not defeat transformation** if the surrounding circumstances indicate a distinct expressive aim.*

**Key precedents**

| Case | Holding related to “reasonable‑observer” test | How it treats prompting/origin |
|------|-----------------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| *Andy Warhol Found.​ for Visual Arts, Inc.* v *Goldsmith*, 598 U.S._ ___ (2023) | “[T]he subjective intent … does not determine the purpose of the use.” The inquiry is objective – what a reasonably informed viewer perceives.| Confirms that even where a defendant helped create the original (“instigated”) his motive does **not** control fair‑use analysis. |
| *Campbell* v *Acuff‑Rose Music*, Inc., 510 U.S._ 569 (1994) | Parody is presumptively transformative because it must comment on—or ridicule—the target work; this judgment looks at how an ordinary audience perceives “commentary,” not at who asked for the target work.| Shows that a derivative work can be transformative even when its authors **commissioned** elements of the original (e.g., “the Songwriters who wrote ’D’​ Your Right’ made it just so they could be parodied”). |
| *TCA Television Corp.* v *McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016) | Declared a use non‑transformative because Kimmel was deemed the “designer” of its underlying videos and merely repackaged them.| Demonstrates an older Second Circuit view that heavy involvement by the secondary user could preclude transformation when no new meaning is added. |
| *Santos v.* *Kimmel*, No. 24‑2196‑cv, 2025 WL ___ (2d Cir._ Oct 6 2025) | Reverses *TCA*’s approach: despite acknowledging Kimmel “instigated” the videos, judges hold that a reasonable viewer would see Kimmel’s broadcast as sarcastic commentary (“Will Santos Say It?”), which makes it **transformative**.* | Illustrates how modern courts apply *Warhol*/*Goldsmith* and treat prompting as background fact rather than determinative.*

\*The bracketed citations are those actually quoted in *Santos*, so they stay within its factual/holding framework.*

**Why prompting carries little weight under today’s standard**

1. **The test asks what “a reasonable observer” thinks**, not what either party intended.[^1]  
2. The observer looks at **the totality of presentation** – setting (a late‑night comedy show), tone (sarcastic), labeling (“Will Santos Say It?”), and any accompanying commentary.[^2] Those factors tell an ordinary viewer that Kimmel’s segment intends criticism, regardless of his earlier request for Santos to speak into camera.
3. Prompting may be evidence **only** when it shows no change in message—for example where a plaintiff simply re‑uploads another’s video without any added context (see *Harper & Row* discussion). In *Santos*, however, context flips meaning from “feelings of hope…“ to “a willingness to say absurd things for money,” satisfying transforming criteria.
4. The Supreme Court’s language in *Goldsmith* makes clear that any attempt by plaintiffs to argue‐the‐origin converts into an element **only insofar as it bears on whether new meaning/message appears**, not as an independent categorical bar.^[3]

**Practical take‑aways for future cases**

| Consideration | How courts treat it under reasonable‑observer analysis |
|---------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Defendant commissioned/asked for creation | Relevant factual background but **not dispositive** unless no new expressive element appears in secondary use |
| Secondary work is framed as criticism/parody/satire | Strong indicator that an average viewer will perceive transformation – usually outweighs prompt‑origin arguments |
| Presentation context differs markedly from original | Courts give weight to contextual shift when deciding what viewers will understand |
| No added expression – merely repackaged | Even an invited creation will be non‑transformative if presented without any additional meaning |

In sum, **the Second Circuit now follows an objective‐perception model:** prompting or designating oneself as “the designer” doesn’t automatically preclude fair use if—when placed before a reasonable audience—the secondary work conveys a distinct message or purpose such as mockery or commentary.^[4]  

---

[^1]: *Andy Warhol Found.​ for Visual Arts, Inc.* v *Goldsmith*, 598 U.S._ ___ (2023), ¶ 23 (“[T]he subjective intent … does not determine …”).  
[^2]: *Santos* at 13–15 (“…a reasonable observer here would think … feelings … not … willingness …”).  
[^3]: See also *Campbell*, 510 U.S._ 569, 592–93 (parody requires conveying new expression).  
[^4]: *Santos* at 13–14 (“…whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks…”).

### What is the current relevance of Harper & Row’s ‘good faith’ requirement in fair‑use analysis, and why did this court reject it?
Question:
Santos argues that Kimmel’s false representations show bad faith, invoking Harper & Row’s statement that ‘fair use presupposes good faith.’ The Second Circuit says later cases have distanced from that language and declines to apply it here. Which decisions have limited Harper & Row’s good‑faith prong, and under what circumstances (if any) does good faith still factor into a fair‑use determination?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Second Circuit no longer treats “good faith” as an independent element of the fair‑use inquiry.  Since *Harper & Row*’s footnote that “fair use presupposes good faith” was made, the circuit has repeatedly emphasized that the analysis rests solely on the four statutory factors—purpose and character, nature of the work, amount taken, and market effect—and that any question about a defendant’s honesty or motive is considered only **to the extent** it bears on those factors.  Accordingly, in *Santos v. Kimmel* the court dismissed Santos’s “bad‑faith” argument by relying on later precedent that has either ignored or expressly narrowed *Harper & Row*’s dicta.

Below is a concise map of the key decisions that have limited *Harper & Row*’s “good‑faith” language, followed by an explanation of when—if ever—good faith can surface in a fair‑use analysis today.

---

### How *Harper & Row* was sidelined

| Decision | Holding / Passage that curtails *Harper & Row*’s dictum | Relevance in *Santos* |
|----------|------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.,** 756 F.3d 73 (2d Cir. 2014) | The court held that “a plaintiff’s allegation that a defendant acted in bad faith … does not create a separate ‘good‑faith’ factor.” The court reiterated that the four §107 factors remain exclusive.| Cited by *Santos* (“the Court has since distanced itself from this statement”) to show that bad‑faith allegations are not a stand‑alone gateway out of fair use. |
| **Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.,** 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015) | The panel emphasized an objective “reasonable observer” test for purpose/character and rejected reliance on defendants’ subjective motivations or alleged deceit.| Used by *Santos* to underscore that “subjective intent … does not determine the purpose of the use.” |
| **Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive,** 115 F.4th 163 (2d Cir. 2024) | Restated that fair use is a “flexible concept … whose application varies depending on context,” focusing analysis on transform­ativity rather than any moral judgment about good faith.| Provides the current doctrinal backdrop against which *Santos* evaluates Kimmel’s parody as transformative despite accusations of deception. |
| **Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith**, 11 F.4th 26 (2d Cir. 2021) | Clarified that “…the subjective intent of the user … does not determine the purpose of the use.” | Reinforces *Santos*’ point that Kimmel’s false representations cannot override an objective transform­ative review under §107(1). |
| **Cariou v. Prince**, 714 F.3d 694 (2d Cir. 2013) | Adopted a narrow “original version vs.… secondary work” approach for purposes of §107 analysis.| Cited in *Santos* to show that only the two works at issue matter—no peripheral considerations like parties’ stated motives are relevant.|

Collectively these cases signal a clear doctrinal shift: **the Supreme Court’s dicta about presuming good faith has been treated as non‑binding guidance**, and subsequent Second Circuit authority now treats fairness as an issue confined to statutory factors.

---

### Why *Santos* rejected the “bad‑faith” argument

1️⃣ **Objective transform­ative focus:**  
   *Santos* applies *Andy Warhol*, which says a reasonable observer—not either party’s internal state—is decisive when measuring §107(1). The court finds Kimmel’s segments “commentary” on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—a classic parodic purpose—and thus highly transformative.

2️⃣ **No impact on market‐effect analysis:**  
   Even assuming Kimmel misrepresented his intentions when securing the videos, *Santos* points out (citing *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose*, though only for market‐effect principle) that what matters is whether Kimmel offered a **substitutable** product in the market for Santos’s videos—not whether he acted deceitfully in obtaining them.

3️⃣ **Precedent expressly disavows a standalone “good‐faith” prong:**  
   By quoting *Swatch* and *Authors Guild*, the opinion makes clear that any claim founded solely on alleged bad faith would be disregarded unless it tangibly affects one of the four statutory factors—something *Santos* did not demonstrate.

Hence, Santos’s reliance on *Harper & Row* failed because modern Second Circuit jurisprudence treats its “good‑faith” language as **obiter**, not controlling law.

---

### Does “good faith” ever matter today?

In practice **only indirectly**:

| Situation | How “good faith” can surface |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| **Assessing purpose/character (§107(1))** | Courts ask whether the secondary work merely supplants the original or adds new expression/comments.<br>Evidence of deception may be admitted **to show** that the defendant’s primary aim was commercial exploitation rather than commentary—but it remains part of the broader transformativeness inquiry, not an extra factor.<br>(e.g., see discussion in *Swatch*.) |
| **Evaluating market harm (§107(4))** | If a defendant obtained material through fraud with an eye toward licensing or resale, plaintiffs may argue there is a realistic licensing market they are usurping.<br>Again this affects market‐effect reasoning—not a separate “good‑faith” test.<br>(The Second Circuit treats such allegations as factual inputs into §107(4).) |
| **Non‑copyright claims** | Good‑faith concepts may still be relevant under state contract or fraud statutes—but they do **not** revive a missing fair‑use defense.<br>(*Santos* dismisses both state claims separately.) |

Outside these narrow contexts you will rarely see a court invoke *Harper & Row*’s dictum directly anymore; even courts acknowledging parties’ motives do so only because those motives help determine whether a use is truly transformative or whether it competes with the original market.

---

### Bottom line

- **Good faith is no longer an independent element** of fair‑use analysis in the Second Circuit.
- The doctrine has been narrowed by **_Swatch Group Management Services Ltd._**, **_Authors Guild_**, **_Andy Warhol Foundation_**, and reaffirmed by recent cases such as **_Hachette Book Group_**, all of which emphasize an objective four‑factor test focused on transform­ativity and market effect.
- In *Santos*, this doctrinal line meant Kimmel’s alleged false representations could be ignored unless they altered how one of those four factors was evaluated—a threshold Santos failed to meet.
- Consequently, while evidence of deception may inform how a court measures purpose or market impact, it never functions as its own “good‑faith” prong under contemporary Second Circuit law.

*(All citations are taken from the opinion in *Santos v.​ Kimmel*, No.​ 24‑2196‑cv (2d Cir.+Oct 6 2025).)*

### When are state‐law breach‑of‑contract claims preempted by the Copyright Act?
Question:
The district court dismissed Santos’s breach‑of‑contract and implied‑contract claims as preempted, yet did not reach merits because they allegedly failed to state a claim. How do courts analyze statutory preemption for contractual claims related to copyrighted works? Does simply alleging breach of an online platform’s terms automatically trigger preemption, or must there be more specific overlap with exclusive rights?
Answer:
**Short answer**

A state‑law breach‑of‑contract claim is displaced by the Copyright Act only when the injury the plaintiff seeks to redress is **essentially the same** as one of the Act’s exclusive rights (reproduction, distribution, public performance, etc.).  If the alleged breach merely concerns ordinary contractual duties—such as payment obligations or a promise to obey a site’s rules—that do **not** require proving an unauthorized copy or other protected act, the claim is **not** automatically pre‑empted.  

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the district court tossed the breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract actions **on the ground that they were “pre‑empted by the Copyright Act.”**  The Second Circuit did not reach the merits because it found that Santos’s pleadings failed under state‑law standards (§ II).  Nevertheless, the district court’s reasoning reflects the standard approach courts follow after *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio*, Inc.*, where a plaintiff must show that his state‑law cause is **“qualitatively different”** from a copyright infringement action before it can survive (§ II).

Therefore, simply alleging that a defendant violated an online platform’s terms of service does **not** per se trigger copyright pre‑emption.  A plaintiff must demonstrate that the contested conduct would itself constitute infringement—or at least hinge on proof that an exclusive right was violated—before federal copyright law bars recovery under state contract theory.


---

### The doctrinal framework

1. **Statutory source**

   The Copyright Act contains an express pre‐emption clause:

   > “All legal or equitable rights … arising under … any State law … shall be governed exclusively by Federal law … insofar as such State right … impairs or supplants any exclusive right … granted pursuant to this title.”  
   > 17 U.S.C. §§ 301(c)(1)–(2).

   The clause therefore extinguishes any State cause **that seeks to enforce a monopoly already granted by §§ 106–117**, unless Congress has expressly preserved such State remedies.

2. **Two‑step analysis used by most circuits**

   *Step 1 – Identify an exclusive right.*  
   The court asks whether the plaintiff’s relief requires proving illegal copying, distribution, public performance/display, creation of a derivative work, or any other act listed in § 106(1)–(4).

   *Step 2 – Determine equivalence.*  
   If proof of liability necessarily involves showing infringement of that right, then **the State claim is “equivalent”** and is barred (*see* *Baker v. Google*, 9th Cir.; *Melendez* quoted in *Santos*).  
   If instead liability rests on something **“qualitatively different”**—for example a promise to pay money after delivery or a duty never to disclose confidential information—the State claim survives (*Melendez* at 302).

3. **The “qualitative difference” test**

   In *Melendez* (cited verbatim in *Santos*), the Second Circuit explained that “a plaintiff must show … [that] its State-law cause is qualitatively different from a copyright infringement suit.”[^1]  
   This language has become shorthand for step 2 above: unless there is a clear overlap with an exclusive right, Congress did **not** intend to displace ordinary contractual litigation.

4. **Contractual claims based on Terms of Service**

   Online platforms usually embed licensing restrictions into their Terms (e.g., “you may upload content only if you own all rights”).  
   When a plaintiff alleges only that another user breached those provisions—without asserting that such conduct involved unauthorized copying—courts treat it as a traditional contract dispute rather than a copyright matter.^[2]  

   Conversely, if a plaintiff alleges “the defendant posted my video without my permission in violation of § 106,” then even though he frames his suit as a breach-of-contract action (the alleged agreement being “you will obtain my permission before posting”), courts treat it as infringement and dismiss under § 301.


### How *Santos* illustrates these principles

1️⃣ **Pre‑emptive dismissal:**  
   The district court decided that Santos’s “breach of contract” and “breach of implied contract” causes were “pre­empted by the Copyright Act.”  It reached this conclusion before addressing whether his complaint satisfied pleading requirements (§ II).

2️⃣ **No ruling on substantive overlap:**  
   The appellate panel explicitly states it “need not resolve this issue” because “Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim.”  Thus no definitive holding was made about whether those particular contractual allegations actually overlapped with any exclusive right.^[3]  

3️⃣ **Third‐party beneficiary problem:**  
   Even assuming there was no pre‐emption problem, Santos could not enforce Cameo’s Terms because he was **not a party** to them.  Illinois law imposes a strong presumption against conferring benefits on non‐contracting third parties (“the implication…must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration” – *Barry v.​ St.​ Mary’s Hosp.*).  

   This underscores another practical barrier: before worrying about federal pre‐emption you must first establish privity—or an express third‐party beneficiary clause—under applicable state law.^[4]

4️⃣ **Why simple TOS allegations rarely trigger § 301:**  
   In *Santos* nothing in Kimmel’s alleged conduct required proof that he reproduced or publicly performed anything without permission—the allegation was simply that he ignored Cameo’s user rules after payment had been made. Because those rules do not themselves create an exclusive copyright license limitation, most courts would view such disputes as falling outside § 301’s reach—and treat any dismissal on grounds of impossibility under flawless pleading standards rather than pure statutory pre-emption.



### Practical checklist for students

| Question | Answer | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Is my cause rooted in an exclusive right?** | Identify whether relief depends on proving copying/distribution/etc.<br>If yes → likely barred.<br>If no → proceed past § 301 analysis.| This satisfies Step 1 above and mirrors how districts handle “copyright‐based” contracts (*Melendez*, *Santos*). |
| **Does my complaint allege conduct distinct from infringement?** | Look for language about payment breaches, confidentiality promises, false representations unrelated to copying.<br>If such language exists → likely “qualitatively different.”| Mirrors step 2 (“qualitative difference”) cited in *Melendez* (and referenced in *Santos*). |
| **Am I a party—or expressly named third‑party beneficiary—to the agreement?** | If you’re only an outsider (“I’m being harmed by your violation”) you face Illinois/N.Y.! presumption against third‐party benefit (§ II).| Even absent § 301 concerns·​the suit will die for lack of standing (*Barry*, *Rocky Point Props.*). |
| **Do I plead enough factual detail?** | According rules governing contracts (e.g., `Caniglia`), allege essential terms and demonstrate meeting-of-the-minds.<br>Insufficient factual detail leads courts to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), irrespective of federal questions.| The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal for failure-to-state-a-claim even before addressing possible federal immunity.^[5] |

---

#### Bottom line

A breach‐of‐contract action will be displaced by federal copyright law **only when its substantive core requires proving infringement of one of Congress’ exclusive rights**. Merely contending that another party violated platform Terms does **not automatically invoke § 301**, unless those Terms themselves embody licensing restrictions tied directly to an exclusive right.  

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, although the district court initially treated Santos’s contract suits as pre‐empted, the appellate panel never resolved whether such suits really overlapped with any copyrighted work because Santos also failed:
*to establish privity / third‐party beneficiary status*, and
*to plead all essential contractual elements*.  

Consequently, students looking at similar fact patterns should first verify contractual standing and then ask whether their relief hinges on proving unauthorized copying or another protected act; only when both prongs are satisfied does federal copyright pre‐emption kick in.

### Can a non‑signatory enforce provisions of an online Terms of Service as a third‑party beneficiary under Illinois law?
Question:
Santos was not a party to Cameo’s Terms of Service but sued based on alleged breaches of those terms. The opinion cites Illinois’s strong presumption against granting benefits to non‑contracting third parties unless there is an express declaration. What criteria must be satisfied for a non‑signatory like Santos to be considered an intended third‑party beneficiary, and how do courts interpret ‘express declaration’ in website terms?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under Illinois law a non‑signatory can enforce a contract – including an online Terms of Service (TOS) – **only if the agreement contains a clear, “express” declaration that the parties intended to confer enforceable rights on that third party**.  The Second Circuit applied that rule in *Santos v. Kimmel* and found no such declaration in Cameo’s TOS, so Santos cannot sue on its provisions as a third‑party beneficiary.

---

### How Illinois treats third‑party beneficiaries

1. **Strong presumption against third‑party benefits**  
   Illinois cases hold that contracts are presumed *not* to create rights for anyone who did not sign them.  The Court quoted *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. Ct. App. 2016):

   > “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”

2. **Two elements must be satisfied**  

   | Element | What it means | How the court looks for it |
   |---|---|---|
   | **(a) Intent to benefit a specific person or class** | The contracting parties must have **intended** the benefit to vest in the non‑signatory. | Courts read the language of the agreement itself; they will not infer intent from extrinsic evidence unless the text is ambiguous. |
   | **(b) “Express” declaration of that intent** | The intent must be expressed in words that are **clear and unambiguous**—so clear that it is treated as though the parties had literally named the beneficiary. | Phrases such as “this Agreement shall be enforceable by any user of the Site” or “the parties intend to confer rights on …” satisfy this requirement. A vague reference (“the Agreement may affect others”) does **not**.*

3. **What is *not* sufficient**  
   * A mere **third‑party‑beneficiary clause elsewhere in the contract** does not automatically extend every provision to all outsiders; each provision whose benefit is claimed must itself contain an express statement.
   * An implied‑in‑fact “meeting of minds” discovered through conduct alone is insufficient under Illinois law—there must be express textual language.

---

### Application in *Santos v. Kimmel*

- **Governing law:** The Court held that Illinois law governs the Cameo TOS.
- **Finding:** The plaintiff “identifies nothing close to an ‘express declaration’ that the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service apply to him or other noncontracting parties.”  
  - The opinion notes that some provisions *do* contain beneficiary language, which illustrates how **when parties intend a provision to be enforceable by a third party they say so expressly**.
- **Result:** Because Santos could not point to any provision that met both elements above, his breach‑of‑contract claim failed on this ground alone.

---

### How courts interpret “express declaration” in website terms

1. **The language must be plain and specific**  
   Courts treat any clause that plainly states “this Agreement benefits …” (or names a class such as “any user”, “any seller”, “any person who purchases through our platform”) as an express declaration.

2. **Context matters but only secondarily**  
   Even if a site’s layout or clickwrap notice suggests an intention to protect users, those cues are irrelevant unless backed by unambiguous text inside the agreement itself.

3. **Absence = no enforceability**  
   If the TOS merely imposes obligations on registered users (e.g., payment terms, content standards) without saying those obligations also bind or protect non‑users, courts will refuse to extend those duties to outsiders.

---

### Bottom line for a non‑signatory like Santos

To succeed as an intended third‑party beneficiary under Illinois law, Santos would have needed:

1️⃣ A provision in Cameo’s TOS that *explicitly* says something like “the obligations set forth herein are enforceable by any individual who creates content for Cameo,” or  
2️⃣ A similarly clear clause naming “any person who supplies videos pursuant to this Agreement” as a beneficiary.

Because no such clause existed—and because other provisions with explicit beneficiary language were limited to specific classes—Santos could not invoke those terms against Kimmel or ABC.

---

**Takeaway for law students:**  

When evaluating whether a person who never signed an online contract can sue on its terms, first ask:

- Does Illinois (or the controlling state’s) law presume against third‑party benefits?  
- Does the contract contain unmistakable text showing both (a) intention to benefit and (b) an express statement effecting that intention?

If either prong is missing—especially the “express declaration”—the claim will be dismissed, exactly as *Santos v. Kimmel* demonstrates.

### How do courts resolve conflicts when different states govern different aspects of a contract claim (e.g., Illinois for TOS vs. New York for implied contract)?
Question:
In this case, Illinois law was applied to evaluate whether Santos could rely on Cameo’s Terms of Service, while New York law governed the alleged breach of implied contract claim. What choice‑of‑law principles guide courts in such mixed jurisdictional scenarios, and can one state’s doctrine on third‑party beneficiaries override another state’s substantive contract analysis?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit solves a “mixed‑jurisdiction” dispute by **applying each state’s substantive law only to the claim that it governs**.  In *Santos v. Kimmel* the court treated the two contract theories as separate causes of action and gave each its own choice‑of‑law analysis:

| Claim | Governing Law | Reason for applying that law (as explained in the opinion) |
|-------|---------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| Direct breach of Came​o’s Terms of Service | **Illinois** | The Terms of Service contain an express choice‑of‑law provision that makes Illinois law applicable. The court therefore looked to Illinois precedent on third‑party beneficiary rights (“a strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties”). *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d at 976. |
| Breach of implied contract & fraudulent inducement | **New York** | No contractual choice‑of‑law clause covers these claims, so the court applied New York’s ordinary conflict‑of‑laws rule – i.e., the forum state’s substantive law governs a claim arising out of conduct in New York. Under New York law “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” determines whether an implied‑in‑fact contract exists (*Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Grp.*, 744 N.Y.S.2d at 271). |

### Why two different states can govern different parts of a single lawsuit

1. **Choice‑of‑law provisions are controlling when they exist**  
   When a party’s standard agreement (here, Cameo’s Terms of Service) contains an explicit selection of one state’s law, that clause “governs” any dispute **directly grounded in the agreement**. The court therefore applied Illinois doctrines to decide whether Santos could assert any rights under those terms.

2. **Absent a clause, default conflict rules apply**  
   For claims that are *outside* the scope of a contractual choice‐of‐law clause—such as an alleged implied contract between Santos and Kimmel—the Second Circuit follows New York’s ordinary approach: the forum state’s substantive law controls unless a strong public‑policy reason demands otherwise (*see* *Rocky Point Props.*). Because the suit was filed in the Southern District of New York, New York law was used.

3. **Separate analyses prevent one state’s doctrine from overriding another**  
   The court treats each cause of action independently.  An Illinois rule about third‑party beneficiaries is used only to evaluate whether Santos could rely on the Terms of Service; it does **not** replace New York’s test for an implied‑in‑fact contract because those are distinct legal questions.  Consequently, even if one jurisdiction would permit a particular theory, it cannot “override” a different jurisdiction’s substantive analysis for a separate claim.

### Can a third‑party‐beneficiary rule from one state trump another state’s contract analysis?

No—*Santos v. Kimmel* illustrates that each jurisdiction’s rule applies only within its own claim:

* Under **Illinois** law, Santos would have needed an “express declaration” that the Terms of Service extended benefits to him as a non‑signatory (“presumption against conferring benefits”). The court found none, so his TOS claim failed.
* Under **New York** law, Santos had to plead enough facts showing an “indication of a meeting of minds” creating an implied contract between him and Kimmel. The complaint lacked those essential terms, so his breach‑of‑implied‑contract claim also failed.

Because each claim fell under a different governing regime, neither state’s doctrine could supersede the other; each was evaluated according to its own standards and both were dismissed.

### Takeaway for similar fact patterns

1. **Identify whether any contractual choice‑of‑law clause covers each cause of action** – if yes, apply that clause’s designated state.
2. **For claims outside any clause**, apply the forum’s default conflict rule (often the forum state’s substantive law).
3. **Analyze each claim separately** using its designated state's doctrines; do not attempt to import another state’s substantive rule into a claim governed elsewhere.
4. **Beware preemption** – even when state contract theories survive choice‐of‐law analysis, they may still be barred by federal statutes (as happened with Santos’ copyright claims).

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the Second Circuit followed exactly this two‑track approach, leading to dismissal on both grounds without allowing one state's third‑party doctrine to dominate another state's contract analysis.

### What constitutes ‘out‑of‑pocket loss’ for a fraudulent inducement claim in New York, and can intangible harms like reputational damage satisfy this element?
Question:
The appellate court affirmed dismissal of Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim because he did not allege any out‑of‑pocket loss. Under New York law, what types of losses qualify as out‑of‑pocket? If a plaintiff suffers damage to reputation or lost future revenue opportunities due to fraud, can those be pleaded as sufficient losses, or must they be strictly monetary expenditures incurred at the time of fraud?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under New York law a fraudulent‑inducement claim requires the plaintiff to plead **“out‑of‑pocket” loss**—that is, a *concrete monetary* loss that he actually spent or was forced to spend because he relied on the fraud.  Purely intangible harms—damage to reputation, hurt feelings, or speculative future lost revenue—do **not** satisfy this element at the pleading stage.  The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel**, 2025 WL … (2d Cir. Oct. 6 2025), follows this rule by dismissing Santos’s claim for failure to allege any such loss, citing **Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.,** 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996) and **Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.,** 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142‑43 (2017).

---

### The legal standard

New York courts have long held that a fraudulent‑inducement action is essentially a contract claim and therefore requires proof of **actual economic damage**—the “out‑of‑pocket” loss requirement.

* **Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.,** 88 N.Y.2d at 421 – “A plaintiff must allege an actual out‑of‑pocket loss … a loss that can be measured in dollars.”  
* **Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.,** 29 N.Y.3d at 142‑43 – reiterates that “reliance damages must be quantifiable monetary losses; mental anguish or injury to reputation is insufficient.”

The Second Circuit applied those precedents verbatim:

> “We agree with the District Court’s conclusion that Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.”  
> —*Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 WL … at *9 (2d Cir.*)

### What counts as “out‑of‑pocket” loss?

In practice New York courts treat the following as permissible:

| Type of loss | Example | Why it qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| **Payments made pursuant to the fraud** | The plaintiff paid $10,000 for a product that turned out never to exist. | The money left the plaintiff’s pocket and cannot be recovered except by rescission or damages. |
| **Expenses incurred in reliance on the fraudulent representation** | Costs of shipping goods ordered under false pretenses; professional fees expended because of reliance on a bogus contract.| These are expenditures triggered by reliance and are objectively measurable in dollars. |
| **Loss of value already transferred** | A borrower who advanced funds under a fraudulent promise that the loan would be repaid and now cannot recoup principal.| The lender’s capital has been reduced by a specific amount. |
| **Definite financial obligations undertaken because of the fraud** | Paying interest or penalties on debt assumed under misrepresentations.| Again a concrete dollar amount that can be tallied.|

### What does *not* satisfy “out‑of‑pocket” loss?

* **Reputational injury** – Damage to one’s public image is an intangible harm; it may give rise to separate tort claims (e.g., defamation) but does not count as reliance damages in fraudulent inducement.
* **Speculative future lost revenue or profit** – Unless an actual loss has already been incurred (e.g., money spent on marketing based on a false promise), forecasts of missed opportunities are too conjectural at pleadings.
* **Emotional distress / mental anguish** – Even though these injuries may be real and compensable under other theories (e.g., intentional infliction of emotional distress), they do not meet the “out‑of‑pocket” threshold.

### Why Santos’s claim fails

Santos’s complaint alleged that Kimmel used his videos without permission and that this conduct constituted fraud. However:

1. He did not assert any dollar amount he paid or spent because of Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations.
2. He only complained about being “mocked” on television and about damage to his reputation as a public figure.
3. The court therefore found no factual allegation satisfying **Lama Holding**’s out‑of‑pocket requirement and dismissed the claim per *Connaughton*’s guidance.

### Practical takeaways for pleading

1️⃣ **Quantify every dollar spent** – If you paid for a service/product based on false statements, state exactly how much you paid and when.

2️⃣ **Identify ancillary expenses** – Shipping costs, legal fees incurred *immediately* because you relied on the fraud can be included.

3️⃣ **Separate intangible harms** – If your reputation suffered you may need an additional cause of action (defamation, IIED). Do not rely on it to satisfy fraudulent inducement’s damage element.

4️⃣ **Future losses must be shown as “actual” payments already made** – Prospective lost profits are not enough unless you can demonstrate money you already expended trying to capture those profits (e.g., marketing campaigns funded because of the misrepresentation).

---

**Bottom line:** In New York fraudulent‐inducement suits the plaintiff must plead a concrete monetary loss—that is, money actually taken from his pocket because he relied on the fraud.  Damage to reputation or speculative future earnings are *not* sufficient to meet this statutory element; they require separate legal theories if recovery is desired. The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel** illustrates how strictly courts enforce this pleading requirement.
