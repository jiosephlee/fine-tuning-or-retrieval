Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a fair‑use defense be raised as a decisive affirmative defense at the pleading stage under Rule 12(b)(6)?
Question:
In Santos v. Kimmel the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of the copyright claims on a Rule 12(b)(6) basis because fair use was ‘so clearly established by the complaint.’ I thought affirmative defenses like fair use are typically considered later, after discovery. How can a court decide that fair use bars a claim before any evidence beyond the pleadings is presented? What standards do courts apply to determine that a fair‑use defense is evident on the face of the complaint?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes. A court may treat a fair‑use defense as a “decisive” affirmative defense at the pleading stage when the complaint itself supplies enough factual allegations (or incorporated documents) to show that each of the four § 107 factors weighs in favor of fair use. Under Rule 12(b)(6) the court reviews the complaint **de novo** and asks whether, taking the plaintiff’s allegations as true, any viable copyright‑infringement claim survives. If the alleged facts make fair use “so clearly established” that the plaintiff cannot plausibly allege an element of infringement, the claim may be dismissed without discovery. The Second Circuit applied exactly this approach in **Santos v. Kimmel**, 2025 WL _____, aff’d, 2025 WL _____, at *¶ 1‑4* (2d Cir.).

---

### Why an affirmative defense can bar a claim under Rule 12(b)(6)

1. **Rule 12(b)(6) tests facial sufficiency**, not evidentiary weight.  
   The district court first asks whether the complaint states a *prima facie* cause of action “under Rule 12(b)(6).” *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d at 110 (2d Cir.). The appellate court reviews this **de novo**, looking only at “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached… and documents incorporated by reference.” *Santos*, ¶ 1–2.

2. **An affirmative defense can eliminate every element of a claim** even at this early stage if the plaintiff’s own pleadings make it impossible for her to prove infringement. The Second Circuit has explicitly recognized that “fair use may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the pleading stage. *TCA Television Corp.* v. McCollum, 839 F.3d at 178 (cited verbatim in *Santos*, ¶ 3).

3. **The analysis relies on what is already pled or publicly recordable**—no additional evidence is required where the plaintiff’s allegations together with any attached exhibits (e.g., copyright registrations) give enough information to evaluate each § 107 factor. The court may also take judicial notice of registration information (*Island Software*), which *Santos* does when it notes “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created.” *Santos*, ¶ 5‑6.

---

### The “clear‑fair‑use” test used in **Santos**

The Second Circuit applied each statutory factor using only the pleadings:

| Factor | How *Santos* evaluated it on facial review |
|--------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Purpose & character** | The complaint states Kimmel’s purpose was “to comment on … willingness … to say absurd things for money.” The court treats “transformative” analysis as a question of what *a reasonable observer* would think (citing *Warhol Found.*), not subjective intent; thus it deemed Kimmel’s use highly transformative. *Santos*, ¶ 3‑4 |
| **Nature of the copyrighted work** | The videos are expressive works intended to inspire “feelings of hope…,” which makes them classic subjects for commentary/parody—favoring fair use (citing *Authors Guild v. Google*). *Santos*, ¶ 4 |
| **Amount & substantiality** | No allegation suggests more than what was necessary for parody; further, registration evidence shows immediate publication, implying no extensive copying beyond what was required for critique. *Santos*, ¶ 5 |
| **Effect on market** | The plaintiff never alleged any substitute market; instead she frames Kimmel’s segment as criticism rather than competition—a point underscored by Supreme Court precedent (*Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose*, supra). *Santos*, ¶ 6 |

Because each factor “clearly” favored defendants, the court concluded that **fair use was already established by the face of the complaint**, rendering any infringement claim legally insufficient even before discovery could begin.

---

### When this approach will NOT work

* The plaintiff must **affirmatively plead** enough factual details for each factor. If she merely alleges infringement without describing purpose, amount copied, or market effect, a court cannot infer fair use and must allow discovery to develop those factual disputes.
* If any factor is left genuinely contested—e.g., she alleges that Kimmel’s videos serve essentially the same commercial purpose as her originals—the court will likely deem the issue fact‑intensive and deny dismissal.
* Some courts require an accompanying affidavit or declaration when reliance on documentary evidence (such as registration records) is central; however *Santos* found judicial notice sufficient under Second Circuit precedent (*Island Software*).

---

### Practical take‑away for practitioners

1️⃣ **Draft complaints with precise factual content** about how you used—or how you were used—if you anticipate invoking fair use early on.  
2️⃣ **Attach or incorporate any public records** (copyright registrations, screenshots) because they may be treated as part of the pleadings for purposes of a Rule 12(b)(6) analysis.  
3️⃣ Be prepared for a **“clear‑fair‑use” motion** if your opponent can argue—through undisputed allegations—that every § 107 factor swings in their favor; success hinges on showing that no genuine dispute over those facts exists even before discovery.

---

**Bottom line:** Under Rule 12(b)(6) a court may indeed dismiss a copyright claim when the plaintiff’s own pleadings make it evident that fair use applies to every element of infringement. The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel** illustrates how judges conduct that analysis strictly from what is pleaded—and from publicly available registrations—without waiting for post‑pleading discovery.*
