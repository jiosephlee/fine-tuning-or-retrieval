Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Procedural History and Underlying Facts  

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, No. 24‑2196‑cv (S.D.N.Y. Oct. 6 2025), the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit reviewed a district‑court decision that dismissed a copyright infringement suit and three related state‑law claims.  The appellate opinion proceeds by first reciting the factual background that gave rise to the litigation, then describing how the case moved through the federal courts before arriving before the Second Circuit.  Understanding this procedural trajectory is essential for any student learning how an appellate opinion is framed and how the parties’ alleged conduct fits within the pleadings that survived—or failed to survive—the initial motions to dismiss.

---

## Identification of Plaintiff George Santos and Defendants Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney  

The plaintiff is **George Santos**, a former member of the United States House of Representatives who, after leaving office, began offering personalized video messages for fee on the online marketplace **Cameo**.  Santos’ status as a former congressman is not dispositive for the legal issues but helps explain why he became a subject of public parody.  

The defendants are three distinct corporate entities:  

1. **Jimmy Kimmel**, a comedian who hosts the late‑night television program *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (“JKL”).  
2. **American Broadcasting Companies, Inc.** (“ABC”), the network that broadcasts JKL and which aired the allegedly infringing videos.  
3. **The Walt Disney Company**, ABC’s parent corporation, which was joined in its capacity as the ultimate owner of ABC’s assets.  

All three defendants are sued jointly because they are alleged participants in the production, transmission, and distribution of the videos at issue.  The complaint frames each defendant’s role as essential to the alleged infringement—Kimmel as author and performer, ABC as broadcaster, and Disney as corporate parent responsible for authorizing or permitting the broadcast.

---

## Cameo Video Requests: Fictitious Names and Santos’s Fulfillment  

*Cameo* is an internet platform where users can commission short, personalized video recordings from public figures or entertainers for a fee agreed upon by the parties.  In this case, Jimmy Kimmel created several fictitious user accounts on Cameo—each using a different pseudonym—to request video messages from Santos that would later be used for comedic effect on JKL.

The complaint alleges that each request specified a particular phrase or theme that Kimmel wanted Santos to repeat verbatim; examples include “Will you say X?” where “X” was an absurd or intentionally provocative statement designed to elicit humor when aired on television.  Santos accepted each commission under his usual Cameo terms—charging an agreed‑upon amount, recording a video that matched the request’s specifications, and delivering the final product through Cameo’s digital delivery system.

From a factual standpoint, these transactions are pivotal because they establish **(i)** who initially created each video (Santos), **(ii)** that Santos performed his work in response to a paid request (a commercial transaction), and **(iii)** that Kimmel was not merely a passive viewer but an active initiator who shaped the content through his requests.

---

## Broadcast on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* – The “Will Santos Say It?” Segment  

After receiving each personalized video from Santos, Kimmel incorporated them into a recurring sketch titled “Will Santos Say It?” The segment aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a broader series of comedic parodies targeting public figures perceived as willing participants in absurd or sensational statements for monetary gain.

During each broadcast episode, Kimmel introduced the clip by noting that he had “ordered” a video from Santos via Cameo under an assumed name, then played Sot​as’s recorded response while delivering sarcastic commentary meant to mock both Santos’s willingness to comply with any request and his prior public statements about personal ambition and perseverance.  The clipping was not presented merely as an isolated performance; it was framed within editorial commentary—a hallmark element used by courts when evaluating whether a secondary use is “transformative.”

These factual details provide context for two critical legal inquiries made by both parties:

1. Whether airing Santos’s videos—originally created under contract with Cameo—constitutes an unauthorized reproduction or public performance infringing any exclusive rights held by Santos under Section 106 of the Copyright Act.
2. Whether Kimmel’s editorial framing renders his use “fair” because it adds new meaning or message distinct from purely replicating Santos’s original expressive content.

The answer ultimately depends on how courts assess these facts against statutory factors—a process explained later in *Santos v.* Kimmel’s fair‑use analysis—but at this stage they ground every claim in concrete events that were pleaded in court filings and attached as exhibit material (e.g., screenshots from Cameo confirming payment amounts; excerpts from JKL episodes displaying the segment).

---

## District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) Dismissal of Copyright Infringement Claims  

Following suit filing in **the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York**, defendants moved to dismiss all counts under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure **12(b)(6)**—the rule permitting dismissal when “the complaint … fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.”*1* The District Court applied its well‑established “face‑validity” standard under *Bell Atlantic Corp.v.Farrell*, 492 U.S._125_ (1989): at this stage courts consider only allegations in the complaint plus any documents incorporated by reference or attached as exhibits; they do not weigh credibility or evidence outside those pleadings.*2*

Judge Cote concluded that even if all factual allegations were true:

1. **Fair use barred liability** on every alleged copyright claim because 
   - The purpose was commentary/parody—a transformative secondary purpose;
   - The nature of Santos’s videos (original works created at his own expense) did not outweigh Kimmel’s expressive intent;
   - The amount taken—whole videos—was justified given their integral role within commentary;
   - No substitutive market existed for comedy parodies such as those aired on JKL.
   This conclusion relied heavily on Second Circuit precedent interpreting §107 factors (*e.g.*, *Andy Warhol Found.* v.Goldsmith*, 11 F.4th 26 (2d Cir. 2021); *Hachette Book Grp.* v.Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163 (2d Cir.<2024>)). 

2. **State‑law claims were either preempted** by federal copyright law (for breach‑of‑contract actions grounded in alleged violation of contractual rights tied directly to copyrighted works) or **failed to state viable causes** because:
   - Plaintiffs could not establish themselves as parties to Cameo’s Terms of Service (“the contract applies only between Cameo and its users”; see also Illinois presumption against third‑party benefit);
   - No concrete showing existed that defendants entered into any binding agreement with Santos beyond their separate roles as broadcasters;
   - Fraudulent inducement required proof of *out‑of‑pocket loss*, which was absent from Santo’s pleading.*3*

Thus **the district court entered final judgment dismissing every count**, granting defendants’ motion under Rule 12(b)(6). This dismissal set up an appeal because it effectively terminated all relief sought by Santos at both federal copyright (§106) level and related state‑law expectations concerning contract performance—a classic scenario where parties seek appellate review when they dispute whether their facts meet threshold pleading standards or whether statutory preemption applies.*

---

## Dismissal of State‑Law Breach‑of‑Contract and Fraudulent Inducement Claims  

Even though federal copyright preemption concerns dominated much of the discussion above, plaintiffs also asserted three independent statutory cause‑of‑action under New York law:

1. **Breach of contract** – Alleging deficiency in performance under terms allegedly embedded within Cameo’s user agreement.
2. **Breach of implied contract** – Claiming an unwritten understanding between parties based upon repeated transactions via Cameo.
3. **Fraudulent inducement** – Arguing that Kimmel misrepresented his intent when ordering videos so plaintiffs would suffer damages based on reliance upon false statements.

The district court addressed each claim individually:

- For both breach claims it held preemption applied because any alleged breach would ultimately affect exclusive rights enumerated in §106—the very rights protected by federal copyright statutes (*see* *Melendez v.Sirius XM Radio*, Inc., 50 F.4th 294 (2d Cir.<2022>)). However—the court noted—preemption need not be decided because Plaintiffs’ allegations were insufficiently particularized: they did not identify which specific operative provisions allegedly breached nor demonstrate possession of enforceable contractual rights[^1]. Consequently even assuming no preemptive bar existed they would still fail under pleading standards requiring clear articulation of essential contract terms (*Caniglia v.Chi.Trib.*).

- Regarding fraudulent inducement it required proof that Plaintiff suffered *actual* monetary loss caused directly by Defendants’ alleged deception—a requirement articulated in *Lama Holding Co.* v.Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.​2d 413 (1996).   Plaintiff's complaint merely alleged reputational harm or abstract injury without identifying “out‑of‑pocket” expenditures directly linked to Defendants’ misrepresentations.; thus dismissed *sine die*.*

These rulings illustrate two key procedural ideas students should keep in mind:

1. **Pleading specificity matters**—even when alleging state torts or contracts intertwined with copyrighted material.
2. **Preemption can act as an automatic bar**, but appellate courts may deem it unnecessary if lower courts find independent sufficiency problems with pleadings.*

---

## Appeal Filing in the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  

Within thirty days after entry of judgment—consistent with **28 U.S.C.§ 2101(a)**—plaintiff filed a **notice of appeal** raising both record­–based objections (“the district court erred in applying fair use at pleading stage”) and legal arguments concerning improper dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6).    

Because both parties had already presented all relevant documents through discovery motions prior to judgment—and no additional fact‐finding was necessary—the Second Circuit accepted jurisdiction over an appeal focused squarely on whether *the complaint sufficiently stated a claim* based on existing pleadings plus any documents incorporated therein (*DiFolco v.MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F._3d 104 (2d Cir.<2010>)).  

The appellate standard is **de novo** review for motions challenging facial sufficiency; unlike summary judgment challenges where courts apply heightened scrutiny (*Anderson v.Liberty Lobby*, 477 U.S._242_). Consequently Judge Lohier read everything placed before her—including exhibits such as registration certificates confirming issuance dates—and evaluated whether any set‐of‐facts could plausibly support each element alleged by plaintiff.[^2] This approach mirrors typical circuit practice when examining Rule 12(b)(6) rulings—a point emphasized early in *Santos*’s opinion (“We review de novo…”) thus providing law students an explicit illustration how appellate courts articulate their scope I when handling pleadings disputes.*

Ultimately ***Santos v.* Kimmel* affirmed* everything below—a decisive outcome underscoring two procedural lessons:

- **A well‐pleaded complaint must anticipate transformation analysis**, even at its earliest stage.
- **State‐law claims anchored solely in contracts derived from online platform agreements require meticulous factual detail**, lest they be deemed non‑viable at dismissal stage regardless of broader theoretical merits.

---

### Takeaways for Reading Procedural Narratives  

When approaching an appellate opinion such as *Santos v.* Kimmel,* students should map out three layers:

1️⃣ The underlying fact pattern—the actual conduct giving rise to litigation (Cameo requests → videos → televised parody).  
2️⃣ The procedural posture—how each claim survived or perished at earlier stages (Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal based on fair use & lack-of-fact pleading).  
3️⃣ The mechanics governing review (de novo standard for pleadings; jurisdictional timeliness; reliance on annexed exhibits).

By dissecting these elements systematically—as this chapter has done—you gain practice reading opinions not merely for substantive holdings but for understanding how courts articulate procedural reasoning around fact patterns similar across many modern copyright disputes involving digital platforms and media personalities.

# Rule 12(b)(6) Pleading Standard and Judicial Notice in Copyright Cases  

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed a district‑court dismissal of both copyright‑infringement and state‑law claims lodged by former Congressman George Santos against Jimmy Kimmel and his corporate employers.  The appellate court’s analysis hinges on two procedural pillars that frequently arise in copyright litigation: the pleading standards that govern a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), and the court’s ability to take judicial notice of publicly filed copyright registrations when it evaluates those pleadings.  This chapter dissects those pillars, showing how they operate together at the pleading stage and illustrating their application in *Santos*.

---

## The Twombly‑Iqbal Plausibility Standard for Rule 12(b)(6) Motions  

A motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) tests whether the plaintiff’s complaint “states a claim upon which relief can be granted.”  Since *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S._ 544 (2007), and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S._ 662 (2009), federal courts have applied a “plausibility” threshold rather than a mere “conceivable” test.  The plaintiff must plead enough factual content to permit the court to draw a reasonable inference that his claim is viable—more than labels and conclusions taken alone.  Facts must “nudge” the claim from possible to plausible.^1  

In *Santos*, the district court concluded that Santos’s allegations did not rise above speculation with respect to infringement; they simply recited that Kimmel aired videos Santos had created for a Cameo request.  The Second Circuit reiterated that “the plausibility standard requires more than an accusation” and affirmed that the complaint failed this threshold because it offered no factual basis showing an unauthorized reproduction or distribution beyond what was already permitted by Kimmel’s contract with Cameo.  Consequently, the appellate court sustained the dismissal without requiring any additional discovery.^2  

For students, the key takeaway is that once a plaintiff asserts an ultimate legal conclusion—here, “defendants infringed my copyright”—the allegation must be anchored in concrete facts (e.g., how many copies were made, whether they were uploaded without permission).  Without such anchoring, the claim is facially implausible and subject to dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6).

---

## De Novo Review Versus Deference to District‑Court Findings  

When an appeal challenges a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, the reviewing court proceeds “de novo.”  This means it re‑examines the pleadings anew rather than deferring to any factual determinations made by the district court.^3 In *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010), the Second Circuit explained that de novo review entails looking at “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference.”^4 The appellate court does not give weight to any judgments about credibility or weight of evidence because such issues have not yet been litigated—the parties have only advanced their narratives in pleading form.

Nevertheless, de novo review does not mean an absolute free‑for‑all; an appellate court must still apply ordinary standards of legal sufficiency.  If the district court dismissed on legal grounds supported by undisputed facts contained in the complaint—such as an unequivocal fair‑use defense—the appellate panel may affirm those legal conclusions even though it conducts its own independent assessment of whether those facts exist on their face.^5 In *Santos*, no factual dispute existed about Kimmel’s purpose (“to comment on Santos’s willingness …”), so both courts could agree on fair use without further evidence.

---

## Scope of Factual Allegations Considered at the Pleading Stage  

Because Rule 12(b)(6) operates before discovery, an appellate court’s inquiry is confined strictly to what is **asserted** in the complaint and any attached documentation.^6 *DiFolco* expressly limits review to three categories: (1) factual allegations set forth in paragraph form; (2) exhibits physically attached to the pleading; and (3) documents incorporated by reference—such as contracts or registration certificates—that are part of the record at this early stage.^7  

In *Santos*, Santos attached copies of his Cameo agreements and screenshots of his video deliveries as exhibits; he also incorporated his copyright registrations by reference (see Appendix 160–69).  The Second Circuit treated those documents as part of its evidentiary slate for purposes of evaluating plausibility.^8 This approach ensures that a plaintiff cannot rely on vague or conclusory statements but must provide concrete identifiers—dates, titles, or descriptions—that give the court a reliable foundation for assessing each element of its claim.

The practical implication for students is twofold: first, every allegation intended to support liability must be detailed enough that an attentive reader can infer its relevance; second, attaching or properly referencing reliable documents transforms those items from “potential evidence” into “facts considered” for purposes of a Rule 12(b)(6) analysis.

---

## Judicial Notice Power Over Publicly Filed Copyright Registrations  

A distinctive feature of copyright cases is that registration information resides in an official government database: the United States Copyright Office’s public registry.  Courts may take judicial notice of such records under Federal Rule of Evidence 201—a rule allowing fact‑finding without formal proof when matters are “not subject to reasonable dispute” because they are readily ascertainable from reliable sources.^9 In *Island Software & Computation Services, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005), the Second Circuit held that “copyright registrations…as published in …the Copyright Office’s registry” are suitable subjects for judicial notice.^10  

Applying this doctrine in *Santos*, the appellate panel looked directly at Santos’s registration certificates—documents filed on or before creation dates—to determine when each video was deemed “published” under §101 of Title 17.^11 Because those dates were publicly verifiable through judicial notice, no additional testimony was required even though discovery had not yet begun.  
For law students, recognizing judicial notice helps explain why seemingly technical details such as publication dates can be weighed at plea stage: they are matters of public record whose authenticity does not depend on contested testimony.

---

## Why Fair‑Use Analysis Can Be Conducted on the Face of a Complaint  

Fair use is traditionally treated as an affirmative defense evaluated after infringement liability is established.  However, when a plaintiff’s complaint articulates facts that unmistakably place each fair‑use factor on one side of its balancing test, courts may dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6).  The rationale is straightforward: if no plausible set of facts could overcome a complete defense identified from the plaintiff’s own allegations, then no genuine dispute exists warranting further litigation.^12  

In *Santos*, every allegation pointed toward a classic parody/commentary context: Kimmel solicited videos expressly so he could lampoon Santos on national television (“Will Santos Say It?”).  The complaint also identified that Santos himself portrayed his videos as messages of hope—a depiction contrary to Kimmel’s satiric framing—allowing reasonable observers to view Kimmel’s use as transformative.^13 Moreover—using judicial notice—the registration records confirmed contemporaneous publication dates supporting factor (2) (“nature”) while there was no indication that Kimmel’s broadcast usurped any market for Santos’s original videos—a point encapsulated by *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music*’s market analysis turn‑phrase.^14 Because all four statutory fair‑use factors were either undisputed or well supported by admitted facts, both district and appellate courts concluded that an infringement claim could not plausibly survive even if taken at face value.

The pedagogical lesson here is twofold: first, plaintiffs must anticipate potential affirmative defenses when drafting complaints and either distinguish their conduct from recognized categories (e.g., parody versus mere copying); second, defendants may rely on well‑pleaded factual content alone—including publicly noticed registration data—to move for dismissal before discovery if those facts make success on infringement unlikely.*

---

### Summary  

The *Santos* opinion offers a textbook illustration of how modern pleading doctrine intersects with copyright law’s unique evidentiary landscape:

* **Plausibility** under *Twombly*–*Iqbal* demands more than bare legal conclusions; concrete facts must render each element believable.
* **De novo** appellate review treats pleadings afresh while still applying established legal standards.
* **Scope** is limited strictly to what is asserted plus any attached or incorporated documents (*DiFolco*).
* **Judicial notice** permits immediate reliance on public copyright registrations (*Island Software*) without waiting for discovery.
* **Fair‐use** may defeat an infringement claim outright when plaintiff‑pleaded facts already satisfy—or overwhelmingly favor—the statutory factors.

Understanding these doctrines equips law students to read opinions like *Santos* with an eye toward how procedural thresholds shape substantive outcomes right at litigation’s outset.

# Fair Use Analysis in *Santos v. Kimmel*

The Second Circuit’s decision affirms a district‑court dismissal of George Santos’s copyright infringement claim against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC on a Rule 12(b)(6) basis.  The appellate court concurred that the defendant’s use of Santos’s Cameo videos was protected by the fair‑use doctrine embodied in 17 U.S.C. § 107.  To understand why the court reached this result, a law student must be able to follow the court’s step‑by‑step application of each statutory factor, see how “transformative” use is measured by a reasonable‐observer test rather than by parties’ subjective intentions, recognize why the nature of the underlying works mattered little, grasp why using an entire work can still be fair when it serves parody or criticism, and appreciate how the lack of market harm sealed the analysis.  The following sections break down each element of the fair‑use inquiry as it appears in *Santos*.



## Purpose and Character – The Transformative Use Doctrine

Section 107’s first factor asks whether the secondary use adds something new with a different “purpose or character.”  The Second Circuit has long held that “the heart of the matter is whether the new work is ‘transformative,’ i.e., whether it merely supersedes the original or instead adds something new” (see **Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith**, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021)).  

In *Santos*, the district court focused on the **purpose** behind Kimmel’s broadcast excerpts: they were part of a recurring segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” that lampooned Santos’s willingness to utter absurd statements for money.  The court stressed that a **reasonable observer**, not either party’s self‑described intent, determines whether a use is transformative (Warhol Found., 11 F.4th at 37).  The plaintiff asserted that his own purpose in creating each video—“feelings of hope…encouragement”—should defeat a finding of transformation.  The court rejected this argument because courts do not weigh a copyright holder’s subjective motive against that of the secondary user when applying the first factor (see **Goldsmith**, supra).  

The panel reiterated that even when the secondary user “instigated” creation of the original work—as was claimed by Kimmel—it does not automatically negate transformation so long as the new context conveys criticism or commentary distinct from the original expressive purpose (see **TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum**, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016)).  Accordingly, both the district court and this affirming opinion concluded that **the first factor strongly favored fair use** because Kimmel’s broadcast functioned as sarcastic critique rather than a simple replication of Santos’s encouraging message.



## Nature of the Copyrighted Works

The second statutory factor considers whether the underlying work is “creative” or “informational” and whether it has been published (see **U.S.C. § 107**).  Creative works receive stronger protection than factual ones, but publication diminishes this advantage because published works have already entered public discourse.

Here, each Cameo video was **published** on the same day it was recorded—a fact established through copyright registrations attached to Santos’s complaint (see Appendix 160‑69).  Because publication was undisputed, any weight against Santos derived from this factor was limited: once a work is publicly disseminated its creator’s exclusive rights are already subject to broader considerations such as commentary or parody (see **Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive**, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024)).  

The district court therefore treated this factor as **neutral or slightly unfavorable** to Santos but insufficient to outweigh the strong transformation shown by Kimmel’s presentational context.



## Amount and Substantiality – Whole Works Used in Parody

Under § 107(1)(3), courts examine both the quantitative amount taken and its qualitative significance relative to the original work’s heart(s).   The general rule disfavors copying large portions—but an exception arises when copying “the entire work…is necessary” for effective parody or criticism (see **Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music**, Inc., 510 U.S._569_, 592 (1994)).

Kimmel aired **the entire Cameo videos** within his televised jokes—a fact admitted in Santos’s complaint (“Kimmel aired … videos on JKL”).  The district court acknowledged this complete appropriation but held it permissible because:

1️⃣ The full video was required to convey **the comedic premise** (“Will Santos Say It?”), mirroring what critics must replicate when satirizing an utterance;
2️⃣ The usage served **critical commentary** rather than mere entertainment copying;
3️⃣ Precedent recognizes whole‑work borrowing as acceptable where **parody necessitates reproducing recognizable elements** (e.g., **Campbell**, supra).

Thus, although copying “the heart” ordinarily tips against fair use, here it was justified by necessity—a point emphasized by both district and appellate judges.^1



## Effect on Market – No Substitute Market Exists

The fourth factor asks whether the secondary use usurps market demand for either “the original work” or any potential derivative market.^[2] Parody enjoys particular protection because courts recognize that a licensable market for critical reviews rarely exists—the very act of critiquing often renders licensing impractical.^[3]

In *Santos*, defendants did not create a commercial substitute for Santos’s Cameo videos; viewers watched them only within a comedy segment aimed at mockery rather than purchase or licensing substitutes for Santos’s motivational message content.^[4]  

The court highlighted Kaplowitz reasoning from *Campbell*: “…creators … will license critical reviews or lampoons … removes such uses from … potential licensing market.”^5 Consequently:

- There was **no evidence** presented showing loss of revenue from prospective buyers who might have wanted non‑parodic versions;
- Consequently there was **no threat** that Kimmel’s broadcast would diminish any existing market—including one predicated upon satire itself;
- The plaintiff failed even to articulate a “potential market” harmed by his videos’ being parodied—a critical pleading deficiency under *Hachette* where plaintiffs must allege concrete harm rather than speculative notions.^[6]

Because no economically viable substitute existed—and because duplication served only commentary—the fourth statutory factor weighed decisively toward fair use.



## Rejection of Plaintiff’s Bad‑Faith Argument

Santos claimed Kimmel acted in bad faith by misrepresenting his intent (“false representations”), invoking *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc.* v Nation Enters., 471 U.S._539_, 562‑63 (1985), where Justice Stevens noted “fair use presupposes good faith.”  

The district court—and subsequently this panel—refused to give decisive weight to alleged bad faith:

- Post‑*Harper* jurisprudence has narrowed any good‑faith requirement; courts now treat bad faith merely as one element among many factual considerations rather than an automatic bar (“courts have since distanced themselves from this statement,” *Santos* ¶ 15)^[7];
- Moreover, even assuming bad faith existed, it could **not overthrow** other factors showing transformation and lack of market impact (§ 107 does not make good faith an essential prerequisite);
- Cases such as ***Swatch Group Mgmt.* v.* Bloomberg L.P.*,** 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) have held that even aggressive commercial motives do not preclude fair use when commentary predominates.

Thus salutary reliance on *Harper* failed because modern doctrine treats good‑faith considerations flexibly—and only insofar as they help shape overall balancing.^[8]



## Flexibility in Fair Use – Judicial Guidance Relied Upon

Throughout its analysis *Santos* leaned heavily upon Second Circuit precedents emphasizing fair use’s adaptable nature:

- **Andy Warhol Found.,** which focused exclusively on “whether [the] new work is transformative,” thereby allowing courts discretion based on contextual nuance.
- ***Hachette Book Group*** expressly described unfairness as “a flexible concept … varies depending on context,” authorizing tribunals to dismiss claims at pleadings when six hands point toward fairness.^[9]
- ***Cariou v.* Prince**, which taught courts need consider only “the original version … and [the] second work …” when determining fairness—a principle enabling early dismissal without discovery[^10].

These authorities collectively illustrate why appellate review did not require factual deepening beyond what parties alleged; once plaintiff cannot plausibly show nontransformative purpose or market injury, rule‑12(b)(6) dismissal becomes appropriate.



## Pleading‑Stage Dismissal When Fair Use Is Clear

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests whether a complaint states **a claim upon which relief can be granted**, assuming all well‑pleaded allegations are true.^[11] In copyright cases this entails assessing whether any *prima facie* set of facts could survive under § 107.[^12]  

Because:

1️⃣ The plaintiff conceded Kimmel’s purpose—the mocking commentary;
2️⃣ The underlying works were published;
3️⃣ Full copying was arguably essential to effect critique;
4️⃣ No viable substitute market existed;

the district court concluded—even strictly reading Plaintiff & Defendant pleadings—that all four factors weighed firmly toward fairness.^[13] Consequently “fair use bars [the] copyright claims even at […the] pleading stage” (*Santos* ¶ 23).  

The Second Circuit affirmed this approach under its de novo standard (*DiFolco*v.*MSNBC Cable*, 622 F.3d 104 ,110 (2d Cir.…)), reaffirming that appellate courts may redo these preliminary fact assumptions without deferring specially to lower‑court factual findings.

# State Law Contract Claims: Third‑Party Beneficiary Doctrine, Preemption, and Pleading Deficiencies  

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of former Congressman George Santos’s breach‑of‑contract, breach‑of‑implied‑contract, and fraudulent‑inducement allegations against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC.  The appellate court’s reasoning illustrates three recurring themes in “contract” pleadings that arise in copyright disputes: (1) whether a non‑party can invoke a contract signed by others under the third‑party‑beneficiary doctrine; (2) what factual content a complaint must contain to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion under New York pleading standards; and (3) when a state‑law claim is foreclosed because it intrudes on rights reserved to copyright owners by the federal Copyright Act.  This chapter unpacks each theme as it appears in the opinion, showing how the court applied Illinois and New York case law and why the plaintiff’s allegations fell short of the required pleading floor.



## The Cameo Terms of Service as Alleged Contractual Basis  

Santos’ suit hinges on an alleged contract between Jimmy Kimmel (the “user”) and Cameo, Inc., an online marketplace where personalities sell personalized video greetings.  When Kimmel requested videos from Santos via Cameo, he did so under Cameo’s standard Terms of Service (“ToS”).  Those ToS—displayed on Cameo’s website—govern every transaction between a user and the platform.  Santos contended that Kimmel breached specific provisions of those ToS when he edited Santos’ videos for comedic effect on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (“JKL”) without Santos’ permission or compensation beyond what was originally paid through Cameo.  

Because Santos never entered into any agreement with Cameo—he was merely a content creator hired through the platform—his alleged injury must be rooted either in a direct contract between him and Kimmel (which does not exist) or in an indirect contractual relationship mediated by Cameo’s ToS.  The district court therefore examined whether Santos could enforce rights created by an agreement to which he was not a signatory.



## Illinois Presumption Against Conferring Rights on Non‑Contracting Parties  

The ToS specify that Illinois law governs any dispute arising from their use—a choice-of-law clause common in online contracts.  Accordingly, the district court applied Illinois principles regarding third‑party beneficiaries.  In *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.*, 68 N.E.3d at 976 (Ill. Ct.App. 2016), the Illinois Supreme Court articulated a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties.”  The Court explained that only when an “express declaration” exists—language so clear it is tantamount to an express term—may a non‑signatory enforce contract provisions as a third‑party beneficiary.  

The Second Circuit adopted Barry’s framework verbatim:  

> “The implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” *Barry*, 68 N.E.3d at 976.  

Applying this test, the appellate panel found no clause in Cameo’s ToS granting Santos enforceable rights against Kimmel or ABC.  Indeed, other portions of the ToS contain explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language precisely where such benefits were intended (e.g., “the platform may enforce these terms against users’ agents”), underscoring that when *Cameo* wanted non‑signatories protected it said so expressly.  By contrast, there was nothing in the agreement indicating that creators who sell videos through Cameo obtain enforceable rights against users who subsequently broadcast those videos elsewhere.  

Thus, under Illinois law—notwithstanding any contractual construction at common law—the presumption barred Santos from treating himself as an intended beneficiary of an agreement he never signed.



## Express Declaration vs Implied‑In‑Fact Contracts Under New York Law  

Even if Illinois’ third‑party‐beneficiary bar were circumvented, Santos attempted to argue that an **implied** contract existed between him and Kimmel—essentially that their interaction created an “implied‐in‐fact” agreement obligating Kimmel to treat Santos’ videos according to certain standards.  Because the underlying state–law claims were filed in federal court sitting in New York, New York substantive law governed any implied‐contract analysis.  

In *Rocky Point Props., Inc.* v. Sear‑Brown Grp.*, 744 N.Y.S.2d at 271 (4th Dep’t 2002), New York courts require proof of a mutual intent to be bound (“meeting of minds”) together with evidence of **essential terms** such as price, subject matter, performance obligations, and duration.  Where no explicit agreement is evident, courts look for conduct indicating assent—but still demand “specificity” sufficient for enforcement.[¹]  

The Second Circuit referenced *DG & A Mgmt.* Servs., LLC v. Secs., Inc.* Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div.*, 859 N.Y.S.2d at 306 (3d Dep’t 2008), which stresses that an implied‐in‐fact contract cannot be established merely by alleging “a vague sense” that parties understood each other’s expectations; rather there must be “an indication of a meeting of minds … constituting an agreement.”  

Relying on these precedents, the panel concluded that Santos’ complaint did not identify any **nonconclusive** articulation of essential terms linking Santos directly to Kimmel:

* “The complaint fails to allege … the essential terms … including those specific provisions … upon which liability is predicated.” (*Caniglia v . Chi.* Trib.-N.Y.News Syndicate,* 612 N.Y.S.​2d 146 (1994)).  

In other words, even assuming New York governs Kimmel’s conduct toward Santos—an assumption undermined by the earlier Illinois analysis—the plaintiff offered no factual averments showing what price was paid for each video, what exact promises were made concerning subsequent use, or any governing provision whereby Kimmel owed duties beyond what was stipulated in Cameo’s public ToS.



## Essential Terms Requirement and Failure to Plead Them  

New York follows federal Rule 8(b)(6): a complaint must contain “a short plain statement … sufficient to give ... notice” of allegedly actionable conduct.^[2] The Supreme Court has emphasized that “plausibility” requires more than conclusory labels—it demands factual content enabling a reasonable jury to find each element.^[3] In practice this means alleging **each essential term** when asserting breach of an express or implied contract.^[4]  

*Caniglia* specifically instructs courts that “the complaint must allege…the essential terms … in nonconclusory language.” Because “essential term” depends on context—for services contracts like those at issue here—key elements include:

1️⃣ Identification of parties;
2️⃣ Description of services promised;
3️⃣ Consideration (the amount paid);
4️⃣ Timeframe for performance;
5️⃣ Any limitation on subsequent use.

Santos’ pleading identified only that Kimmel requested videos through Cameo and later aired them; it omitted any factual detail about:

* Whether any clause within Cameo’s ToS restricted post‑delivery use;
* What consideration Kimmel provided beyond payment for each cameo;
* Whether any promise was made—explicitly or implicitly—that Kimmel would not repurpose or broadcast those videos elsewhere;
* Any deadline or quality standard governing delivery.

The appellate panel therefore deemed these omissions fatal under *Caniglia*, finding no “essential terms” alleged with enough particularity to survive dismissal outright.



## Preemption by the Copyright Act  

Even if Santos had satisfied third‑party benefit rules and pled all material contractual elements, his state–law claims could still have been barred by **federal preemption** under § 301 of the Copyright Act (“No State law shall…be construed…to give effect…to any exclusive right within the general scope…”). Courts consider whether a state claim:

1️⃣ **Regulates** matters within copyright protection rather than merely enforcing ancillary obligations;
2️⃣ **Supersedes** one or more exclusive rights reserved for copyright owners;
3️⃣ Is judged so closely related (“repudiation”) that allowing it would undermine uniform national policy.[5]  

In *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio*, Inc.*, 50 F.4th at 302 (2d Cir.​2022), the Second Circuit held that state contract claims arising from alleged misuse of copyrighted works can be preempted when they operate as alternative routes for enforcing exclusive rights—regardless of whether plaintiffs characterize them as “qualitatively different.”^[6]  

Applying *Melendez*, the district court concluded that Santos’ breach-of-contract theories essentially sought control over how his copyrighted videos were used—a core exclusive right reserved for him under § 106(1). Because his contractual allegations turned on whether Kimmel could republish his videos without permission—a question squarely addressed by copyright infringement analysis—the claims were therefore subsumed within federal jurisdiction and barred absent proof they targeted only unrelated obligations such as payment mechanisms unrelated to copying or distribution.^[7]  

The Second Circuit noted it did **not** need to fully resolve this preemption issue because Santos’ pleadings already failed on more fundamental grounds (*i.e.*, lack of third‑party beneficiary status and missing essential terms). Nevertheless, recognizing preemption underscores why plaintiffs bring copyright causes directly instead of couching them inside state contracts when dealing with reproductions or public performances.



## The District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) Analysis  

The appellate panel reiterated its de novo duty to assess whether **any** plausible set of facts—not just those assumed true by either party—could support each claim.[^[8]] Consequently:

1️⃣ **Fair Use** rendered all copyright causes moot at the pleading stage.
2️⃣ For **state–law contract claims**, applying Illinois third‑party doctrine shut down any attempt by Santos to enforce Cameo’s ToS against non‑signatories.
3️⃣ Even assuming contractual standing under New York law, failure to allege essential terms—and thereby provide fair notice—rendered his breach claims facially deficient.
4️⃣ Finally, even if pleadings had been adequate, § 301 preemption would likely have extinguished any remaining contractual theory because it attempted to regulate exclusive rights over copyrighted material.[^[9]] 

Given these cumulative deficiencies, affirmation was unavoidable under *DiFolco*’s standard for reviewing Rule 12(b)(6) dismissals.

### Takeaways for Future Practitioners  

* **Third-party beneficiaries are exceptions:** In jurisdictions like Illinois there is a strong default rule barring enforcement by those who never signed an agreement unless an express statement provides otherwise.
* **Choice-of-law matters:** When contracts specify governing law distinct from forum law—as here (Illinois vs.New York)—both sets of principles may become relevant—to determine standing under one system and defectiveness under another.
* **Pleadings must articulate each essential element:** Merely alleging “a breach” without naming specific contractual clauses or describing consideration satisfies none of *Caniglia*’s requirements.
* **Preemption acts as an additional gatekeeper:** Even well‐pleaded state remedies may vanish if they operate as alternative enforcement mechanisms for exclusive statutory rights.
* **Rule 12(b)(6) review remains fact‑agnostic:** Courts evaluate theories purely on what plaintiffs allege; they do not reach out for external evidence until after dismissal is survived.
