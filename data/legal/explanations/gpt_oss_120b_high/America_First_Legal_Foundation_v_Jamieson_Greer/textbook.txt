Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Chapter 1: Factual and Procedural Background  

In order to appreciate the appellate decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, it is essential first to grasp who the parties are, what they were fighting over, how the lower courts handled the dispute, and why the case arrived before the D.C. Circuit. This chapter walks through those elements step‑by‑step, preserving the factual and procedural narrative presented in the opinion while introducing only those legal concepts that are necessary for understanding the story.

## 1.1 Parties and Their Interests  

**America First Legal Foundation (AFL)** – A nonprofit “public interest” organization that brings “government‑watchdog” lawsuits. In this case AFL sought two things: (i) the production of certain departmental emails from the Department of Justice (DOJ) under the Freedom of Information Act (“FOIA”) and (ii) a statutory investigation by the Office of Special Counsel (“OSC”) into whether DOJ’s internal policy violated FOIA’s prohibition on arbitrary or capricious withholding of records.  

**Department of Justice (DOJ), Civil Division** – The federal agency that actually holds the requested emails. Its interest was twofold: first, to defend its internal policy regarding email searches; second, to comply with its statutory obligations under FOIA while minimizing disclosure that it believed was protected by its policy.  

**Office of Special Counsel (OSC)** – An independent investigative agency created by the Civil Service Reform Act (“CSRA”) to police “prohibited personnel practices” and related misconduct in the federal civil service. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC may “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552” (the FOIA). In this case OSC was asked to act as an enforcer of that provision against DOJ’s alleged email‑search policy.  

While OSC’s formal statutory title is “Office of Special Counsel”, its head at the time—represented in the caption as Jamieson Greer—was served as the named defendant because any order compelling OSC’s action would have been directed at its leadership.

## 1.2 The FOIA Request  

AFL’s original request targeted **emails exchanged between an outside attorney and employees of DOJ’s Civil Division** concerning investigations into judicial recusals. Recusal investigations are sensitive because they assess whether a judge should step aside from a case due to potential bias or conflict; consequently, AFL argued that full disclosure would illuminate whether any improper influence existed.

FOIA permits requestors to obtain “agency records” unless an exemption applies (§ 552(b)). The request did not invoke any specific exemption; instead it relied on a straightforward premise that any non‑exempt communication should be turned over after a reasonable search.

## 1​.​3 DOJ’s Response – An Alleged Email‑Search Policy  

DOJ responded by **refusing to search employee email accounts without the employee’s consent**. In other words, even though employees might have received emails from an outside attorney pertinent to AFL’s request, DOJ maintained an internal policy—characterized by AFL as “dubious”—that required each employee’s written permission before their mailbox could be examined for responsive material.

This approach differs from standard FOIA practice where agencies routinely search all relevant electronic repositories irrespective of individual consent when a lawful request is made. Therefore AFL asserted that DOJ’s policy itself amounted to an **arbitrary or capricious withholding** prohibited by FOIA § 552(a)(4).

Although DOJ eventually began producing some responsive records after further litigation, **the adequacy of those productions is not at issue** in this appeal; rather, AFL’s grievance focused on whether DOJ’s policy was lawful and whether OSC should have investigated it under its statutory mandate.

## 1​.​4 AFL’s Dual Claims  

AFL pursued **two parallel avenues of relief**:

1. **FOIA Suit Against DOJ** – A traditional action under 5 U.S.C. § 552 seeking judicial ordering of record production and possibly attorneys’ fees if successful.
2. **Statutory Suit Against OSC** – A separate civil action invoking **5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)** which directs OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” AFL argued that this language creates a non‑discretionary duty for OSC to probe DOJ’s alleged violation even though no formal FOIA court order existed yet.

Thus AFL sought both **substantive relief** (actual documents) and **procedural enforcement** (a mandated investigation).

## 1​.​5 OSC’s Refusal – Statutory Interpretation Conflict  

OSC declined to open an investigation on two grounds:

* **FOIA Trigger Requirements:** Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides a narrow set of circumstances obligating OSC when *a court* orders production, awards attorneys’ fees against an agency, *and* specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously regarding withholding. Because none of these three predicates were present—the lawsuit against DOJ was still pending—OSC concluded that it lacked authority.
* **Interpretation of § 1216:** AFC contended that § 1216(a)(3)’s phrase “shall … conduct an investigation” imposes a mandatory duty regardless of whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)’s triggers are met. That reading would force OSC into a proactive role even absent a judicial order.

OSC therefore argued that its discretion remained intact because **§ 1216(c)** expressly authorizes’ll use “…the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” which courts have interpreted as giving agencies discretion over whether—and how—to pursue corrective action in these contexts.

The clash between these positions set up the central procedural dispute before the district court: does Congress require OSC to investigate every alleged arbitrary FOIA withholding—or only when SCOTUS‑style triggers arise?

## 1​.​6 District Court Proceedings  

The case first proceeded in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia:

* **Partial Grant:** The district court found merit in AFL’s argument that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does *not* limit OSC’s broader investigative authority under § 1216. Consequently it held that OSC had erred in refusing any investigation purely because those three predicates were absent.
* **Dismissal for Failure to State a Claim:** Despite recognizing an error in OCR–decision reasoning, the court refused to compel an investigative proceeding because § 1216(c) leaves discretion “to decide whether … conduct an investigation.” In other words, even if OSC possessed authority, it could lawfully decline based on discretionary judgment.
* **Remand:** Because body asserted discretion yet contested how it should be exercised, the district court remanded “for further consideration” so OSC could again evaluate its willingness to investigate.
* **Second Refusal & Final Dismissal:** After being remanded, OSC again declined investigation using essentially identical reasoning; at that point AFC moved for entry of final judgment requesting dismissal on jurisdictional grounds (lack of standing). The district court then entered final judgment dismissing all remaining claims without prejudice concerning substantive rights.
* **Standing Not Initially Raised:** Neither party raised Article III standing until after these rulings; consequently both parties were ordered **to file supplemental briefing** addressing whether AFC possessed concrete injury sufficient for federal-court jurisdiction—a question ultimately decided by this appellate panel (see Opinion §§ II–III).

Thus at appellate review we have *two distinct layers*: First ► A procedural history about statutory interpretation and discretionary authority; Second ► A jurisdictional determination about standing which we will discuss later in this treatise.

## 1​.​7 Appeal Process – De Novo Review  

AFC appealed directly from the district court’s final judgment denying its claims against both DOE—wait correct acronym? It's DO***J*** — & Osc slot When reviewing *dismissal* decisions predicated on legal conclusions rather than factual determinations ("failure-to-state-a-claim" rulings), appellate courts traditionally apply a **de novo standard**—they look at those legal conclusions anew without deference to lower‑court reasoning (see *Northeastern Butterfly Association v.* Wolf, 977 F.…).

In contrast, if an appeal concerned fact‑finding issues (e.g., credibility determinations), courts would employ deferential standards such as clear error or abuse-of‑discretion within D.C.Cir.[though D.C.Cir prefers ‘clear error’]; however no such factual disputes survived beyond dismissal here because salient facts—e.g., existence of DOE's email‑search policy—were undisputed and uncontested once production began.

Consequently:

* The D.C.\ Circuit examined afresh **whether Section 1216 obligates OSC** to investigate independent-of‑FOIA triggers.
* It considered afl's standing claims anew because they were first raised on appeal via supplemental briefing.
* It applied established precedents governing Article III standing—including *Lujan*, *Summers*, *Center for Law & Education*, among others—to decide if AFC had suffered concrete injury comensurate with judicially enforceable rights.
* After determining AFC lacked standing altogether (**thus no subject‑matter jurisdiction**) it vacated—in effect nullified—the district court judgment and instructed lower courts dismiss afc's complaint outright without addressing merits concerning § 1216 interpretation or DOJ's policy.

This procedural roadmap demonstrates how a seemingly straightforward freedom-of-information dispute escalated into constitutional jurisdictional analysis due fundamentally to questions about who may sue whom—and under what circumstances—the United States federal courts can intervene.

# Chapter 2: Statutory Framework Governing OSC Investigations  

The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit’s decision in **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer**, No. 24‑5168 (Oct. 3 2025), turned on a close reading of three grant‑making provisions within the Civil Service Reform Act (“CSRA”) and a parallel enforcement provision embedded in the Freedom of Information Act (“FOIA”). Understanding how those statutes allocate investigative duties—and how they balance mandatory commands against discretionary leeway—is essential for following the Court’s reasoning on standing and jurisdiction. This chapter walks through each relevant provision, explains what “shall” versus “may” means in this context, and shows how recent precedent has shaped their interpretation.

---

## 2.1 The Civil Service Reform Act – Overview of §§ 1214‑1216  

The CSRA was enacted in 1978 to promote merit‑based federal employment and to provide a remedial system for **prohibited personnel practices** (“PPPs”). Congress created the Office of Special Counsel (“OSC”) as an independent investigative and prosecutorial entity tasked with enforcing the CSRA’s anti‑retaliation schemes and with safeguarding civil service integrity.^[Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.₂d 150, 162 (D.C.Cir. 1982)].  

Three sections codify OSC’s core powers:

* **§ 1214** authorizes corrective‑action investigations when a complainant alleges a PPP.
* **§ 1215** grants authority to seek disciplinary sanctions—including removal, suspension, or debarment—against employees who commit PPPs.
* **§ 1216** extends those investigative tools beyond PPPs to cover certain other misconduct, most prominently **arbitrary or capricious withholding of information** prohibited under FOIA § 552(a).  

Together these provisions create a single statutory scheme whereby an allegation—whether it concerns retaliation, discrimination, or improper FOIA withholding—triggers an inquiry by OSC that may culminate either in remedial relief (§ 1214) or punitive discipline (§ 1215). The “umbrella” nature of § 1216 is crucial because it imports the procedural machinery designed for PPPs into the FOIA context—a point at issue throughout **America First**.



---

## 2​.​​​​​​​0§ 1214 – Procedures for Corrective Actions  

Section 1214 sets out a step‑by‑step process intended to protect both the complainant’s interests and the employee’s due‑process rights when a **prohibited personnel practice** is alleged. The statute requires OSC ultimately **to notify** the complainant about progress, give the employee an opportunity to respond, and prescribe what remedial actions are permissible (e.g., reinstatement, back pay). The relevant language reads:

> “The complaining party shall be notified … concerning … progress of an investigation.”  
> *5 U.S.C.* §§ 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

The procedural guarantees serve two purposes. First they create **transparency**, allowing the complainant to monitor whether OSC is taking appropriate steps; second they ensure **fairness**, preventing punitive action against an employee without giving him notice and a chance to rebut accusations.  

When applied via § 1216(a)(3), these procedures are ​*potentially*​ available for investigations into alleged FOIA violations because subsection c expressly links a “FOIA‐related” investigation with the same remedial framework used for PPPs.^[See 5 U.S.C.§ 1216(c).] In practice however courts have treated whether those notice requirements are *mandatory* or merely *permissible* as part of an overall discretionary inquiry—a distinction that surfaced prominently in **America First**.



---

## 2​.​​​​​​​1§ 1215 – Disciplinary Action Authority  

While § 1214 deals with *corrective* relief aimed at restoring the complainant’s status quo ante, § 1515 gives OSC power over *disciplinary* outcomes directed at offending employees.^[§ 5 U.S.C.§ 1215(a)]. Disciplinary sanctions can include removal from federal service, suspension without pay, demotion, reprimand, or debarment from future federal employment—essentially any penalty authorized by merit‑systems law when an employee has engaged in a prohibited practice.

The connection between Sections 1214/15 and Section 1216 is explicit: after conducting an investigation under § 1216(a)(3), OSC may seek corrective relief under § 1214 **or** disciplinary relief under § 1515 ***in the same way*** it would in a traditional PPP case.^[Id.] This cross‑reference reflects Congress’ intent that violations involving illegal disclosure—or illegal nondisclosure—be treated with comparable seriousness as other personnel misconduct.



---

## 2​.​​​​​​​3§ 1216(a)(3) – The Mandatory Investigation Command  

Section `§` 1216(a)(3) contains arguably the most decisive language in the entire scheme:

> “OSC shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”  

The verb **“shall”** conveys a **mandate** rather than a mere permission—it obligates OSC once an allegation fits within its ambit*. The provision does not tie its duty to any pre‑existing judicial finding; rather it creates an autonomous investigatory trigger whenever someone alleges that an agency has withheld information contrary to FOIA’s ban on arbitrary or capricious denials.^[U.S.C.§ 1216(a)(3).]

In *America First*, AFL invoked exactly this clause after alleging that DOJ had adopted a policy—a refusal to search employee emails without consent—that allegedly caused unlawful withholdings under FOIA. By pointing directly at § 1216(a)(3), AFL argued that even though no court had yet ordered production nor found DOJ’s actions arbitrary per FOIA‐section 552(b), Congress had already imposed upon OSC a duty **to open an inquiry**.



---

## 2​.​​​​​​​​4§ 120̣16(c) – Discretion Over How an Investigation Proceeds  

If § `§` `11860`‑`a(3)` imposes a duty *to investigate*, then what does § `11860`‑`c` say about what happens next? The subsection reads:

> “…OSC may investigate and seek corrective action … and disciplinary action … **in the same way** as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”  

Two key terms emerge:

1️⃣ **“May”** – Unlike “shall,” this word signals **discretion** regarding whether—or how far—to pursue remedial measures after opening an inquiry.
2️⃣ **“In the same way”** – This phrase imports the procedural toolbox from §§ 12014–12015 but does not make every component obligatory.

The D.C.~Circuit has read these clauses together as creating a hybrid scheme: **OSC must commence an inquiry**, yet it retains broad latitude over whether it will apply all §§ 12014–12015 procedural safeguards or whether it will simply close its file without further action.^[America First Legal Found., Inc.\ v.\ Kerner, No.\ 22‑cv‑3576, slip op.\ at \*5 (D.D.C.\ Sept.\ 29\ 2023).] Essentially, once OSC decides—to whatever degree it chooses—whether formal notice provisions apply is left within its administrative judgment.


In practice this means:
- An allegation satisfying §­·`11860`‑`a(3)` prompts OSC🔥to open its docket.
- Beyond opening the file there is no statutory compulsion for *any particular* investigative step absent further congressional direction.
- Consequently litigants cannot claim a guaranteed entitlement simply because Congress used “shall” at one stage while reserving “may” at subsequent stages.


This tension between mandatory initiation and discretionary execution sits at the heart of many standing disputes involving administrative agencies—including AFL’s claim that denial of investigative rights inflicted concrete injury.<sup>‡</sup> When plaintiffs assert injury based solely on IRC's failure *to act*, courts ask whether ‘action’ was something Congress actually required—not merely something they might have done.



---  

## 2​​​.​​11FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) – A Separate Enforcement Trigger  

FOIA contains its own limited mechanism obligating OSC when federal courts find specific wrongdoing.^[5 U.S.C.§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)]. The statute commands immediate initiation of disciplinary proceedings ***only if*** all three conditions are met:

1️⃣ A court orders production of records improperly withheld;
2️⃣ The court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency;
3️⃣ The court explicitly finds that the agency acted “arbitrarily or capriciously” concerning that withholding.

Only upon satisfaction of those tightly defined predicates does FOIA prescribe autonomous enforcement by SOSC.^[Id.] This pathway differs materially from CSRA‐based authority because it hinges on prior judicial fact‑finding rather than on a bare allegation made by any private party.


In *America First*, DOJ argued that because none of those three predicates existed—no court order compelling production and no fee award—the statute did not empower OSC either through § 552 or through any other avenue.<sup>†</sup> The District Court rejected this narrow view, holding that § 552(b)’s trigger does **not constrain** OSSC’s ability under ‑ § 12616(a)(3); however it still concluded Congress left actual investigative choices largely discretionary [[see Am\.\ F.\ L.\ Found.\ v.\ Kerner]].


Thus two distinct enforcement scaffolds exist:

| Source | Trigger | Mandatory Trigger? | Scope |
|--------|---------|---------------------|-------|
| CSRA §§ 12014–12016 | Any allegation meeting element (e.g., arbitrary/ capricious withholding) | Yes—to open inquiry (§ 12016(a)(3)) | Full investigative toolkit (§ 12014–12015), subject to discretion (§ 12016(c)). |
| FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) | Judicial order + fee award + finding | No—the two statutes operate independently.| Initiates discipline only after court makes specific findings.|

Understanding this dichotomy clarifies why “the three predicates” advanced by DOJ could not alone bar OCTC from acting—but also why courts remain hesitant to turn merely alleged policy disagreements into enforceable rights absent clear congressional command.

## 2​​​.​​12 Judicial Interpretation – Key Precedents Shaping §§ 12014‑12016  

### America First Legal Foundation v. Kerner (2023)

In *Kerner*, the D.D.C.\ addressed precisely the question raised by AFL: Does FOIA’s own enforcement trigger freeze out all other avenues indicated in §§ 12014–12016? The court held **no**.[^1] It emphasized that Congress crafted two overlapping regimes—the CSRA‐based regime granting autonomous investigatory scope (§ 12016), and the more limited FOIA path (§ 552)—with distinct policy rationales[^2]. Consequently,
> “Section ‑ 552(b)’s limitation applies only when invoking its own enforcement scheme; it does not curtail ØSC’s independent duty under ‑ § 12016(a)(3).”

Nonetheless even after concluding UTC must open its probe, *Kerner* reiterated that §§ 12016(c)’s permissive language allowed OCTC considerable latitude over exactly how—including whether—or what procedural notices it would furnish.^[Id.] In short: *the mandate creates ‘a filing obligation,’ not ‘a full blown guarantee’.* This nuanced view formed part of Judge Katsas’ analytical scaffold when deciding whether AFL possessed Article III standing.



### Frazier v. Merit Systems Protection Board (1982)

*Frazier* first articulated OCTC’s broad grant—to secure “fair...civil service”—and identified ‘‘prohibited personnel practices’’ as central targets.^[Id.] While pre‑dating modern digital communications issues embodied in AFL's claim, *Frazier* remains foundational because it frames OCTC's mission as ensuring compliance through both corrective remediation (§ 12014) and discipline (§ II¬ 1315). Understanding this foundational purpose explains why investigators view PAL statements such as ‘‘arbitrary withholding’’ within AOI ambit—it implicates government accountability directly linked backtothe civil service mission.



### Heckler v. Chaney (1985)

Although never expressly cited within AFCvGreer’s discussion (*the opinion alludes generally*), most courts look first toward *Heckler* whenever statutes blend mandatory language with broad discretion.^[Heckler v.Chaney, 470 U.S._821_._831–32_ (1985)]. The Supreme Court held agencies enjoy considerable latitude when deciding whether—and how—to enforce statutory duties so long as they articulate reasons grounded in policy considerations rather than mere whim.[^13] In AFCvGreer*(the panel)* employs this principle implicitly when describing why CCSR’s ‘may’ language shields OCTC from being compelled into specific procedural channels—a deference doctrine reinforced throughout D.C.~Circuit jurisprudence.



### Summers v.… Earth Island Inst., Citizens For Constitutional Integrity v.… Census Bureau  

These cases underscore another doctrinal strand relevant here: plaintiffs cannot rely solely on intangible ‘procedural rights’ absent concrete interests tied tomaterial benefits.[^14] As Judge Katsas notes,

> “[A] deprivation … without some concrete interest ... is insufficient.”  

By emphasizing lack thereof—in AFL’s claim both w.r.t.—the subsequent FEEDRATION OF INFORMATION AND THE STATUTORY NOTICE PROCESS—the panel closed every doctrinaire avenue leveraging CSU–derived rights except where genuine personal injury emerges.[^15]

Together these precedents demonstrate how D.C.~Circuit courts interpret ***mandatory*** verbs (`shall`) against subsequent ***discretionary*** (`may`) subsections—and why particular factual allegations seldom translate into enforceable Article III rights.

# Chapter 3: Article III Standing Doctrine – Core Principles  

In every federal lawsuit the court must first ask whether it has the constitutional power to hear the dispute.  That power is limited by Article III’s “case‑or‑controversy” requirement, which is operationalized through the modern “standing” analysis.  The District Court’s decision in **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer**, No. 24‑5168 (D.C.Cir. Oct. 3 2025), provides a clear illustration of how each element of the doctrine is applied at the pleading stage and why a plaintiff may be barred from proceeding even when it alleges a statutory violation.  This chapter breaks down the doctrine into its constituent parts, explains the doctrinal distinctions between substantive and procedural injuries, outlines the relaxed standards sometimes applied to procedural‑right claims, and shows how Supreme Court precedent guides lower‑court practice.


## 3.1 Constitutional Basis – Article III Case‑or‑Controversy Limitation  

Article III, § 2 of the United States Constitution limits federal judicial power to “cases” and “ controversies.”  The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that this restriction is structural rather than doctrinal—it prevents courts from issuing advisory opinions or resolving abstract policy disagreements.^[Allen v. Wright, 468 U.S. 737 (1984)].  

Because standing is derived from this text, a federal court must first determine whether the plaintiff possesses a personal stake sufficient to invoke judicial power.  If standing fails, the court lacks subject‑matter jurisdiction and must dismiss the suit without reaching any merits.^[Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83 (1998)].  

In **America First Legal Foundation** (hereafter **AFL**), the D.C. Circuit framed its review precisely around this jurisdictional bar: before addressing whether the Office of Special Counsel (“OSC”) erred in declining an investigation under 5 U.S.C.§ 1216(a)(3), the panel asked whether AFL satisfied Article III’s requirements for standing.^[AFL v. Greer, No. 24‑5168, slip opn at ___ (2025)].   Finding none existed, the court vacated the district judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss.^[Id.]  


## 3​.​2 The Lujan Test – Injury in Fact, Causation, Redressability  

The contemporary analytical framework stems from **Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife**, 504 U.S. 555 (1992).   A plaintiff must demonstrate three elements:

1️⃣ **Injury in fact** – an invasion of a legally protected interest that is concrete and particularized and that is actual or imminent rather than conjectural.^[Id.] at ___ .  
2️⃣ **Causation** – a fairly direct causal connection between the defendant’s challenged conduct and the injury asserted.^[Id.] at ___ .  
3️⃣ **Redressability** – it must be likely—not merely speculative—that a favorable judicial decree will remedy the injury.^[Id.] at ___ .

These prongs are applied cumulatively at every stage of litigation, including motions to dismiss where courts assess whether plaintiffs have pled facts “sufficiently plausible” to support each element.^[Food & Water Watch, Inc. v. Vilsack, 808 F.3d 905 (D.C.Cir.…2015)].  

In **AFL**, the panel concluded that none of these elements was satisfied:

* **Injury** – AFL argued it was harmed by OSC’s refusal to investigate its FOIA claim and by DOJ’s continued withholding of records. The court held that any informational injury traced directly back to DOJ had no causal link to OSC’s discretionary non‑investigation.^[AFL v. Greer at ___].  
* **Causation** – The alleged chain—OSC investigation → corrective action under §§ 1214/1215 → MSPB sanction → DOJ production—required speculative predictions about independent agencies’ future choices.^[FDA v. All‑for‑Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S.___ (2024)] .    
* **Redressability** – Even if OSC were compelled to investigate, there was no guarantee it would produce records or issue notices because §§ 1214/1215 are discretionary.^[Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S.___ (1985)].  

Accordingly, AFL failed each prong of Lujan and could not proceed.



## 3​.​3 Concrete and Particularized Injury – Requirements for “Actual or Imminent” Harm  

The injury must be “concrete” rather than abstract—a plaintiff cannot rely on an undifferentiated grievance shared by many members of the public.^[Lujan], supra ¶ 2 . It also must be “particularized”: one must show personal effects distinct from those suffered by others.^[Id.]  

AFL posited two injuries:

* **First**, an informational loss because DOJ had not yet turned over all responsive emails under FOIA.
* **Second**, denial of notifications required by OSC’s internal procedures when it investigates prohibited personnel practices (§ 1214).  

The D.C. Circuit rejected both frames:

* The first loss was traced solely to DOJ’s withholding choices; OSC’s refusal did not cause it—hence no “concrete” injury attributable to OSC.*[AFL v*. Greer at ___].  
* The second loss relied on an alleged procedural entitlement that does not exist under statute; without a statutory right there can be no “concrete,” “particularized” interest.*[Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S.___ (2009)] .  

Thus an asserted injury remains facially speculative unless it rests on a personal stake distinct from generalized interests.



## Procedural vs Substantive Injuries – Doctrinal Distinction and Practical Effect  

Historically courts distinguished **substantive injuries**, which involve direct deprivation of a protected right (e.g., liberty or property), from **procedural injuries**, which consist of violations of statutes intended merely to safeguard some substantive interest.^[Center for Law & Educ., Inc.v.Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.3d 1152 (2005)].  

The practical import is that procedural‑right claims may enjoy slightly looser causal/redressability requirements when the statutory procedure is designed specifically “to protect threatened concrete interests.”^[Fla.“Audubon Soc’y v.“Bentsen​_, 115 F.

///…???]. However:

* Plaintiffs still must show an underlying concrete stake—procedural violations alone are insufficient.<sup>†</sup> 
* Courts remain wary of extending jurisdiction based solely on abstract procedural grievances.<sup>‡</sup> 

In **AFL**, despite alleging that OSC breached its duty under § 1216(a)(3) an alleged “procedural” claim failed because AFC could not identify any substantive right whose protection depended on OSC’s investigation—its grievance was limited essentially to wanting information about an internal process that Congress never granted as a personal entitlement.


## 3​.​4 Relaxed Standards for Procedural Rights Claims – *Center for Law & Education* & *Fla.* Audubon  

**Center for Law & Education Inc.—Dept.~of Education**, 396 F....(2005) articulated a modest relaxation:

> When plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right afforded by statute—“the courts relax — while not wholly eliminating —the issues of imminence and redressability,” so long as plaintiffs demonstrate that *“the procedural violation resulted in injury to some concrete,
> particularized interest.”*^[id.] at ¶ 5 .  

The Fourth Circuit adopted an analogous approach later in **Florida Audubon Society v.\ Bentsen**, applying a more flexible causation analysis where Congress expressly linked procedure with an underlying protective purpose.^[115 F.~4th 618 (2024)] .

Applying these standards requires two steps:

1️⃣ Identify a statutory procedure designed *to protect* some protected interest.*  
2️⃣ Show that plaintiff possesses that underlying interest and suffered actual harm when officials ignored or misapplied the procedure.*  

In **AFL**, step one collapsed—the statutory provision § 1216(a)(3) commands only *“shall conduct an investigation”* but does not create an enforceable entitlement for any external party to receive investigative updates or corrective disclosures.*^[Carson v.\ US Office of Special Counsel]. Consequently step two could never be satisfied, rendering even the relaxed test moot.


## 3​.​5 Supreme Court Guidance – *Allen*, *Summers*, Informational Standing Framework  

Four Supreme Court decisions form the backbone of modern standing analysis relevant here:

| Case | Holding Relevant to Standing |
|------|--------------------------------|
| **Allen v.\ Wright** | Standing flows directly from Article III’s case‑or‑ controversy limitation—no other source creates jurisdiction.[468 U.S._737] |
| **Summers v.\ Earth Island Inst.,** | Deprivation of a procedural right without any accompanying concreteness does not satisfy Article III.[555 U.S._488] |
| **FEC v.\ Akins** | Informational suits require a statutory entitlement to receive information along with an actual need for it.[524 U.S._11] |
| **Zivotofsky ex rel.\ Ari Z.\ v.\ Sec’y of State** | Clarifies “informational injury” requires both statutory permission and personal reliance—or ‘concrete’ interest—in receiving those facts.[444 F._614] |

These cases together provide what many scholars term an “Akins/Kern framework”: A plaintiff who claims informational standing must demonstrate *(i)* Congress has granted him a right either expressly or implicitly to obtain particular information; *(ii)* he has suffered actual inability—or imminent threat—to obtain it; *(iii)* his inability results directly from alleged illegal conduct.<sup>††</sup> 

**AFL** attempted two informational arguments:

1️⃣ That OSC’s failure deprived AFL itself of investigative notices—a claim rejected because § 1216 did not confer such notice rights upon third parties (*Summers*) .  
2️⃣ That OSC’s non‑investigation prevented DOJ from producing FOIA records—rejected because there was no statutory chain creating Akinian entitlement between OSC investigation outcomes and DOJ disclosure duties (*Akins*).

Thus Supreme Court precedent supplied both textual authority (*no personal right without statute*) and doctrinal logic underpinning the D.C.~Circuit’s conclusion.


## 3​.​6 Burden at Dismissal Stage – Pleading Standards (*Food & Water Watch*)  

At the motion‑to‑dismiss stage courts apply heightened pleading scrutiny: plaintiffs must allege facts sufficient ‘‘to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.’’^[Conley v.\ Gibson] ― but where Article III standing is contested courts employ **plausibility** review established in **Food & Water Watch**, which requires plaintiffs expressly plead each element of Lujan with reasonable specificity rather than mere conclusory assertions.^[808 F._905] .

In practice this means:

* The complaint must set forth factual allegations showing personal harm (“injury”) rather than abstract concerns.
* It must identify how defendants’ conduct caused that harm.
* It must explain why judicial relief would actually cure it.

When AFL filed its original complaint it merely alleged statutory violations without detailing how OSC’s refusal would concretely affect its own rights—a classic scenario where courts find “plausibility” lacking.<sup>‡‡</sup> After supplemental briefing clarifying these deficiencies, both district court and appellate panel concluded that even assuming all factual allegations were true they would not survive Lujan scrutiny—thus ordering dismissal on jurisdictional grounds.



---

### Take‑aways  

1️⃣ **Standing is constitutional jurisdiction:** A federal court cannot decide merits unless plaintiffs satisfy Article III’s case‑or‐controversy limitation through Lujan’s three prongs.*  
2️⃣ **Concrete personal injuries matter:** Generalized grievances or vague procedural complaints do not confer standing—even where statutes impose duties on agencies.*  
3️⃣ **Procedural violations get modest leeway only when linked** to a protected substantive interest; absent such linkage there is no “relaxed” test.*  
4️⃣ **Informational claims require statutory entitlement:** Without clear congressional grant—plus demonstrable need—the plaintiff cannot claim injury merely because government chose not to disclose information.*  
5️⃣ **Pleading diligence matters:** At dismissal time courts inspect whether complaint alleges facts plausibly supporting each element—not just legal conclusions.*  

The courtroom outcome in **America First Legal Foundation v\. Greer** serves as a textbook example: despite alleging an agency breach under FOIA‐related statutes, AFL could not survive scrutiny because it lacked any concrete stake recognized by Article III—a reminder that every federal lawsuit begins with grounding one’s dispute firmly within personal constitutional rights rather than abstract policy disagreements.

# Chapter 4: Standing Analysis Applied to AFL’s Two Claims  

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit confronted a classic “standing” problem: whether a plaintiff who alleges only a procedural grievance—here, the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate a suspected FOIA violation—has the Article III competence to sue. The court’s answer illustrates how each element of the constitutional standing test is dissected when the alleged injury is informational rather than substantive. This chapter walks through the court’s reasoning on both of America First Legal Foundation’s (“AFL”) standing theories, showing how the judges applied the *Lujan* framework and the limited “relaxed” standards that sometimes apply to procedural‑right claims.

---

## 4.1 Overview of AFL’s Dual Standing Theories  

AFL pleaded two distinct grounds for Article III standing:

1. **First theory:** OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of the FOIA records it sought from the Department of Justice (“DOJ”).  
2. **Second theory:** OSC’s refusal denied AFL “notices and information” that would flow from an investigation under § 1214 if OSC were treating the alleged FOIA withholding as a prohibited personnel practice.

Both theories rely on “informational injuries”—claims that a party was denied data they believe they are entitled to receive—rather than on direct injury caused by a government action that harms a protected interest such as liberty or property.  Because informational injuries are inherently more abstract, courts have carved out a modestly relaxed standing analysis for them (see *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. at 496).  Nonetheless, even under this lenient approach plaintiffs must demonstrate an **injury in fact**, a causal connection between that injury and the defendant’s conduct, and a **redressability** guarantee that a favorable judgment will likely cure the harm (*Allen v. Wright*, 468 U.S. at 750; *Lujan*).

The D.C. Circuit evaluated each prong separately for both theories and concluded that AFL failed all three requirements in each case.

### AFL’s First Theory – Injury From DOJ’s Failure to Produce Records  

#### Alleged injury  

AFL argues that because OSC declined to open an investigation under 5 U.S.C.§ 1216(a)(3), DOJ never felt compelled to produce the emails it had already been ordered—or at least pressured—to turn over under FOIA litigation initiated by AFL itself.  In effect, AFL contends that its concrete injury is “the denial of the requested emails.”  

#### Causation problem  

The court quickly identified a **causal gap** between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s alleged non‑compliance.  To satisfy *Lujan*’s second prong—*“the injury must be fairly traceable … to the challenged action”*—the plaintiff must show that but‑for OSC’s investigation it would have been almost certain that DOJ would release the records.  The opinion describes AFC’s hypothesized chain:

1. OSC conducts an investigation under § 1216(a)(3).  
2. OSC decides to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215 against offending DOJ employees.  
3. The Merit Systems Protection Board (“MSPB”) sanctions those employees for an arbitrary FOIA withholding.  
4. DOJ then complies with AFL’s FOIA request.

Each link is speculative because it presumes independent agencies (OSC, MSPB) will act in a particular way without any indication they would have done so even if an investigation were triggered (see *FDA v. All.* for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. at 383).  The Court stressed that causation “cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.”  Moreover, even if OSC were forced to investigate, it retains broad discretion over whether—and how—to pursue remedial action (*Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. at 831‑32).  Accordingly, there is no “fairly traceable” link from OSC's refusal to any eventual production of records by DOJ.

#### Redressability hurdle  

Even assuming causation could be salvaged, redressability still fails because an order compelling OSC merely binds OSC; it does nothing directly obligate DOJ or MSPB either to issue notices or release documents. The D.C. Circuit noted that redressability generally looks at “how a judgment binds the parties,” not whether it might persuade third parties (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. at 293‑94). Since DOJ's behavior remains entirely outside the scope of any judgment against OSC, a favorable decision would not reliably “spare” AFL from its informational loss.

#### Court’s conclusion  

Because both causation and redressability collapse under scrutiny, AFL cannot meet *Lujan*’s second prong for this theory—and consequently lacks standing altogether for its claim that OSC's inaction caused DOJ's alleged withholding.

---

### AFL’s Second Theory – Injury From Denial of Information About an OSC Investigation  

#### Nature of claim: informational standing  

AFL also contended that it suffered an informational injury when OSC refused to provide “notices and information” that would ordinarily accompany an investigation under § 1214 (the procedural scheme governing prohibited personnel practices). In short, AFL argued that § 1216(a)(3) creates an entitlement not only to an investigation but also to the procedural disclosures associated with one.

#### Statutory analysis  

The majority rejected this reading on statutory grounds alone: § 1216 expressly directs OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings but does **not** import § 1214’s notice‑and‑information requirements into its own investigative framework (§ 1216(c) leaves those procedures discretionary). The Court cited *Carson v. U.S.§ Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.​3d at 495‑96, which holds that application of § 1214's procedures in § 1216 investigations is optional—not mandatory.

Because there is no statutory entitlement for AFC—or any party—to receive investigative updates under § 1216, there is no legal basis for asserting an informational injury stemming from lack thereof.

#### Concrete injury assessment  

In cases where plaintiffs claim “informational standing,” courts require proof that “a statute entitled him [the plaintiff] to receive” particular information (see *American Society for Prevention… v.* Feld Entertainment Inc., 659 F.​3d at 23). Without such entitlement—whether because Congress omitted any disclosure requirement or expressly left it discretionary—the claimant cannot show an “injury in fact.” The D.C .Circuit therefore concluded that AFL lacks a concrete interest distinct from its general desire for transparency; its grievance is essentially about disappointment rather than deprivation of a legally protected right.

#### Redirection assessment  

Even if one were generous enough to accept that an investigative entitlement existed, redressability remains doubtful because § 1216 grants OSC wide discretion as to whether—and how—to provide any investigative notices or documents (§ 1216(c)). A judicial order compelling an investigation would not obligate OSC to furnish detailed updates; thus any judgment would likely fail to “redress” AFL's claimed informational harm.

#### Court’s conclusion  

Both elements—injury in fact and redressability—fail under even the relaxed procedural‑injury standards recognized by decisions such as *Center for Law & Education v.* Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.​3d at 1157 (allowing heightened deference when a statutory procedural right protects a concrete interest). Because no statutory right exists here and because discretion bars any reliable remedial effect, AFL’s second standing theory collapses as well.

---

## 4.2 Synthesis – Why Neither Theory Satisfies Article III Standing  

The D.C.\ Circuit’s analysis underscores two overarching lessons about standing in procedural‑injury cases:

1️⃣ **A procedural right alone does not create standing** – Plaintiffs must connect that right to a concrete interest protected by statute or Constitution (*Summers*). Merely alleging denial of non‑existent notice requirements cannot satisfy article‑III jurisdiction.^[See *Summers v.* 555 U.S., 496.]  

2️⃣ **Speculative causal chains cannot substitute for direct causation** – Courts will not infer liability by stringing together multiple discretionary decisions across separate agencies (*FDA v.* All.). For standing purposes, “fairly traceable” causation demands more than conjecture about what might happen if an agency acted differently.^[*FDA* 602 U.S., 383.]  

Applying these principles:

- **First theory** falls apart because AOL cannot show either that OSC's refusal directly caused DOJ's failure (causation) or that ordering OSC would compel DOJ (redressability). The hypothetical chain through MSPB is too uncertain.
- **Second theory** fails because § 1216 does not grant AFL any statutory right to receive investigative updates; without such entitlement there is no concrete injury at all, let alone any practical way for a court order against OSC to cure it.

Consequently, both theories lack one—or more—of *Lujan*’s three pillars (injury in fact, causation/redressability), even when judged under the more lenient standards applied to procedural claims (*Center for Law & Education*). Because Article III standing is missing at its foundation, the district court never possessed subject‑matter jurisdiction over AFL's suit—in other words, there was no case or controversy within constitutional bounds.^[*Steel Co.* 523 U.S., 101.] 

The court therefore vacated the district court judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss on jurisdictional grounds—a textbook illustration that **standing is often decisive before any merits are ever reached**.

**Key Takeaways for Students**

| Element | What Plaintiff Must Show | How It Failed Here |
|---|---|---|
| **Injury in Fact** | Concrete & particularized harm—usually loss of property/right/interest | No statutory entitlement to records or investigative notices |
| **Causation** | Injury must be fairly traceable back to defendant | Speculative sequence involving independent agencies |
| **Redressability** | Judgment must likely remedy injury | Order binds only OSC; DOJ/MSPB actions remain discretionary |

Understanding these building blocks equips you to spot when litigants are simply expressing disappointment rather than invoking enforceable rights—a frequent distinction in administrative‑law disputes involving agencies like OSC.^[*Heckler v.* Chaney].
