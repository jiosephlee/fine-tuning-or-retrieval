Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Standing Still: How *America First Legal Foundation* v. Jamieson Greer Refines Procedural Injury Doctrine  

## Why This Case Matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s October 2025 decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* may not have resolved any substantive question about the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to investigate alleged FOIA violations, but it delivers a crisp reminder of the Constitution’s case‑or‑controversy requirement. By holding that a plaintiff cannot sue merely because an agency declined to “investigate” under a statutory grant of discretion, the panel tightens the standards for procedural‑injury standing—especially where the plaintiff’s alleged harm is the denial of information that would flow from an agency’s internal enforcement process rather than from a direct agency act. Litigants who hope to compel investigations, audits or other “watchdog” actions now face a higher hurdle: they must show a concrete, particularized injury that is traceable to the agency’s refusal and redressable by a judicial order.

In practice, this decision nudges FOIA plaintiffs back toward more traditional remedies—mandamus orders compelling record production or suits for declaratory judgments—rather than attempting to leverage OSC’s enforcement machinery as a proxy for FOIA relief. It also signals how courts may treat procedural rights that are “in vacuo” when they carry no independent entitlement to information or benefits.

## The Procedural Context  

OSC was created by Congress in 1978 to police prohibited personnel practices and ensure a “fair…civil service.” Its enforcement toolbox comprises corrective actions under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 and disciplinary actions under § 1215. Section 1216 extends this toolbox to certain non‑personnel misconduct, notably arbitrary or capricious WITHHOLDING of FOIA‑protected information: “OSC shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding….”¹

Statutory commentators have long noted that OSC’s investigative authority under § 1216 is distinct from its limited duty under FOIA itself—U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—which only triggers an OSC inquiry if a court has already ordered production and found an agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. The question before the D.C. Circuit was whether OSC could independently investigate FOIA policy objections even when those three predicate conditions were missing.

The America First Legal Foundation (AFL) argued that § 1216 imposes an *imperative* duty to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent—a policy at issue in AFL’s FOIA suit against DOJ over recusal‑related communications. When OSC declined on the basis that none of the § 552 triggers were satisfied, AFL sued for a declaratory judgment that OSC must investigate and for injunctive relief forcing such an investigation.

The district court found OSC’s reliance on § 552 compelling but ultimately held that it possessed discretion under § 1216(c), remanding for further consideration. AFL appealed—not on whether OSC “should” investigate—but on whether it had standing to demand such an investigation.

## The D.C. Circuit’s Standing Analysis  

### The Classical Requirements  

The panel began with textbook Article III doctrine: plaintiffs must demonstrate (1) an injury in fact that is concrete and particularized, (2) causation linking the injury to the defendant’s conduct, and (3) redressability—i.e., a favorable judicial decision must likely remedy the injury.² *Lujan* provides the bright‑line articulation; *Steel* reminds us that these thresholds are jurisdictional, not merely claim‑making hurdles.

AFL framed its claim around two alleged procedural injuries:

1. **Informational injury** – because OSC allegedly failed to investigate, DOJ allegedly continued withholding records.
2. **Procedural injury** – because OSC allegedly denied AFL notice and information required under Section 1214 when it conducts investigations of “prohibited personnel practices.”

Both theories hinge on treating OSC’s statutory duty as creating a private right enforceable by suit.

### Procedural Rights Are Not Sui Generis Injuries  

Drawing heavily on *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, the D.C. Circuit emphasized that a procedural violation “in vacuo” does not confer Article III standing unless it tangibly harms a plaintiff’s legally protected interest.^3 In other words, merely being denied the benefit of an investigative process does not itself constitute an injury unless there is some concrete stake at stake—often an underlying substantive right like environmental protection, personal liberty, or in FOIA contexts, access to information.

The court therefore required AFL to connect OSC’s refusal directly to its own informational loss—a standard recognized in decisions such as *Center for Law & Education v.* Dep’t of Educ.* (procedural injury cases require proof that denial of procedure causes harm beyond abstract disappointment). The panel found AFL had not met this burden.

#### Causation Breakdown  

AFL attempted a causal chain stretching from OSC’s alleged failure to investigate → possible corrective action under § 1214/§ 1215 → MSPB sanctions → DOJ compliance → release of records. The court rejected this chain as speculative at every step:

- **Speculation about agency choice:** Courts have long held that agencies enjoy discretion in enforcement decisions (*Heckler v. Chaney*). Judicial compulsion does not override discretionary judgment absent clear statutory command.
- **Multiple actors outside review:** The chain involves MSPB decisions and DOJ policy determinations—both beyond AFL’s suit and insulated from direct judicial control.
- **No requisite nexus:** As *FDA v. All‑for Hippocratic Med.* underscores, causation cannot be predicated on conjecture about independent actors' future conduct.

Thus, even assuming OSC had investigated, there was no guarantee—much less likelihood—that any subsequent disciplinary action would force DOJ to produce records demanded by AFC’s original FOIA request.

#### Redressability Hurdle  

Even more decisive was redressability: would ordering OSC to investigate effectively cure AFL's informational grievance? As *Haaland v.* Brackeen reminds us, redressability looks at whether the judgment binds the defendant enough to make relief probable—not whether it might persuade third parties into compliance. Because any downstream effect on DOJ lay outside ESC's sphere of control—and because § 1216 permits discretion even after an investigation—the court concluded any beneficial spillover was too attenuated.

### Procedural Injury via Section 1214 Notice  

AFL also asserted an informational injury based on Section 1214 notice requirements—a procedural safeguard meant for investigations into prohibited personnel practices.^4 By analogy, they argued if OSC treats a § 1216 inquiry like a § 1214 one, they should receive notice about investigation progress—a concrete benefit they were denied.

However, precedent dictates that this borrowing is limited by *Carson v.* U.S.\ Office of Special Counsel which holds that applying § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations remains discretionary.^5 Because CFL does not create a legal entitlement to receive those notices absent explicit statutory language extending them, AFL could not claim “injury” from their absence alone.

Consequently — lacking both causation and redressability — AFL fell short on both fronts of its procedural‑injury theory.

### Bottom Line: No Article III Case  

The opinion concluded succinctly: without a concrete injury traceable and redressable through judicial action against OSC, AFL lacks standing; thus the district court’s judgment is vacated for lack of jurisdiction and remanded for dismissal.^6

## Critical Perspective – Is This Ruling Too Rigid?  

The D.C. Circuit followed precedent closely; yet one might ask whether strict adherence serves congressional intent behind § 1216(3). Congress appears to have wanted OSC—an entity equipped with investigatory tools—to act as a watchdog over agencies’ adherence to FOIA criteria even when no court has yet found arbitrary conduct. By treating that provision as merely discretionary imagination rather than enforceable duty, courts risk muting an important enforcement mechanism designed precisely because FOIA litigation can be slow and resource‑intensive for private litigants.

On the other hand, allowing private parties to compel agencies into investigations raises serious separation‑of‑powers concerns: ``investigative discretion’’ is traditionally immune from judicial commandeering (*Heckler*) absent clear congressional command geared toward public actors rather than private enforcers.[^7] Moreover, opening **every** claim about alleged agency policy misstep into Article III courts could flood courts with pre‑emptive suits lacking genuine harm—a scenario Congress likely sought to avoid by coupling § 552 triggers with OSC's investigatory authority.

In short, while some may see the decision as overly formalist—blocking litigants from using existing statutory mechanisms—the reasoning aligns with long‑standing jurisprudence limiting Article III jurisdiction when plaintiffs assert injuries rooted solely in abstract policy disagreements rather than demonstrable harm upon themselves.

## Doctrinal Implications  

1. **Clarifying Procedural‑Injury Limits** – The opinion refines how courts will evaluate claims founded on statutory procedural rights absent explicit entitlement provisioned by law. The three‑prong Lujan test remains untouched but its application now emphatically requires genuine concrete harm—not merely disappointment at its being denied.
   
2 **Scope of Discretion Under Section 1216** – By emphasizing that §§ 1216(c) books discretion even after an investigation commences—parallel language exists in other enforcement statutes like FDIC's consumer protection provisions—the ruling underscores Congress’ intention for flexible policing rather than mandatory investigative mandates.
   
3 **FOIA Enforcement Landscape** – Plaintiffs seeking “hit‑and‑run” enforcement via special counsel will now see standing as another formidable obstacle; they must instead focus on direct challenges such as mandamus actions against agencies refusing disclosure (§ 552(d)) or seek class action status where appropriate injuries are more readily proved.
   
4 **Potential Spillover To Other Statutes** – The logic will likely be extended when litigants invoke other oversight bodies (e.g., Federal Trade Commission commissioners’ investigations under Section 5(a)(11), Department of Labor wage‐and‐hour investigators) hoping to compel investigations rather than remediate direct violations.
   
5 **Preservation Of Agency Discretion** – This decision buttresses longstanding deference doctrines (*Heckler*, *Whitman*) by reaffirming that any statutory grant permitting “investigation” does not alone create judicially enforceable rights unless coupled with specific remedial language—particularly relevant where statutes articulate “may” rather than “shall” regarding internal proceedings.
   

## Downstream Effects  

Practically speaking, we anticipate:

- **Strategic Shift** – Litigation groups will allocate resources toward direct FOIA suits demanding production or pursuing sanctions once noncompliance is adjudicated—not toward persuading OSC—or similar entities—to act preemptively.
- **Legislative Response** – If Congress truly intended robust oversight by special counsels absent judicial trigger events (as suggested by SALC's mooted name), we might see amendments explicitly granting ``private right of action'' language—or at least clarifying notice/entitlement provisions—to overcome standing deficits.
- **Administrative Remedies** – Agencies may reinforce internal grievance processes giving requesters clearer pathways (e.g., mandatory notification when requests are denied based on email search policies) so plaintiffs can articulate personal injuries without invoking external oversight bodies.
- **Judicial Caution** – Other circuits will likely cite *America First* when faced with procedural–injury claims involving discretionary investigative statutes; this may reduce frivolous suits seeking injunctions against agency inactivity but could also dampen legitimate enforcement pressure where statutory language suggests mandatory oversight.
   

## Conclusion  

*America First Legal Foundation v.* Greer does not reshape substantive ALT policy on FOIA investigations; instead it sharpens doctrinal edges around procedural standing under Article III—a reminder that invoking investigative authority doesn’t automatically manufacture justiciable controversy. For litigants eyeing administrative agencies’ investigatory powers as leverage against alleged information withholding conspiracies, their path forward now must include demonstrable personal harm traceable directly back to those agencies—all within clear redressability parameters laid out by Supreme Court precedent.

Until Congress decides otherwise—or perhaps rescues its own enforcement toolbox through clearer language—the courts will remain faithful stewards of constitutional limits: sitting still when presented only with abstract grievances while reserving authority for cases where real injury demands real remedy.*
