Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Two Paths, One Goal: The Tension Between FOIA’s Built‑in OSC Enforcement (§ 552) and the Separate Scheme in §§ 1214–1216  

## Why the case matters  

When a federal agency refuses a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request, plaintiffs traditionally rely on the courts to compel disclosure.  Yet Congress gave the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) a parallel enforcement track: after a court orders production under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to discipline the official responsible.  At the same time, §§ 1214–1216 of the Civil Service Reform Act let OSC treat certain FOIA withholdings as “prohibited personnel practices” and investigate them under essentially the same machinery it uses for whistle‑blower retaliation claims.  

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in **America First Legal Foundation v. Greer**, No. 24‑5168 (Oct. 3 2025), does not resolve which statute governs OSC’s duty when a party alleges an arbitrary withholding absent a court order. Instead, it sidesteps the clash by holding that the plaintiff lacks Article III standing.  That procedural outcome leaves a substantive question alive—how should courts interpret Congress’s dual‑track design?  The answer will shape everything from agency compliance cultures to the strategic calculus of FOIA litigants who hope to lever OSC’s internal teeth instead of taking every dispute through a federal district court.

## The statutory landscape  

### The “trigger” regime in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)  

FOIA’s enforcement provision is straightforward: if a court orders an agency to produce records and finds that the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously, then OSC must investigate “promptly.”  The language creates an *after‑the‑fact* duty that only springs into life once a judicial determination exists.  The statute does not give parties any stand‑alone right to compel OSC action; instead it ties OSC’s hands to a judicial finding of illegality.

### §§ 1214–1216: A broader investigative toolbox  

Section 1214 furnishes procedures for investigating “prohibited personnel practices” (PPPs), including notice requirements and the possibility of corrective or disciplinary relief before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB).  Section 1216 expands those procedures beyond traditional PPPs, expressly authorizing investigations into “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” In other words, when an agency withholds information in violation of FOIA, that conduct can be treated as a PPP for purposes of OSC’s enforcement machinery.  

Section 1216(c) further says that OSC may pursue corrective action “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” implying that any procedural safeguards attached to PPP investigations could flow over onto FOIA investigations as well—*if* OSC chooses to use them.  

The tension lies in whether § 1216 creates an independent duty for OSC to launch an investigation whenever an alleged FOIA violation is reported (“mandatory” view), or whether it merely gives OSC discretionary authority alongside its trigger‑based duty under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) (“discretionary” view).

## What Greer says (and doesn’t say)  

In **Greer**, America First Legal Foundation (AFL) complained that the Department of Justice refused to search employee emails without consent—a policy AFL argued was contrary to FOIA’s requirement that agencies search all responsive records regardless of employee privacy interests unless an exception applies.  After DOJ began producing responsive records in an unrelated suit, AFL turned to OSC, invoking § 1216(a)(3) and asserting that OSC had a non‑negotiable duty to investigate its policy claim.

The district court found—on its own—an error in OSC’s reliance on § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)’s trigger language but ultimately declined to order an investigation because it read § 1216(c)’s discretion provision as limiting any mandatory duty.  On appeal, Judge Katsas framed the entire dispute around standing rather than statutory interpretation and held that AFL lacked Article III injury because any alleged informational harm was traceable back to DOJ, not to OSC’s investigatory choices.  Consequently, the appellate panel vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

Crucially, **Greer** does not decide whether § 1216 imposes a statutory “must investigate” command independent of a court order; it merely postpones that question indefinitely by finding no standing at all.

## Prior circuit attempts at harmony  

Courts have wrestled with this clash before:

* **America First Legal Foundation v. Kerner**, No. 22‑cv‑3576 (D.D.C. Sept. 29 2023) – The district court held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not preclude OSC from investigating alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings under § 1216 even when the trigger provision is unmet, but stayed short of mandating such investigations.
* **Carson v. Office of Special Counsel**, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011) – The Sixth Circuit recognized that while § 1216 grants investigatory authority over FOIA violations, discretion remains because Section 1214 procedural protections are not automatically applicable.
* **United States v. OLC**, 483 F.Supp.2d 1235 (D.D.C. 2007) – A D.C.~District Court suggested that Congress intended both regimes as complementary tools: one reactive (post‑judgment), one proactive (policy complaints).

Collectively these rulings depict a fractured doctrinal picture: some judges read §§ 1214–1216 as giving OSC *the power* but *not* *the obligation* to act absent a judicial finding; others see potential conflict but defer resolution pending concrete cases where plaintiffs can demonstrate injury sufficient for standing.

## What would it mean if § 1216 were read as mandatory?  

If courts embraced a mandatory reading—requiring OSC **to** investigate any complaint alleging arbitrary FOIA withholding regardless of whether a court has already declared such conduct illegal—the impact would be profound:

1. **Preemptive oversight** – Agencies could be reined in earlier because OSC would have an investigatory lever before litigants endure costly district‑court battles.
2. **Increased workload for OSC** – A flood of FOIA policy complaints could strain limited resources unless Congress allocates additional funding.
3. **Potential chilling effect on agency policy** – Knowing that even innocuous policy statements could trigger an investigation might push agencies toward more cautious interpretations—or conversely toward broader exemptions protected by other statutes.
4. **Redefinition of abstract “injury”** – Plaintiffs could claim concrete injury simply from being denied procedural notices about an investigation—a shift toward recognizing procedural rights as stand-alone injuries under Article III jurisprudence.
5. **Judicial deference dominoes** – Because Section 552’s trigger language would become subordinate rather than superseding, courts might be less inclined to find agencies’ discretionary interpretations de facto final after they survive initial lawsuits.

In short, treating § 1216 as obligatory would tilt FOIA enforcement toward administrative self‑policing while potentially diminishing courts’ role as ultimate arbiters unless parties still seek judicial review after an unfavorable administrative outcome.

## What would it mean if § 1216 remains discretionary?  

A discretionary reading preserves today’s status quo:

* Plaintiffs who cannot secure a favorable district‑court judgment still lack any strong foothold for forcing agency compliance beyond traditional litigation.
* Agencies retain broad latitude over internal search policies—like DOJ’s email‐search rule—knowing OSC may elect not to intervene.
* Courts continue bearing the brunt of enforcing FOIA rights through injunctions and awards of attorney fees—the primary leverage many litigants rely upon.
* Legislative intent stays ambiguous but arguably reflects congressional caution against creating an additional administrative watchdog whose mandate could overlap wetly with existing judicial remedies.

The downside is obvious: many private entities like America First Legal Foundation must endure protracted litigation just to get at policies they allege are unlawful even though those policies do not yet rise into enforceable judgments against agencies.

## Legislative fixes – cutting through ambiguity  

Congress has two relatively clean options:

1️⃣ **Amend § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** – Replace “shall promptly initiate” with language expressly linking its duty back to any credible allegation investigated under §§ 1214–1216 *or* create parallel language establishing parallel duties so both provisions operate side by side without conflict.
   
2️⃣ **Clarify §§ 1214–1216** – Insert explicit text stating that “the authority granted under section 1216(a)(3) includes a mandatory investigative duty whenever alleged arbitrary withholding is reported,” and define which procedural safeguards (notice requirements from § 1214(b)) must accompany such investigations.
   
A third hybrid approach could preserve discretion but impose *minimum* reporting obligations—for example mandating that after refusing an investigation based on discretion, OSC must issue written findings outlining its rationale and any remedial steps taken internally—a move akin to what some whistleblower statutes require.

Any amendment should also address redressability concerns raised by Greer: clarifying what relief courts can order against OSC (e.g., mandating issuance of notices or compelling remedial action) would help future plaintiffs overcome standing hurdles tied forcefully to concrete injuries rather than speculative downstream effects.

## Downstream effects – what lawyers and agencies should watch  

For FOIA practitioners:

* Expect continued reliance on traditional district‑court motions until Congress clarifies or courts definitively interpret §§ 1214–1216.
* When drafting complaints against agencies’ internal policies (e.g., email search rules), consider framing arguments around concrete harms distinct from mere policy disagreement—perhaps focusing on reputational injury or loss of advocacy efficacy—to satisfy heightened standing requirements.
* Keep tabs on any new OMB guidance regarding best practices for email searches; agencies may voluntarily align their policies with emerging standards preemptively if they sense legislative pressure building around this issue.

For government agencies:

* Reevaluate internal rules about searching employee communications—especially given recent litigation exposing potential mismatches between privacy concerns and statutory duties.
* Document decision‑making processes thoroughly; robust intra‑agency records can help mitigate risks should OSC decide later to intervene under either statutory scheme.
* Monitor budgetary proposals related to OSC; increased funding may signal congressional intent toward stronger oversight via §§ 1214–1216.

For policymakers:

* Review existing data on how often ESC investigations are launched under § 1200 versus after judicial orders; quantitative analysis could illuminate which track provides more effective compliance incentives.
* Engage stakeholders—including civil society groups like America First Legal Foundation—in drafting amendments so reforms balance agency flexibility with citizen access rights without spawning another administrative quagmire.

## Conclusion  

**America First Legal Foundation v. Greer** exemplifies how procedural doctrines—standing doctrine above all—can curtain substantial statutory questions from judicial scrutiny for years longer than either Congress nor litigants intended them to be hidden behind. While Greer left open whether §§ 1214–​1216 impose *a duty* independent from § 552’s trigger clause, it underscored how thin Article III requirements are when plaintiffs claim only procedural injuries without concrete informational benefits tied directly back to the defendant agency.

Policymakers now face a clear choice: codify an unambiguous duty for OSC over arbitrary FOIA withholding—or accept continued reliance on courts despite their backlog and expense—to achieve Congress’ original goal point-blank—“fair, efficient, lawful civil service.” Whichever path they take will dictate whether future vigilance over government secrecy stems more from whistleblowers’ internal complaints or from plaintiffs’ courtroom battles—a decision worth watching closely for anyone who values transparent government today and tomorrow.
