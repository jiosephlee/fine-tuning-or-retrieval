Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What is ‘informational standing’ and does a statutory entitlement automatically confer it?
Question:
The opinion discusses ‘informational injury’ and references cases like *Zivotofsky v. Sec’y of State* and *AFC v. Feld*. If a statute (e.g., § 552 or § 1216) grants a party a right to receive certain information, does that automatically give rise to Article III standing when that information is withheld? Or must there be an additional concrete injury beyond the mere denial of the statutory right?
Answer:
**Informational standing – what it is and when a statutory right to information creates it**

---

### 1.  The basic rule

“Informational standing” is just another name for the *injury‑in‑fact* requirement of Article III when the plaintiff’s injury consists of being **denied information that a statute gives him a legal right to receive**.  
The Supreme Court has phrased the doctrine repeatedly:

* “A plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a ‘statute entitled him to receive.’” – *American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld*, 659 F.3d at 23 (quoting *Zivotofsky* (2006)).  
* The injury must be “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent.” – *Lujan* (1992); *Akers v. FEC*, 524 U.S. at 21 (the “informational‑injury” formulation).

Thus, two things are required:

1. **Statutory entitlement** – the law must expressly give the plaintiff a right **to obtain** the particular information.
2. **Denial or threat of denial** – the plaintiff must show he has been—or will be—prevented from receiving that information, creating a concrete personal harm.

If both prongs are satisfied, the plaintiff may proceed under ordinary Article III standing; no additional “substantive” harm is needed.

---

### 2.  A statutory grant is **not** sufficient by itself

The DC Circuit makes clear that *the existence of a statutory grant alone does not automatically confer standing*. The courts still require an actual or imminent denial that translates into a concrete injury.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 WL …, Judge Katsas wrote:

> “A plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a ‘statute entitled him to receive.’ … But here … § 1216 … creates no entitlement to receive information.”  

The court therefore rejected AFL’s claim that § 1216 gave it standing simply because the statute tells OSC it “shall conduct an investigation.” The provision merely authorizes OSC’s **investigative power**, leaving discretion as to whether any notice or data are provided to the complainant. Because no statutory right **to receive** information was created, there was no informational injury at all.

---

### 3.  How the court applied the rule

| Theory advanced by AFL | Why it failed |
|---|---|
| **(a) Injured by DOJ’s refusal under FOIA (§ 552)** | The alleged injury traces back to DOJ, not OSC; there is no causal link between OSC’s discretionary decision not to investigate and DOE’s withholding (speculative chain). Hence no redressability and no personal stake in OSC’s conduct. |
| **(b) Injured by lack of notices/info from OSC under § 1216** | § 1216 does **not** grant AFL any right “to receive” OSC‑generated notices or investigative reports. The statute only empowers OSC to *investigate*, with discretion over how much information it gives parties (see *Carson v. USC*). Without an affirmative entitlement, there can be no “informational injury.” |

Both theories collapse because they either lack *concrete* causation/redressability (*a*) or lack an underlying *statutory entitlement* (*b*).

---

### 4.  Contrast with statutes that do confer informational standing

- **FOIA (§ 552)** – directly provides every person “the right to… request access” to agency records. A denial here **does** create an informational injury because Congress gave each requester an enforceable right to obtain those records.
- **Other statutes** – many grant agencies authority (*e.g.*, § 1216) but stop short of promising any particular individual receipt of documents or notices; those statutes do **not** by themselves generate informational standing for requesters.

The distinction matters even when a statute appears “rights‑bearing.” If the text merely says an agency *may* disclose or *shall* investigate without saying “the requester shall receive X,” courts treat any alleged denial as lacking an enforceable entitlement and therefore lacking an informational injury.

---

### 5.  Procedural‑right claims are treated similarly

The opinion also stresses that merely losing a procedural guarantee—“a procedural right in vacuo”—doesn’t give Article III jurisdiction unless the procedural right protects some underlying concrete interest (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. at 496). AFL’s claim that OSC violated its procedural due‑process rights under § 1216 therefore required, first, a demonstration that those procedures protected an independent substantive interest (the receipt of notices). Because none existed, the procedural claim could not supply standing either.

---

### 6.  Bottom line for your question

> **Does a statutory entitlement automatically confer informational standing when that information is withheld?**

No. A statute must actually **grant** the plaintiff a right **to receive** particular information, and the plaintiff must show he has been—or will be—denied that information in a way that harms him concretely and personally. If the statute merely authorizes an agency’s investigation or creates discretion (as § 1216 does), then denial of any agency‑generated material does not satisfy either prong of informational standing.

Consequently:

* A FOIA request denied by an agency triggers informational standing because § 552 creates an enforceable right to those records.
* An allegation that OSC failed to investigate under § 1216 does **not** generate informational standing because § 1216 confers no such receipt right; it only creates investigative authority subject to discretion.

The DC Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal* therefore illustrates precisely why courts look beyond mere statutory language— they require both an enforceable entitlement and an actual/imminent denial before recognizing an informational injury for Article III purposes.
