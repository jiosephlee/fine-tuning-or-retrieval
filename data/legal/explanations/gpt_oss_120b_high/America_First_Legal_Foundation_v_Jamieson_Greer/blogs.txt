# Standing Still: How *America First Legal Foundation* v. Jamieson Greer Refines Procedural Injury Doctrine  

## Why This Case Matters  

The D.C. Circuit’s October 2025 decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* may not have resolved any substantive question about the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to investigate alleged FOIA violations, but it delivers a crisp reminder of the Constitution’s case‑or‑controversy requirement. By holding that a plaintiff cannot sue merely because an agency declined to “investigate” under a statutory grant of discretion, the panel tightens the standards for procedural‑injury standing—especially where the plaintiff’s alleged harm is the denial of information that would flow from an agency’s internal enforcement process rather than from a direct agency act. Litigants who hope to compel investigations, audits or other “watchdog” actions now face a higher hurdle: they must show a concrete, particularized injury that is traceable to the agency’s refusal and redressable by a judicial order.

In practice, this decision nudges FOIA plaintiffs back toward more traditional remedies—mandamus orders compelling record production or suits for declaratory judgments—rather than attempting to leverage OSC’s enforcement machinery as a proxy for FOIA relief. It also signals how courts may treat procedural rights that are “in vacuo” when they carry no independent entitlement to information or benefits.

## The Procedural Context  

OSC was created by Congress in 1978 to police prohibited personnel practices and ensure a “fair…civil service.” Its enforcement toolbox comprises corrective actions under 5 U.S.C. § 1214 and disciplinary actions under § 1215. Section 1216 extends this toolbox to certain non‑personnel misconduct, notably arbitrary or capricious WITHHOLDING of FOIA‑protected information: “OSC shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding….”¹

Statutory commentators have long noted that OSC’s investigative authority under § 1216 is distinct from its limited duty under FOIA itself—U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—which only triggers an OSC inquiry if a court has already ordered production and found an agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. The question before the D.C. Circuit was whether OSC could independently investigate FOIA policy objections even when those three predicate conditions were missing.

The America First Legal Foundation (AFL) argued that § 1216 imposes an *imperative* duty to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent—a policy at issue in AFL’s FOIA suit against DOJ over recusal‑related communications. When OSC declined on the basis that none of the § 552 triggers were satisfied, AFL sued for a declaratory judgment that OSC must investigate and for injunctive relief forcing such an investigation.

The district court found OSC’s reliance on § 552 compelling but ultimately held that it possessed discretion under § 1216(c), remanding for further consideration. AFL appealed—not on whether OSC “should” investigate—but on whether it had standing to demand such an investigation.

## The D.C. Circuit’s Standing Analysis  

### The Classical Requirements  

The panel began with textbook Article III doctrine: plaintiffs must demonstrate (1) an injury in fact that is concrete and particularized, (2) causation linking the injury to the defendant’s conduct, and (3) redressability—i.e., a favorable judicial decision must likely remedy the injury.² *Lujan* provides the bright‑line articulation; *Steel* reminds us that these thresholds are jurisdictional, not merely claim‑making hurdles.

AFL framed its claim around two alleged procedural injuries:

1. **Informational injury** – because OSC allegedly failed to investigate, DOJ allegedly continued withholding records.
2. **Procedural injury** – because OSC allegedly denied AFL notice and information required under Section 1214 when it conducts investigations of “prohibited personnel practices.”

Both theories hinge on treating OSC’s statutory duty as creating a private right enforceable by suit.

### Procedural Rights Are Not Sui Generis Injuries  

Drawing heavily on *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, the D.C. Circuit emphasized that a procedural violation “in vacuo” does not confer Article III standing unless it tangibly harms a plaintiff’s legally protected interest.^3 In other words, merely being denied the benefit of an investigative process does not itself constitute an injury unless there is some concrete stake at stake—often an underlying substantive right like environmental protection, personal liberty, or in FOIA contexts, access to information.

The court therefore required AFL to connect OSC’s refusal directly to its own informational loss—a standard recognized in decisions such as *Center for Law & Education v.* Dep’t of Educ.* (procedural injury cases require proof that denial of procedure causes harm beyond abstract disappointment). The panel found AFL had not met this burden.

#### Causation Breakdown  

AFL attempted a causal chain stretching from OSC’s alleged failure to investigate → possible corrective action under § 1214/§ 1215 → MSPB sanctions → DOJ compliance → release of records. The court rejected this chain as speculative at every step:

- **Speculation about agency choice:** Courts have long held that agencies enjoy discretion in enforcement decisions (*Heckler v. Chaney*). Judicial compulsion does not override discretionary judgment absent clear statutory command.
- **Multiple actors outside review:** The chain involves MSPB decisions and DOJ policy determinations—both beyond AFL’s suit and insulated from direct judicial control.
- **No requisite nexus:** As *FDA v. All‑for Hippocratic Med.* underscores, causation cannot be predicated on conjecture about independent actors' future conduct.

Thus, even assuming OSC had investigated, there was no guarantee—much less likelihood—that any subsequent disciplinary action would force DOJ to produce records demanded by AFC’s original FOIA request.

#### Redressability Hurdle  

Even more decisive was redressability: would ordering OSC to investigate effectively cure AFL's informational grievance? As *Haaland v.* Brackeen reminds us, redressability looks at whether the judgment binds the defendant enough to make relief probable—not whether it might persuade third parties into compliance. Because any downstream effect on DOJ lay outside ESC's sphere of control—and because § 1216 permits discretion even after an investigation—the court concluded any beneficial spillover was too attenuated.

### Procedural Injury via Section 1214 Notice  

AFL also asserted an informational injury based on Section 1214 notice requirements—a procedural safeguard meant for investigations into prohibited personnel practices.^4 By analogy, they argued if OSC treats a § 1216 inquiry like a § 1214 one, they should receive notice about investigation progress—a concrete benefit they were denied.

However, precedent dictates that this borrowing is limited by *Carson v.* U.S.\ Office of Special Counsel which holds that applying § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations remains discretionary.^5 Because CFL does not create a legal entitlement to receive those notices absent explicit statutory language extending them, AFL could not claim “injury” from their absence alone.

Consequently — lacking both causation and redressability — AFL fell short on both fronts of its procedural‑injury theory.

### Bottom Line: No Article III Case  

The opinion concluded succinctly: without a concrete injury traceable and redressable through judicial action against OSC, AFL lacks standing; thus the district court’s judgment is vacated for lack of jurisdiction and remanded for dismissal.^6

## Critical Perspective – Is This Ruling Too Rigid?  

The D.C. Circuit followed precedent closely; yet one might ask whether strict adherence serves congressional intent behind § 1216(3). Congress appears to have wanted OSC—an entity equipped with investigatory tools—to act as a watchdog over agencies’ adherence to FOIA criteria even when no court has yet found arbitrary conduct. By treating that provision as merely discretionary imagination rather than enforceable duty, courts risk muting an important enforcement mechanism designed precisely because FOIA litigation can be slow and resource‑intensive for private litigants.

On the other hand, allowing private parties to compel agencies into investigations raises serious separation‑of‑powers concerns: ``investigative discretion’’ is traditionally immune from judicial commandeering (*Heckler*) absent clear congressional command geared toward public actors rather than private enforcers.[^7] Moreover, opening **every** claim about alleged agency policy misstep into Article III courts could flood courts with pre‑emptive suits lacking genuine harm—a scenario Congress likely sought to avoid by coupling § 552 triggers with OSC's investigatory authority.

In short, while some may see the decision as overly formalist—blocking litigants from using existing statutory mechanisms—the reasoning aligns with long‑standing jurisprudence limiting Article III jurisdiction when plaintiffs assert injuries rooted solely in abstract policy disagreements rather than demonstrable harm upon themselves.

## Doctrinal Implications  

1. **Clarifying Procedural‑Injury Limits** – The opinion refines how courts will evaluate claims founded on statutory procedural rights absent explicit entitlement provisioned by law. The three‑prong Lujan test remains untouched but its application now emphatically requires genuine concrete harm—not merely disappointment at its being denied.
   
2 **Scope of Discretion Under Section 1216** – By emphasizing that §§ 1216(c) books discretion even after an investigation commences—parallel language exists in other enforcement statutes like FDIC's consumer protection provisions—the ruling underscores Congress’ intention for flexible policing rather than mandatory investigative mandates.
   
3 **FOIA Enforcement Landscape** – Plaintiffs seeking “hit‑and‑run” enforcement via special counsel will now see standing as another formidable obstacle; they must instead focus on direct challenges such as mandamus actions against agencies refusing disclosure (§ 552(d)) or seek class action status where appropriate injuries are more readily proved.
   
4 **Potential Spillover To Other Statutes** – The logic will likely be extended when litigants invoke other oversight bodies (e.g., Federal Trade Commission commissioners’ investigations under Section 5(a)(11), Department of Labor wage‐and‐hour investigators) hoping to compel investigations rather than remediate direct violations.
   
5 **Preservation Of Agency Discretion** – This decision buttresses longstanding deference doctrines (*Heckler*, *Whitman*) by reaffirming that any statutory grant permitting “investigation” does not alone create judicially enforceable rights unless coupled with specific remedial language—particularly relevant where statutes articulate “may” rather than “shall” regarding internal proceedings.
   

## Downstream Effects  

Practically speaking, we anticipate:

- **Strategic Shift** – Litigation groups will allocate resources toward direct FOIA suits demanding production or pursuing sanctions once noncompliance is adjudicated—not toward persuading OSC—or similar entities—to act preemptively.
- **Legislative Response** – If Congress truly intended robust oversight by special counsels absent judicial trigger events (as suggested by SALC's mooted name), we might see amendments explicitly granting ``private right of action'' language—or at least clarifying notice/entitlement provisions—to overcome standing deficits.
- **Administrative Remedies** – Agencies may reinforce internal grievance processes giving requesters clearer pathways (e.g., mandatory notification when requests are denied based on email search policies) so plaintiffs can articulate personal injuries without invoking external oversight bodies.
- **Judicial Caution** – Other circuits will likely cite *America First* when faced with procedural–injury claims involving discretionary investigative statutes; this may reduce frivolous suits seeking injunctions against agency inactivity but could also dampen legitimate enforcement pressure where statutory language suggests mandatory oversight.
   

## Conclusion  

*America First Legal Foundation v.* Greer does not reshape substantive ALT policy on FOIA investigations; instead it sharpens doctrinal edges around procedural standing under Article III—a reminder that invoking investigative authority doesn’t automatically manufacture justiciable controversy. For litigants eyeing administrative agencies’ investigatory powers as leverage against alleged information withholding conspiracies, their path forward now must include demonstrable personal harm traceable directly back to those agencies—all within clear redressability parameters laid out by Supreme Court precedent.

Until Congress decides otherwise—or perhaps rescues its own enforcement toolbox through clearer language—the courts will remain faithful stewards of constitutional limits: sitting still when presented only with abstract grievances while reserving authority for cases where real injury demands real remedy.*

# Mandate or Discretion? Interpreting Section 1216’s “Shall Conduct” Amid Judicial Deference  

## Why This Case Matters  

When a nonprofit like America First Legal Foundation (AFL) asks the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate an alleged “arbitrary” denial of a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request, the dispute seems procedural on its face—but the stakes are anything but. The D.C. Circuit’s October 2025 opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* puts the spotlight on a clash between two seemingly contradictory provisions in the Civil Service Reform Act: § 1216(a)(3)’s command that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholding, and § 1216(c)’s grant of “discretion” to shape how—if at all—such investigations proceed.  

The appellate panel ultimately dismissed AFL’s suit not on the merits of that clash but because AFL lacked Article III standing to sue. Still, the opinion offers a useful window into how courts will treat “shall” language when a statute also supplies discretionary authority, and it raises practical questions about whether courts can ever compel an agency—via mandamus or otherwise—to act when Congress has left its toes on a policy ledge. For litigants seeking to use OSC as a FOIA‑enforcement lever, for scholars tracking the boundaries of judicial deference, and for policymakers interested in tightening FOIA oversight, *Greer* is a pivotal data point.

## The Statutory Landscape: Mandatory Language Meets Discretion  

Section 1216(a)(3) of title 5 United States Code says plainly:

> “OSC … shall conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”  

At first glance, “shall” reads as an unequivocal command—an unmistakable congressional directive that leaves no room for agency discretion about *whether* to investigate alleged FOIA violations.

Yet just three paragraphs later § 1216(c) states:

> “OSC may investigate … and seek corrective action … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”  

The operative verb here—“may”—reintroduces discretion into the mix, effectively telling us that even though OSC is authorized *to* investigate, it is not compelled *to* do so in every case that fits § 1216(a)(3)’s description.

The District Court recognized this tension in *America First Legal Foundation v. Kerner* (2023 WL 9546644), concluding that while OSC’s refusal was “based on a legal error,” it could not order an investigation because § 1216(c) “committed that decision to OSC’s discretion.” The D.C. Circuit affirmed—that view was correct—but stopped short of deciding which statutory provision controls when they conflict. Instead it sidestepped the substantive clash by focusing on standing.

## Standing as a Shortcut Out of Substantive Interpretation  

The appellate panel turned first to Article III standing because both parties had glossed over it at earlier stages. Using *Lujan*’s three-prong test—injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability—the court found AFL’s assertions wanting:

1. **Injury‑in‑fact** – AFL claimed two harms: (a) continued denial of records by DOJ and (b) deprivation of “notice and information” normally required under § 1214 when OSC investigates prohibited personnel practices. The court held neither amounted to a concrete injury tied directly to OSC’s refusal because any resulting informational loss could be pinned squarely on DOJ’s own non‑compliance with FOIA, not on OSC’s investigative decision.
   
2. **Causation** – Even accepting that a statutory duty existed, linking OSC’s alleged inaction to DOJ’s continued withholding required speculative jumps through several independent actors—OSC could issue an investigation report; MSPB could impose discipline; DOJ could respond by complying with FOIA—all of which are beyond judicial control or even near certainty.
   
3. **Redressability** – A favorable judgment ordering OSC to investigate would not guarantee production of records from DOJ or any tangible benefit for AFL; rather it would merely create a possibility that another agency might later act—a classic example where courts refuse to extend jurisdiction because the relief is too indirect.

Because AFL failed even these relaxed procedural‑injury standards—see *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S._488_ (2009)—the D.C. Circuit dismissed for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction without reaching whether § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall” creates an enforceable duty.

### Why Standing Matters for Statutory Interpretation  

The decision illustrates a familiar procedural shortcut: when plaintiffs cannot survive standing objections, courts avoid wading into thorny interpretive battles over competing statutory commands. Yet this avoidance leaves open—and arguably unsettled—the core question: does “shall conduct an investigation” impose a binding duty on OSC notwithstanding later discretionary language? The remainder of this commentary fills in what *Greer* hints at but does not pronounce upon.

## Chevron Deference and the “Shall” Question  

Under *Chevron U.S.A., Inc.* v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S._837_ (1984), courts undertake a two‑step inquiry when faced with ambiguous statutory language:

1. **Step One:** Determine whether Congress has spoken directly on the issue.
2. **Step Two:** If ambiguous, ask whether the agency’s interpretation is permissible.

If Congress unequivocally commands "shall," then Step One wins—no deference needed because there is no ambiguity about agency duty; any agency refusal would be unlawful (see *Morrison v. Olson*, 487 U.S._654_, 666 (1988)). But here step one hits trouble: § 1216(a)(3)’s mandatory verb collides with § 1216(c)’s permissive phrasing which SECURELY nests within the same section—making clear legislative intent murky at best.

Courts have traditionally resolved such clashes by employing a presumption against absurdity: Congress rarely intends two mutually exclusive commands without providing reconciliation guidance (§ § 33 U.S.C._1518_, etc.). The D.C. Circuit seems persuaded here that § 1216(c)'s discretionary clause qualifies as such guidance—suggesting Congress intended “shall” only insofar as it creates authority, not an obligation in every circumstance fitting § 1216(a)(3).

But can we apply Chevron at all? Some scholars argue that when statutory text uses overtly mandatory language (“shall”) alongside permissive language (“may”), courts should parse them as addressing different aspects (e.g., *Hart v. Massanari*, 525 F._3d_1300). Maybe § 1216(a)(3) establishes jurisdiction (“OSC shall have authority…”) while § 1216(c) governs exercise (“OSC may proceed at its discretion”). Under this reading *Greer*’s avoidance makes sense—the duty exists but its implementation is largely left to agency judgment—a classic dual‑step scenario where Chevron Step Two would likely uphold any reasonable exercise—or non‑exercise—by OSC because there is no contrary textual command demanding uniform action.

If we adopt such an interpretation, judicial deference remains robust: courts would only intervene where OSC’s decision appears arbitrary or capricious under APA standards—but not merely because they disagree with its policy calculus regarding whether given allegations merit examination under limited resources.

## Heckler v.Chaney: The Precedent Against Compelling Discretionary Enforcement  

Even if we conclude that § 1216(a)(3) imposes at least nominal duty upon OSC—that is, it must **consider** an allegation—it still faces Hedgehog protection from *Heckler v.Chaney*, 470 U.S._821_ (1985). In *Heckler*, the Supreme Court held that agency decisions **not** to enforce are generally unreviewable because agencies enjoy wide discretion absent clear statutory prohibition against non‑enforcement or where agency justifications are rationally related to permissible policy objectives.

Applying *Heckler* here means that even assuming Congress gave IRC (“Investigative Regulatory Command”)—a modest investigatory duty—the mere choice **not** to pursue it would likely fall within protected agency discretion unless:

1. The statute provides no room for judgment (i.e., unequivocal command without any qualifier); or  
2 . The agency’s refusal contravenes express procedural requirements (like failing to give notice where notice is mandated).

Because ¬§ 1216(c)’s permissive language suggests Congress anticipated agency judgment calls about resource allocation and investigative priorities—a hallmark scenario where *Heckler* shields agencies—the appellate panel sensibly concluded there is insufficient justification for judicial compulsion absent concrete injury evidence linking non‑investigation directly back to AFC's interests.

## Does “Shall Conduct” Translate Into Compulsory Action?  

Statutory scholars distinguish between two senses of “shall”: **(a)** mandatory imperatives directing agency behavior ("must do") versus **(b)** grant-of-power verbs encasing jurisdiction ("shall have authority") (*Markus v.Terranova Development Corp.*, 116 S.Ct._1197_). In OCRIC contexts like securities regulation (“the SEC shall...”) courts often read such language as binding because surrounding provisions use prescriptive mechanics lacking alternative avenues—a standard reinforced by cases like *NLRB v.McKay*, 366 U.S._498_ (1961). 

Conversely, administrative statutes concerned with enforcement protocols routinely embed both mandatory triggers ("shall initiate") alongside discretionary levers ("may prescribe"). For instance, FAR Part 15 states contractors "shall" submit proposals while permitting agencies "may accept late proposals after waiver." Where both appear together within close proximity—or within same paragraph—the consensus leans toward interpreting them as operating jointly rather than conflictingly (*Gorman v.-USAID*, No 14‑4419).

Looking at § 1216 side by side: 

- Subsection (a)(3) declares what *subject matter* triggers governance ("any allegation concerning…");
- Subsection (c) clarifies who decides **how**—not just **whether**—to proceed ("OSC may investigate…in same way…").

Thus one plausible construction treats ‑(a)(3) as setting jurisdictional scope rather than creating absolute duty—a range-finding step wherein once within scope Importantly² , oscillation onto investigative path becomes subjectively filtered by ℙ36c's "may". This aligns with the D.C.’s earlier finding echoing Carson federal precedent:

> In *Carson v.Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F._3d_487_ (Sixth Cir., 2011), we held that OSC's choice whether to follow Section 1214 procedural formalities during a Section​ 1216 investigation remains discretionary.[^1]

This suggests even after hopping into Section​ 1216's realm via ‑(a)(3), there remains competent authority—and precedent—to forego typical procedural niceties without violating statutory obligation—a strong indicator against treating ‑(a)(3)’s “shall” as absolute mandamus trigger.

[^1]: The Sixth Circuit recognized yet again how regulatory schemes often blend grant-of-power verbiage (“may”) with later procedural kernel dependent upon agency policy considerations — indicating Congress accepted discretion concerning administrative staffing actions once investigatory thresholds are met.

# Mandate or Discretion? Interpreting Section 1216’s “Shall Conduct” Amid Judicial Deference  

## Why This Case Matters  

When America First Legal Foundation (AFL) asked the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to probe alleged arbitrary denial of its Freedom of Information Act request, it seemed like another jurisdictional tussle over who gets to enforce FOIA rules. Yet the D.C.~Circuit’s October 2025 opinion in **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer**, No. 24‑5168, reaches far beyond those facts: it forces us to confront how courts should treat plain‑language commands such as “OSC **shall** conduct an investigation” when they sit alongside later provisions granting broad discretion (“OSC **may** investigate…in the same way …”). The answer shapes not only what remedies watchdog groups can chase through the courts but also how much latitude federal agencies retain when enforcing transparency statutes across Washington.

## The Statutory Landscape: A Mandatory Verb Meets Discretion  

Section 1216(a)(3) reads:

> “OSC shall conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”

At first glance this appears unequivocal—a congressional edict obligating OSC to open its investigative file whenever someone alleges unlawful FOIA withholding.

Three paragraphs later (§ 1216(c)) says:

> “OSC may investigate … and seek corrective action … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”

The shift from **shall** to **may** injects discretion into what might otherwise be seen as compulsory action. If taken at face value:

1️⃣ **§ 1216(a)(3)** creates a jurisdictional trigger—once an allegation falls within its scope, OSC has authority **to investigate** if it chooses.
2️⃣ **§ 1216(c)** governs how—and crucially whether—that authority is exercised, permitting imaginative policy choices ranging from full investigations complete with notice requirements under § 1214(…)​to simply declining pursuit altogether.

In *America First Legal Foundation v.* Kerner (2023 WL 9546644), the district court acknowledged this tension, finding OSC’s refusal legally erroneous yet refusing to order an investigation because § 1216(c) left decision-making power squarely with OSC. The D.C.~Circuit affirmed without pronouncing which provision wins when they appear contradictory; instead it sidestepped substantive resolution by holding AFL lacked Article III standing.



## Standing as a Procedural Shortcut  

Article III requires plaintiffs demonstrate three elements—injury in fact, causation, redressability (*Lujan*). AFL tried two theories:

* It argued that without an OSC investigation DOJ would never produce its records.
* It claimed being denied notice about any potential investigation itself caused concrete informational harm.

The panel rejected both theories emphatically:

* **Injury-in-fact:** Any failure by DOJ to turn over documents originates from DOJ itself—not from whatever investigative step OSC might take later.
* **Causation:** A chain linking greenlighting IRC8 electronic search shows high speculative uncertainty involving three distinct actors (OSC → MSPB → DOJ). Such speculation violates established limits on causation (*FDA v.^Allfor Hippocratic Med.*).
* **Redressability:** Even if honestly ordered everything downstream falls outside controlling jurisdiction; compulsion would bind only Oshtechn about his own processes—it cannot command MSPB discipline nor dictate DOJ production decisions (*Haaland v.Brackeen*).

Because AFL couldn’t meet even this relaxed procedural‑injury standard (*Summers*) , standing collapsed before any merits were reached—and consequently no definitive interpretation was issued about whether §§ 1216(a)(3)/(c) conflict creates enforceable duties.

## Why This Decision Matters  

The D.C.~Circuit’s October 2025 opinion in **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer**, No.​ 24‑5168 (2025), may look like another lost standing challenge against a federal watchdog agency—but its doctrinal implications stretch far beyond one nonprofit’s inability to sue. At issue is how courts should interpret starkly different verbs embedded in the same statute: Section 5 U.S.C.§ 1216(a)(3)’s command that the Office of Special Counsel (**OSC**) ***shall*** investigate any allegation alleging arbitrary withholding of information prohibited by FOIA versus § 1216(c)’s grant that OSC ***may*** investigate and pursue corrective action “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”  

If judges read the word “shall” as creating an enforceable duty regardless of later permissive language, litigants could obtain mandamus orders compelling OSC—or any agency whose statute mixes mandatory triggers with discretionary clauses—to act whenever those triggers appear satisfied. If instead courts treat “shall” merely as conferring jurisdiction while allowing broad discretion under the subsequent clause, agencies retain substantial leeway over enforcement priorities and most civil society suits seeking compulsory investigations will founder on procedural grounds alone. The outcome therefore affects every citizen‑group strategy built around forcing administrative oversight through statutes like §§ 221–226 governing whistleblower protection or Clean Water Act citizen suits where similar textual tensions surface.

# Two Paths, One Goal: The Tension Between FOIA’s Built‑in OSC Enforcement (§ 552) and the Separate Scheme in §§ 1214–1216  

## Why the case matters  

When a federal agency refuses a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request, plaintiffs traditionally rely on the courts to compel disclosure.  Yet Congress gave the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) a parallel enforcement track: after a court orders production under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to discipline the official responsible.  At the same time, §§ 1214–1216 of the Civil Service Reform Act let OSC treat certain FOIA withholdings as “prohibited personnel practices” and investigate them under essentially the same machinery it uses for whistle‑blower retaliation claims.  

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in **America First Legal Foundation v. Greer**, No. 24‑5168 (Oct. 3 2025), does not resolve which statute governs OSC’s duty when a party alleges an arbitrary withholding absent a court order. Instead, it sidesteps the clash by holding that the plaintiff lacks Article III standing.  That procedural outcome leaves a substantive question alive—how should courts interpret Congress’s dual‑track design?  The answer will shape everything from agency compliance cultures to the strategic calculus of FOIA litigants who hope to lever OSC’s internal teeth instead of taking every dispute through a federal district court.

## The statutory landscape  

### The “trigger” regime in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)  

FOIA’s enforcement provision is straightforward: if a court orders an agency to produce records and finds that the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously, then OSC must investigate “promptly.”  The language creates an *after‑the‑fact* duty that only springs into life once a judicial determination exists.  The statute does not give parties any stand‑alone right to compel OSC action; instead it ties OSC’s hands to a judicial finding of illegality.

### §§ 1214–1216: A broader investigative toolbox  

Section 1214 furnishes procedures for investigating “prohibited personnel practices” (PPPs), including notice requirements and the possibility of corrective or disciplinary relief before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB).  Section 1216 expands those procedures beyond traditional PPPs, expressly authorizing investigations into “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” In other words, when an agency withholds information in violation of FOIA, that conduct can be treated as a PPP for purposes of OSC’s enforcement machinery.  

Section 1216(c) further says that OSC may pursue corrective action “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” implying that any procedural safeguards attached to PPP investigations could flow over onto FOIA investigations as well—*if* OSC chooses to use them.  

The tension lies in whether § 1216 creates an independent duty for OSC to launch an investigation whenever an alleged FOIA violation is reported (“mandatory” view), or whether it merely gives OSC discretionary authority alongside its trigger‑based duty under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) (“discretionary” view).

## What Greer says (and doesn’t say)  

In **Greer**, America First Legal Foundation (AFL) complained that the Department of Justice refused to search employee emails without consent—a policy AFL argued was contrary to FOIA’s requirement that agencies search all responsive records regardless of employee privacy interests unless an exception applies.  After DOJ began producing responsive records in an unrelated suit, AFL turned to OSC, invoking § 1216(a)(3) and asserting that OSC had a non‑negotiable duty to investigate its policy claim.

The district court found—on its own—an error in OSC’s reliance on § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)’s trigger language but ultimately declined to order an investigation because it read § 1216(c)’s discretion provision as limiting any mandatory duty.  On appeal, Judge Katsas framed the entire dispute around standing rather than statutory interpretation and held that AFL lacked Article III injury because any alleged informational harm was traceable back to DOJ, not to OSC’s investigatory choices.  Consequently, the appellate panel vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

Crucially, **Greer** does not decide whether § 1216 imposes a statutory “must investigate” command independent of a court order; it merely postpones that question indefinitely by finding no standing at all.

## Prior circuit attempts at harmony  

Courts have wrestled with this clash before:

* **America First Legal Foundation v. Kerner**, No. 22‑cv‑3576 (D.D.C. Sept. 29 2023) – The district court held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not preclude OSC from investigating alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings under § 1216 even when the trigger provision is unmet, but stayed short of mandating such investigations.
* **Carson v. Office of Special Counsel**, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011) – The Sixth Circuit recognized that while § 1216 grants investigatory authority over FOIA violations, discretion remains because Section 1214 procedural protections are not automatically applicable.
* **United States v. OLC**, 483 F.Supp.2d 1235 (D.D.C. 2007) – A D.C.~District Court suggested that Congress intended both regimes as complementary tools: one reactive (post‑judgment), one proactive (policy complaints).

Collectively these rulings depict a fractured doctrinal picture: some judges read §§ 1214–1216 as giving OSC *the power* but *not* *the obligation* to act absent a judicial finding; others see potential conflict but defer resolution pending concrete cases where plaintiffs can demonstrate injury sufficient for standing.

## What would it mean if § 1216 were read as mandatory?  

If courts embraced a mandatory reading—requiring OSC **to** investigate any complaint alleging arbitrary FOIA withholding regardless of whether a court has already declared such conduct illegal—the impact would be profound:

1. **Preemptive oversight** – Agencies could be reined in earlier because OSC would have an investigatory lever before litigants endure costly district‑court battles.
2. **Increased workload for OSC** – A flood of FOIA policy complaints could strain limited resources unless Congress allocates additional funding.
3. **Potential chilling effect on agency policy** – Knowing that even innocuous policy statements could trigger an investigation might push agencies toward more cautious interpretations—or conversely toward broader exemptions protected by other statutes.
4. **Redefinition of abstract “injury”** – Plaintiffs could claim concrete injury simply from being denied procedural notices about an investigation—a shift toward recognizing procedural rights as stand-alone injuries under Article III jurisprudence.
5. **Judicial deference dominoes** – Because Section 552’s trigger language would become subordinate rather than superseding, courts might be less inclined to find agencies’ discretionary interpretations de facto final after they survive initial lawsuits.

In short, treating § 1216 as obligatory would tilt FOIA enforcement toward administrative self‑policing while potentially diminishing courts’ role as ultimate arbiters unless parties still seek judicial review after an unfavorable administrative outcome.

## What would it mean if § 1216 remains discretionary?  

A discretionary reading preserves today’s status quo:

* Plaintiffs who cannot secure a favorable district‑court judgment still lack any strong foothold for forcing agency compliance beyond traditional litigation.
* Agencies retain broad latitude over internal search policies—like DOJ’s email‐search rule—knowing OSC may elect not to intervene.
* Courts continue bearing the brunt of enforcing FOIA rights through injunctions and awards of attorney fees—the primary leverage many litigants rely upon.
* Legislative intent stays ambiguous but arguably reflects congressional caution against creating an additional administrative watchdog whose mandate could overlap wetly with existing judicial remedies.

The downside is obvious: many private entities like America First Legal Foundation must endure protracted litigation just to get at policies they allege are unlawful even though those policies do not yet rise into enforceable judgments against agencies.

## Legislative fixes – cutting through ambiguity  

Congress has two relatively clean options:

1️⃣ **Amend § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** – Replace “shall promptly initiate” with language expressly linking its duty back to any credible allegation investigated under §§ 1214–1216 *or* create parallel language establishing parallel duties so both provisions operate side by side without conflict.
   
2️⃣ **Clarify §§ 1214–1216** – Insert explicit text stating that “the authority granted under section 1216(a)(3) includes a mandatory investigative duty whenever alleged arbitrary withholding is reported,” and define which procedural safeguards (notice requirements from § 1214(b)) must accompany such investigations.
   
A third hybrid approach could preserve discretion but impose *minimum* reporting obligations—for example mandating that after refusing an investigation based on discretion, OSC must issue written findings outlining its rationale and any remedial steps taken internally—a move akin to what some whistleblower statutes require.

Any amendment should also address redressability concerns raised by Greer: clarifying what relief courts can order against OSC (e.g., mandating issuance of notices or compelling remedial action) would help future plaintiffs overcome standing hurdles tied forcefully to concrete injuries rather than speculative downstream effects.

## Downstream effects – what lawyers and agencies should watch  

For FOIA practitioners:

* Expect continued reliance on traditional district‑court motions until Congress clarifies or courts definitively interpret §§ 1214–1216.
* When drafting complaints against agencies’ internal policies (e.g., email search rules), consider framing arguments around concrete harms distinct from mere policy disagreement—perhaps focusing on reputational injury or loss of advocacy efficacy—to satisfy heightened standing requirements.
* Keep tabs on any new OMB guidance regarding best practices for email searches; agencies may voluntarily align their policies with emerging standards preemptively if they sense legislative pressure building around this issue.

For government agencies:

* Reevaluate internal rules about searching employee communications—especially given recent litigation exposing potential mismatches between privacy concerns and statutory duties.
* Document decision‑making processes thoroughly; robust intra‑agency records can help mitigate risks should OSC decide later to intervene under either statutory scheme.
* Monitor budgetary proposals related to OSC; increased funding may signal congressional intent toward stronger oversight via §§ 1214–1216.

For policymakers:

* Review existing data on how often ESC investigations are launched under § 1200 versus after judicial orders; quantitative analysis could illuminate which track provides more effective compliance incentives.
* Engage stakeholders—including civil society groups like America First Legal Foundation—in drafting amendments so reforms balance agency flexibility with citizen access rights without spawning another administrative quagmire.

## Conclusion  

**America First Legal Foundation v. Greer** exemplifies how procedural doctrines—standing doctrine above all—can curtain substantial statutory questions from judicial scrutiny for years longer than either Congress nor litigants intended them to be hidden behind. While Greer left open whether §§ 1214–​1216 impose *a duty* independent from § 552’s trigger clause, it underscored how thin Article III requirements are when plaintiffs claim only procedural injuries without concrete informational benefits tied directly back to the defendant agency.

Policymakers now face a clear choice: codify an unambiguous duty for OSC over arbitrary FOIA withholding—or accept continued reliance on courts despite their backlog and expense—to achieve Congress’ original goal point-blank—“fair, efficient, lawful civil service.” Whichever path they take will dictate whether future vigilance over government secrecy stems more from whistleblowers’ internal complaints or from plaintiffs’ courtroom battles—a decision worth watching closely for anyone who values transparent government today and tomorrow.

# The Diminishing Shield: What This D.C. Circuit Ruling Means for Future FOIA Oversight and Transparency Advocacy  

## Why This Case Matters  

When America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sued the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) over what it portrayed as an unlawful refusal to investigate a DOJ email‑search policy, it was not merely chasing a single grievance. The suit sought to turn an internal watchdog—created by Congress in the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) to police “prohibited personnel practices”—into a tool anyone could summon against opaque agency behavior. By holding that AFL lacked Article III standing, the D.C. Circuit effectively closed off one of the few statutory avenues through which private litigants can compel federal oversight of alleged Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations. In an era when NGOs increasingly rely on procedural‑injury theories to keep agencies honest, the decision signals a tightening of judicial gates at a time when transparency advocates most need them.

## The Court’s Standing Analysis in a Nutshell  

Judge Katsas’s opinion does not decide whether § 1216(a)(3) obligates OSC “to conduct an investigation” of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings; it sidesteps that question altogether because AFL cannot survive the constitutional standing prerequisite. The panel reiterates the classic Lujan test—injury‑in‑fact, causation, redressability—and reminds us that procedural rights alone do not confer jurisdiction unless they protect a concrete interest already harmed by the defendant’s conduct.

AFL advanced two theories. First, it claimed that OSC’s refusal left it without the records it sought from DOJ—a classic informational injury tied directly to DOJ’s alleged non‑compliance rather than OSC’s silence. The court found this chain too attenuated: proving causation would require speculation about how OSC might have acted, how MSPB would have responded, and how DOJ would subsequently change its behavior—an evidentiary leap barred by precedents such as *FDA v. All.* for Hippocratic Med.* and *Heckler v. Chaney*. Second, AFL argued that its own “procedural” injury—the denial of any investigative notice mandated by § 1214—was sufficient because statutes grant individuals an entitlement to information generated by an investigation. The panel rejected this as well, noting that § 1216 merely mirrors § 1214’s enforcement scheme without extending its notice requirements or establishing a private right of information.

In both instances the court concluded that AFC’s grievance was either too remote or too abstract to satisfy Article III’s concrete‑injury requirement, let alone survive the stricter redressability prong applied in procedural‑injury cases (*Center for Law & Education* doctrine). Accordingly, the appellate panel vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction—a textbook “no‑standing” disposal.

## Procedural‑Injury Doctrine Takes Another Hit  

The decision fits neatly into a broader trajectory where federal courts are narrowing what counts as an actionable procedural injury for NGOs and partisan groups. In *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity* (2024), *Fla.* Audubon Society (1996), and more recent environmental cases from the Sixth Circuit (*Carson v.*), litigants have been turned away when their alleged injuries turned out to be “rights in vacuo.” While those decisions pertained largely to disparate statutory schemes—environmental statutes, education funding rules—the scaffolding is identical here: absent a tangible stake beyond “I wanted the agency to do its job,” courts deem plaintiffs outside Article III’s domain.

The D.C. Circuit explicitly leaned on Supreme Court dicta from *Summers v.* Earth Island and *Zivotofsky* which stress that procedural rights must buttress a concrete interest already implicated by statute before they can be judicially enforceable. By refusing even “relaxed” procedural standing where the alleged injury was purely informational—without any statutory entitlement—the panel raises the bar substantially higher than earlier decisions that allowed limited procedural claims when plaintiffs could show an imminent threat or concrete risk (*Allen v.* Wright).

## Implications for OSC’s Role as a FOIA Watchdog  

OSC occupies an uneasy niche: it sits at the intersection of personnel management and whistleblower protection while also being charged under § 1216(c) with investigating arbitrary FOIA denials “in the same way” as prohibited personnel practices. Practically speaking, enforcement has often been initiated only after a court order forces an agency to produce withheld records—a narrow trigger envisioned by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). AFL’s reading—that § 1216 creates an independent duty irrespective of any judicial find—is precisely what this appellate decision renders unrecoverable through private suit.

Consequently, advocacy groups can no longer count on litigation against OSC itself as a leverage point when challenging agency transparency policies. Even if another organization could demonstrate concrete harm—for example, if an agency explicitly told an NGO it would not release records unless OSC intervenes—the same causation hurdles will apply because any relief hinges on speculative agency action downstream from an OSC investigation.

The practical upshot is twofold. First, agencies may feel emboldened to adopt restrictive email‑search practices or other legal fictions about consent because there is now less risk of being forced into an internal investigation by litigants lacking standing. Second, those same agencies lose a modest check that historically made them think twice before invoking “consent” defenses in responding to FOIA requests—a defense many scholars argue undermines FOIA’s “presumption of openness.”

## Downstream Effects Across Transparency Litigation  

While this case deals with one specific statutory nexus (CSRA + FOIA), its reasoning reverberates across other statutes where watchdog entities bear investigative responsibilities without explicit private rights of action—for instance, EPA’s Office of Inspector General investigations into alleged Clean Air Act violations or HHS Office for Civil Rights’ probes under HIPAA privacy rules. Courts looking at these regimes will likely apply similar strictures on procedural‑injury standing unless Congress has crafted clear remedial paths (e.g., citizen suits).

Moreover, inside federal courts we may see more grass‑roots groups resorting directly to Section 552(b) actions—the traditional civil suit against an agency—for enforcement instead of trying first “upstream” at agencies tasked with oversight duties they cannot compel through litigation alone. This strategic pivot aligns with recent trends observed after *Katz v.* districts where litigants abandon attempts at injunctive relief against non‑court actors (like OIGs) in favor of direct record production suits predicated on established FOIA jurisprudence (*National Security Archive*, *National Archives* cases).  

But relying exclusively upon direct district‑court actions carries its own downsides—chiefly high litigation costs and limited ability to impose institutional reforms beyond record production or attorney‑fee awards unless accompanied by broader whistleblower tactics or public pressure campaigns. 

## Policy Responses: Legislative Fixes and Tactical Shifts  

Given how narrowly constructed Article III standing can cripple statutory watchdogs’ effectiveness, Congress faces two clear options if it wishes to preserve OSC as a viable transparency enforcer:

1️⃣ **Clarify Mandate Language** – Amend § 1216(a)(3) so that “shall conduct an investigation” is unequivocally mandatory rather than discretionary—and pair this amendment with explicit language obligating OSSM‒style notice requirements akin to those in § 1214(b)(3). Such text would make denial itself more readily challengeable as “failure-to-investigate” rather than requiring plaintiffs first prove abstract injury.

2️⃣ **Create a Private Right of Action** – Insert language authorizing any “person” adversely affected by OSC’s refusal to investigate—to sue directly in federal court for injunctive relief and declaratory judgments (and possibly nominal damages). Congressional intent would then be clear that Congress expects judicial enforcement against non‑compliant oversight bodies—a move reminiscent of amendments added after *Allen*-era litigation concerning environmental compliance statutes.

Absent legislative action—a realistic prospect given current congressional gridlock—advocacy groups may need pragmatic adjustments:

- **Target Agency Conduct Directly** – File standard FOIA lawsuits within 20 days after denial while simultaneously exhausting all available administrative remedies inside SOC/OSC offices so any claim regarding failure-to-investigate becomes moot.
  
- **Leverage Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** – Focus efforts on obtaining court orders compelling production first; once such orders are issued judges automatically trigger OSC’s mandatory disciplinary review under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), thereby sidestepping reliance on speculative investigatory duties.
  
- **Strategic Partnerships** – Align with whistleblowers who are themselves directly subjectto disciplinary sanctions under §§ 1214–1215; placing them front-and-center can turn otherwise abstract injuries into personal stakes satisfying concrete injury requirements.
  
- **Public Pressure Campaigns** – Use media scrutiny combined with Freedom‑of‑Information litigation outcomes to pressure agencies into preemptively changing policies—in effect treating public opinion as another remedial lever when courts will not compel internal investigations directly.
  
These tactical shifts won’t restore the legal certainty lost from seasonal reliance on OSC investigations but could at least keep personnel‑policy scrutiny alive within existing avenues.

## Looking Ahead  

Judge Katsas’s opinion underscores how constitutional gatekeeping remains paramount—even when statutory schemes appear designed expressly for outsider enforcement. By dismissing AFL on pure standing grounds, the D.C. Circuit has left open two critical questions: Is § 1216 truly mandatory? And should Congress provide individuals a direct remedy when an oversight entity refuses even its discretionary duties? Until those answers arrive—most likely via fresh legislation rather than judicial reinterpretation transparency advocacy will lean back onto tried–and–true tools like direct FOIA litigation while exploring new collaborative models with insiders capable of making personal injuries cognizable under proper standards.

In short, today’s ruling doesn’t just silence one nonprofit; it narrows an entire continent-wide strategy aimed at holding bureaucracies accountable through their own statutory watchdogs. Whether future congressional action will resurrect those pathways—or whether litigants will find fresh loopholes—is still very much up for debate—but one thing is clear: vigilance over federal transparency will have to adapt quickly if hope isn’t pinned solely on forcing internal investigations through our courts alone.
