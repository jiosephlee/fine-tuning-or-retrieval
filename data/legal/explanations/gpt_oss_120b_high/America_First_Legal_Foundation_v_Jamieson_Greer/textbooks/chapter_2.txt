Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Statutory Framework Governing OSC Investigations  

The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit’s decision in **America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer**, No. 24‑5168 (Oct. 3 2025), turned on a close reading of three grant‑making provisions within the Civil Service Reform Act (“CSRA”) and a parallel enforcement provision embedded in the Freedom of Information Act (“FOIA”). Understanding how those statutes allocate investigative duties—and how they balance mandatory commands against discretionary leeway—is essential for following the Court’s reasoning on standing and jurisdiction. This chapter walks through each relevant provision, explains what “shall” versus “may” means in this context, and shows how recent precedent has shaped their interpretation.

---

## 2.1 The Civil Service Reform Act – Overview of §§ 1214‑1216  

The CSRA was enacted in 1978 to promote merit‑based federal employment and to provide a remedial system for **prohibited personnel practices** (“PPPs”). Congress created the Office of Special Counsel (“OSC”) as an independent investigative and prosecutorial entity tasked with enforcing the CSRA’s anti‑retaliation schemes and with safeguarding civil service integrity.^[Frazier v. MSPB, 672 F.₂d 150, 162 (D.C.Cir. 1982)].  

Three sections codify OSC’s core powers:

* **§ 1214** authorizes corrective‑action investigations when a complainant alleges a PPP.
* **§ 1215** grants authority to seek disciplinary sanctions—including removal, suspension, or debarment—against employees who commit PPPs.
* **§ 1216** extends those investigative tools beyond PPPs to cover certain other misconduct, most prominently **arbitrary or capricious withholding of information** prohibited under FOIA § 552(a).  

Together these provisions create a single statutory scheme whereby an allegation—whether it concerns retaliation, discrimination, or improper FOIA withholding—triggers an inquiry by OSC that may culminate either in remedial relief (§ 1214) or punitive discipline (§ 1215). The “umbrella” nature of § 1216 is crucial because it imports the procedural machinery designed for PPPs into the FOIA context—a point at issue throughout **America First**.



---

## 2​.​​​​​​​0§ 1214 – Procedures for Corrective Actions  

Section 1214 sets out a step‑by‑step process intended to protect both the complainant’s interests and the employee’s due‑process rights when a **prohibited personnel practice** is alleged. The statute requires OSC ultimately **to notify** the complainant about progress, give the employee an opportunity to respond, and prescribe what remedial actions are permissible (e.g., reinstatement, back pay). The relevant language reads:

> “The complaining party shall be notified … concerning … progress of an investigation.”  
> *5 U.S.C.* §§ 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).

The procedural guarantees serve two purposes. First they create **transparency**, allowing the complainant to monitor whether OSC is taking appropriate steps; second they ensure **fairness**, preventing punitive action against an employee without giving him notice and a chance to rebut accusations.  

When applied via § 1216(a)(3), these procedures are ​*potentially*​ available for investigations into alleged FOIA violations because subsection c expressly links a “FOIA‐related” investigation with the same remedial framework used for PPPs.^[See 5 U.S.C.§ 1216(c).] In practice however courts have treated whether those notice requirements are *mandatory* or merely *permissible* as part of an overall discretionary inquiry—a distinction that surfaced prominently in **America First**.



---

## 2​.​​​​​​​1§ 1215 – Disciplinary Action Authority  

While § 1214 deals with *corrective* relief aimed at restoring the complainant’s status quo ante, § 1515 gives OSC power over *disciplinary* outcomes directed at offending employees.^[§ 5 U.S.C.§ 1215(a)]. Disciplinary sanctions can include removal from federal service, suspension without pay, demotion, reprimand, or debarment from future federal employment—essentially any penalty authorized by merit‑systems law when an employee has engaged in a prohibited practice.

The connection between Sections 1214/15 and Section 1216 is explicit: after conducting an investigation under § 1216(a)(3), OSC may seek corrective relief under § 1214 **or** disciplinary relief under § 1515 ***in the same way*** it would in a traditional PPP case.^[Id.] This cross‑reference reflects Congress’ intent that violations involving illegal disclosure—or illegal nondisclosure—be treated with comparable seriousness as other personnel misconduct.



---

## 2​.​​​​​​​3§ 1216(a)(3) – The Mandatory Investigation Command  

Section `§` 1216(a)(3) contains arguably the most decisive language in the entire scheme:

> “OSC shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”  

The verb **“shall”** conveys a **mandate** rather than a mere permission—it obligates OSC once an allegation fits within its ambit*. The provision does not tie its duty to any pre‑existing judicial finding; rather it creates an autonomous investigatory trigger whenever someone alleges that an agency has withheld information contrary to FOIA’s ban on arbitrary or capricious denials.^[U.S.C.§ 1216(a)(3).]

In *America First*, AFL invoked exactly this clause after alleging that DOJ had adopted a policy—a refusal to search employee emails without consent—that allegedly caused unlawful withholdings under FOIA. By pointing directly at § 1216(a)(3), AFL argued that even though no court had yet ordered production nor found DOJ’s actions arbitrary per FOIA‐section 552(b), Congress had already imposed upon OSC a duty **to open an inquiry**.



---

## 2​.​​​​​​​​4§ 120̣16(c) – Discretion Over How an Investigation Proceeds  

If § `§` `11860`‑`a(3)` imposes a duty *to investigate*, then what does § `11860`‑`c` say about what happens next? The subsection reads:

> “…OSC may investigate and seek corrective action … and disciplinary action … **in the same way** as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.”  

Two key terms emerge:

1️⃣ **“May”** – Unlike “shall,” this word signals **discretion** regarding whether—or how far—to pursue remedial measures after opening an inquiry.
2️⃣ **“In the same way”** – This phrase imports the procedural toolbox from §§ 12014–12015 but does not make every component obligatory.

The D.C.~Circuit has read these clauses together as creating a hybrid scheme: **OSC must commence an inquiry**, yet it retains broad latitude over whether it will apply all §§ 12014–12015 procedural safeguards or whether it will simply close its file without further action.^[America First Legal Found., Inc.\ v.\ Kerner, No.\ 22‑cv‑3576, slip op.\ at \*5 (D.D.C.\ Sept.\ 29\ 2023).] Essentially, once OSC decides—to whatever degree it chooses—whether formal notice provisions apply is left within its administrative judgment.


In practice this means:
- An allegation satisfying §­·`11860`‑`a(3)` prompts OSC🔥to open its docket.
- Beyond opening the file there is no statutory compulsion for *any particular* investigative step absent further congressional direction.
- Consequently litigants cannot claim a guaranteed entitlement simply because Congress used “shall” at one stage while reserving “may” at subsequent stages.


This tension between mandatory initiation and discretionary execution sits at the heart of many standing disputes involving administrative agencies—including AFL’s claim that denial of investigative rights inflicted concrete injury.<sup>‡</sup> When plaintiffs assert injury based solely on IRC's failure *to act*, courts ask whether ‘action’ was something Congress actually required—not merely something they might have done.



---  

## 2​​​.​​11FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) – A Separate Enforcement Trigger  

FOIA contains its own limited mechanism obligating OSC when federal courts find specific wrongdoing.^[5 U.S.C.§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)]. The statute commands immediate initiation of disciplinary proceedings ***only if*** all three conditions are met:

1️⃣ A court orders production of records improperly withheld;
2️⃣ The court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency;
3️⃣ The court explicitly finds that the agency acted “arbitrarily or capriciously” concerning that withholding.

Only upon satisfaction of those tightly defined predicates does FOIA prescribe autonomous enforcement by SOSC.^[Id.] This pathway differs materially from CSRA‐based authority because it hinges on prior judicial fact‑finding rather than on a bare allegation made by any private party.


In *America First*, DOJ argued that because none of those three predicates existed—no court order compelling production and no fee award—the statute did not empower OSC either through § 552 or through any other avenue.<sup>†</sup> The District Court rejected this narrow view, holding that § 552(b)’s trigger does **not constrain** OSSC’s ability under ‑ § 12616(a)(3); however it still concluded Congress left actual investigative choices largely discretionary [[see Am\.\ F.\ L.\ Found.\ v.\ Kerner]].


Thus two distinct enforcement scaffolds exist:

| Source | Trigger | Mandatory Trigger? | Scope |
|--------|---------|---------------------|-------|
| CSRA §§ 12014–12016 | Any allegation meeting element (e.g., arbitrary/ capricious withholding) | Yes—to open inquiry (§ 12016(a)(3)) | Full investigative toolkit (§ 12014–12015), subject to discretion (§ 12016(c)). |
| FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) | Judicial order + fee award + finding | No—the two statutes operate independently.| Initiates discipline only after court makes specific findings.|

Understanding this dichotomy clarifies why “the three predicates” advanced by DOJ could not alone bar OCTC from acting—but also why courts remain hesitant to turn merely alleged policy disagreements into enforceable rights absent clear congressional command.

## 2​​​.​​12 Judicial Interpretation – Key Precedents Shaping §§ 12014‑12016  

### America First Legal Foundation v. Kerner (2023)

In *Kerner*, the D.D.C.\ addressed precisely the question raised by AFL: Does FOIA’s own enforcement trigger freeze out all other avenues indicated in §§ 12014–12016? The court held **no**.[^1] It emphasized that Congress crafted two overlapping regimes—the CSRA‐based regime granting autonomous investigatory scope (§ 12016), and the more limited FOIA path (§ 552)—with distinct policy rationales[^2]. Consequently,
> “Section ‑ 552(b)’s limitation applies only when invoking its own enforcement scheme; it does not curtail ØSC’s independent duty under ‑ § 12016(a)(3).”

Nonetheless even after concluding UTC must open its probe, *Kerner* reiterated that §§ 12016(c)’s permissive language allowed OCTC considerable latitude over exactly how—including whether—or what procedural notices it would furnish.^[Id.] In short: *the mandate creates ‘a filing obligation,’ not ‘a full blown guarantee’.* This nuanced view formed part of Judge Katsas’ analytical scaffold when deciding whether AFL possessed Article III standing.



### Frazier v. Merit Systems Protection Board (1982)

*Frazier* first articulated OCTC’s broad grant—to secure “fair...civil service”—and identified ‘‘prohibited personnel practices’’ as central targets.^[Id.] While pre‑dating modern digital communications issues embodied in AFL's claim, *Frazier* remains foundational because it frames OCTC's mission as ensuring compliance through both corrective remediation (§ 12014) and discipline (§ II¬ 1315). Understanding this foundational purpose explains why investigators view PAL statements such as ‘‘arbitrary withholding’’ within AOI ambit—it implicates government accountability directly linked backtothe civil service mission.



### Heckler v. Chaney (1985)

Although never expressly cited within AFCvGreer’s discussion (*the opinion alludes generally*), most courts look first toward *Heckler* whenever statutes blend mandatory language with broad discretion.^[Heckler v.Chaney, 470 U.S._821_._831–32_ (1985)]. The Supreme Court held agencies enjoy considerable latitude when deciding whether—and how—to enforce statutory duties so long as they articulate reasons grounded in policy considerations rather than mere whim.[^13] In AFCvGreer*(the panel)* employs this principle implicitly when describing why CCSR’s ‘may’ language shields OCTC from being compelled into specific procedural channels—a deference doctrine reinforced throughout D.C.~Circuit jurisprudence.



### Summers v.… Earth Island Inst., Citizens For Constitutional Integrity v.… Census Bureau  

These cases underscore another doctrinal strand relevant here: plaintiffs cannot rely solely on intangible ‘procedural rights’ absent concrete interests tied tomaterial benefits.[^14] As Judge Katsas notes,

> “[A] deprivation … without some concrete interest ... is insufficient.”  

By emphasizing lack thereof—in AFL’s claim both w.r.t.—the subsequent FEEDRATION OF INFORMATION AND THE STATUTORY NOTICE PROCESS—the panel closed every doctrinaire avenue leveraging CSU–derived rights except where genuine personal injury emerges.[^15]

Together these precedents demonstrate how D.C.~Circuit courts interpret ***mandatory*** verbs (`shall`) against subsequent ***discretionary*** (`may`) subsections—and why particular factual allegations seldom translate into enforceable Article III rights.

### Summation  

The statutes governing OTPC investigations form a layered edifice:
1️⃣ **CSRA §§ 12 14‑12 16 impose**   – A categorical command (`shall`) obligating ESCOTICUS TUBULAR setting off investigators toward any allegation covering arbitrary/inadequate disclosures.
2️⃣ **Section ‑⁠12⁠16(c)**   – A deliberate release valve (`may`) granting agency executives freedom over spill-over steps—including use—or nonuse — of typical PPP processes (`notice`, `opportunity‐to‐respond`, `corrective relief`).  
3️⃣ **FOIA ‑⁠§⁡  552(b)**   – A narrowly tailored trigger predicated upon judicial findings which operates independently yet does not nullify OSTC'S ARGENTINEGOAL OF INVESTIGATION.` 

The D.C.~Circuit has consistently read these components together—a mandatorily triggered filing obligation tempered by executive discretion—as seen most clearly in ***America First Legal Foundation v.​Kerner*** (2023). By locating where agencies must act versus where they may elect otherwise…students can now discern why ***America First Legal Foundation v.​Jamieson Greer*** ultimately decided AFC lacked Article III standing: their grievance rested mainly upon non‑mandated procedural steps—which falls outside protection offered either by `§­ 12⁁216(c)`’s permissive clause nor by any concretely enunciated personal right.*   Understanding this statutory architecture equips future practitioners with precisely what they're looking for next time they encounter questions about agency investigatory duties versus merely optional administrative processes.
