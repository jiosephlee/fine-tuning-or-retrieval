Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Standing Analysis Applied to AFL’s Two Claims  

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit confronted a classic “standing” problem: whether a plaintiff who alleges only a procedural grievance—here, the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate a suspected FOIA violation—has the Article III competence to sue. The court’s answer illustrates how each element of the constitutional standing test is dissected when the alleged injury is informational rather than substantive. This chapter walks through the court’s reasoning on both of America First Legal Foundation’s (“AFL”) standing theories, showing how the judges applied the *Lujan* framework and the limited “relaxed” standards that sometimes apply to procedural‑right claims.

---

## 4.1 Overview of AFL’s Dual Standing Theories  

AFL pleaded two distinct grounds for Article III standing:

1. **First theory:** OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of the FOIA records it sought from the Department of Justice (“DOJ”).  
2. **Second theory:** OSC’s refusal denied AFL “notices and information” that would flow from an investigation under § 1214 if OSC were treating the alleged FOIA withholding as a prohibited personnel practice.

Both theories rely on “informational injuries”—claims that a party was denied data they believe they are entitled to receive—rather than on direct injury caused by a government action that harms a protected interest such as liberty or property.  Because informational injuries are inherently more abstract, courts have carved out a modestly relaxed standing analysis for them (see *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. at 496).  Nonetheless, even under this lenient approach plaintiffs must demonstrate an **injury in fact**, a causal connection between that injury and the defendant’s conduct, and a **redressability** guarantee that a favorable judgment will likely cure the harm (*Allen v. Wright*, 468 U.S. at 750; *Lujan*).

The D.C. Circuit evaluated each prong separately for both theories and concluded that AFL failed all three requirements in each case.

### AFL’s First Theory – Injury From DOJ’s Failure to Produce Records  

#### Alleged injury  

AFL argues that because OSC declined to open an investigation under 5 U.S.C.§ 1216(a)(3), DOJ never felt compelled to produce the emails it had already been ordered—or at least pressured—to turn over under FOIA litigation initiated by AFL itself.  In effect, AFL contends that its concrete injury is “the denial of the requested emails.”  

#### Causation problem  

The court quickly identified a **causal gap** between OSC’s inaction and DOJ’s alleged non‑compliance.  To satisfy *Lujan*’s second prong—*“the injury must be fairly traceable … to the challenged action”*—the plaintiff must show that but‑for OSC’s investigation it would have been almost certain that DOJ would release the records.  The opinion describes AFC’s hypothesized chain:

1. OSC conducts an investigation under § 1216(a)(3).  
2. OSC decides to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215 against offending DOJ employees.  
3. The Merit Systems Protection Board (“MSPB”) sanctions those employees for an arbitrary FOIA withholding.  
4. DOJ then complies with AFL’s FOIA request.

Each link is speculative because it presumes independent agencies (OSC, MSPB) will act in a particular way without any indication they would have done so even if an investigation were triggered (see *FDA v. All.* for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. at 383).  The Court stressed that causation “cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.”  Moreover, even if OSC were forced to investigate, it retains broad discretion over whether—and how—to pursue remedial action (*Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. at 831‑32).  Accordingly, there is no “fairly traceable” link from OSC's refusal to any eventual production of records by DOJ.

#### Redressability hurdle  

Even assuming causation could be salvaged, redressability still fails because an order compelling OSC merely binds OSC; it does nothing directly obligate DOJ or MSPB either to issue notices or release documents. The D.C. Circuit noted that redressability generally looks at “how a judgment binds the parties,” not whether it might persuade third parties (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. at 293‑94). Since DOJ's behavior remains entirely outside the scope of any judgment against OSC, a favorable decision would not reliably “spare” AFL from its informational loss.

#### Court’s conclusion  

Because both causation and redressability collapse under scrutiny, AFL cannot meet *Lujan*’s second prong for this theory—and consequently lacks standing altogether for its claim that OSC's inaction caused DOJ's alleged withholding.

---

### AFL’s Second Theory – Injury From Denial of Information About an OSC Investigation  

#### Nature of claim: informational standing  

AFL also contended that it suffered an informational injury when OSC refused to provide “notices and information” that would ordinarily accompany an investigation under § 1214 (the procedural scheme governing prohibited personnel practices). In short, AFL argued that § 1216(a)(3) creates an entitlement not only to an investigation but also to the procedural disclosures associated with one.

#### Statutory analysis  

The majority rejected this reading on statutory grounds alone: § 1216 expressly directs OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings but does **not** import § 1214’s notice‑and‑information requirements into its own investigative framework (§ 1216(c) leaves those procedures discretionary). The Court cited *Carson v. U.S.§ Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.​3d at 495‑96, which holds that application of § 1214's procedures in § 1216 investigations is optional—not mandatory.

Because there is no statutory entitlement for AFC—or any party—to receive investigative updates under § 1216, there is no legal basis for asserting an informational injury stemming from lack thereof.

#### Concrete injury assessment  

In cases where plaintiffs claim “informational standing,” courts require proof that “a statute entitled him [the plaintiff] to receive” particular information (see *American Society for Prevention… v.* Feld Entertainment Inc., 659 F.​3d at 23). Without such entitlement—whether because Congress omitted any disclosure requirement or expressly left it discretionary—the claimant cannot show an “injury in fact.” The D.C .Circuit therefore concluded that AFL lacks a concrete interest distinct from its general desire for transparency; its grievance is essentially about disappointment rather than deprivation of a legally protected right.

#### Redirection assessment  

Even if one were generous enough to accept that an investigative entitlement existed, redressability remains doubtful because § 1216 grants OSC wide discretion as to whether—and how—to provide any investigative notices or documents (§ 1216(c)). A judicial order compelling an investigation would not obligate OSC to furnish detailed updates; thus any judgment would likely fail to “redress” AFL's claimed informational harm.

#### Court’s conclusion  

Both elements—injury in fact and redressability—fail under even the relaxed procedural‑injury standards recognized by decisions such as *Center for Law & Education v.* Dep’t of Educ., 396 F.​3d at 1157 (allowing heightened deference when a statutory procedural right protects a concrete interest). Because no statutory right exists here and because discretion bars any reliable remedial effect, AFL’s second standing theory collapses as well.

---

## 4.2 Synthesis – Why Neither Theory Satisfies Article III Standing  

The D.C.\ Circuit’s analysis underscores two overarching lessons about standing in procedural‑injury cases:

1️⃣ **A procedural right alone does not create standing** – Plaintiffs must connect that right to a concrete interest protected by statute or Constitution (*Summers*). Merely alleging denial of non‑existent notice requirements cannot satisfy article‑III jurisdiction.^[See *Summers v.* 555 U.S., 496.]  

2️⃣ **Speculative causal chains cannot substitute for direct causation** – Courts will not infer liability by stringing together multiple discretionary decisions across separate agencies (*FDA v.* All.). For standing purposes, “fairly traceable” causation demands more than conjecture about what might happen if an agency acted differently.^[*FDA* 602 U.S., 383.]  

Applying these principles:

- **First theory** falls apart because AOL cannot show either that OSC's refusal directly caused DOJ's failure (causation) or that ordering OSC would compel DOJ (redressability). The hypothetical chain through MSPB is too uncertain.
- **Second theory** fails because § 1216 does not grant AFL any statutory right to receive investigative updates; without such entitlement there is no concrete injury at all, let alone any practical way for a court order against OSC to cure it.

Consequently, both theories lack one—or more—of *Lujan*’s three pillars (injury in fact, causation/redressability), even when judged under the more lenient standards applied to procedural claims (*Center for Law & Education*). Because Article III standing is missing at its foundation, the district court never possessed subject‑matter jurisdiction over AFL's suit—in other words, there was no case or controversy within constitutional bounds.^[*Steel Co.* 523 U.S., 101.] 

The court therefore vacated the district court judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss on jurisdictional grounds—a textbook illustration that **standing is often decisive before any merits are ever reached**.

**Key Takeaways for Students**

| Element | What Plaintiff Must Show | How It Failed Here |
|---|---|---|
| **Injury in Fact** | Concrete & particularized harm—usually loss of property/right/interest | No statutory entitlement to records or investigative notices |
| **Causation** | Injury must be fairly traceable back to defendant | Speculative sequence involving independent agencies |
| **Redressability** | Judgment must likely remedy injury | Order binds only OSC; DOJ/MSPB actions remain discretionary |

Understanding these building blocks equips you to spot when litigants are simply expressing disappointment rather than invoking enforceable rights—a frequent distinction in administrative‑law disputes involving agencies like OSC.^[*Heckler v.* Chaney].
