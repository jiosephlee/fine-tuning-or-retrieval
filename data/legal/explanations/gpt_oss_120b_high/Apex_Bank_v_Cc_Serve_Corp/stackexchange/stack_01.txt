Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What standard of review does the Federal Circuit apply to TTAB factual findings versus legal conclusions on appeal?
Question:
I'm confused by the opinion’s statement that "We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence." How does this dual standard work in practice? Which elements of a likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis are considered "legal conclusions" and which are "findings of fact"? And how does "substantial evidence" differ from a "de novo" review when assessing the Board’s decision?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit’s approach to an appeal from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) is set out early in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, 2025 WL … :

> “We review the Board’s **legal conclusions de novo**, and its **findings of fact** for **substantial evidence**.”  
> *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc.*, 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006).

Understanding how that bifurcated standard operates requires separating two things that are intertwined in every likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis:

1. **What the Board actually *finds*—the empirical data it records**, and  
2. **How the Board *applies* the law to those data—its weighting and ultimate “likelihood‑of‑confusion” conclusion**.

Below is a quick road‑map that follows the reasoning in *Apex* and shows which portions of the DuPont 13‑factor test fall into each category, together with a short explanation of what “substantial‑evidence” versus “de novo” means in practice.

---

### 1. The two standards defined

| Standard | Review level | What it protects |
|----------|--------------|------------------|
| **De novo** | No deference. The appellate court may disregard the Board’s reasoning and substitute its own judgment on any pure question of law or any question that requires weighing factors.| *Legal conclusions*—e.g., “the second DuPont factor weighs heavily in favor of likelihood‑of‑confusion” or “the board erred by limiting the relevant public.” |
| **Substantial‑evidence** | Highly deferential. The board’s finding stands so long as **reasonable minds could accept** the record as adequate support.| *Findings of fact*—e.g., “the parties’ services are highly similar,” “there are nine third‑party Aspire marks used on credit‑card services,” or “the public consists primarily of credit‑card consumers.” |

The opinion cites *M2 Software* for this split and then explains why likelihood‑of‑confusion itself is a **question of law** built upon underlying factual findings:

> “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts… Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” (*Apex* at 7–8; *M2 Software* at 1381–1382).

Thus every factor contains **facts** (what the record says) *and* a **legal judgment** (how much those facts count toward confusion).

---

### 2. Which DuPont factors are “facts” and which are “law”?

| DuPont factor | Typical factual inquiry (substantial‑evidence) | Typical legal judgment (de novo) |
|---------------|-------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **1 – Similarity of marks** | Appearance, sound, connotation (“the marks look like…”, “they sound like…”). | Whether that similarity **“weighs”** in favor of confusion—i.e., how much overall commercial impression matters (*Apex* vacates this factor because the board’s weighting was deemed legally flawed). |
| **2 – Similarity of goods/services** | Whether services overlap (“credit card services” vs. “banking and financing”). (*Apex* affirms this finding because it was supported by substantial evidence). | Whether that overlap makes confusion “likely”—a legal conclusion reviewed de novo (but here no error found). |
| **3 – Strength of mark** | Evidence about secondary meaning or commercial reputation (“CC Serve’s mark is inherently distinctive”). | How strength influences likelihood—that is a weighting decision reviewed de novo. |
| **4 – Actual confusion** | Survey results, anecdotal instances (“consumers have mistaken one for the other”). | Whether isolated instances rise to a presumption of confusion—a legal assessment subject to de novo review. |
| **5 – Channels of trade / consumer care** | Where and how products are sold (“online banking portal vs. retail bank branches”). | Whether those channels increase or decrease confusion—again a legal weighting decision. |
| **6 – Number & nature of similar marks** | Count & type of third‑party Aspen marks (“nine Aspire marks used on credit‑card services”). (*Apex* vacates because board incorrectly limited scope—a legal error). | Whether that number signals weakness or strength—de novo weighing. |
| **7–13 – Remaining factors (e.g., fame, intent)** | Any empirical data required by the factor (e.g., fame surveys). | Legal evaluation as to how those facts affect likelihood—de novo.|

In short:

*All* factual details listed above are reviewed for **substantial evidence** —the appellate court looks at whether any reasonable mind could accept those details as true given the TTAB record.

Every time the Board says something like “the second factor weighs heavily in favor” or “the sixth factor does not indicate weakness,” it is making a **legal conclusion** about how much weight the facts deserve; such conclusions are reviewed **de novo**.

---

### 3. How “substantial evidence” differs from “de novo”

1. **Substantial Evidence**  
   - The court asks only whether there exists enough relevant proof for a reasonable mind to reach the same fact conclusion as the TTAB did.
   - It does *not* reweigh competing pieces of evidence.
   - Example from *Apex*: The second factor was upheld because the record showed enough sources—dictionary definitions, third‑party registrations—to support that credit‑card services and banking services are “highly similar.”

2. **De Novo Review**  
   - The court discards any deferential presumption and re‑examines every piece of proof itself.
   - It may give different weight to circumstances, adopt alternative precedents, or even ignore an entire factor if it finds it irrelevant.
   - Example from *Apex*: The Sixth factor was vacated because the board *legally* limited its analysis to credit‑card services only—a restriction that conflicted with its earlier finding under Factor 2 that services were “highly similar.” That limitation was deemed an error in applying the law rather than an evidentiary problem; thus it warranted de novo correction.

---

### 4. Practical takeaways for an appellant

1. **Identify what you want to attack:**  
   - If you think the TTAB misread or misapplied case law—or weighted a factor incorrectly—argue that there is an error in its **legal conclusion**, which gets de novo review.
   - If you believe the TTAB relied on insufficient or unreliable data—e.g., miscounted third‑party marks—you argue lack of **substantial evidence**, forcing the court to look at whether any reasonable mind could accept those facts.

2. **Structure your brief accordingly:**  
   - Start each argument with a heading indicating whether you are challenging a *finding* (substantial‑evidence) or a *conclusion* (de novo).  
   - Cite directly from the TTAB record when attacking facts; cite statutes/precedent when attacking legal reasoning—a format mirrored by Judge Hughes’ discussion in *Apex*.

3. **Remember that every Likelihood‑of‑Confusion factor contains both elements:** even after you win on a factual basis under one standard, you may still need to persuade the court on weight under another—exactly what happened when *Apex* affirmed Factor 2 but vacated Factors 6 and 1.

---

### 5. Bottom line

The Federal Circuit treats an appeal from the TTAB as a two–track process:

- **Factual track:** Every empirical determination entered by the Board must be backed by **substantial evidence**; otherwise it will be reversed.
- **Legal track:** Every step where the Board decides how those facts fit within Lanham Act doctrines—including which DuPont factors count, how they should be weighted, and what constitutes “likelihood of confusion”—is scrutinized **de novo**.

In *Apex Bank*, this split explains why the court left alone the board’s factual finding that Apex’s services were highly similar (substantial evidence), but rewrote its legal analysis concerning how many third‑party Aspire marks matter under Factor 6—and consequently remanded Factor 1—for lack of proper legal application (de novo). Understanding where each standard applies lets you target your appellate arguments precisely where they have bite.
