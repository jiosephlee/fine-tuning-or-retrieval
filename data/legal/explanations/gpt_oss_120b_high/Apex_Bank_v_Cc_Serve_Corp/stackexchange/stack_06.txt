Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Is it permissible for the TTAB to define "credit card services" after its removal from the USPTO ID Manual, and how is such a definition reviewed on appeal?
Question:
The Board created its own definition of credit‑card services because that entry was deleted from the ID Manual. Is it within TTAB authority to craft such definitions ex nihilo? On appeal, what standard do courts apply when evaluating whether those definitions are valid or supported by substantial evidence?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes. The TTAB may supply its own description of a service when the USPTO’s ID Manual no longer contains a entry for that service, so long as the description is rooted in ordinary meaning, dictionaries or industry practice and is supported by the record. On appeal a court treats that description as a **finding of fact**—it reviews it for **substantial‑evidence support**. The court reviews the *legal* conclusions that flow from the description (e.g., whether the services are “highly similar” under the DuPont factors) **de novo**.

---

### 1.  Does the TTAB have authority to “create” a definition?

The Lanham Act gives the TTAB broad fact‑finding power in opposition/registration proceedings.  The Board must decide whether two marks are likely to cause confusion on the basis of the **goods and services** asserted by each party.  When a term is not defined in an official source (here, the USPTO ID Manual), the Board may look to ordinary language usage, dictionaries, industry practice, or other registrations to determine what the applicant meant.

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Board explained that after “the entry for ‘credit‑card services’ was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual,” it “determined” that “credit card services” includes issuing cards, counseling on debt, processing payments, authorization, monitoring alerts, account management and credit‑score access”【J.A. 25】.  The opinion does **not** hold that this was beyond the Board’s power; rather, it treats the description as part of the factual record used to evaluate similarity under DuPont factor 2.

The Federal Circuit therefore accepts that:

* The Board may fill a lexical gap left by the manual’s deletion;
* Its description must be grounded in evidentiary material (the opinion notes reliance on dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” as well as on third‑party registrations【J.A. 27‑28】【J.A. 33‑34】).

Thus, within this case there is no indication that defining “credit‑card services” ex nihilo exceeded TTAB authority.

---

### 2.  How is such a definition reviewed on appeal?

The Federal Circuit reiterates its standard of review:

> “We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382【M2 Software citation】.

* **Findings of fact** – including any factual determination about what a term means – are subject to **substantial‑evidence** review.  Substantial evidence means “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion”【Consol. Edison】.

* **Legal conclusions** – such as applying DuPont factor 2 (similarity of goods/services) or deciding whether likelihood‑of‑confusion exists – are reviewed **de novo**.

Applying this framework:

* The court **affirmed** the Board’s second‑factor finding because “substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar”【J.A. 35】. The description of “credit‑card services” therefore survived substantial‑evidence scrutiny.
* By contrast, when discussing factors 1 and 6 (mark similarity and third‑party use), the court stressed that any errors there would be corrected under their respective standards (de novo for legal analysis; substantial evidence for factual determinations). The opinion did not vacate factor 2 because it found an adequate evidentiary basis for the Board’s service definition.

In short: **the TTAB’s service definition is treated as a factual finding**; an appellant must show that no reasonable mind could accept it based on the record to overturn it on appeal.

---

### 3.  Practical take‑aways for practitioners

| Issue | TTAB authority | Standard on appeal |
|-------|----------------|--------------------|
| Defining an omitted service term | Permitted if grounded in ordinary meaning/dictionaries/industry usage and supported by record | Substantial‑evidence review (fact); de novo review of any legal conclusion drawn from that definition |
| Challenging such a definition | Must present contrary dictionary/industry evidence or show absence of any supporting material | Show lack of substantial evidence; otherwise appellate court will defer to Board’s determination |
| Effect on likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis | Definition feeds into DuPont factor 2 (goods/services similarity) and possibly other factors | Legal application of DuPont factors reviewed de novo; factual basis for similarity reviewed substantively |

---

### 4.  Bottom line

The Federal Circuit in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* acknowledges that when an entry disappears from the ID Manual, **the TTAB can articulate its own description of those services**, provided it relies on ordinary meanings and evidentiary support.  On appeal those descriptions receive **substantial‑evidence scrutiny**, not a fresh de novo rewriting—only the subsequent legal conclusions about confusion are re‑examined de novo.

So if you confront a TTAB decision that defines “credit card services” (or any other term) after its manual entry has been removed, expect an appellate court to uphold that definition unless you can demonstrate that *no reasonable mind* could accept it based on the record presented at trial before the Board.
