Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How does a trademark’s commercial strength or weakness influence its scope of protection under the first DuPont factor?
Question:
The court vacated the Board’s first‑factor analysis because reconsideration of the sixth factor could affect the mark’s commercial impression. What role does commercial strength play in assessing similarity of appearance, sound, connotation, and overall impression? Why might an inherently distinctive mark still receive only a narrow scope if deemed weak?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the DuPont test the “commercial impression” of a mark is part of the **first** factor – it informs the overall comparison of appearance, sound, connotation and “overall impression.”  A mark that enjoys **commercial strength** (broad consumer recognition, few competing uses) is treated as having a *broad* scope of protection, so even modest visual or phonetic similarities can create a likelihood‑of‑confusion finding.  Conversely, a mark that is **commercially weak**—for example because many similar marks already exist in the same marketplace—is given only a *narrow* scope; consumers are presumed able to distinguish it from others, and greater similarity is required before confusion is likely.  The Federal Circuit’s decision in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 WL …** makes this relationship explicit by vacating the Board’s first‑factor analysis on the ground that a proper sixth‑factor assessment could alter the determination of commercial strength and therefore change the “commercial impression” used in the first‑factor analysis.

---

### 1. What “commercial strength/weakness” means for the first DuPont factor  

> “The first DuPont factor considers the ‘similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and **commercial impression**.’”  
> *DuPont*, 476 F.2d at 1361; see also *In re I.AM.Symbolic*, 866 F.3d at 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017).

The opinion explains that **commercial impression** is not an isolated concept—it is shaped by evidence about how strongly the mark has penetrated the relevant market:

> “Evidence of third‑party use of similar marks … is relevant to show that a mark is relatively **weak** and entitled to only a **narrow** scope of protection.”  
> *Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot*, 396 F.3d at 1373 (citing *Jack Wolfskin*); see also *Juice Generation* (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Thus, when evaluating appearance, sound, or connotation, the Board must ask:

* Does the public see this mark as highly distinctive because it has achieved fame or extensive use? → **Strong commercial impression → broad protection**.
* Or do consumers see this mark as one among many similar ones because numerous other “Aspire” marks operate in credit‑card/finance services? → **Weak commercial impression → narrow protection**.

Because “commercial impression” feeds directly into the overall similarity analysis, any change in how strong or weak the mark is perceived will reshuffle how much weight is given to visual or phonetic resemblances.

### 2. Why an inherently distinctive mark can still receive a narrow scope  

An **inherently distinctive** word (e.g., “ASPIRE”) automatically satisfies one prong of Section 2(b) – but it does **not** guarantee a *broad* protective radius.  The opinion notes:

> “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength… [and] … [it] is entitled to the ‘normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.’”  
> *Apex Bank*, J.A. 51.

The “normal scope” for an inherently distinctive mark presumes **some baseline level of consumer recognition**, but if evidence shows **weakness**—for instance a crowded field with many “Aspire” marks—the Court treats that baseline as **narrower** than it would be for a strong mark:

* The sixth factor revealed that there were nine “Aspire‑formative” marks in credit‑card services, and—while not “voluminous”—the Board concluded they were insufficient to prove “considerable” use indicating weakness.
* The Court held that this analysis was flawed because it excluded other financial‑service uses; once all similar goods are considered, the weight given to commercial weakness could increase and shrink even an inherently distinctive mark’s protective umbrella.

Consequently, even though “ASPIRE” is inherently distinctive on its face, if it lacks demonstrable secondary meaning or market dominance, courts will limit its protection to avoid overextending its reach into a field where consumers already distinguish many similar marks.

### 3. How this ties back to Apex Bank’s vacatur  

The Federal Circuit vacated both the sixth‑factor finding (which measures market crowding) **and** the first‑factor finding because:

1. The sixth factor determines whether **commercial weakness** exists.
2. That determination directly shapes the **commercial impression** component of the first factor.
3. A different conclusion on weakness could either broaden or narrow the overall similarity analysis under appearance/sound/connotation.

Hence, until the Board re‑examines how many similar “Aspire” marks exist across all banking‑related services (the proper sixth‑factor inquiry), it cannot correctly gauge the mark’s commercial strength and therefore cannot correctly assess its overall commercial impression under the first factor.

---

#### Bottom line for law students  

* The first DuPont factor’s “commercial impression” is not just an aesthetic judgment—it reflects how strongly consumers associate a mark with its source.
* Evidence about **market crowding** (sixth factor) informs whether that impression is *strong* (few similar marks → broad protection) or *weak* (many similar marks → narrow protection).
* An inherently distinctive mark can still be confined to a narrow protective radius if evidence shows it lacks that market dominance—i.e., if it resides in a crowded field where consumers have learned to differentiate among similar signs.  

This interplay explains why Apex Bank’s appeal required remand on both factors: without an accurate assessment of commercial strength/weakness, any conclusion about visual or phonetic similarity would be premature.
