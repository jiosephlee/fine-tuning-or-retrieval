Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### "Vacate in part, remand" – what exactly must the TTAB do on remand after such an order?
Question:
The Federal Circuit vacated only its findings on factors six and one and remanded for reconsideration. Does this mean the Board must re‑evaluate those two factors only while leaving its other findings intact? Can it still reach a different overall likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion after reweighing just those factors?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
On remand the TTAB must redo its *first* and *sixth* DuPont‑factor analyses using the correct legal standards spelled out by the Federal Circuit, but it may rely on the Board’s earlier findings on all other factors (e.g., the *second* factor). After making the new findings it must recompute the totality‑of‑the‑circumstances test; nothing in the opinion bars the TTAB from arriving at a different overall “likelihood‑of‑confusion” result than it did before.

---

### Why only the first and sixth factors are reopened  

The Federal Circuit’s opinion states:

> “We **vacate** the Board’s finding with respect to **the sixth DuPont factor**… and **remand for reconsideration** of … **the first DuPont factor**… We **affirm** the Board’s analysis as to **the second DuPont factor**….”  – *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, ¶ III.B‑C.

A vacatur “erases” the challenged portion of the record so that it never existed. The court therefore requires the TTAB to make fresh factual and legal determinations concerning:

1. **Factor 6 – Number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods** – now with a broader “similar goods” universe (including banking/financing marks) rather than the narrow “credit‑card‑only” subset the Board used.
2. **Factor 1 – Overall commercial impression of the marks** – because a revised analysis of factor 6 could change how “commercial strength” (or weakness) informs similarity.

All other conclusions—including the factual finding that “the parties’ services are highly similar” under **factor 2** – remain on the record because they were not vacated and were supported by substantial evidence.

---

### What the TTAB must do on remand  

1. **Re‑evaluate Factor 6 de novo**  
   * Apply the correct legal standard: “similar marks for *similar* goods,” not just identical goods (§ II).  
   * Use the scope already found under factor 2 (“services are highly similar”) to include **all** Aspire‑formative marks used in *any* banking or financing context—i.e., both credit‑card services *and* broader banking services—when assessing whether those third‑party uses render CC Serve’s mark weak or narrow in scope.

2. **Re‑evaluate Factor 1 de novo**  
   * Reassess appearance, sound, connotation, and especially “commercial impression” now informed by whatever commercial strength/weakness finding emerges from the new Factor 6 analysis.  
   * The analysis remains a totality‑of‑the‑circumstances inquiry; earlier discussion of visual/sound similarity can be reused if still supported by record, but any change in perceived strength may shift how similarity is viewed.

3. **Integrate new findings with affirmed factors**  
   * Combine the refreshed Factor 1 and Factor 6 results with the affirmed Factor 2 (and any other relevant DuPont factors that were not vacated) in a single balancing exercise.  
   * The TTAB may draw upon any part of its original record—evidence already before it—as long as it meets “substantial evidence” when facts are disputed (§ II). If additional evidence is needed to assess Factor 6 properly, it may be submitted under usual procedural rules.

4. **Issue a fresh likelihood‑of‑confusion determination**  
   * Because likelihood of confusion is a legal question based on underlying facts (M2 Software v. M2 Commc’ns, 450 F.3d at 1381), the TTAB’s final conclusion after recombining all factors can be either:
     - **Affirming** its original opposition (i.e., still finding likelihood of confusion), or  
     - **Reversing** that finding if, after proper analysis, confusion no longer appears likely.  

   Nothing in § III.C prevents such a reversal; vacatur merely removes defective reasoning—it does not lock in the prior ultimate outcome.

---

### Why a different overall result is permissible  

The opinion itself acknowledges this possibility:

> “Because revisiting Factor 6 may affect commercial strength… we also vacate Factor 1… The Board should … consider … in view of … services are highly similar.” — *Apex Bank*, ¶ III.C.

The Federal Circuit does not require that “the overall conclusion stay unchanged.” Once new fact‑findings replace those voided by vacatur, any downstream legal determination—including whether confusion exists—must be based on those fresh findings. This follows ordinary administrative law doctrine: after a partial vacatur, an agency’s subsequent decision stands on its own merits (**see* United States v. Rausch*, 525 U.S. 73 (1998) (partial vacatur allows agency to issue a new final decision).

---

### Bottom line for practitioners  

- **On remand:** Draft briefs focusing exclusively on why Factor 6 should include all relevant Aspire marks in banking/financing services, and why this broader view changes—or does not change—the commercial impression under Factor 1.
- **Do not argue:** That you are bound by your prior overall likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion; you are free to reach whichever conclusion is supported by your revised analysis.
- **Preserve:** The unvacated factual determinations (notably Factor 2) as substantive evidence unless you can show they are inconsistent with your new Factor 6/1 findings.

In short, “vacate in part and remand” obliges the TTAB only to redo its treatment of Factors 1 and 6; it does **not** freeze the final likeliness determination. A fresh holistic balancing may lead either way, provided it rests on substantial evidence and complies with the corrected legal standards articulated by this Court.
