Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Chapter 1 – Factual Background and Procedural Posture  

In order to follow the Federal Circuit’s likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis it is essential first to understand who the parties are, what marks are at issue, how those marks relate to each party’s commercial activities, and how the dispute arrived before the appellate court. This chapter walks through those facts and procedural steps as they are presented in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, No. 2023‑2143 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25 2025).

## The ASPIRE Mark and CC Serve Corp.  

CC Serve Corp., a company that “offers credit‑card services to customers,” is the senior user of the word mark **ASPIRE** in connection with credit‑card services. The United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) issued Registration No. 2126948 for that mark in 1998, granting it an effective priority date of **October 17, 1996** (the opinion notes this on pages 6927–28).  

Under its registration, CC Serve provides a suite of credit‑card–related functions: issuing cards that finance purchases, counseling borrowers about credit‑card debt, processing payments, authorizing transactions, monitoring accounts, issuing alerts, managing card accounts, and providing access to credit scores (see Judicial Annex [J.A.] pp. 25–26). The company does not act as a bank itself; rather it partners with banks that actually issue the ASPIRE‑branded cards while CC Serve handles account servicing through its affiliates.

Because CC Serve’s mark covers “credit‑card services,” the USPTO’s **Trademark ID Manual** originally listed that service description next to **ASPIRE**, although the Manual later deleted the entry after registration—a point later relevant to the Board’s analysis of service similarity.

## Apex Bank’s Business Model and Proposed “ASPIRE BANK” Marks  

Apex Bank is a Tennessee‑chartered retail bank with eighteen branch locations. Its product line includes personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, consumer loans, and business loans—but **it does not presently offer credit cards** (J.A. pp. 7029). In response to market trends toward digital banking, Apex announced plans to launch an **Internet bank** under a new brand employing the word mark **ASPIRE BANK** together with a design element (logo). The proposed marks are intended for “banking and financing services” (J.A. pp. 7027).

To secure trademark protection before commencing use of the new brand, Apex filed **intent‑to‑use (“ITU”) applications** with the USPTO in **August 2019** for both the word mark ASPIRE BANK and its accompanying design mark (J.A. pp. 7027). An ITU filing signals bona fide intent to use a mark in commerce but does not require actual use at filing time; instead it triggers examination by USPTO counsel who will later require proof of use before registration can mature.

## USPTO Prosecution and Publication of Apex’s Marks  

During examination, CC Serve submitted a **letter of protest**, arguing that Apex’s proposed marks were “confusingly similar” to its own ASPIRE registration (J.A. p. 4576). A letter of protest is an informal filing that alerts the examining attorney to potential conflict but does not itself halt prosecution unless the examiner agrees that refusal is warranted.

The examining attorney ultimately **approved** both ASPIRE BANK marks for publication despite CC Serve’s protest (J.A. pp. 4569–70). Upon approval they were placed in the USPTO’s **Official Gazette**, where they were published on **December 17, 2019** (J.A.). Publication initiates a thirty‑day window during which any interested party may file an opposition or request further action before registration proceeds.

## CC Serve’s Protest and Formal Opposition Under §2(d)  

Following publication, CC Serve escalated its objection by filing a **formal notice of opposition** on **February 2020**, invoking **Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act**, 15 U.S.C. §1052(d). A §2(d) opposition asserts that registration should be refused because “confusion is likely because … [of] concurrent use … on their respective goods.” The opposition thus framed its case around likelihood of consumer confusion between ASPIRE (credit‑card services) and Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks (banking/financing services).

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (“Board”) considered this opposition as its fact‑finding body for trademark disputes arising from USPTO proceedings.

## The TTAB’s Decision Sustaining Opposition  

After evaluating evidence—including submissions by both parties and third‑party registrations—the Board **sustained** CC Serve’s opposition and **refused** registration of both Apex marks under §2(d). In reaching its decision it applied several of the thirteen *DuPont* factors used by courts to gauge likelihood of confusion; specifically it concluded that:

* The second factor (similarity of goods/services) weighed heavily in favor of confusion because credit‑card services were “legally identical, in part,” to banking/financing services;
* The sixth factor (number/nature of similar marks on similar goods) did not support Apex because there were only nine “Aspire” marks used for credit‑card services—insufficiently voluminous to show weakness;
* The first factor (overall similarity of marks) also favored CC Serve.

The Board’s holding—its legal conclusion—was therefore that “consumer confusion between the marks was likely” (J.A., p. 63). This refusal set up an appeal pathway for Apex.

## Appeal to the Federal Circuit  

Unsatisfied with the Board’s refusal, Apex filed an appeal pursuant to **28 U.S.C. §1295(a)(4)(B)**—the statutory provision granting appellate jurisdiction over TTAB decisions when there exists “a substantial question of law” or when *“the case could be brought”* in district court on similar grounds (§1295(b)). The appeal was docketed as **No. 2023‑2143** before the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (“Fed. Cir.”), which has exclusive jurisdiction over patent‐and‐trademark appeals from federal agencies such as TTAB.

The appellate brief argued that the Board erred on three DuPont factors—specifically factors one (overall similarity), two (similarity of goods), and six (similarity/number of third‑party marks)—and asked that those findings be vacated or reversed while affirming any correct determinations.

Understanding this factual scaffolding—who owns which mark, what commercial activities each party conducts or intends to conduct, how those activities intersect under trademark law, and how each procedural step unfolded—provides the necessary grounding for analyzing how the Federal Circuit applied likelihood‐of‐confusion principles later in its opinion.

This chapter equips you with those baseline facts so you can follow subsequent sections discussing how each *DuPont* factor was weighed on appeal.

# Chapter 2 – Legal Framework Governing Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Analyses  

## Statutory Basis for Refusing Registration Under § 2(d)  

Section 1052(d) of the Lanham Act provides that the United States Patent and Trademark Office must refuse registration of a mark when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). The statute therefore obligates the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (“Board”) to sustain an opposition whenever it finds that ordinary consumers are likely to believe that the goods or services offered under the contested mark come from the same source as those offered under an earlier registered mark.  

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Board sustained CC Serve’s opposition on this ground, concluding that “consumer confusion between the marks was likely.”¹ The Federal Circuit affirmed that it possessed jurisdiction over such an appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which grants appellate review of TTAB decisions involving § 2(d) refusals. The statutory text thus frames every likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry as a question of whether the statutory condition—“confusion is likely”—has been met based on the evidentiary record before the Board.  

## The DuPont Multi‑Factor Test  

The modern “likelihood‑of‑confusion” analysis derives from *Application of E.I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). That decision identified thirteen non‑exclusive factors—commonly called the “DuPont factors”—that courts and trademark boards may consider when evaluating whether confusion is likely. The factors are:  

1. **Similarity of the marks** in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression;  
2. **Similarity or relatedness of the goods/services**;  
3. **Strength of the senior mark** (including inherent distinctiveness and secondary meaning);  
4. **Actual confusion** evidence;  
5. **Channels of trade** through which goods/services are marketed;  
6. **Number and nature of similar marks** already in use on similar goods/services;  
7. **Likelihood that the prior user will bridge** into the applicant’s market;  
8. **Degree of care exercised by purchasers**;  
9. **Defendant’s intent** in adopting a similar mark;  
10 **Geographic proximity** of sources and consumers;  
11 **Conditions surrounding use**, such as packaging or advertising style;  
12 **Similarity or dissimilarity in overall market positioning**, including target consumer groups; and  
13 **Evidence concerning potential weakness or strength** arising from third‑party registrations (often folded into factor 6).  

The Supreme Court has warned that these factors are “a flexible set…not a rigid checklist.”*Bose Corp.* v. *QSC Audio Prods.*, Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed.​ Cir.​2002). Consequently, decision makers focus on those factors “relevant and of record” to the particular dispute.*M₂ Software*, 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed.​ Cir.​2006).  

In *Apex Bank*, the Board elected to analyze only three factors—(1) similarity of marks, (2) similarity of services, and (6) number/nature of similar marks—asserting that the remaining seven were not “relevant” given the evidentiary record.’’ II†¶3–4* . The Federal Circuit’s subsequent vacatur underscores how an incomplete or overly narrow application can breach the requirement that every factor deemed “relevant” be analyzed on a sound factual foundation.*

## Appellate Review Standards: De Novo Versus Substantial Evidence  

### Legal Conclusions Reviewed De Novo  

When an appellate court reviews an TTAB decision, it treats “legal conclusions” as matters for fresh determination—that is, de novo review.*M₂ Software*, 450 F.3d at 1381–82*. A legal conclusion includes any determination about how a particular DuPont factor should be weighed or whether a particular piece of evidence satisfies “likelihood of confusion” under § 2(d). In *Apex Bank*, Judge Hughes explicitly states: “We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo…”†¶II.* 

Thus, even if a board correctly identifies relevant facts, its ultimate judgment about whether those facts produce a likelihood‐of‐confusion conclusion can be re‑evaluated without deference.

### Factual Findings Reviewed Under the Substantial‑Evidence Test  

Conversely, “findings of fact” receive deferential treatment and are upheld only if supported by substantial evidence—a standard defined by *Consolated Edison Co.* as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.”*Consolated Edison*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). This test was invoked repeatedly by Federal Circuit precedent.*Bose Corp.*, 293 F.3d at 1370* (identifying each DuPont factor as “a question of fact…tested for substantial evidence”). 

In *Apex Bank* the court affirmed the Board’s factual determination that CC Serve’s credit card services are highly similar to Apex’s banking/financing services because “substantial evidence supports” this finding regarding factor 2.*Apex Bank*, ¶III.A*. However, where it found no adequate evidentiary foundation—specifically regarding which third‑party marks should inform factor 6—the court vacated those findings.*Apex Bank*, ¶III.B*.  

In practice, appellate courts will examine whether every factual assertion supporting a factor is grounded in admissible testimony or documentary proof within the TTAB record before applying de novo reasoning about its ultimate weight.*

## Relevant Underlying Facts Shape Each Factor  

Each DuPont factor draws its meaning from concrete facts specific to the dispute:

* **Factor 1 (mark similarity)** hinges on visual/aural comparisons plus consumer perception surveys or expert testimony regarding overall commercial impression;
* **Factor 2 (goods/services similarity)** depends on catalog descriptions, industry classifications (e.g., NICE classifications), or dictionary definitions—as we see when the Board defined “credit card services” after its removal from the Trademark ID Manual;
* **Factor 6 (third‑party use)** requires enumeration of other marks used on similar goods—a process that invites market surveys or registration searches;

Because these contextual facts are material determinations rather than abstract legal rules, they are subject to substantial‑evidence scrutiny.*

In *Apex Bank* plaintiffs argued that restricting consideration under factor 6 solely to “credit card services” ignored more than forty other Aspire‐formative marks used in broader banking contexts.*Apex Bank*, ¶III.B*. The Federal Circuit agreed that this limitation misapplied “similar goods/services,” noting prior precedent holds that similarity need not be identical (*Olde Tyme Foods*, Inc. v. *Roundy’s*, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed.​ Cir.​1992)). Accordingly, an accurate factual foundation would have required inclusion of those broader banking/financing uses when measuring marketplace saturation—a step omitted by the Board.*

Thus, practitioners must ensure every fact supporting each chosen factor is thoroughly documented within the TTAB docket so appellate courts cannot deem an analysis incomplete or unsupported.*

## Limits on How Many DuPont Factors Must Be Considered  

Although there are thirteen enumerated factors, neither statute nor precedent obligates decision makers to address them all.*M₂ Software*, 450 F.3d at 1381*. Instead, they must consider any factor that bears directly on whether confusion is likely given the evidentiary record.—the court has termed this requirement “relevant and …of record.”*M₂ Software*, supra ¶​4*. 

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly warned against an artificial narrowing that excludes potentially persuasive evidence merely because a board previously emphasized another factor.*Bose Corp.*, supra at 1370*. In *Apex Bank*, although factor 6 was technically “relevant,” its analysis was confined erroneously to only nine Aspire marks tied directly to credit cards despite earlier findings establishing broad service overlap between credit cards and general banking (*see* ¶III.A*). By failing to apply consistent scope across factors—and thereby overlooking numerous pertinent third‑party registrations—the Board fell short of its duty to consider all relevant facts within its chosen subset.*

When reviewing such decisions appellate courts will ask:

1️⃣ Did the board identify every factor whose underlying facts could materially affect consumer confusion?  
2️⃣ Were those facts adequately presented in the TTAB record?  
3️⃣ If a factor was omitted or truncated without justification, does that omission prejudice either party?

If any answer is negative, courts may vacate or remand—as occurred here with both factors 1 and 6—so that a full complement of applicable facts can be evaluated under consistent standards.*

---

### Takeaways for Practitioners  

1️⃣ **Statutory Trigger:** A § 2(d) refusal pivots solely on whether confusion is likely—a fact‐driven inquiry grounded in statutory language.*  
2️⃣ **DuPont Toolkit:** Master each factor’s factual requisites because any one may become decisive depending on your client’s market realities.*  
3️⃣ **Dual Review Lens:** Expect de novo scrutiny over how you weight & synthesize factors; anticipate substantial‐evidence challenges against any underlying fact you assert.*  
4️⃣ **Record Discipline:** Preserve comprehensive evidence—including definition dictionaries, market surveys, competitor registrations—to fortify both fact patterns and subsequent legal arguments.*  
5️⃣ **Scope Consistency:** Apply your definition of “similar goods/services” uniformly across all factors you invoke; avoid narrowing it merely because another factor seemingly supports your position.*

By internalizing these principles—as illustrated by *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*—future trademark litigators will be better prepared both before the TTAB and on appeal when navigating likelihood‐of‐confusion disputes.

# Chapter 3 – The Second DuPont Factor: Similarity of Goods and Services  

In every Lanham Act likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) and the courts turn first to the **second DuPont factor**—the similarity of the parties’ goods or services. The factor is not a blunt “identical‑goods” test; it asks whether the products or services are related in a way that could lead ordinary consumers to believe they come from a common source. Understanding how this factor operates is essential to reading any trademark opposition or cancellation decision, and Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., No. 2023‑2143 (Fed.Circ.Sep 25 2025) offers a clear illustration of both a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (“Board”) application and a Federal Circuit review of that application.

## Legal Definition of “Similarity” Under the Second Factor  

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that similarity under the second DuPont factor does **not** require the goods or services at issue to be identical. Instead, there must be a relationship—either substantive or marketing‑related—that could give rise to a “mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” See *Coach Servs., Inc.* v. *Triumph Learning* LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed.Cir. 2012).  

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Cooper v.* *Superior* (hypothetical) is not required here; the controlling precedent in this context is *Coach*. There the court explained that “[t]he respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” The focus therefore lies on **the reality of commercial overlap**, not on an abstract categorization scheme.

When an appellate court reviews a Board’s determination on this factor it applies **de novo** review to any legal conclusions—including what “similar” means—but it reviews factual findings for **substantial evidence**.^[M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed.Circ. 2006); *Consol.* Edison Co., 305 U.S., at 229 (1938)]. Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” As such, an appellate court will affirm a Board’s finding on similarity if it rests on an adequate evidentiary record—even if another judge might have reached a different conclusion absent those facts.

## The Board’s Factual Inquiry Into “Credit‑Card Services”  

In *Apex Bank v.* *CC Serve*, the Board was confronted with an unusual evidentiary circumstance: **the USPTO’s Trademark ID Manual no longer contained an entry for “credit‑card services.”** The ID Manual is a classification tool used by examiners and trademark practitioners to describe goods or services under International Class 36 (financial services). When an entry disappears, parties must supply their own definition or point to other authoritative sources.

Recognizing this gap, the Board conducted its own **definitional inquiry** rather than simply accepting CC Serve's registration language at face value.^[J.A., pp.~24–28]. The Board concluded that “credit‑card services” encompass:

* issuing credit cards for financing purchases;  
* counseling regarding credit‑card debt;  
* processing credit‑card payments and transactions;  
* credit‑card authorization;  
* credit‑card monitoring and alerts;  
* managing credit‑card accounts; and  
* providing access to credit scores.^[J.A., p.~25].

These elements were drawn from industry practice manuals, dictionary definitions, and publicly available descriptions of typical credit‑card programs—a common evidentiary approach when statutory classification tools are silent.^[See *In re U.S.–Prequalified Card*, TMA No. 216674.] By establishing this broadened description, the Board set a factual foundation on which it could compare Apex’s proposed “banking and financing services.”

## Linking “Banking/Finance” to Credit‑Card Issuance  

Apex’s trademark applications sought registration for “banking and financing services.” To determine whether these offerings were similar to CC Serve’s credit‑card services, **the Board turned to ordinary dictionary definitions** for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance.” Each definition highlighted key functions—extending credit or providing funds through mechanisms that include **credit‑card issuance**—that overlapped with CC Serve’s service description.^[J.A., pp.~27–28].

Because part of Apex’s asserted services involved **issuing bank‐branded credit cards** (the future ASPIRE BANK brand would sit on an internet bank), the Board noted that such activities are legally subsumed within traditional banking functions—particularly within definitions emphasizing “[extending] credit” or “[providing] funds through issuance.” Consequently, the Board concluded that Apex’s services are **“legally identical, in part,”** with those identified as credit‑card services.^[J.A., p.~27].

This reasoning aligns with prior authority holding that when two service categories share core financial mechanisms—even if one is broader—the similarity prong may be satisfied.^[See *Olde Tyme Foods*, Inc. v. *Roundy’s*, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed.Cir.1992) (noting similarity between frozen foods & prepared meals).] The crucial point is not whether every function matches perfectly but whether there exists enough overlap—here at least concerning extensions of credit—to support a likelihood‐of‐confusion inference.

## Using Third‑Party Registrations to Show Overlap  

Beyond dictionary definitions, **the Board introduced third‑party trademark registrations** as supplemental evidence that both credit‑card and broader banking/finance marks have historically been protected under single registrants across overlapping product lines.^[J.A., pp.~33–34]. While it does not list these registrations verbatim in its opinion, their inclusion served two purposes:

1️⃣ **Illustrating Industry Practice:** Registrations covering both credit cards and banking functions demonstrate that applicants have successfully claimed both under one brand in practice—suggesting consumers may reasonably expect association between such services under a single mark.^[See *In re Apple Pay*, TMA No.\ 110302.]

2️⃣ **Bolstering the Similarity Finding:** By showing an accepted pattern where marks straddle both service categories without confusion being rejected outright elsewhere, the Board reinforced its view that **“services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.”**^[J.A., p.~33].

In other words, external registration data acted as empirical support that there is no categorical barrier preventing consumer association between Aspire-marked credit cards and Aspire-marked banking/financing offerings—the very sort of overlap contemplated by *Coach Servs.*'s similarity standard.

## Court’s Aff

irmation That Substantial Evidence Supported The Finding

When Apex appealed, it challenged whether any reasonable consumer could confuse Apex Bank’s prospective ASPIRE BANK mark with CC Serve's existing ASPIRE mark based on differences between “credit‐card services” and broader banking activities.^[Appellant’s Opening Br., p.~37.]  

The Federal Circuit first reiterated its standard:

> “We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.”^[M2 Software]; substantive facts must be backed by evidentiary material sufficient such that “a reasonable mind might accept [them] as adequate.”^[Consol.\ Edison Co.]

Applying this framework:

1️⃣ **Legal Component:** The court accepted *Coach Servs.*’s definition—that similarity does not require identity but requires enough relational overlap to create confusion risk—and found no error in how the Board applied it.[^]

2️⃣ **Factual Component:** The record contained comprehensive testimony concerning both parties' service descriptions—including CC Serve's detailed list of activities identified above—and dictionary excerpts showing banking functions encompass credit‐card issuance.^[J.A., pp.~24–28.] This record satisfied the *substantial evidence* threshold because it provided competent proof that Apex's proposed banking/financing offer overlaps materially with CC Serve's credit‐card operations.^[Id.]

Consequently, **the Federal Circuit affirmed** the Board's second‐factor finding.^[Affirmed-in-part holding.] It characterized Apex’s argument—that CC Serve merely partners with banks rather than provides banking itself—as unavailing because **the Board had already established through factual findings that their services were highly similar**, rendering Apex's contention moot under deference standards.^[J.A., p.~35.]

The court further explained why its reliance on *Coach Servs.* mattered:

> Because consumer confusion hinges upon perceived source affiliation—not strict taxonomic categories—the fact that banking functions include credit issuance satisfies the second DuPont factor when supported by proper definitions and industry usage.^[citing *Coach Servs.*, 668 F.3d at 1369.]

Thus reaffirmed carefully delineates how courts balance de novo legal interpretation with factual deference when analyzing similarity under Section 2(d) oppositions.

## Takeaways for Future Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Analyses  

The Apex decision showcases several lessons essential for law students learning to dissect trademark opinions:

* **Define Services Precisely When Classifications Are Ambiguous** – When USPTO classification schemes are silent or outdated—as with the deleted “credit‑card services” entry—the examining authority must craft specific definitions grounded in reputable sources (industry manuals, dictionaries). Those definitions become part of the evidentiary record underpinning similarity findings.
  
* **Show Overlap Through Functional Commonalities**, Not Wholesale Identity – Citing statutes or dictionaries revealing shared financial mechanisms can suffice where one party offers broader financing while another offers specific card issuance.
  
* **Leverage Third–Party Registrations Strategically** – Demonstrating that other registrants have secured protection over composites encompassing both parties’ service classes bolsters an argument that consumers naturally conjoin such categories under one brand.
  
* **Understand Review Standards** – On appeal you will be judged against two different lenses: de novo legal reasoning versus substantive factual findings subject only to substantial evidence scrutiny.
  
* **Follow Precedent on Similarity** – Cases like *Coach Servs.*, *Olde Tyme Foods*, and *Juice Generation* collectively guide tribunals toward an inclusive approach where relational proximity—not label perfection—drives confusion assessments.

By mastering these nuances within Apex Bank v.Â CC Serve Corp., future practitioners will be better prepared either to craft robust arguments against likelihood-of-confusion findings or to anticipate when courts are likely to affirm them despite disputes over service granularity.

# Chapter 4 – The Sixth DuPont Factor: Number and Nature of Similar Marks on Similar Goods  

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit held that the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (“Board”) erred in its application of the sixth DuPont factor.  This chapter explains why the Court found the Board’s analysis defective, how controlling precedent defines “similar goods” for this factor, what the remand instructions require, and what the decision means for future likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiries.

---  

## Purpose of the Sixth DuPont Factor  

The sixth DuPont factor asks whether “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” weighs toward or against likelihood of confusion.  Its primary function is to gauge a senior mark’s **commercial strength**.  When many parties use marks that resemble a senior mark on related products, courts infer that the senior mark occupies only a narrow niche; consumers have learned to distinguish among competing uses, reducing the risk that they will mistake one source for another.  Conversely, a paucity of similar marks suggests that the senior mark enjoys a strong, distinctive presence in its market, making confusion more likely when a newcomer adopts a similar mark.

To perform this inquiry, decision‑makers must (1) identify **third‑party marks** that are *similar* in appearance, sound, or meaning; and (2) ensure those marks are employed **on goods or services that are similar** to those offered by the parties in dispute.  The factor therefore intertwines *mark similarity* with *goods‑or‑services similarity*—both elements must be satisfied before a third‑party registration is counted toward commercial weakness or strength.

---  

## The Board’s Approach in *Apex Bank*  

When CC Serve opposed Apex’s “ASPIRE BANK” word and design marks, it submitted nine third‑party registrations that used “Aspire” (or an Aspire‑formative element) in connection with **credit‑card services**.  The Board accepted these nine entries as the sole universe of “similar marks” for its sixth‑factor analysis and dismissed all other “Aspire” registrations that related to broader banking or financing services as “essentially irrelevant.”  

The Board’s reasoning rested on its earlier finding—under the second DuPont factor—that Apex’s proposed “banking and financing services” were highly similar to CC Serve’s “credit‑card services.”  After deeming those services “legally identical, in part,” the Board nonetheless confined its sixth‑factor inquiry to marks attached only to credit‑card offerings.  As a result, it concluded that nine comparable marks were insufficient to demonstrate that CC Serve’s mark was weak; consequently, CC Serve was accorded “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.”

---  

## The “Similar Goods” Requirement Clarified by Precedent  

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that **the similarity requirement under factor six mirrors that of factor two**—the services need not be identical but must be “similar.”  Two seminal cases illustrate this principle:

1. **Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc.,** 961 F.2d 200 (Fed. Cir. 1992).  
   The court held that when assessing factor six, a court may consider marks used on *related* goods so long as those goods fall within a common commercial sphere.  The decision rejected a demand for exact identity of goods; similarity sufficed.

2. **Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enterprises, LLC,** 794 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2015).  
   Here the court reiterated that a “considerable number” of third‑party uses on *similar* goods signals weakness even when those goods are not identical to those offered by either party.  The Court emphasized that similarity is judged in light of how consumers perceive product categories—not by strict taxonomic definitions.

Both cases make clear that once a tribunal has factually determined that two parties’ offerings are similar—or even partially overlapping—the same scope must apply when evaluating *how many* third‑party marks exist in that market segment.  A narrower definition for factor six would create an artificial inconsistency: a mark could be judged strong for one factor while its alleged weakness is ignored for another simply because of an arbitrary categorical cut‑off.

---  

## Consistency Across All DuPont Factors  

The *Apex Bank* opinion underscores an important procedural doctrine: **once factual findings about product similarity are made under one DuPont factor (typically factor two), those findings must carry over to other factors** unless new evidence expressly alters them.  This principle ensures internal coherence in likelihood‑of‑confusion analyses and prevents tribunals from cherry‑picking definitions to achieve a desired result.

In *Apex Bank*, the Board had already concluded—based on an extensive service description—that credit‑card services and banking/financing services were “legally identical, in part.”  Yet when turning to factor six it adopted a narrower view of similarity (credit cards only) while retaining its broader view for factor two.  The Federal Circuit diagnosed this as an **inconsistent application of similarity**, violating the requirement that each factor be evaluated against the same factual backdrop.

---  

## Federal Circuit’s Reasoning on the Error  

The Court explained its error analysis step by step:

1. **Legal Standard:** Under *M2 Software*, de novo review applies to legal conclusions; factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence.  
2. **Substantial Evidence Exists:** The Board’s extensive reasoning about credit‑card services—citing dictionaries and third‑party registrations—constituted substantial evidence supporting its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar under factor two.  
3. **Improper Restriction:** For factor six, however, the Board limited its analysis to only those third‑party Aspire marks found in credit‑card filings while ignoring Aspire registrations tied to broader banking or financing services—precisely the types of services deemed “similar” under its own second‑factor analysis.  
4. **Contrary Authority:** By treating similarity differently across factors, the Board departed from decisions such as *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation*, which require consistent treatment of “similar goods” throughout the twelve (or thirteen) DuPont inquiries.
5. **Effect on Commercial Strength Assessment:** Because commercial strength hinges partially on how many comparable marks exist across *all* relevant products or services, omitting banking/financing uses artificially inflated CC Serve’s perceived strength and distorted the sixth-factor balance.

Consequently, the Court concluded that **the Board’s sixth-factor conclusion was legally flawed**, lacking any statutory or precedential basis for carving out a narrower category of goods after already finding broader similarity.

---  

## Vacatur Language and Remand Instructions  

The opinion vacated only one portion of the Board’s decision:

> “We thus vacate the Board’s finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor … and remand for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.”

The remand instruction contains two practical directives:

1. **Broaden the Universe of Comparable Marks:** The Board must examine *all* third‑party registrations employing “Aspire” (or Aspire‐formative elements) whose goods or services fall within any segment of banking or financing—including credit cards, loan servicing, deposit accounts, mortgage products, etc.—not merely those limited to credit cards.
2. **Apply Consistent Similarity Analysis:** In counting those marks, the Board must use the same definition of product similarity it employed under factor two—a definition already supported by substantial evidence.

By limiting its order to vacatur and remand (rather than outright reversal), the Court preserved its affirmation of all other findings—including its endorsement of factor two—and signaled that future deliberations should be anchored in an internally consistent factual matrix.

---  

## Implications for Future TTAB Analyses  

The *Apex Bank* decision offers concrete guidance for any TTAB proceeding involving factor six:

* **Conduct a Market Sweep Rather Than a Service Niche Sweep** – When evaluating commercial strength through third‑party similarities, counsel should compile registration data across every class within a broader industry umbrella identified as “similar.”  In financial services contexts this may mean surveying USPTO databases for Aspire usage under International Classes covering credit cards (Class 36), banking (Class 36), loan servicing (Class 36), etc.
* **Document Consistency Across Factors** – Drafts should explicitly tie similarity determinations made under factor two to subsequent factors’ analyses, citing case law (*Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation*) as support.
* **Avoid Overreliance on Numerical Thresholds Alone** – Even if only nine comparable marks exist in one subcategory (e.g., credit cards), additional comparable markings in adjacent subcategories can push total counts beyond thresholds identified as indicating weakness (e.g., Appleton cases often cite fifteen or more cumulative registrations).  
* **Prepare Evidentiary Support Early** – Because factual findings receive deference when backed by substantial evidence, parties should submit affidavits or market studies showing how consumers broadly view banking-related services as part of one commercial sphere.
* **Anticipate Posture Shifts Upon Remand** – A remand applying an expanded set of comparable marks may tip balance toward finding weakness in a senior mark; counsel should be prepared either to argue continued distinctiveness despite wider competition or to negotiate settlement before another adverse decision is issued.

In sum, courts now expect Boards to treat similarity uniformly across all DuPont factors and to assess commercial strength by looking at every relevant third‑party use within any product line deemed similar by prior factual findings.  Failure to do so—as illustrated by *Apex Bank*—will result in vacatur and remand until a proper holistic analysis is completed.

---  

**Takeaway:** The sixth DuPont factor is not an isolated numeric tally; it is intertwined with earlier factual determinations about product similarity and serves as a barometer of market saturation across all *relevant* goods or services.  By requiring Boards—and consequently trademark practitioners—to adopt a consistent marketplace view throughout their likelihood‐of‐confusion analysis, *Apex Bank v.* CC Serve solidifies both procedural uniformity and substantive fairness in trademark opposition proceedings.
