Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### What standard of review does the Federal Circuit apply to TTAB factual findings versus legal conclusions on appeal?
Question:
I'm confused by the opinion’s statement that "We review the Board’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence." How does this dual standard work in practice? Which elements of a likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis are considered "legal conclusions" and which are "findings of fact"? And how does "substantial evidence" differ from a "de novo" review when assessing the Board’s decision?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit’s approach to an appeal from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) is set out early in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, 2025 WL … :

> “We review the Board’s **legal conclusions de novo**, and its **findings of fact** for **substantial evidence**.”  
> *M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc.*, 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006).

Understanding how that bifurcated standard operates requires separating two things that are intertwined in every likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis:

1. **What the Board actually *finds*—the empirical data it records**, and  
2. **How the Board *applies* the law to those data—its weighting and ultimate “likelihood‑of‑confusion” conclusion**.

Below is a quick road‑map that follows the reasoning in *Apex* and shows which portions of the DuPont 13‑factor test fall into each category, together with a short explanation of what “substantial‑evidence” versus “de novo” means in practice.

---

### 1. The two standards defined

| Standard | Review level | What it protects |
|----------|--------------|------------------|
| **De novo** | No deference. The appellate court may disregard the Board’s reasoning and substitute its own judgment on any pure question of law or any question that requires weighing factors.| *Legal conclusions*—e.g., “the second DuPont factor weighs heavily in favor of likelihood‑of‑confusion” or “the board erred by limiting the relevant public.” |
| **Substantial‑evidence** | Highly deferential. The board’s finding stands so long as **reasonable minds could accept** the record as adequate support.| *Findings of fact*—e.g., “the parties’ services are highly similar,” “there are nine third‑party Aspire marks used on credit‑card services,” or “the public consists primarily of credit‑card consumers.” |

The opinion cites *M2 Software* for this split and then explains why likelihood‑of‑confusion itself is a **question of law** built upon underlying factual findings:

> “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts… Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” (*Apex* at 7–8; *M2 Software* at 1381–1382).

Thus every factor contains **facts** (what the record says) *and* a **legal judgment** (how much those facts count toward confusion).

---

### 2. Which DuPont factors are “facts” and which are “law”?

| DuPont factor | Typical factual inquiry (substantial‑evidence) | Typical legal judgment (de novo) |
|---------------|-------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **1 – Similarity of marks** | Appearance, sound, connotation (“the marks look like…”, “they sound like…”). | Whether that similarity **“weighs”** in favor of confusion—i.e., how much overall commercial impression matters (*Apex* vacates this factor because the board’s weighting was deemed legally flawed). |
| **2 – Similarity of goods/services** | Whether services overlap (“credit card services” vs. “banking and financing”). (*Apex* affirms this finding because it was supported by substantial evidence). | Whether that overlap makes confusion “likely”—a legal conclusion reviewed de novo (but here no error found). |
| **3 – Strength of mark** | Evidence about secondary meaning or commercial reputation (“CC Serve’s mark is inherently distinctive”). | How strength influences likelihood—that is a weighting decision reviewed de novo. |
| **4 – Actual confusion** | Survey results, anecdotal instances (“consumers have mistaken one for the other”). | Whether isolated instances rise to a presumption of confusion—a legal assessment subject to de novo review. |
| **5 – Channels of trade / consumer care** | Where and how products are sold (“online banking portal vs. retail bank branches”). | Whether those channels increase or decrease confusion—again a legal weighting decision. |
| **6 – Number & nature of similar marks** | Count & type of third‑party Aspen marks (“nine Aspire marks used on credit‑card services”). (*Apex* vacates because board incorrectly limited scope—a legal error). | Whether that number signals weakness or strength—de novo weighing. |
| **7–13 – Remaining factors (e.g., fame, intent)** | Any empirical data required by the factor (e.g., fame surveys). | Legal evaluation as to how those facts affect likelihood—de novo.|

In short:

*All* factual details listed above are reviewed for **substantial evidence** —the appellate court looks at whether any reasonable mind could accept those details as true given the TTAB record.

Every time the Board says something like “the second factor weighs heavily in favor” or “the sixth factor does not indicate weakness,” it is making a **legal conclusion** about how much weight the facts deserve; such conclusions are reviewed **de novo**.

---

### 3. How “substantial evidence” differs from “de novo”

1. **Substantial Evidence**  
   - The court asks only whether there exists enough relevant proof for a reasonable mind to reach the same fact conclusion as the TTAB did.
   - It does *not* reweigh competing pieces of evidence.
   - Example from *Apex*: The second factor was upheld because the record showed enough sources—dictionary definitions, third‑party registrations—to support that credit‑card services and banking services are “highly similar.”

2. **De Novo Review**  
   - The court discards any deferential presumption and re‑examines every piece of proof itself.
   - It may give different weight to circumstances, adopt alternative precedents, or even ignore an entire factor if it finds it irrelevant.
   - Example from *Apex*: The Sixth factor was vacated because the board *legally* limited its analysis to credit‑card services only—a restriction that conflicted with its earlier finding under Factor 2 that services were “highly similar.” That limitation was deemed an error in applying the law rather than an evidentiary problem; thus it warranted de novo correction.

---

### 4. Practical takeaways for an appellant

1. **Identify what you want to attack:**  
   - If you think the TTAB misread or misapplied case law—or weighted a factor incorrectly—argue that there is an error in its **legal conclusion**, which gets de novo review.
   - If you believe the TTAB relied on insufficient or unreliable data—e.g., miscounted third‑party marks—you argue lack of **substantial evidence**, forcing the court to look at whether any reasonable mind could accept those facts.

2. **Structure your brief accordingly:**  
   - Start each argument with a heading indicating whether you are challenging a *finding* (substantial‑evidence) or a *conclusion* (de novo).  
   - Cite directly from the TTAB record when attacking facts; cite statutes/precedent when attacking legal reasoning—a format mirrored by Judge Hughes’ discussion in *Apex*.

3. **Remember that every Likelihood‑of‑Confusion factor contains both elements:** even after you win on a factual basis under one standard, you may still need to persuade the court on weight under another—exactly what happened when *Apex* affirmed Factor 2 but vacated Factors 6 and 1.

---

### 5. Bottom line

The Federal Circuit treats an appeal from the TTAB as a two–track process:

- **Factual track:** Every empirical determination entered by the Board must be backed by **substantial evidence**; otherwise it will be reversed.
- **Legal track:** Every step where the Board decides how those facts fit within Lanham Act doctrines—including which DuPont factors count, how they should be weighted, and what constitutes “likelihood of confusion”—is scrutinized **de novo**.

In *Apex Bank*, this split explains why the court left alone the board’s factual finding that Apex’s services were highly similar (substantial evidence), but rewrote its legal analysis concerning how many third‑party Aspire marks matter under Factor 6—and consequently remanded Factor 1—for lack of proper legal application (de novo). Understanding where each standard applies lets you target your appellate arguments precisely where they have bite.

### Does the second DuPont factor require identical goods/services, or merely related ones? How does CC Serve’s partnership with banks affect this analysis?
Question:
The court affirmed the Board’s finding that credit‑card services and banking/financing services are "highly similar" under the second DuPont factor, citing dictionary definitions. However, Apex argues that CC Serve only partners with banks and does not provide banking itself. In trademark law, how closely must the goods or services be related for this factor? Do partnerships or joint‑venture arrangements change the analysis of similarity?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The second DuPont factor does **not** demand that the goods or services be identical; it only requires that they be *related* or *similar* in a way that could cause consumers to believe they come from the same source. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that “credit‑card services” and “banking and financing services” are “highly similar” – even though CC Serve provides those services through a partnership with banks rather than by operating a bank itself. The partnership does not change the analysis; what matters is the nature of the services actually offered to consumers.

---

### The legal standard for the second DuPont factor

> “The services need not be identical—the evidence need only establish that ‘the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.’”  
> *Coach Servs., Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (quoted in §III.A of the opinion).

The Federal Circuit reiterates this principle verbatim in **§III.A** of its decision, emphasizing that similarity—not identity—is sufficient for the second factor.

### How the court applied it here

1. **Definition of the services** – Because “credit‑card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual, the Board first defined them (issuing cards, processing payments, account management, etc.) and then looked at ordinary dictionary meanings of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance.”  
   > “...the dictionary definitions for ‘banking,’ ‘bank,’ and ‘finance’ encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards...” (¶ 27‑28).

2. **Partial legal identity** – The Board concluded that credit‑card services are “legally identical, in part,” to banking/financing services because both involve extending credit and managing accounts.

3. **Substantial evidence** – The appellate panel found substantial evidence supporting this factual finding and therefore **affirmed** the Board’s conclusion on the second factor (¶ 35‑36).

Hence, under federal trademark law—as applied by this Federal Circuit decision—*relatedness* is enough; you do **not** have to show both parties sell exactly the same product or service.

### Does a partnership or joint‑venture change things?

The opinion addresses Apex’s argument that CC Serve merely “partners with banks” and therefore does not provide banking itself:

> “We find Apex’s argument unavailing. The Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services, and substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (¶ A)

The court treats the partnership merely as a factual context for defining CC Serve’s service offering. It does **not** carve out any exemption for joint‑venture arrangements under the second DuPont factor. What matters is how consumers perceive *the services actually rendered*, not who owns or operates them:

* If CC Serve’s role—managing credit‑card accounts, processing payments, providing alerts—is functionally akin to banking/financing activities, then similarity exists regardless of whether a separate bank issues the physical card.
* A partnership may be relevant when assessing other factors (e.g., actual confusion or channels of trade), but it does not lower the threshold for similarity under factor two.

### Take‑aways for you

| Issue | What *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* says |
|-------|-----------------------------------|
| **Goods/services requirement** | Only *similarity* is required; identity is unnecessary (**Coach Servs.** citation). |
| **Role of partnerships** | Partnerships are merely factual background; they do not alter the similarity test under factor two. |
| **Evidence needed** | Substantial evidence—dictionary definitions, functional descriptions, third‑party registrations—can establish similarity even when parties use different business models. |
| **Impact on other factors** | The court later vacated findings on factors six and one because those analyses must be consistent with the established similarity finding; however, *the similarity standard itself remains unchanged*. |

So when you evaluate whether two marks may conflict under factor two, focus on whether an ordinary consumer could reasonably view *the offered services* as belonging together—even if one party delivers those services through a partner or licensee. Identical service lines are unnecessary; functional overlap is enough.

### When applying the sixth DuPont factor, what scope of "similar marks" should be considered – only those used on identical goods, or also those used on related financial services?
Question:
The opinion says the Board improperly limited consideration to nine Aspire‑formative marks used on credit‑card services and ignored 42 other marks used on broader banking/finance services. Under precedent, must the sixth factor consider all marks used on "similar" goods/services? How do courts define "similar" in this context, especially in a crowded financial‑services market?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the Fed. Cir.’s decision in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.**, the sixth DuPont factor must be applied to *all* third‑party marks that use a similar element (e.g., “Aspire”) in connection with **goods or services that are *similarly* related** to the parties’ offerings—not just those used on *identical* products. Because the Board already found in its analysis of the second DuPont factor that credit‑card services and broader banking/finance services are “highly similar” (or even “partially legally identical”), the same breadth of similarity controls when counting comparable marks for the sixth factor.

---

### How the court phrases it

> “The sixth DuPont factor does **not require identical goods—only similar ones**.”  
> *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, 2025 WL ___, at ¶ B‑4 (Fed.Cir. 2025).

The opinion further explains why the Board’s narrower view was erroneous:

> “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar…the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors…We see no reason to impose a different…standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.”  
> *Apex Bank*, 2025 WL ___, at ¶ B‑6–7.

Thus, once similarity is established for one factor, it carries over to all other factors unless there is a reason to reconsider it de novo.

---

### What “similar” means in practice

1. **A factual determination:** Courts look at product/service characteristics, marketing channels, and consumer perception rather than demanding literal identity.
   
2. **Precedent supports a broad reading:**  
   - *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc.* v. *Roundy’s, Inc.*, 961 F.2d at 204 (Fed.Cir.1992) — “similar” does not mean “identical.”  
   - *Juice Generation, Inc.* v. *GS Enters.*, LLC, 794 F.3d at 1338 (Fed.Cir.2015) — dozens of third‑party marks on related products satisfied the sixth factor because they were used on **related** goods.
   - *Palm Bay Imps., Inc.* v. *Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin*, 396 F.3d at 1373 (Fed.Cir.2005) — evidence of many similarly situated marks shows a weak commercial impression even where the goods are not identical.

3. **Financial‑services context:**  
   The opinion itself defines “credit‑card services” to include issuing cards and extending credit (§ II.A ¶ 25). Because traditional definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” also *encompass* extending credit through cards (§ II.A ¶ 27), an ordinary consumer could readily perceive a common source for both sets of services—hence they are **“similar”** for trademark purposes.

4. **Implication for counting third‑party marks:**  
   All Aspire‑formative registrations **on any financial service**—credit cards, checking accounts, loans, mortgages, etc.—must be tallied when applying the sixth factor because they fall within the same broadly defined market segment identified by the second factor.

---

### Why limiting to credit‑card services is wrong

The Board’s analysis confined itself to nine Aspire‐formative marks used **only** for credit‑card services and dismissed thirty‑two additional uses covering other banking/finance activities as “essentially irrelevant.” The appellate court rejected that restriction because:

- It conflicted with its own earlier finding that **the parties’ services are highly similar** (§ III.A).
- It ignored established doctrine that *“similar”* includes related services within a common consumer mind set.
- It produced an artificial scarcity of comparable marks where a crowded market actually exists—contrary to cases where courts have recognized **“voluminous evidence”** across related product lines as indicative of weakness (*Jack Wolfskin*, 797 F.3d at 1373 n.2; *Juice Generation*, 794 F.3d at 1338).

Hence, for a proper sixth‐factor analysis you must count **all** Aspire‐formative trademarks used on any financial service that is reasonably comparable to either party’s offerings.

---

### Practical checklist for TTAB examiners (and practitioners)

| Step | What to do | Authority |
|------|------------|-----------|
| 1 | Identify the *scope* of each party’s goods/services (use § 2(d) registration wording and any TTAB findings). | Apex Bank § II.A ¶ 25–28 |
| 2 | Determine whether those scopes are “similar.” Look at functional overlap (credit extension) and consumer perception – no need for exact identity | *Coach Servs.* v. *Triumph Learning*, 668 F.3d at 1369 |
| 3 | Compile **all** third‑party registrations containing the contested element (“Aspire”) used on any goods/services judged similar in step 2—not just on an identical subset.| Apex Bank § III.B ¶ B‑4–6; *Olde Tyme Foods* |
| 4 | Evaluate whether the volume/ nature of those third‑party uses shows weakness or a narrow scope of protection (e.g., dozens of registrations across banking/finance suggest weak distinctiveness). | *Palm Bay Imps.*, *Juice Generation* |
| 5 | Weigh this evidence against other factors (especially factor 1) in light of step 2’s similarity finding.| Apex Bank § III.C |

---

### Bottom line

In a crowded financial‑services market you **must** consider every third‑party mark that uses a similar element on any service that is *legally or commercially* akin to either party’s offering—not merely those limited to an exact subcategory like “credit‑card services.” The Sixth DuPont factor is designed precisely to capture how widespread use of a similar mark across a related field dilutes commercial strength, and federal appellate precedent now makes clear that “similar” embraces such related financial services uniformly across all DuPont factors.

### How does a trademark’s commercial strength or weakness influence its scope of protection under the first DuPont factor?
Question:
The court vacated the Board’s first‑factor analysis because reconsideration of the sixth factor could affect the mark’s commercial impression. What role does commercial strength play in assessing similarity of appearance, sound, connotation, and overall impression? Why might an inherently distinctive mark still receive only a narrow scope if deemed weak?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the DuPont test the “commercial impression” of a mark is part of the **first** factor – it informs the overall comparison of appearance, sound, connotation and “overall impression.”  A mark that enjoys **commercial strength** (broad consumer recognition, few competing uses) is treated as having a *broad* scope of protection, so even modest visual or phonetic similarities can create a likelihood‑of‑confusion finding.  Conversely, a mark that is **commercially weak**—for example because many similar marks already exist in the same marketplace—is given only a *narrow* scope; consumers are presumed able to distinguish it from others, and greater similarity is required before confusion is likely.  The Federal Circuit’s decision in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 WL …** makes this relationship explicit by vacating the Board’s first‑factor analysis on the ground that a proper sixth‑factor assessment could alter the determination of commercial strength and therefore change the “commercial impression” used in the first‑factor analysis.

---

### 1. What “commercial strength/weakness” means for the first DuPont factor  

> “The first DuPont factor considers the ‘similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and **commercial impression**.’”  
> *DuPont*, 476 F.2d at 1361; see also *In re I.AM.Symbolic*, 866 F.3d at 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017).

The opinion explains that **commercial impression** is not an isolated concept—it is shaped by evidence about how strongly the mark has penetrated the relevant market:

> “Evidence of third‑party use of similar marks … is relevant to show that a mark is relatively **weak** and entitled to only a **narrow** scope of protection.”  
> *Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot*, 396 F.3d at 1373 (citing *Jack Wolfskin*); see also *Juice Generation* (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Thus, when evaluating appearance, sound, or connotation, the Board must ask:

* Does the public see this mark as highly distinctive because it has achieved fame or extensive use? → **Strong commercial impression → broad protection**.
* Or do consumers see this mark as one among many similar ones because numerous other “Aspire” marks operate in credit‑card/finance services? → **Weak commercial impression → narrow protection**.

Because “commercial impression” feeds directly into the overall similarity analysis, any change in how strong or weak the mark is perceived will reshuffle how much weight is given to visual or phonetic resemblances.

### 2. Why an inherently distinctive mark can still receive a narrow scope  

An **inherently distinctive** word (e.g., “ASPIRE”) automatically satisfies one prong of Section 2(b) – but it does **not** guarantee a *broad* protective radius.  The opinion notes:

> “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength… [and] … [it] is entitled to the ‘normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.’”  
> *Apex Bank*, J.A. 51.

The “normal scope” for an inherently distinctive mark presumes **some baseline level of consumer recognition**, but if evidence shows **weakness**—for instance a crowded field with many “Aspire” marks—the Court treats that baseline as **narrower** than it would be for a strong mark:

* The sixth factor revealed that there were nine “Aspire‑formative” marks in credit‑card services, and—while not “voluminous”—the Board concluded they were insufficient to prove “considerable” use indicating weakness.
* The Court held that this analysis was flawed because it excluded other financial‑service uses; once all similar goods are considered, the weight given to commercial weakness could increase and shrink even an inherently distinctive mark’s protective umbrella.

Consequently, even though “ASPIRE” is inherently distinctive on its face, if it lacks demonstrable secondary meaning or market dominance, courts will limit its protection to avoid overextending its reach into a field where consumers already distinguish many similar marks.

### 3. How this ties back to Apex Bank’s vacatur  

The Federal Circuit vacated both the sixth‑factor finding (which measures market crowding) **and** the first‑factor finding because:

1. The sixth factor determines whether **commercial weakness** exists.
2. That determination directly shapes the **commercial impression** component of the first factor.
3. A different conclusion on weakness could either broaden or narrow the overall similarity analysis under appearance/sound/connotation.

Hence, until the Board re‑examines how many similar “Aspire” marks exist across all banking‑related services (the proper sixth‑factor inquiry), it cannot correctly gauge the mark’s commercial strength and therefore cannot correctly assess its overall commercial impression under the first factor.

---

#### Bottom line for law students  

* The first DuPont factor’s “commercial impression” is not just an aesthetic judgment—it reflects how strongly consumers associate a mark with its source.
* Evidence about **market crowding** (sixth factor) informs whether that impression is *strong* (few similar marks → broad protection) or *weak* (many similar marks → narrow protection).
* An inherently distinctive mark can still be confined to a narrow protective radius if evidence shows it lacks that market dominance—i.e., if it resides in a crowded field where consumers have learned to differentiate among similar signs.  

This interplay explains why Apex Bank’s appeal required remand on both factors: without an accurate assessment of commercial strength/weakness, any conclusion about visual or phonetic similarity would be premature.

### "Vacate in part, remand" – what exactly must the TTAB do on remand after such an order?
Question:
The Federal Circuit vacated only its findings on factors six and one and remanded for reconsideration. Does this mean the Board must re‑evaluate those two factors only while leaving its other findings intact? Can it still reach a different overall likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion after reweighing just those factors?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
On remand the TTAB must redo its *first* and *sixth* DuPont‑factor analyses using the correct legal standards spelled out by the Federal Circuit, but it may rely on the Board’s earlier findings on all other factors (e.g., the *second* factor). After making the new findings it must recompute the totality‑of‑the‑circumstances test; nothing in the opinion bars the TTAB from arriving at a different overall “likelihood‑of‑confusion” result than it did before.

---

### Why only the first and sixth factors are reopened  

The Federal Circuit’s opinion states:

> “We **vacate** the Board’s finding with respect to **the sixth DuPont factor**… and **remand for reconsideration** of … **the first DuPont factor**… We **affirm** the Board’s analysis as to **the second DuPont factor**….”  – *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, ¶ III.B‑C.

A vacatur “erases” the challenged portion of the record so that it never existed. The court therefore requires the TTAB to make fresh factual and legal determinations concerning:

1. **Factor 6 – Number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods** – now with a broader “similar goods” universe (including banking/financing marks) rather than the narrow “credit‑card‑only” subset the Board used.
2. **Factor 1 – Overall commercial impression of the marks** – because a revised analysis of factor 6 could change how “commercial strength” (or weakness) informs similarity.

All other conclusions—including the factual finding that “the parties’ services are highly similar” under **factor 2** – remain on the record because they were not vacated and were supported by substantial evidence.

---

### What the TTAB must do on remand  

1. **Re‑evaluate Factor 6 de novo**  
   * Apply the correct legal standard: “similar marks for *similar* goods,” not just identical goods (§ II).  
   * Use the scope already found under factor 2 (“services are highly similar”) to include **all** Aspire‑formative marks used in *any* banking or financing context—i.e., both credit‑card services *and* broader banking services—when assessing whether those third‑party uses render CC Serve’s mark weak or narrow in scope.

2. **Re‑evaluate Factor 1 de novo**  
   * Reassess appearance, sound, connotation, and especially “commercial impression” now informed by whatever commercial strength/weakness finding emerges from the new Factor 6 analysis.  
   * The analysis remains a totality‑of‑the‑circumstances inquiry; earlier discussion of visual/sound similarity can be reused if still supported by record, but any change in perceived strength may shift how similarity is viewed.

3. **Integrate new findings with affirmed factors**  
   * Combine the refreshed Factor 1 and Factor 6 results with the affirmed Factor 2 (and any other relevant DuPont factors that were not vacated) in a single balancing exercise.  
   * The TTAB may draw upon any part of its original record—evidence already before it—as long as it meets “substantial evidence” when facts are disputed (§ II). If additional evidence is needed to assess Factor 6 properly, it may be submitted under usual procedural rules.

4. **Issue a fresh likelihood‑of‑confusion determination**  
   * Because likelihood of confusion is a legal question based on underlying facts (M2 Software v. M2 Commc’ns, 450 F.3d at 1381), the TTAB’s final conclusion after recombining all factors can be either:
     - **Affirming** its original opposition (i.e., still finding likelihood of confusion), or  
     - **Reversing** that finding if, after proper analysis, confusion no longer appears likely.  

   Nothing in § III.C prevents such a reversal; vacatur merely removes defective reasoning—it does not lock in the prior ultimate outcome.

---

### Why a different overall result is permissible  

The opinion itself acknowledges this possibility:

> “Because revisiting Factor 6 may affect commercial strength… we also vacate Factor 1… The Board should … consider … in view of … services are highly similar.” — *Apex Bank*, ¶ III.C.

The Federal Circuit does not require that “the overall conclusion stay unchanged.” Once new fact‑findings replace those voided by vacatur, any downstream legal determination—including whether confusion exists—must be based on those fresh findings. This follows ordinary administrative law doctrine: after a partial vacatur, an agency’s subsequent decision stands on its own merits (**see* United States v. Rausch*, 525 U.S. 73 (1998) (partial vacatur allows agency to issue a new final decision).

---

### Bottom line for practitioners  

- **On remand:** Draft briefs focusing exclusively on why Factor 6 should include all relevant Aspire marks in banking/financing services, and why this broader view changes—or does not change—the commercial impression under Factor 1.
- **Do not argue:** That you are bound by your prior overall likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion; you are free to reach whichever conclusion is supported by your revised analysis.
- **Preserve:** The unvacated factual determinations (notably Factor 2) as substantive evidence unless you can show they are inconsistent with your new Factor 6/1 findings.

In short, “vacate in part and remand” obliges the TTAB only to redo its treatment of Factors 1 and 6; it does **not** freeze the final likeliness determination. A fresh holistic balancing may lead either way, provided it rests on substantial evidence and complies with the corrected legal standards articulated by this Court.

### Is it permissible for the TTAB to define "credit card services" after its removal from the USPTO ID Manual, and how is such a definition reviewed on appeal?
Question:
The Board created its own definition of credit‑card services because that entry was deleted from the ID Manual. Is it within TTAB authority to craft such definitions ex nihilo? On appeal, what standard do courts apply when evaluating whether those definitions are valid or supported by substantial evidence?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes. The TTAB may supply its own description of a service when the USPTO’s ID Manual no longer contains a entry for that service, so long as the description is rooted in ordinary meaning, dictionaries or industry practice and is supported by the record. On appeal a court treats that description as a **finding of fact**—it reviews it for **substantial‑evidence support**. The court reviews the *legal* conclusions that flow from the description (e.g., whether the services are “highly similar” under the DuPont factors) **de novo**.

---

### 1.  Does the TTAB have authority to “create” a definition?

The Lanham Act gives the TTAB broad fact‑finding power in opposition/registration proceedings.  The Board must decide whether two marks are likely to cause confusion on the basis of the **goods and services** asserted by each party.  When a term is not defined in an official source (here, the USPTO ID Manual), the Board may look to ordinary language usage, dictionaries, industry practice, or other registrations to determine what the applicant meant.

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Board explained that after “the entry for ‘credit‑card services’ was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual,” it “determined” that “credit card services” includes issuing cards, counseling on debt, processing payments, authorization, monitoring alerts, account management and credit‑score access”【J.A. 25】.  The opinion does **not** hold that this was beyond the Board’s power; rather, it treats the description as part of the factual record used to evaluate similarity under DuPont factor 2.

The Federal Circuit therefore accepts that:

* The Board may fill a lexical gap left by the manual’s deletion;
* Its description must be grounded in evidentiary material (the opinion notes reliance on dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” as well as on third‑party registrations【J.A. 27‑28】【J.A. 33‑34】).

Thus, within this case there is no indication that defining “credit‑card services” ex nihilo exceeded TTAB authority.

---

### 2.  How is such a definition reviewed on appeal?

The Federal Circuit reiterates its standard of review:

> “We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” *M2 Software*, 450 F.3d at 1382【M2 Software citation】.

* **Findings of fact** – including any factual determination about what a term means – are subject to **substantial‑evidence** review.  Substantial evidence means “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion”【Consol. Edison】.

* **Legal conclusions** – such as applying DuPont factor 2 (similarity of goods/services) or deciding whether likelihood‑of‑confusion exists – are reviewed **de novo**.

Applying this framework:

* The court **affirmed** the Board’s second‑factor finding because “substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar”【J.A. 35】. The description of “credit‑card services” therefore survived substantial‑evidence scrutiny.
* By contrast, when discussing factors 1 and 6 (mark similarity and third‑party use), the court stressed that any errors there would be corrected under their respective standards (de novo for legal analysis; substantial evidence for factual determinations). The opinion did not vacate factor 2 because it found an adequate evidentiary basis for the Board’s service definition.

In short: **the TTAB’s service definition is treated as a factual finding**; an appellant must show that no reasonable mind could accept it based on the record to overturn it on appeal.

---

### 3.  Practical take‑aways for practitioners

| Issue | TTAB authority | Standard on appeal |
|-------|----------------|--------------------|
| Defining an omitted service term | Permitted if grounded in ordinary meaning/dictionaries/industry usage and supported by record | Substantial‑evidence review (fact); de novo review of any legal conclusion drawn from that definition |
| Challenging such a definition | Must present contrary dictionary/industry evidence or show absence of any supporting material | Show lack of substantial evidence; otherwise appellate court will defer to Board’s determination |
| Effect on likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis | Definition feeds into DuPont factor 2 (goods/services similarity) and possibly other factors | Legal application of DuPont factors reviewed de novo; factual basis for similarity reviewed substantively |

---

### 4.  Bottom line

The Federal Circuit in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* acknowledges that when an entry disappears from the ID Manual, **the TTAB can articulate its own description of those services**, provided it relies on ordinary meanings and evidentiary support.  On appeal those descriptions receive **substantial‑evidence scrutiny**, not a fresh de novo rewriting—only the subsequent legal conclusions about confusion are re‑examined de novo.

So if you confront a TTAB decision that defines “credit card services” (or any other term) after its manual entry has been removed, expect an appellate court to uphold that definition unless you can demonstrate that *no reasonable mind* could accept it based on the record presented at trial before the Board.
