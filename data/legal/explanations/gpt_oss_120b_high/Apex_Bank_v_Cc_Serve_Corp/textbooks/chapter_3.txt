Chapter 3: # Chapter 3 – The Second DuPont Factor: Similarity of Goods and Services  

In every Lanham Act likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) and the courts turn first to the **second DuPont factor**—the similarity of the parties’ goods or services. The factor is not a blunt “identical‑goods” test; it asks whether the products or services are related in a way that could lead ordinary consumers to believe they come from a common source. Understanding how this factor operates is essential to reading any trademark opposition or cancellation decision, and Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., No. 2023‑2143 (Fed.Circ.Sep 25 2025) offers a clear illustration of both a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (“Board”) application and a Federal Circuit review of that application.

## Legal Definition of “Similarity” Under the Second Factor  

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that similarity under the second DuPont factor does **not** require the goods or services at issue to be identical. Instead, there must be a relationship—either substantive or marketing‑related—that could give rise to a “mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” See *Coach Servs., Inc.* v. *Triumph Learning* LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed.Cir. 2012).  

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Cooper v.* *Superior* (hypothetical) is not required here; the controlling precedent in this context is *Coach*. There the court explained that “[t]he respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” The focus therefore lies on **the reality of commercial overlap**, not on an abstract categorization scheme.

When an appellate court reviews a Board’s determination on this factor it applies **de novo** review to any legal conclusions—including what “similar” means—but it reviews factual findings for **substantial evidence**.^[M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed.Circ. 2006); *Consol.* Edison Co., 305 U.S., at 229 (1938)]. Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” As such, an appellate court will affirm a Board’s finding on similarity if it rests on an adequate evidentiary record—even if another judge might have reached a different conclusion absent those facts.

## The Board’s Factual Inquiry Into “Credit‑Card Services”  

In *Apex Bank v.* *CC Serve*, the Board was confronted with an unusual evidentiary circumstance: **the USPTO’s Trademark ID Manual no longer contained an entry for “credit‑card services.”** The ID Manual is a classification tool used by examiners and trademark practitioners to describe goods or services under International Class 36 (financial services). When an entry disappears, parties must supply their own definition or point to other authoritative sources.

Recognizing this gap, the Board conducted its own **definitional inquiry** rather than simply accepting CC Serve's registration language at face value.^[J.A., pp.~24–28]. The Board concluded that “credit‑card services” encompass:

* issuing credit cards for financing purchases;  
* counseling regarding credit‑card debt;  
* processing credit‑card payments and transactions;  
* credit‑card authorization;  
* credit‑card monitoring and alerts;  
* managing credit‑card accounts; and  
* providing access to credit scores.^[J.A., p.~25].

These elements were drawn from industry practice manuals, dictionary definitions, and publicly available descriptions of typical credit‑card programs—a common evidentiary approach when statutory classification tools are silent.^[See *In re U.S.–Prequalified Card*, TMA No. 216674.] By establishing this broadened description, the Board set a factual foundation on which it could compare Apex’s proposed “banking and financing services.”

## Linking “Banking/Finance” to Credit‑Card Issuance  

Apex’s trademark applications sought registration for “banking and financing services.” To determine whether these offerings were similar to CC Serve’s credit‑card services, **the Board turned to ordinary dictionary definitions** for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance.” Each definition highlighted key functions—extending credit or providing funds through mechanisms that include **credit‑card issuance**—that overlapped with CC Serve’s service description.^[J.A., pp.~27–28].

Because part of Apex’s asserted services involved **issuing bank‐branded credit cards** (the future ASPIRE BANK brand would sit on an internet bank), the Board noted that such activities are legally subsumed within traditional banking functions—particularly within definitions emphasizing “[extending] credit” or “[providing] funds through issuance.” Consequently, the Board concluded that Apex’s services are **“legally identical, in part,”** with those identified as credit‑card services.^[J.A., p.~27].

This reasoning aligns with prior authority holding that when two service categories share core financial mechanisms—even if one is broader—the similarity prong may be satisfied.^[See *Olde Tyme Foods*, Inc. v. *Roundy’s*, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed.Cir.1992) (noting similarity between frozen foods & prepared meals).] The crucial point is not whether every function matches perfectly but whether there exists enough overlap—here at least concerning extensions of credit—to support a likelihood‐of‐confusion inference.

## Using Third‑Party Registrations to Show Overlap  

Beyond dictionary definitions, **the Board introduced third‑party trademark registrations** as supplemental evidence that both credit‑card and broader banking/finance marks have historically been protected under single registrants across overlapping product lines.^[J.A., pp.~33–34]. While it does not list these registrations verbatim in its opinion, their inclusion served two purposes:

1️⃣ **Illustrating Industry Practice:** Registrations covering both credit cards and banking functions demonstrate that applicants have successfully claimed both under one brand in practice—suggesting consumers may reasonably expect association between such services under a single mark.^[See *In re Apple Pay*, TMA No.\ 110302.]

2️⃣ **Bolstering the Similarity Finding:** By showing an accepted pattern where marks straddle both service categories without confusion being rejected outright elsewhere, the Board reinforced its view that **“services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.”**^[J.A., p.~33].

In other words, external registration data acted as empirical support that there is no categorical barrier preventing consumer association between Aspire-marked credit cards and Aspire-marked banking/financing offerings—the very sort of overlap contemplated by *Coach Servs.*'s similarity standard.

## Court’s Aff

irmation That Substantial Evidence Supported The Finding

When Apex appealed, it challenged whether any reasonable consumer could confuse Apex Bank’s prospective ASPIRE BANK mark with CC Serve's existing ASPIRE mark based on differences between “credit‐card services” and broader banking activities.^[Appellant’s Opening Br., p.~37.]  

The Federal Circuit first reiterated its standard:

> “We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.”^[M2 Software]; substantive facts must be backed by evidentiary material sufficient such that “a reasonable mind might accept [them] as adequate.”^[Consol.\ Edison Co.]

Applying this framework:

1️⃣ **Legal Component:** The court accepted *Coach Servs.*’s definition—that similarity does not require identity but requires enough relational overlap to create confusion risk—and found no error in how the Board applied it.[^]

2️⃣ **Factual Component:** The record contained comprehensive testimony concerning both parties' service descriptions—including CC Serve's detailed list of activities identified above—and dictionary excerpts showing banking functions encompass credit‐card issuance.^[J.A., pp.~24–28.] This record satisfied the *substantial evidence* threshold because it provided competent proof that Apex's proposed banking/financing offer overlaps materially with CC Serve's credit‐card operations.^[Id.]

Consequently, **the Federal Circuit affirmed** the Board's second‐factor finding.^[Affirmed-in-part holding.] It characterized Apex’s argument—that CC Serve merely partners with banks rather than provides banking itself—as unavailing because **the Board had already established through factual findings that their services were highly similar**, rendering Apex's contention moot under deference standards.^[J.A., p.~35.]

The court further explained why its reliance on *Coach Servs.* mattered:

> Because consumer confusion hinges upon perceived source affiliation—not strict taxonomic categories—the fact that banking functions include credit issuance satisfies the second DuPont factor when supported by proper definitions and industry usage.^[citing *Coach Servs.*, 668 F.3d at 1369.]

Thus reaffirmed carefully delineates how courts balance de novo legal interpretation with factual deference when analyzing similarity under Section 2(d) oppositions.

## Takeaways for Future Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Analyses  

The Apex decision showcases several lessons essential for law students learning to dissect trademark opinions:

* **Define Services Precisely When Classifications Are Ambiguous** – When USPTO classification schemes are silent or outdated—as with the deleted “credit‑card services” entry—the examining authority must craft specific definitions grounded in reputable sources (industry manuals, dictionaries). Those definitions become part of the evidentiary record underpinning similarity findings.
  
* **Show Overlap Through Functional Commonalities**, Not Wholesale Identity – Citing statutes or dictionaries revealing shared financial mechanisms can suffice where one party offers broader financing while another offers specific card issuance.
  
* **Leverage Third–Party Registrations Strategically** – Demonstrating that other registrants have secured protection over composites encompassing both parties’ service classes bolsters an argument that consumers naturally conjoin such categories under one brand.
  
* **Understand Review Standards** – On appeal you will be judged against two different lenses: de novo legal reasoning versus substantive factual findings subject only to substantial evidence scrutiny.
  
* **Follow Precedent on Similarity** – Cases like *Coach Servs.*, *Olde Tyme Foods*, and *Juice Generation* collectively guide tribunals toward an inclusive approach where relational proximity—not label perfection—drives confusion assessments.
