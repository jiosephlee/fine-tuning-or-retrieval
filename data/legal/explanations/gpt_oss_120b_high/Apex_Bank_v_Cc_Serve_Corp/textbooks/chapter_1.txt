Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1 – Factual Background and Procedural Posture  

In order to follow the Federal Circuit’s likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis it is essential first to understand who the parties are, what marks are at issue, how those marks relate to each party’s commercial activities, and how the dispute arrived before the appellate court. This chapter walks through those facts and procedural steps as they are presented in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, No. 2023‑2143 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25 2025).

## The ASPIRE Mark and CC Serve Corp.  

CC Serve Corp., a company that “offers credit‑card services to customers,” is the senior user of the word mark **ASPIRE** in connection with credit‑card services. The United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) issued Registration No. 2126948 for that mark in 1998, granting it an effective priority date of **October 17, 1996** (the opinion notes this on pages 6927–28).  

Under its registration, CC Serve provides a suite of credit‑card–related functions: issuing cards that finance purchases, counseling borrowers about credit‑card debt, processing payments, authorizing transactions, monitoring accounts, issuing alerts, managing card accounts, and providing access to credit scores (see Judicial Annex [J.A.] pp. 25–26). The company does not act as a bank itself; rather it partners with banks that actually issue the ASPIRE‑branded cards while CC Serve handles account servicing through its affiliates.

Because CC Serve’s mark covers “credit‑card services,” the USPTO’s **Trademark ID Manual** originally listed that service description next to **ASPIRE**, although the Manual later deleted the entry after registration—a point later relevant to the Board’s analysis of service similarity.

## Apex Bank’s Business Model and Proposed “ASPIRE BANK” Marks  

Apex Bank is a Tennessee‑chartered retail bank with eighteen branch locations. Its product line includes personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, consumer loans, and business loans—but **it does not presently offer credit cards** (J.A. pp. 7029). In response to market trends toward digital banking, Apex announced plans to launch an **Internet bank** under a new brand employing the word mark **ASPIRE BANK** together with a design element (logo). The proposed marks are intended for “banking and financing services” (J.A. pp. 7027).

To secure trademark protection before commencing use of the new brand, Apex filed **intent‑to‑use (“ITU”) applications** with the USPTO in **August 2019** for both the word mark ASPIRE BANK and its accompanying design mark (J.A. pp. 7027). An ITU filing signals bona fide intent to use a mark in commerce but does not require actual use at filing time; instead it triggers examination by USPTO counsel who will later require proof of use before registration can mature.

## USPTO Prosecution and Publication of Apex’s Marks  

During examination, CC Serve submitted a **letter of protest**, arguing that Apex’s proposed marks were “confusingly similar” to its own ASPIRE registration (J.A. p. 4576). A letter of protest is an informal filing that alerts the examining attorney to potential conflict but does not itself halt prosecution unless the examiner agrees that refusal is warranted.

The examining attorney ultimately **approved** both ASPIRE BANK marks for publication despite CC Serve’s protest (J.A. pp. 4569–70). Upon approval they were placed in the USPTO’s **Official Gazette**, where they were published on **December 17, 2019** (J.A.). Publication initiates a thirty‑day window during which any interested party may file an opposition or request further action before registration proceeds.

## CC Serve’s Protest and Formal Opposition Under §2(d)  

Following publication, CC Serve escalated its objection by filing a **formal notice of opposition** on **February 2020**, invoking **Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act**, 15 U.S.C. §1052(d). A §2(d) opposition asserts that registration should be refused because “confusion is likely because … [of] concurrent use … on their respective goods.” The opposition thus framed its case around likelihood of consumer confusion between ASPIRE (credit‑card services) and Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks (banking/financing services).

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (“Board”) considered this opposition as its fact‑finding body for trademark disputes arising from USPTO proceedings.

## The TTAB’s Decision Sustaining Opposition  

After evaluating evidence—including submissions by both parties and third‑party registrations—the Board **sustained** CC Serve’s opposition and **refused** registration of both Apex marks under §2(d). In reaching its decision it applied several of the thirteen *DuPont* factors used by courts to gauge likelihood of confusion; specifically it concluded that:

* The second factor (similarity of goods/services) weighed heavily in favor of confusion because credit‑card services were “legally identical, in part,” to banking/financing services;
* The sixth factor (number/nature of similar marks on similar goods) did not support Apex because there were only nine “Aspire” marks used for credit‑card services—insufficiently voluminous to show weakness;
* The first factor (overall similarity of marks) also favored CC Serve.

The Board’s holding—its legal conclusion—was therefore that “consumer confusion between the marks was likely” (J.A., p. 63). This refusal set up an appeal pathway for Apex.

## Appeal to the Federal Circuit  

Unsatisfied with the Board’s refusal, Apex filed an appeal pursuant to **28 U.S.C. §1295(a)(4)(B)**—the statutory provision granting appellate jurisdiction over TTAB decisions when there exists “a substantial question of law” or when *“the case could be brought”* in district court on similar grounds (§1295(b)). The appeal was docketed as **No. 2023‑2143** before the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (“Fed. Cir.”), which has exclusive jurisdiction over patent‐and‐trademark appeals from federal agencies such as TTAB.

The appellate brief argued that the Board erred on three DuPont factors—specifically factors one (overall similarity), two (similarity of goods), and six (similarity/number of third‑party marks)—and asked that those findings be vacated or reversed while affirming any correct determinations.

## Procedural Timeline  

| Date | Event | Significance |
|------|-------|--------------|
| Oct 17 1996 | Effective priority date for CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration | Establishes seniority over later filings |
| 1998 | USPTO issues Registration No.​2126948 for ASPIRE | Gives CC Serve enforceable rights against later users |
| Aug 2019 | Apex files ITU applications for ASPIRE BANK word & design marks | Initiates trademark prosecution; no use yet required |
| Dec 17 2019 | Publication of Apex marks in Official Gazette | Triggers opposition window |
| Feb 2020 | CC Serve files formal §2(d) opposition | Begins adversarial proceeding before TTAB |
| Oct 2020?* | TTAB issues decision sustaining opposition (date implicit ) | Denies registration; creates right of appeal |

\*The exact decision date is not stated in the opinion excerpt but precedes Apex’s appeal timeline; only its content matters for our purposes.

Understanding this factual scaffolding—who owns which mark, what commercial activities each party conducts or intends to conduct, how those activities intersect under trademark law, and how each procedural step unfolded—provides the necessary grounding for analyzing how the Federal Circuit applied likelihood‐of‐confusion principles later in its opinion.

--- 

In summary, *Apex Bank v.* *CC Serve* centers on a senior user (“ASPIRE”) protecting its credit‑card brand against a junior user seeking registration of “ASPIRE BANK” for banking services—a classic scenario where courts must parse service similarity and market overlap through established statutory routes (*Lanham Act* §§1(e), 2(d)) and procedural mechanisms (*Trademark Trial & Appeal Board*, *Federal Circuit* review). This chapter equips you with those baseline facts so you can follow subsequent sections discussing how each *DuPont* factor was weighed on appeal.
