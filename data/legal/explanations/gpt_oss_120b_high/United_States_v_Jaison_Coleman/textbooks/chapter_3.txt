Chapter 3: # Chapter 3 – Voluntariness Analysis in United States v. Coleman  

In *United States v. Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit examined whether Lisa Coleman’s alleged consent to police entry into the family home was “freely and voluntarily” given under the Fourth Amendment’s consent exception.  This chapter walks a law‑student through the court’s application of the voluntariness standard—focusing on the officer’s conduct, Lisa’s capacity to refuse, and the totality‑of‑circumstances factors that guided the appellate court’s finding of no clear error.



## Assessment of Lisa’s Authority Over the Premises  

Consent may be supplied by any occupant who possesses **common authority** over a residence—meaning a reasonable expectation that both parties may control entry and use of the dwelling (see *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974); *United States v. Cellitti*, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004)).  The district court correctly characterized Lisa as a co‑habitant spouse/partner who shared such authority with Jaison Coleman.  Because she lived with him and exercised daily control over who entered the home (e.g., opening doors for visitors), her expression of permission satisfied the statutory requirement that a person with “legitimate expectations of privacy” may authorize a search—even though she was not the criminal defendant herself.



## “Initial Refusal” Demonstrates Capacity to Deny Consent  

The dialogue captured on body camera shows Lisa’s first response to Officer Kizer’s request:  

> “I’d prefer you not.”  

Under *United States v. Hicks*, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008), an **initial refusal** carries weight because it evidences an uncoerced ability to say “no.”  The Seventh Circuit stressed that an officer may make a subsequent request **so long as** it is not accompanied by force or intimidation—a principle reaffirmed in *United States v. Han*, 105 F.4d 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024).  Here, Lisa’s clear “no” at the outset signaled she retained agency over her decision to admit police.



## “Use of ‘need’ Language” – Conveying Urgency Without Coercion  

Officer Kizer later said:

> “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure …”

Coleman argued that this phrasing rendered his request coercive.  The circuit rejected that view, noting that urgency alone does **not** equate to compulsion when expressed in a calm tone and coupled with a genuine concern for child safety.  In *Hicks*, the court held that an officer’s “urgency” language is permissible provided it does not cross into threats or undue pressure—a standard satisfied here because Lisa subsequently answered “Okay” without protest.



## Absence of Custody  

A crucial element in evaluating voluntariness is whether the person is **in custody**—that is, whether a reasonable person would feel they are not free to leave (*Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S._543_, 548 (1968)).  The record shows Lisa remained on her porch, could have left at any time, and was never detained or restrained by police.  Consistent with *Figueroa‑Espana*, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007), lack of custody weighs heavily toward finding consent voluntary.



## Length of Interaction  

The total exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa lasted only **a few minutes** before she opened the door and invited officers inside.  In *Figueroa‑Espana* the Seventh Circuit identified prolonged questioning or detainment as an indicator of coercion; a brief encounter—especially one centered on immediate child‑safety concerns—does not suggest undue pressure.



## Presence/Absence of Physical Coercion or Threats  

The body‑camera footage depicts Officer Kizer speaking calmly, without any physical force or explicit threats (“you will be arrested if you don’t…”).  Under *Bumper*, physical or psychological compulsion undermines voluntariness; its absence therefore supports a finding that consent was freely given.



## Totality‑of‑Circumstances Factors Applied  

The Seventh Circuit applied its well‑established “totality‑of‑circumstances” test (**United States v. Han**, supra) and examined each factor discussed in *Figueroa‑Espana*:

1️⃣ **Age/Intelligence/Education** – Lisa is an adult capable of understanding police questions; there is no evidence she lacked mental capacity.

2️⃣ **Advisement of Rights** – While Miranda warnings are irrelevant for Fourth‑Amendment searches (*Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S._218_, 219 (1973)), none were required here because consent does not rely on knowledge of constitutional rights.

3️⃣ **Duration of Detention** – As noted above, interaction was brief; there was no prolonged confinement before consent was sought.

4️⃣ **Immediacy versus Repeated Requests** – Officer Kizer asked twice for entry; after an initial refusal he made a second request framed by concern (“need”).  The second request came without any change in her circumstances except his reiterated articulation of child‑safety concerns—precisely what *Han* describes as an acceptable reiteration when still voluntary.

5️⃣ **Physical Coercion** – None observed; video corroborates a non‑coercive demeanor.

6️⃣ **Custodial Status** – She was free to leave at any time, satisfying this factor against coercion.



Taken together, these factors leaned heavily toward **voluntary consent**, mirroring prior appellate holdings.



## Precedent Comparison  

The court anchored its analysis in three key precedents:

* **United States v. Hicks** – Emphasized that expressing urgency does not transform a request into unlawful coercion so long as it lacks intimidation.
* **United States v. Han** – Reiterated that courts evaluate voluntariness under a flexible totality‑of‑circumstances model.
* **United States v. Figueroa‑Espana** – Enumerated concrete factors—age, custodial context, length of questioning—that guide courts’ inquiries.

In each case the Seventh Circuit upheld consensual entries where officers acted respectfully, did not detain individuals, and avoided overt pressure—all facts mirrored in *Coleman*.  This consistency underscores that *Coleman* did not depart from established jurisprudence but rather applied familiar standards to its factual tableau.



## Court’s Conclusion: No Clear Error in Finding Voluntary Consent  

After reviewing video evidence and applying the totality test, the Seventh Circuit concluded:

> “We see no clear error in the district court’s finding that Lisa consented…,” 

reasoning that Lisa possessed authority over the home, demonstrated an ability to refuse (“I’d prefer you not”), ultimately assented voluntarily after a non‑coercive second request, and was never under custodial restraint or physical duress.  

Because every element of the voluntariness inquiry tilted toward free choice—not coercion—the appellate court affirmed the district court’s denial of Coleman’s motion to suppress and upheld his conviction for unlawful firearm possession.



---  

**Takeaway for Students:**  
When dissecting a Fourth‑Amendment consent issue:

1️⃣ Identify who holds common authority over premises.
2️⃣ Look for any **express refusal**, which signals capacity.
3️⃣ Scrutinize language indicating urgency—determine if it crosses into intimidation.
4️⃣ Confirm there is no custodial confinement.
5️⃣ Measure interaction length against expectations of briefness.
6️⃣ Verify absence of physical or psychological pressure.
7️⃣ Apply each totality‑of‑circumstances factor methodically.
8️⃣ Compare your analysis with controlling precedent—*Hicks*, *Han*, *Figueroa‑Espana*—to gauge consistency.

In *United States v. Coleman* these steps led the Seventh Circuit to deem Lisa Coleman’s consent voluntary and lawful under Fourth Amendment doctrine.
