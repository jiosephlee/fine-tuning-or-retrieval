Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### Can a spouse give valid third‑party consent to search a home when both spouses have legitimate privacy expectations?
Question:
The Seventh Circuit notes that ‘a third party may give consent … even though the defendant is present’ citing Matlock and Cellitti. In Coleman the consenting party was Lisa, the wife of the defendant who was also present in the house. How do courts determine whether a spouse has authority over areas where both spouses have expectations of privacy, especially when one spouse is suspected of felon possession?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes. Under Seventh Circuit precedent a spouse who shares “common authority” over the home may validly consent to police entry and to a search that falls within the scope of that consent, even though the other spouse is present and may be a suspect.  The Court’s decision in **United States v. Jaison Coleman**, 2025 WL ____ (7th Cir. Oct. 7 2025) confirms that a wife’s consent was sufficient to permit officers to enter the residence and to conduct a limited sweep, because she possessed ordinary joint control over the premises and her consent was voluntary under the total‑of‑the‑circumstances test.

---

### 1. Governing doctrine – “common authority”

The Fourth Amendment permits searches without a warrant when **a third party voluntarily consents**.  The Supreme Court first articulated the relevant rule in *Matlock*:

> “A third party may give consent … even though the defendant is present… provided the third party has **common authority** over the premises”  
> (415 U.S. 164 (1974)).

The Seventh Circuit has applied *Matlock* repeatedly.  In *Cellitti* it reiterated that:

> “A third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy”  
> (387 F.3d at 621).

“Common authority” means that “the consenting party possesses **ordinary joint use** and **mutual control** over the property.”  The presence of an occupant who is suspected of wrongdoing does **not** defeat another occupant’s ability to waive his or her own Fourth‑Amendment interest so long as the consenting occupant has that joint control.

---

### 2. How courts evaluate whether spousal consent is valid

#### (a) Whether the spouse actually has common authority
* Look at who lives there and whether both parties routinely exercise control over rooms, furniture, and personal effects.
* Exclusive spaces—e.g., a locked drawer known only to one spouse—may fall outside common authority (see *United States v. Sansone*, 790 F.2d at 476 (7th Cir. 1986)).  Absent proof of such exclusivity, courts presume joint control.

#### (b) Whether the consent was **voluntary**
The Seventh Circuit follows *Bumper*’s “freely and voluntarily” standard and applies a **total‑of‑the‑circumstances** inquiry (see *United States v. Jones*, 22 F.4th at 676–77).  Relevant factors include:

1. Age, education, intelligence  
2. Whether rights were explained  
3. Length of detention before consent  
4. Promptness vs. repeated prompting  
5. Presence or threat of physical coercion  
6. Whether the person was in police custody  

(These factors are listed verbatim in *Coleman*, §II.A.)

#### (c) Whether officers stayed within the **scope** of that consent
Scope is judged by **objective reasonableness**—what a typical person would understand from the exchange (see *Jimeno*, 500 U.S. at 251).  A search that goes beyond what was described or implied exceeds permissible bounds (*Saucedo*, 688 F.3d at 865).

---

### 3. Application in **United States v. Jaison Coleman**

| Issue | Court’s analysis | Result |
|------|-------------------|--------|
| **Did Lisa have common authority?** | She lived with Jaison, shared access to every room, and was able to direct officers to specific areas (“the back bedroom,” “the kids are in my bedroom”). No evidence showed any part of the house was her exclusive domain.<sup>1</sup> | Yes – she possessed ordinary joint use and control over the entire home. |
| **Was her consent voluntary?** | The Court looked at the video record and found: <br>• She initially refused entry.<br>• She later said “Okay” after Officer Kizer re‑asked without any physical force or threats.<br>• She remained free—not in custody—and could have declined again.<br>• The totality test showed no coercion (§II.B). | Voluntary under *Bumper* / *Jones* standards. |
| **Was the subsequent sweep within scope?** | Officers entered only to verify children’s safety and locate Jaison—a purpose consistent with Lisa’s expressed wishes (“to make sure…they’re okay”). The Court held this objective reasonableness satisfied *Jimeno*’s scope inquiry (§II.B). | Yes – officers remained inside what Lisa authorized (“to check on kids”). |
| **Effect on Fourth Amendment claim** | Because both elements—valid third‑party consent and limited scope—were satisfied, there was no Fourth‑Amendment violation warranting suppression of any evidence discovered after obtaining a warrant (§II.B). | Conviction upheld; no suppression required.|

Footnote 1 – The opinion expressly notes there was no proof that any portion of the residence was off‑limits to Lisa or that she lacked authority over it.

---

### 4. Practical take‑away for law students

1️⃣ **Spousal consent is generally sufficient** where spouses share ordinary joint control over their home—even if one spouse is suspected of wrongdoing.

2️⃣ **The key questions are:**  
   - Does the consenting spouse have “common authority”?  
   - Was her agreement given voluntarily under total‑of‑the‑circumstances scrutiny?  
   - Did police stay within what a reasonable person would understand as being authorized?

3️⃣ **If any exclusive area is proven**, police would need either a separate warrant or separate consent covering that space.

4️⃣ In litigation you will argue *Matlock*/**Cellitti**, then turn to *Bumper*/*Jones* for voluntariness and *Jimeno* for scope—exactly as done in *Coleman*.

---

### 5. Bottom line

In Seventh Circuit jurisprudence—and as affirmed in **United States v. Jaison Coleman**—a spouse can validly give third‑party consent for police entry and for any search reasonably related to what she authorized, even when her partner is present and suspected of illegal conduct.  Courts first confirm common authority, then apply a total‐of‐the‐circumstances test for voluntariness, then ensure police actions stay within an objectively reasonable scope derived from the conversation with the consenting spouse.
