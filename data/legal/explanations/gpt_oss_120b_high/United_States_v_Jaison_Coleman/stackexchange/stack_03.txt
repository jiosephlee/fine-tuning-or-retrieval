Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### What is the objective‑reasonableness test for determining whether police exceeded the scope of consent during an interior sweep?
Question:
After obtaining Lisa’s consent, officers entered the home, canvassed multiple rooms, located Coleman in another bedroom and questioned him before securing a warrant. Under Jimeno’s ‘objective reasonableness’ standard and Saucedo’s guidance on scope, how do courts decide what a typical homeowner would understand as permitted by their assent? Does searching beyond rooms where children were known to be located exceed that scope?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Seventh Circuit uses **“objective‑reasonableness”** – the same standard set out in *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991) – to decide whether police have gone farther than the consent they were given.  After confirming that consent was voluntary, a court asks:

> *“what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent?”*  
> (*United States v. Coleman*, 2025 WL ??? at ¶ 15‑B)【source】.

That “typical reasonable person” inquiry is applied **to the scope** of the assent, not just its existence.  As *Saucedo* puts it, “the proper inquiry for evaluating the scope of consent is ‘objective reasonableness’”【source】.  In practice the courts look at:

1. **The language used by both parties** – e.g., “Okay… we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids] are okay.”  
2. **The context of the request** – a domestic‑violence call where safety of children is at issue.  
3. **The homeowner’s ability to limit or revoke consent** – Lisa was repeatedly asked for permission and could have said “no,” which she did before eventually saying “Okay.”  
4. **The purpose communicated by the police** – protect children and determine whether anyone inside (including her husband) posed a danger.

If those factors show that a reasonable homeowner would expect police to **look around the house** in order to locate all occupants and assess safety, then moving from room‑to‑room is within consent.  If instead police were to open locked drawers or search for contraband unrelated to safety, a reasonable homeowner would not have authorized that.

### How *Coleman* applies the test

In *United States v. Jaison Coleman* (7th Cir. 2025), Judge Scudder held that:

* Lisa’s statement “Okay” after Officer Kizer said he “almost need[s] to come inside to make sure [the kids] are okay” **gave officers permission** not only to enter but also to **scan the interior** for any threat to child safety.  
* The officers’ subsequent walk‑through of every bedroom—including one where Coleman was later found—was therefore **within the scope of that permission** because their stated purpose required locating every person in the home and confirming there was no immediate danger.  
* The court expressly found “no infirmity…that …they …respect[ed] the consent”【source】and affirmed that no excess occurred even though officers entered rooms where children were *not* known to be located.

Thus, under Jimeno’s objective‑reasonableness standard, a typical homeowner who says “Okay” in response to an officer’s request framed as a safety check would understand that police may conduct a **general interior sweep** necessary to fulfill that safety check.  Searching beyond rooms where children are known to be does **not** exceed the scope so long as it serves the protective purpose communicated at the time consent was given.

### Bottom line

To determine whether police exceeded consent:

1. Re‑create – from an outsider’s perspective – what a **reasonable homeowner** would infer from what was said.
2. If the inference includes a **protective sweep** of any area needed to locate occupants or assess danger, then movement into additional rooms is permitted.
3. Any investigative activity unrelated to that protective purpose (e.g., searching for weapons or evidence without a warrant) would likely be outside consent and therefore unlawful.

In *Coleman* the Seventh Circuit concluded that officers stayed within that objectively reasonable scope; consequently their entry into every bedroom was deemed permissible under Lisa’s assent.
