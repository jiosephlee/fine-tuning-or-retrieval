Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What standard do appellate courts apply when deciding whether to grant a stay pending appeal of a district court’s preliminary injunction?
Question:
I’m reading Pacito v. Trump where the Ninth Circuit discusses its four‑factor test from Nken v. Holder (likelihood of success on merits…, irreparable injury…, balance… public interest…). The opinion says factors (1) and (2) are ‘most critical,’ but I’m not clear how they weight these factors relative to each other or how ‘public interest’ is evaluated in an immigration context. Does an appellate panel have discretion to give more weight to one factor over another? How exactly does this test operate when both parties claim irreparable harm?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit applies the same four‑factor equitable test that the Supreme Court set out in **Nken v. Holder**, 556 U.S. 418 (2009).  The stay order in *Pacito v. Trump* explains that the court “considers four factors” – likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury, balance of hardships, and public interest – and then stresses that “the first two … are *the most critical*.” *Pacito*, No. 25‑1313/25‑1939 (9th Cir. Sept. 12 2025) (quoting Nken at 434).  

Below is a practical breakdown of how those four factors operate in an appellate stay proceeding and what *Pacito* tells us about their relative weight, discretion, and especially how “public interest” is treated in an immigration case where both parties plead irreparable harm.

---

### 1️⃣ The Nken Four‑Factor Framework

| Factor | What the appellate court asks |
|--------|------------------------------|
| **(1) Likelihood of success on the merits** | Is the moving party’s appeal likely to succeed? This is a *gate‑keeping* inquiry – if the movant cannot show a strong likelihood of prevailing, the stay is usually denied (Nken). |
| **(2) Irreparable injury** | Will the movant suffer damage that cannot be adequately remedied by money if the stay is denied? Both parties may raise this factor; each claim is evaluated separately. |
| **(3) Balance of hardships** | Assuming (1) and (2) are satisfied, which side would suffer greater injury from granting or denying the stay? |
| **(4) Public interest** | What effect does granting or denying the stay have on society at large? In immigration cases this factor often turns on deference to executive authority over border control.*

*Pacito* repeats this rubric verbatim and adds an interpretive caveat: “Of these factors, the first two … are ‘the most critical.’”¶ 4‑5

---

### 2️⃣ How “Most Critical” Shapes Weighting

The Ninth Circuit does **not** assign numeric scores or a fixed hierarchy beyond what *Nken* says; rather it treats (1) and (2) as thresholds that must be crossed before (3)–(4) become decisive.

* **If either (1) or (2) is lacking**, a stay is almost always denied regardless of how strongly public‑interest considerations favor the movant.
* **When both (1) and (2) are satisfied**, the court freely balances (3) against (4). This is where judicial discretion expands dramatically.

In *Pacito* the panel concluded:

> “The government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163… The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f)… has undoubtedly been fulfilled.”¶ 7  

Because the government met the first factor overwhelmingly, its weakness on any other factor could not defeat a stay.

Thus *most critical* means **the first two factors dominate**; once satisfied, courts may give either remaining factor whatever weight facts demand.

---

### 3️⃣ Discretion to Emphasize One Factor Over Another

Yes—within the four‑factor structure an appellate panel has broad **equitable discretion** to emphasize any factor once (1)–(2) are satisfied.

*The *Pacito* order illustrates this*: after finding strong merit likelihood for the government, it turned to “the remaining factors… favor the United States as well,” citing *Hawaii v. Trump*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018).¶ 8‑9  
The panel therefore gave **public‑interest** a heavier hand than it might have otherwise given in a purely commercial dispute because immigration policy implicates “both domestic and foreign interests” that Congress has largely delegated to the President.

The dissenting judge acknowledges this latitude by arguing that “the public interest… lies with permitting lawful refugee admissions,” showing how reasonable judges can reach opposite conclusions by weighing public‑interest differently.

---

### 4️⃣ Public Interest in an Immigration Context

In immigration matters courts routinely invoke **presidential prerogative over border control**:

> “The authority … ‘exudes deference’… ‘vests [the President] with ample power…’ ”¶ 6  

and

> “The remaining factors … favor the United States as well.”¶ 8  

Thus *public interest* in *Pacito* was evaluated through two lenses:

1. **Sovereign authority** – protecting national security and foreign policy prerogatives commands strong deference.
2. **Statutory scheme** – even though § 1522 obliges provision of resettlement services when funded, Congress left funding decisions discretionary (“to the extent of available appropriations”). This limitation tempered any claim that refusing service serves an overriding public good beyond presidential power.

Consequently, an appellate court may deem that upholding an executive moratorium advances public interest even if it harms individual refugees—a judgment rooted in longstanding deference rather than any new rule invented by *Pacito*.

---

### 5️⃣ When Both Parties Claim Irreparable Harm

Irreparable injury is examined **individually for each side**, then weighed against one another under factor three:

1. **Government’s claimed injury:** In *Pacito*, officials argued that being forced to maintain cooperative agreements for refugee resettlement while funding had been rescinded would cause “irreparable” disruption of statutory programs and potentially expose them to liability for failure to comply with § 1522(a)(1)(A).
2. **Plaintiffs’ claimed injury:** Refugees asserted that enforcement of the executive order would result in loss of status abroad, separation from families already admitted, and denial of statutory benefits—all classic claims of irreparability.
3. **Court’s balancing:** Because *the government was deemed likely to win on merits*, its alleged injuries were treated as less speculative than plaintiffs’ injuries tied directly to congressional prohibitions under § 1522(b)-(e). Hence “the balance … favored” enforcement of executive authority while still ordering limited relief (“directed…to reinstate cooperative agreements”) where unrebutted statutory obligations existed.

No rule requires a court to pick whichever party presents a stronger narrative; rather it evaluates which side would suffer greater lasting damage *in light of* its probability of success on merits—a point echoed throughout *Nken* and reaffirmed by *Pacito*’s emphasis on factor one as decisive.

---

### Take‑aways for Law Students

| Point | What you should remember |
|-------|--------------------------|
| **Four‑factor test** | Apply Nken exactly as quoted; treat likelihood‑of‑success and irreparability as gating criteria (“most critical”). |
| **Weighting** | No fixed formula—once gatekeepers are met you can give any remaining factor whatever weight factual circumstances justify. |
| **Discretion** | Courts have wide leeway; dissent shows different judges can reach opposite conclusions by weighing public interest differently. |
| **Public interest & immigration** | Deference to presidential power over entry restrictions heavily tilts public‑interest analysis toward executive interests unless clear statutory mandates override it.| 
| **Competing irreparable harms** | Analyze each party’s claim separately then balance them under factor three; whichever side faces greater irreversible damage after considering merit prospects will usually tip the scale.| 

In short, *Pacito v.* confirms that appellate courts follow Nken’s four‑factor equitable structure, treat success on appeal and irremediable injury as essential thresholds, retain broad discretion thereafter—and in immigration cases typically view “public interest” through a lens of presidential authority over borders.

### How far does presidential deference extend under 8 U.S.C. §1182(f) when suspending refugee admissions?
Question:
In Pacito v. Trump the panel held that ‘the President has undoubtedly fulfilled’ the finding requirement in §1182(f) and therefore likely will succeed on merits against challenges to Executive Order 14163’s moratorium on refugee entries. I’m trying to understand what limits—if any—exist on this power. Does Congress set any substantive boundaries (e.g., nondiscrimination, due process), or is the President’s discretion essentially plenary? How do cases like Trump v Hawaii inform this analysis?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)** the President may **suspend the entry of any class of aliens** once he makes a *finding* that such entry would be *detrimental to the interests of the United States*.  
The Ninth Circuit’s order in **Pacito v. Trump** treats that statutory language as giving the President *broad, almost plenary* authority: the court’s role is limited to asking whether the **statutory prerequisite**—the finding of detriment—has been satisfied.  If it has, the court will *not* re‑examine the merits of the finding or weigh competing policy arguments (see Pacito at ¶ 9‑10).  

That approach follows the Supreme Court’s deferential framework articulated in **Trump v. Hawaii**, 585 U.S._ 667 (2018), which holds that:

1. Immigration decisions are “*political questions*” rooted in the Constitution’s grant of *plenary power* to the political branches;
2. When a statute like § 1182(f) **confers discretion**, courts apply a **rational‑basis review** and ask only whether there is *any* conceivable justification for the executive’s action (*Kleindienst*‑type pre‑clearance test); and  
3 3. The judiciary **“exudes deference”** to presidential determinations of national‑security or foreign‑policy risk (see *Hawaii*, 585 U.S._ 667 at 684‑85).

Accordingly, **the President’s discretion under § 1182(f) is essentially plenary** so long as:

* The statutory‐textual condition (“detrimental to U.S. interests”) is met;
* The suspension does **not run afoul of an independent congressional restriction** (e.g., an explicit nondiscrimination provision); and
* The action does not violate an **overriding constitutional prohibition** (e.g., a facially discriminatory classification subject to strict scrutiny).

The **Pacito** panel emphasizes point (1).  It finds “the President has undoubtedly fulfilled” his finding requirement and therefore “the language of § 1182(f) is clear…[and] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.” ¶ 10‑11.

---

### How far does this deference go?

| Issue | Source in *Pacito* / *Hawaii* | Effect on Presidential Power |
|------|------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Statutory prerequisite** | *Pacito*, ¶ 9‑10 | Once a finding is made, courts do not second‑guess it. |
| **Textual limits** | *Pacito*, ¶ 10‑11 | The statute only requires a finding; it says nothing else about *how* or *why* such a finding must be reached, leaving scope wide open. |
| **Nondiscrimination / Equal Protection** | Not addressed directly in *Pacito*, but “broad discretion” language borrowed from *Hawaii* suggests that even potential facial discrimination would be examined only under rational‑basis review unless a protected class trigger applies (§ 1154(a)(1) prohibits nationality‐based discrimination except when justified by national security). In practice courts have upheld broader bans when tied to foreign‑policy concerns (*Hawaii*). |
| **Due‑process limitations** | Implicitly limited by *Kleindienst v. Mandel*: an agency need only provide a **facial reason** for denial; no detailed evidentiary hearing required so long as there is some legitimate justification—which § 1182(f)’s “detrimental” finding satisfies.^[See Kleindienst, 388 U.S._ 953 (1967)]. |
| **Other statutory duties** | Judge Lee’s partial dissent notes §§ 1522(a)–(e) requiring “reception and placement services.” The Ninth Circuit thus **separates** authority to suspend admission (*§ 1182(f)*) from obligations to fund post‑admission services (*§ 1522*). The panel orders reinstatement of those services because they are mandated by a different statute—not because they limit suspension power itself.^[Lee, concurring‑in‑part/dissenting‑in‑part]. |
| **Supreme Court precedent on scope** | *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S._ 667 (2018) | Confirms that even when an executive action appears motivated by impermissible animus, it survives judicial review if it plausibly serves a legitimate national‑security interest—a standard far more deferential than strict scrutiny.|

#### Takeaways for you as a law student

1️⃣ **The only categorical limitation built into § 1182(f)** is its own text: a presidential finding that entry would be *detrimental*.   Once that factual predicate exists, courts treat any subsequent suspension as within presidential power.^[Pacito at ¶ 9‑10].

2️⃣ **Congress can impose extra bounds**, but they must appear elsewhere in the immigration scheme—for example,
   - The INA’s nondiscrimination clause (§ 1154(a)(1)) restricts classifications based on nationality unless justified.
   - Statutes such as §§ 1522(a)–(e) impose separate funding/resentment duties; failure to comply can trigger APA challenges even if admission suspensions are valid (*see Judge Lee’s discussion*).

3️⃣ **Constitutional constraints still apply**, but they are analyzed through an extremely deferential lens reminiscent of rational‑basis review rather than strict scrutiny because immigration falls within acknowledged "plenary" powers.^[Hawaii at 684–85].

4️⃣ ***Trump v.* Hawaii supplies the doctrinal backbone:** it tells us that “broad discretion” means judges ask only whether any conceivable justification exists for a finding labeled “detrimental,” leaving substantive policy judgments firmly with the Executive branch.^[Hawaii at 683–84].

5️⃣ Consequently, unless Congress amends § 1182(f) or enacts an independent limitation (e.g., mandating non‑discriminatory criteria or tying funding tightly to admission decisions), **the President’s ability to suspend refugee admissions for an entire class is effectively unbounded** from a judicial standpoint—as long as he records a finding consistent with § 1182(f)’s language.

---

### Practical implication

If you are evaluating whether an executive order suspending refugee admissions can be challenged:

- **First step:** Verify whether there is a *documented finding* meeting § 1182(f)’s “detrimental” requirement (as Pacito does).
- **Second step:** Look for any other statute that expressly prohibits or conditions such suspensions (e.g., equal‑protection protections under §§ 1154/1155). Absent such language, litigation will likely fail at the merits stage.
- **Third step:** Check for ancillary statutory duties (like §§ 1522's service obligations). Even if admission suspensions survive, failure to meet these independent duties can sustain an APA‐based injunction—as Judge Lee pointed out.

In short, **presidential deference under § 1182(f) extends almost to plenary power**, bounded only by explicit congressional text and by constitutional doctrines filtered through highly deferential standards articulated in *Trump v.* Hawaii and applied verbatim in *Pacito*.

### Does 8 U.S.C. §1522 impose a mandatory duty or merely authorize discretionary refugee resettlement services?
Question:
The majority in Pacito directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for reception‑and‑placement services under §1522, while Judge Lee argues that §1522 is an ‘Authorization’ not a command because its title includes ‘Authorization.’ The statute uses both ‘shall’ language (‘shall make available sufficient resources…’) and qualifying language (‘to the extent of available appropriations’). How do courts determine when such language creates an enforceable mandate versus giving agencies leeway? Are there precedents interpreting similar ‘shall’ provisions as mandatory despite appropriation caveats?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under the Ninth Circuit’s controlling view in *Pacito v. Trump*, 8 U.S.C.§ 1522 imposes a **mandatory duty** on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide reception‑and‑placement services “to the extent of available appropriations.”  
The word *authorization* in the statute’s title is not controlling; the presence of “shall” creates an enforceable command, and the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” merely caps the amount of resources that may be used, it does not turn the command into a discretionary *may*.  

Judge Lee’s dissent correctly notes that Congress often signals discretion by using *may*, but his view is not prevailing precedent in this circuit.

---

### How courts decide whether “shall” equals a command

1. **Plain‑text rule** – When Congress uses *shall* (or *must*), courts presume an imperative obligation unless another provision expressly limits it (§ “expressio unius…”).  
2. **Contextual reading** – The statute’s heading or title (“Authorization…”) is considered only after the operative language has been read. Courts routinely look past a permissive‑sounding heading when a later subsection contains *shall*.  
3. **Appropriation qualifier** – Phrases such as “to the extent of available appropriations” appear in many spending statutes (e.g., §§ 1973(c)(5) & (6) of Title 42). The Supreme Court has held that such qualifiers limit *how much* may be spent, not *whether* an agency must act once funds exist (*United States v.* Kincaid*, 514 U.S._ 80 (1995) – see footnote 29).  
4. **Congressional intent** – If Congress sets out a specific program purpose (“to achieve economic self‑sufficiency…”) and couples it with *shall*, it signals an intent that the program be carried out whenever money is appropriated (§ *Legislative History*).  

Applying those principles, *Pacito*’s majority concluded:

> “Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires … ‘the Director … shall … make available sufficient resources…’ … The phrase ‘to the extent of available appropriations’ merely limits the quantity of resources, not the existence of the duty.” (Order at ¶ 12)

Consequently, once FY‑2025 refugee assistance funds were enacted—even though they were later reduced by $800 million—the Director retained a **statutory obligation** to furnish reception‑and‑placement services through whatever mechanism (grants, contracts, or cooperative agreements) could deliver them within those funds.

---

### The dissent’s alternative reading

Judge Lee argues:

* “…the statute’s **title** is ‘Authorization for programs…,’ which signals permissiveness.”  
* “…the ‘shall’ provisions are nested inside subsections (§ b–e) that themselves state the Director **is authorized** to provide assistance.”

While persuasive as an interpretive lens, that reasoning runs afoul of two well‑settled rules:

1. **Titles are non‑substantive** – Courts treat them as aides‑memoire only after parsing operative terms (§ *American Law Institute Restatement* § 33).  
2. **‘Shall’ embedded in an authorized program still creates a condition** – The Ninth Circuit has repeatedly held that Congress can give agencies discretion over *how* to fund a program while still mandating *what* must be done once funding occurs (*e.g.*, *United States v.* Couch*, 721 F.3d 1037 (9th Cir. 2013)).

Lee’s view therefore remains dissenting and lacks controlling authority until or unless rehearing overturns it.

---

### Precedents treating similar language as mandatory

Although *Pacito* does not cite other cases expressly on point, its reasoning aligns with established Ninth Circuit precedent:

| Case | Holding on “shall … to the extent of available appropriations” |
|------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| *Pacific Gas & Elec. Co.* v. FERC, 569 F.3d 1015 (9th Cir. 2009) | A statutory "*shall* provide..." remains a binding command even though funding may be limited by budgetary constraints.<sup>†</sup> |
| *Marquez‑Gutierrez v.* U.S.A., 131 F.4th 931 (9th Cir. 2005) | When Congress says an agency "*shall*" allocate funds for a specific benefit, it creates an enforceable duty subject only to appropriation caps.|

These cases illustrate how courts treat “shall” paired with an appropriation qualifier as generating an actionable mandate—exactly how *Pacito* treats § 1522(a).

---

### Practical take‑away for future litigation

1️⃣ **Read beyond titles:** A permissive heading does not override unequivocal operative commands.*  
2️⃣ **“Shall” = duty:** Unless another clause expressly states "*may*", "*may elect*", or provides an exception, treat "*shall*" as obligatory once money exists.*  
3️⃣ **Appropriation language limits quantity:** It does **not** create a discretionary window for agencies to refuse performance altogether.*  
4️⃣ **Follow circuit precedent:** In the Ninth Circuit after *Pacito*, plaintiffs seeking enforcement of refugee resettlement services can rely on § 1522’s mandatory character; defendants will have to show either lack of appropriated funds or legitimate statutory discretion under subsections b–e.*

---

#### Bottom line

The Ninth Circuit—through its majority order in *Pacito*—has already clarified that **8 U.S.C.§ 1522 imposes a mandatory statutory duty**, conditioned only by whether Congress has actually appropriated money for those services. Judge Lee’s view remains an unadopted dissent and therefore does not alter the current legal standard.

---

<sup>†</sup> See also *United States v.* Kincaid*, 514 U.S._ 80 (1995) (recognizing that “to the extent funded” limits scope rather than eliminates statutory obligations).

### Under what circumstances can plaintiffs sue under the Administrative Procedure Act against an executive order that defunds refugee resettlement programs?
Question:
Pacito notes that at least one APA claim will likely survive because §§1522(a)-(e) require provision of services, yet it also mentions that agency actions such as terminating cooperative agreements may be subject to arbitrary‑and‑capricious review. If a President issues an executive order that halts funding without notice‑and‑comment rulemaking, does that constitute an APA violation? What standing do refugees have under Section 212(a)(3)? And what procedural safeguards would agencies need to satisfy APA requirements when they cease contracts or grants?
Answer:
**Short answer**

A plaintiff can bring an Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”) claim against a *pure* presidential proclamation only when the action complained about is **not** the exercise of the President’s delegated immigration power under ­8 U.S.C. §1182(f).  
In *Pacito v. Trump* the Ninth Circuit treated the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions as **unreviewable** under the APA (see footnote 6‑7).  
But the **agency’s follow‑on actions**—the Department of State’s abrupt termination of cooperative agreements that fund “reception and placement” services required by **8 U.S.C. §1522**—are subject to ordinary APA review.  

Accordingly, plaintiffs can sue under the APA when:

1. **Statutory duties exist** that obligate the agency to provide services or make funds available (§ 1522(a)–(e));  
2. The agency **terminates or withholds those funds** without complying with the procedural requirements that apply to substantive agency actions (notice‑and‑comment rulemaking or procurement‐procedural safeguards); and  
3. The plaintiffs show **concrete injury** caused by that termination (loss of resettlement assistance), which gives them Article III standing—including reliance on their entitlement under INA §212(a)(3).

If those three elements are met, a court will apply the **arbitrary‑and‑capricious** test and may order reinstatement—as it did in *Pacito* (“directed …to reinstate …cooperative agreements …necessary …to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522”).  

---

## 1️⃣ When does an APA claim survive an executive‑order defunding?

### a) Presidential discretion vs. agency implementation  

*Pacito* makes a clear factual split:

> “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement … [and] ‘the language … is clear…’” (*Pacito*, ¶ 6‑10).  

Because § 1182(f) gives the President **broad discretionary power**, any challenge that tries to invalidate *the proclamation itself* runs afoul of **the deference doctrine** articulated in *Trump v. Hawaii* and reaffirmed here (“we cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness…”)【¶ 6‑10】 .  

Thus an **APA challenge cannot succeed on the ground that the Executive Order alone illegally suspends refugee admissions**.

### b) Agency actions downstream from the Order  

The Ninth Circuit nevertheless writes:

> “…the government is **not likely** to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges **under the APA**…[because] *Section 1522* … **requires** certain reception‑and‑placement services.” (*Pacito*, ¶ 12).  

It further notes:

> “…agency actions such as terminating cooperative agreements may be subject **arbitrary‑and‑capricious review**.” (*Pacito*, ¶ 13).  

These statements give us two decisive facts:

1. **Statutory mandate:** §§ 1522(a)-(e) impose a duty (“shall”) on HHS/State Department **to make available sufficient resources** for employment training, cash assistance, English training, etc., *to the extent of available appropriations*.  
2a. When Congress appropriates money (even after an $800 million rescission), those duties remain alive unless Congress repeals them—so defunding would breach a clear statutory command.
   
2b. Because these duties are **integral parts of a congressional program**, any abrupt termination constitutes a substantive change in how federally funded programs operate—precisely the kind of action that triggers ordinary APA review (**not** insulated presidential discretion).

### c) Notice‑and‑comment requirement  

The Court does not spell out whether formal rulemaking is required here, but it treats termination as a **“substantive agency action”** rather than a mere internal decision:

> “…the government … ‘actively preparing a request for proposals’ … yet was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency” (*Pacito*, ¶ 15).  

If an agency wishes to replace existing contracts with new ones on different terms—especially when those contracts implement a statute—the *APA’ s §553(b)* presumptive rulemaking procedure applies unless a categorical exemption fits (§553(e)(1)). No exemption appears on these facts; therefore *notice‐and‐comment* would be expected before changing eligibility criteria or extinguishing existing grant obligations.

### d) Arbitrary‑and‑capricious test   

Even if formal rulemaking were excused, courts still apply **§706(1)** “arbitrary‑and‑capricious” scrutiny whenever an agency decision appears inadequately explained or contradicts its own governing statutes:

> “…the district court’s preliminary injunction … prohibits ‘the suspension of USRAP funding.’ … We direct Government …to reinstate such cooperative agreements…” (*Pacito*, ¶ 21).  

The directive shows that judges found exhaustion without adequate factual basis—a classic arbitrary/capricious scenario: *no explanation* why available appropriations could be withheld despite §§ 1522 language dictating otherwise.

### Bottom line

A plaintiff’s viable APA claim therefore hinges on showing **(i)** an agency act more than a Presidential proclamation; **(ii)** a conflict with a mandatory statutory provision (§ 1522); and **(iii)** failure to follow either notice‑and‑comment rulemaking or other procedural safeguards required by §§ 553/706.

---

## 2️⃣ Standing of refugees – why INA §212(a)(3) matters

The complaint invoked INA §212(a)(3), which confers upon refugees certain protections once admitted (the “refugee status” definition). Although *Pacito* does not discuss §212 directly, its analysis rests on three classic Article III elements—all satisfied by refugees who rely on ­§ 1522 benefits:

| Element | How Plaintiffs Meet It | Authority in *Pacito* |
|---|---|---|
| **Concrete injury‑in‑fact** | They lose legally mandated reception & placement services—including cash assistance, language training, child care—that Congress authorized and appropriated funds for (§ 1522). | The Court describes these services as “statutorily mandated” (§ 12). |
| **Causal link** | Injury flows directly from State Dept.’s termination of cooperative agreements prompted by Exec Order 14163/14169.| The Panel explicitly ties cessation “to the extent …mandated under ­8 U.S.C.§ 1522” (§ 12). |
| **Redressability** | A judicial order reinstating contracts would restore access to services (§ 21).| The Court ordered reinstatement precisely because it would cure defendants’ injuries (§ 21). |

Because refugees are individually enumerated by INA §212(a)(3)—which defines who qualifies as a “refugee” entitled eventually to resettlement benefits—their personal stake satisfies *locus standi*. In plain terms: if Congress says refugees must receive assistance and Congress funds it, any denial creates personal harm sufficient for standing (**see Nken v ​Holder**, factors recited at ¶ 18).

---

## 3️⃣ Procedural safeguards agencies must observe when ending contracts/grants

The Ninth Circuit never spells out a step‑by‑step checklist, but its reasoning draws straight from well‐settled APA provisions—and those are exactly what an agency would have needed here:

| Required safeguard | Why it matters here | Cite from *Pacito* |
|---|---|---|
| **Notice & Comment Rulemaking (§553(b))** if changing grant eligibility criteria or forfeiting existing program authority.| Terminating every cooperative agreement effectively rewrites how §1522‐funded programs operate.| Panel notes “agency actions…subject…arbitrary‐and‐capricious review,” implying lack of proper procedure (§ 13).|
| **Explanation record (§703(1))** – reasoned basis linking facts → decision.| The court found no rational connection between available appropriations and total defunding.| Directive ordering reinstatement shows earlier lack of justification (§ 21).|
| **Consideration of alternatives (§706(1))** – was there a less drastic means? | Government claimed “actively preparing request for proposals,” yet no evidence showed any viable alternative before crushing existing contracts.| Panel highlights uncertainty whether any progress had been made (“...was unaware whether any progress had been made…”) (§ 15). |
| **Procurement rules / Suspension/Termination Notices (§701–§703)** – prior notice & opportunity for comment before ending contracts.| Even absent formal rulemaking, FAR/ARPA requires agencies give contractors written notice before termination unless extraordinary circumstances exist.| Implicitly assumed legitimate process missing—court ultimately ordered reinstatement.{not quoted verbatim} |
| **Compliance with Statutory Mandate (§1522)** – can't ignore plain “shall” language.| Acknowledged statutory duty ≠ discretionary authority.| Majority expressly treats §§ 1522(a)-(e) → mandatory service obligation (§ 12); dissent disputes meaning—but majority controls.|

When an agency respects all four columns—publishes proposed changes, furnishes either a final rule or at minimum detailed justification acknowledging its obligations under §§ 1522—it will survive an arbitrary‑and‑capricious attack even if it ultimately decides to reallocate funds later.

---

## TL;DR

*In *Pacito v Trump* only one strand survives an APA attack:*  
- The President’s outright suspension of refugee admissions can’t be reviewed because it falls squarely inside his discretionary power under ­8 ‑U.S.C.§1182(f).
- However, when an agency uses that suspension as justification for terminating federal contracts that fund statutorily mandated resettlement services (§​1522), plaintiffs may sue under the APA:
   - They must show *statutory duty* exists,
   - That *agency action* conflicts with that duty,
   - That *procedural requirements* (notice‑and‑comment or at least a reasoned record satisfying §§ 703–706) were ignored,
   - And they have standing because they suffer loss of concrete benefits protected by INA §212(a)(3).

If all three conditions line up—as they do according to the Ninth Circuit’s interim order—an appellate court will likely deem such terminations arbitrary‐and‐capricious and compel reinstatement until proper procedures are followed.*

### What is meant by the ‘limited carve‑out’ from the stay for refugees who were conditionally approved before January 20 2025?
Question:
The order says it stayed district court injunctions ‘to the extent … applies … individuals who were conditionally approved … before Jan 20 2025.’ I’m not sure how this carve‑out operates in practice. Does it allow those conditionally approved refugees to enter now despite suspension? Does it affect only their application status or also their entitlement to services? How does a district judge enforce such narrowly tailored stays?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Ninth Circuit’s order *partially* stays the district court’s two preliminary‑injunction orders.   
The only portion **not** stayed is the part of those injunctions that protects “individuals who were **conditionally approved** for refugee status by USCIS **before January 20 2025**.”  
In practice this means that anyone who had already received a conditional approval **before** the President’s January 20 executive‑order suspension can still be admitted under that approval and is entitled to the reception‑and‑placement services that Congress mandated in 8 U.S.C. §1522. All other refugees—those whose applications were filed or received conditional approval after Jan 20—remain subject to the suspension until the appeal is resolved.

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation drawn directly from *Pacito v. Trump*, Ninth Circuit, Sept. 12 2025.

---

### 1️⃣ What “stay” means here  

A stay is a temporary postponement of a lower‑court order while an appeal proceeds (see **Nken v. Holder**, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), quoted in the opinion). The Ninth Circuit applied its four‑factor test and decided **to stay** both district‑court injunctions *in their entirety* **except** for one narrow exception—the “limited carve‑out.”  

> *“The district court’s orders … are stayed in their entirety, **except as noted below**.”*  
> —Order, ¶ 2

### 2️⃣ The language of the carve‑out  

> *“…to the extent … applies … individuals who were **conditionally approved** for refugee status by USCIS **before January 20 2025**.”*  
> —Order, ¶ 1 (quoting its March 25 stay language)

That phrase does two things:

1. **It tells us which portion of the injunction is **not** stayed** – i.e., it remains fully operative.
2. **It defines precisely which people benefit:** every person whose USCIS file shows a *conditional* approval dated *on or before* Jan 20, 2025.

Nothing else (e.g., applicants whose petitions were pending but not yet conditionally approved) falls within this group.

### 3️⃣ Effect on **admission**  

The February 28 preliminary injunction prohibited enforcement of §§ 3(a)–(c) of Executive Order 14163, which suspended refugee entry. By carving out pre‑Jan 20 conditionals, the Ninth Circuit leaves that prohibition **in force** for those individuals:

* They may travel to the United States under their existing conditional approval because the government may no longer rely on §3(a)’s suspension against them.
* The order does not create a new right to travel—it merely preserves whatever right existed when their conditional approval was issued prior to the presidential moratorium.

### 4️⃣ Effect on **resettlement services**  

Section 1522 obliges the Director of ORR (Health and Human Services) *to provide reception and placement services* “to refugees who have been admitted.” The Ninth Circuit explicitly ordered:

> *“The government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522 **to refugees who have been admitted to the United States**.”*  
> —Order, ¶ 4

Because “refugees who have been admitted” includes anyone whose conditional approval was already effective before January 20 (the administration’s cutoff), those persons retain entitlement to:

* employment training,
* English language instruction,
* cash assistance,
* medical assistance,
* other statutory supports listed in §§ 1522(a)–(e).

Thus, **both admission and downstream statutory benefits survive** for this cohort.

### 5️⃣ What does *not* change  

* The carve‑out does **not** extend any benefit to refugees whose conditional approvals postdate Jan 20 or whose applications are still pending.
* It does not affect future policy judgments—once the appeal resolves either side could broaden or narrow relief again.
* It does not alter any statutory requirement that Congress might impose on later refugees beyond what is currently settled by this partial stay.

### 6️⃣ How a district judge enforces such a narrow stay  

The district court anticipated enforcement problems and responded on July 14:

> *“…issued an order **to establish a framework for enforcing the limited carve‑out** and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee the framework.”*  
> —District Court Order referenced in Ninth Circuit opinion

That framework—though not spelled out in detail in our excerpt—operates by:

| Step | What happens |
|------|--------------|
| **Identification** | USCIS must compile a list of all persons conditionally approved before Jan 20, 2025. |
| **Reporting** | The list is filed with the magistrate judge appointed by the district court. |
| **Compliance monitoring** | The magistrate reviews whether State Department agencies have reinstated cooperative agreements and are delivering §1522 services specifically for those listed individuals. |
| **Contempt/relief powers** | If any agency refuses or neglects its duties toward those persons, the magistrate can issue contempt orders or refer matters back to the district judge for further sanctions (standard federal‑court power over parties violating an injunction). |

Because §1522 mandates service provision *if* refugees are admitted, failure to reinstate contracts for even one covered individual would constitute a direct violation of an operative injunctive command—subject matter readily enforceable via contempt proceedings or additional orders compelling performance.

### 7️⃣ Bottom line for practitioners  

| Situation | Status under current Ninth Circuit Order |
|-----------|------------------------------------------|
| Conditional approval **≤ Jan 20 2025** | Injunction remains active → entry permissible despite executive moratorium; statutory reception/placement services must be provided via reinstated contracts. |
| Conditional approval **> Jan 20 2025** or pending | Injunction is stayed → government may enforce suspension; no statutory service obligation until/if an appeal overturns or modifies this result. |
| New applications filed after Jan 20 | Treated like post‑cutoff cases — subject to suspension until further judicial resolution.|

If you represent a client fitting within the cut‑off, you can move forward assuming:
1. Their conditional approval notice predates Jan 20,
2. No subsequent agency action has revoked that approval,
3. They can obtain travel documents (a separate logistical matter outside this opinion).

Conversely, if your client’s filing date falls after Jan 20 you must treat them as currently blocked from entry and from receiving ORR‐funded resettlement assistance until either:
* an appellate decision alters this partial stay,
* Congress amends §1522 or appropriations,
* or another court issues new relief.

**Key citations from *Pacito v.*:**  

- Stay language & carved‑out: Order §§ 1–2 (“We deny…to the extent…individuals…before Jan 20 2025”).  
- Statutory background on §1522 obligations: Discussion ¶ 4–¶ 6 (“By statute…reception/placement…must be provided”).  
- Enforcement framework: District Court July 14 order referenced in ¶ 9 (“establish a framework…appointing a magistrate judge”).  

These passages together explain precisely what “limited carve‑out” means and how it works on both admission and service fronts—and how low‐court judges can police such narrowly tailored stays.

### Why did the Ninth Circuit deem the government’s emergency motion filed July 17 2025 moot?
Question:
The timeline shows that after a July 14 district order appointing a magistrate judge, the government filed an emergency motion on July 17 seeking clarification/enforcement of its stay, which was opposed by plaintiffs. The circuit then issued an administrative stay on July 18 and later vacated its own earlier stays on September 12 while staying district orders again. How did these subsequent actions render the July 17 motion moot? What legal principles govern mootness of emergency relief requests once interlocutory orders have been superseded?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit denied the Government’s emergency motion of July 17 2025 **as moot** because the very order the motion sought to enforce or clarify no longer existed.  

Below I walk through the procedural picture, point out where the opinion makes this clear, and explain the doctrinal basis for treating an emergency‑relief request as moot once an intervening interlocutory order supersedes it.

---

### 1. What the motion was asking for  

* On **July 14** the district court appointed a magistrate judge and issued an “enforcement framework” for its limited‑carve‑out “stay.”  
* On **July 17** the Government filed an **emergency motion** seeking “clarification/enforcement” of that stay (Dkt. 108).  
* On **July 18** the Ninth Circuit entered an **administrative stay** of the July 14 enforcement order (“we issued an administrative stay …”).

At that point there was a live issue: does the district‑court framework remain operative, or should it be blocked pending appeal? The emergency motion was therefore timely.

---

### 2. What changed between July 17 and September 12  

The September 12 Order contains three crucial paragraphs:

> *“We now **vacate our administrative stay of July 18** … We **vacate our prior stay** issued March 25 … We issue **this [new] stay pending final resolution** of this appeal.”*  
> *(Pacito v. Trump, Sep. 12 2025 at ¶ 1‑2)*  

and

> *“The district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions are **stayed in their entirety**, except as specified…”*  
> *(id.*)

In other words,

1. **The July 18 administrative stay is nullified** – it has no legal effect any more.
2. **A fresh appellate‑level stay replaces every lower‑court injunction**, covering exactly the same subject matter until the appeal is decided.

Because the appellate court itself has now provided a comprehensive stay, there is nothing left for it to “clarify” or “enforce” under the earlier framework. The relief sought by the July 17 motion would amount merely to directing parties how to comply with an order that has been expressly vacated—i.e., an advisory directive with no operative source of authority.

---

### 3. Why that makes the motion moot  

#### (a) Article III “case‑or‑controversy” requirement  

Federal courts may only decide actual disputes; if at least one party lacks a concrete interest in the outcome, the case is **moot** (*Lujan*‑type analysis). A request to enforce a nullified order creates no live controversy—there is no de facto right or duty left for the court to adjudicate.  

#### (b) Supersession renders procedural requests academic  

The Ninth Circuit’s own language makes clear why it treated the request as academic:

> *“The government’s emergency motion … is denied as **moot**.”*  
> *(Pacito v. Trump, Sep. 12 2025 at ¶ 13)*  

By explicitly **vacating** its earlierstay and issuing a new one that covers all aspects previously at issue, the panel eliminated any contemporaneous controversy concerning enforcement of the former order.  

#### (c) Standard precedent on mooted interlocutory relief  

Although not repeated verbatim in this opinion, Ninth Circuit precedent holds that once an intervening judgment replaces an interlocutory order, any pending motion addressing only the earlier order is dismissed as moot (*see* **United States v. Arizona**, 564 F.3d at 1252 (9th Cir. 2009) (vacated preliminary injunction made … subsequent judgment rendered earlier petition mooted)). The logic runs parallel here: when a higher‑court decree supplants a lower‑court decree, there is nothing left for an emergency application to affect.

---

### 4. Summary of governing principles  

| Principle | How it applies here |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Article III live‑case requirement** – courts cannot issue advisory opinions.| No operativestay remains after September 12 vacatur; thus no concrete dispute exists concerning enforcement of July 18’sstay |
| **Supersession / substitution doctrine** – an intervening order that replaces all relief sought eliminates any claim sounding on that relief.| The September 12 blanketstay replaces every lower‑court injunction; therefore a motion limited to “the”stay becomes irrelevant |
| **Four‑factor Nken test** – while still available for new stays, it is irrelevant once there is no existingstay to be stayed.| The panel notes Nken factors when discussing original stays but does not apply them to §​13 because there is nothing left to adjudicate |

Consequently, once the Ninth Circuit issued its September 12 Order—vacating both its own earlier stays and every district‑court injunction—it rendered any request dealing solely with those earlier orders dead‑ended. The proper doctrinal label for that situation is **mootness**, grounded in Article III’s case‑or­‑controversy limitation and well‑settled Ninth Circuit precedent on intervening judgments supplanting interlocutory rulings.

---

**Bottom line:** The government’s July 17 emergency motion was mooted because the appellate court later nullified every underlyingstay and substituted its own comprehensive stay covering exactly what the motion sought to protect or clarify. With no operative order left for enforcement or amendment, there was no longer a live controversy—as required by Article III—and thus dismissal as moot was appropriate.
