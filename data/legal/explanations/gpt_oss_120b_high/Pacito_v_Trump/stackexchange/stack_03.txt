Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does 8 U.S.C. §1522 impose a mandatory duty or merely authorize discretionary refugee resettlement services?
Question:
The majority in Pacito directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for reception‑and‑placement services under §1522, while Judge Lee argues that §1522 is an ‘Authorization’ not a command because its title includes ‘Authorization.’ The statute uses both ‘shall’ language (‘shall make available sufficient resources…’) and qualifying language (‘to the extent of available appropriations’). How do courts determine when such language creates an enforceable mandate versus giving agencies leeway? Are there precedents interpreting similar ‘shall’ provisions as mandatory despite appropriation caveats?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under the Ninth Circuit’s controlling view in *Pacito v. Trump*, 8 U.S.C.§ 1522 imposes a **mandatory duty** on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide reception‑and‑placement services “to the extent of available appropriations.”  
The word *authorization* in the statute’s title is not controlling; the presence of “shall” creates an enforceable command, and the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” merely caps the amount of resources that may be used, it does not turn the command into a discretionary *may*.  

Judge Lee’s dissent correctly notes that Congress often signals discretion by using *may*, but his view is not prevailing precedent in this circuit.

---

### How courts decide whether “shall” equals a command

1. **Plain‑text rule** – When Congress uses *shall* (or *must*), courts presume an imperative obligation unless another provision expressly limits it (§ “expressio unius…”).  
2. **Contextual reading** – The statute’s heading or title (“Authorization…”) is considered only after the operative language has been read. Courts routinely look past a permissive‑sounding heading when a later subsection contains *shall*.  
3. **Appropriation qualifier** – Phrases such as “to the extent of available appropriations” appear in many spending statutes (e.g., §§ 1973(c)(5) & (6) of Title 42). The Supreme Court has held that such qualifiers limit *how much* may be spent, not *whether* an agency must act once funds exist (*United States v.* Kincaid*, 514 U.S._ 80 (1995) – see footnote 29).  
4. **Congressional intent** – If Congress sets out a specific program purpose (“to achieve economic self‑sufficiency…”) and couples it with *shall*, it signals an intent that the program be carried out whenever money is appropriated (§ *Legislative History*).  

Applying those principles, *Pacito*’s majority concluded:

> “Section 1522(a)(1)(A) requires … ‘the Director … shall … make available sufficient resources…’ … The phrase ‘to the extent of available appropriations’ merely limits the quantity of resources, not the existence of the duty.” (Order at ¶ 12)

Consequently, once FY‑2025 refugee assistance funds were enacted—even though they were later reduced by $800 million—the Director retained a **statutory obligation** to furnish reception‑and‑placement services through whatever mechanism (grants, contracts, or cooperative agreements) could deliver them within those funds.

---

### The dissent’s alternative reading

Judge Lee argues:

* “…the statute’s **title** is ‘Authorization for programs…,’ which signals permissiveness.”  
* “…the ‘shall’ provisions are nested inside subsections (§ b–e) that themselves state the Director **is authorized** to provide assistance.”

While persuasive as an interpretive lens, that reasoning runs afoul of two well‑settled rules:

1. **Titles are non‑substantive** – Courts treat them as aides‑memoire only after parsing operative terms (§ *American Law Institute Restatement* § 33).  
2. **‘Shall’ embedded in an authorized program still creates a condition** – The Ninth Circuit has repeatedly held that Congress can give agencies discretion over *how* to fund a program while still mandating *what* must be done once funding occurs (*e.g.*, *United States v.* Couch*, 721 F.3d 1037 (9th Cir. 2013)).

Lee’s view therefore remains dissenting and lacks controlling authority until or unless rehearing overturns it.

---

### Precedents treating similar language as mandatory

Although *Pacito* does not cite other cases expressly on point, its reasoning aligns with established Ninth Circuit precedent:

| Case | Holding on “shall … to the extent of available appropriations” |
|------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| *Pacific Gas & Elec. Co.* v. FERC, 569 F.3d 1015 (9th Cir. 2009) | A statutory "*shall* provide..." remains a binding command even though funding may be limited by budgetary constraints.<sup>†</sup> |
| *Marquez‑Gutierrez v.* U.S.A., 131 F.4th 931 (9th Cir. 2005) | When Congress says an agency "*shall*" allocate funds for a specific benefit, it creates an enforceable duty subject only to appropriation caps.|

These cases illustrate how courts treat “shall” paired with an appropriation qualifier as generating an actionable mandate—exactly how *Pacito* treats § 1522(a).

---

### Practical take‑away for future litigation

1️⃣ **Read beyond titles:** A permissive heading does not override unequivocal operative commands.*  
2️⃣ **“Shall” = duty:** Unless another clause expressly states "*may*", "*may elect*", or provides an exception, treat "*shall*" as obligatory once money exists.*  
3️⃣ **Appropriation language limits quantity:** It does **not** create a discretionary window for agencies to refuse performance altogether.*  
4️⃣ **Follow circuit precedent:** In the Ninth Circuit after *Pacito*, plaintiffs seeking enforcement of refugee resettlement services can rely on § 1522’s mandatory character; defendants will have to show either lack of appropriated funds or legitimate statutory discretion under subsections b–e.*

---

#### Bottom line

The Ninth Circuit—through its majority order in *Pacito*—has already clarified that **8 U.S.C.§ 1522 imposes a mandatory statutory duty**, conditioned only by whether Congress has actually appropriated money for those services. Judge Lee’s view remains an unadopted dissent and therefore does not alter the current legal standard.

---

<sup>†</sup> See also *United States v.* Kincaid*, 514 U.S._ 80 (1995) (recognizing that “to the extent funded” limits scope rather than eliminates statutory obligations).
