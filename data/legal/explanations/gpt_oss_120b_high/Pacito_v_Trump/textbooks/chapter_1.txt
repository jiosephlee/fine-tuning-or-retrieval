Chapter 1: # Chapter 1 – Fact Pattern and Procedural History  

The dispute that reached the Ninth Circuit arose from two presidential directives issued early in 2025 that abruptly altered the United States’ refugee‑admissions regime.  Plaintiffs—a group of refugees who had either already been admitted or whose applications had been approved, together with three nonprofit organizations that contracted with the State Department to provide overseas processing and domestic resettlement services—asserted that the directives violated both the Refugee Act of 1980 and procedural requirements imposed by the Administrative Procedure Act.  The litigation quickly produced a series of district‑court injunctions, motions for stays, and appellate interventions that illustrate how an immigration‑policy case travels through the federal courts.  The following sections walk through each pivotal event while highlighting the procedural mechanisms that law students must recognize when reading an appellate opinion.


## Executive Order No. 14163 (“Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program”) – Suspension of Refugee Entry  

On **January 20, 2025**, President Trump signed Executive Order No. 14163, entitled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” The order invoked **8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f)** and **1185(a)**—the statutory grant that permits the President “to suspend the entry … of any class of aliens … whenever… [the] entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” In § 3(a) of the order the President declared that “entry … of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program would be detrimental” and directed an immediate **suspension** of refugee admissions pending further findings.  

Because § 1182(f) contains no temporal limitation on presidential discretion, executive‑branch attorneys argued that any judicial review should be highly deferential. The government would later rely on **Trump v. Hawaii**, which emphasized “the authority… ‘exudes deference’” when evaluating challenges under this provision. By contrast, plaintiffs contended that Congress had encoded a “general rule” governing refugee admissions in the **Refugee Act**, citing **Negusie v. Holder**, and therefore argued that unilateral suspension exceeded statutory boundaries.


## Executive Order No. 14169 (“Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid”) – Termination of USRAP Funding  

A separate directive issued **January 20, 2025**—Executive Order No. 14169 (“Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid”)—was employed by the Department of State on **January 24** to suspend **all funding** associated with the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). While EO 14163 dealt with entry restrictions, EO 14169 targeted financial support by withdrawing appropriated funds used both overseas during refugee vetting and domestically for reception‑and‑placement services administered after admission.  

Plaintiffs framed this defunding claim as an **Administrative Procedure Act** violation because Congress had authorized refugee‑resettlement assistance through specific statutory programs (§ 1522). They argued that eliminating funding without notice or reasonign contravened required rulemaking procedures.


## Plaintiffs: Admitted Refugees and Three NGOs with Cooperative Agreements  

The plaintiff class consisted of two distinct groups:

1. **Refugees already admitted** or whose applications had been conditionally approved prior to January 20, 2025, thereby possessing a concrete interest in being allowed entry without interruption.
2. **Three nonprofit organizations**—each maintaining a cooperative agreement with the State Department—to conduct overseas processing or deliver domestic reception‑and‑placement services once refugees arrived in the United States.

These NGOs claimed standing based on contractual rights threatened by termination of funding and alleged injury tied directly to their ability to perform statutorily mandated services under **8 U.S.C.§ 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)** (“public or private nonprofit agencies”). Their participation clarified why plaintiffs sought both an adjudication over immigration policy (the suspension itself) and over administrative compliance (the removal of funding).


## District Court Preliminary Injunctions (February 25 Oral Order; February 28 Written Order)  

After filing suit in **the Western District of Washington** in early February 2025, plaintiffs moved for immediate equitable relief so that they could retain their admission status and preserve funded resettlement programs while litigation proceeded.  

On **February 25**, District Judge ___ orally entered a **preliminary injunction** prohibiting enforcement of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of EO 14163. An oral injunction operates like any interlocutory injunction—it immediately restrains conduct even though formally recorded later—but its brevity means parties typically await a written version for clarity regarding scope.[^1]  

In deciding whether to grant such provisional relief, courts apply a four‑part test derived from **Nken v.\ Holder**, requiring proof that (1) plaintiff is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) irreparable injury will occur absent relief; (3) issuance will not cause excessive harm to defendants; and (4) public interest favors issuance.[^2] The district court found both prism elements satisfied: without an injunction refugees would be barred from entry despite prior approval—a classic irreparable injury—and there was no plausible showing that permitting temporary admissions would substantially harm governmental objectives at that stage.

Two days later—in response to additional litigation concerning fund deferrals—the court issued a **written order** on **February 28** clarifying its earlier pronouncement. That written decree broadened protection by also enjoining any suspension of USRAP funding under EO 14163’s § 3(b). By doing so it linked Stewartian concerns over immigration entry with administrative concerns over appropriated resources.^[3] The written form ensured litigants could rely upon concrete language when preparing subsequent appeals or compliance measures.


## Government’s Notices Of Appeal (February 28 And March 25) And Motions For Stays  

Following any interlocutory decision, parties may file a **notice of appeal**, which severs reviewability from lower‑court judgment while preserving jurisdiction over collateral matters.^[4] The government lodged its first notice on **February 28**, appealing the February‑25 oral injunction against entry restrictions deeming itself likely successful on meritorious grounds under § 1182(f). A second notice arrived on **March 25**, targeting both the expanded February‑28 funding prohibition and a separate preliminary injunction dated **March 24** requiring reinstatement of previously terminated cooperative agreements.^[5]  

Because an appeal automatically imposes a “stay‑pending‑appeal” rule only when circumstances justify it—a principle codified indirectly in Rule 4(b) Governing Federal Appellate Procedure—the government submitted multiple motions asking this circuit panel either de‑novo or collegially convened “stay” orders while its appeals were pending.^[6]  

The panel evaluated these requests using precisely those four factors articulated in **Nken**, labelling success‐on‐the‐merits prospects concerning immigration authority “likely” given broad presidential discretion demonstrated in previous Supreme Court holdings.^[7] Accordingly it granted a **partial stay** on **March 25**, carving out an exception allowing individuals whose refugee status had been conditionally approved before Jan.\ 20\@​\*30​\*​\*​\*​\*​\*to continue proceeding toward admission—a narrow accommodation designed both to protect alleged irreparable injury claims while preserving deference toward presidential power over immigration policy.^[8] Subsequent clarification orders dated **April 21** and **May 9** refined what officials labeled “the limited carveout,” spelling out which categories remained subject to enforcement.\


## Interim District‑Court Actions – Termination Of Agreements (February 26), July 14 Enforcement Framework  

Even as appellate litigation unfolded, executive actors continued implementing policy adjustments independently from judicial constraints—a point underscored by events occurring after initial injunctive relief aired:

*On February 26*, shortly after receiving plaintiff claims yet before fully complying with server rulings, Secretary ___ ordered termination of ***all*** cooperative agreements furnishing reception‑and‑placement services domestically—and all-but‑one agreement supporting overseas processing functions.^[9] This maneuver effectively nullified contractual rights sought by petitioning NGOs even though it risked violating §\§1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)’s authorization scheme requiring agencies provide such support through contracts or grants.^[10]

In response promptly after reviewing subsequent pleadings concerning dramatic service disruption,the District Court entered another preliminary injunction on ***March 24*** obligating reinstatement of those cooperative arrangements.^[11]
