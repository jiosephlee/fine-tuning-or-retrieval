Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2 – Statutory Authority Underpinning the Dispute  

The appellate panel’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* pivots on two intersecting statutory regimes.  
First, the President relied on **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)**—the immigration‑entry suspension provision—to halt refugee admissions under Executive Order 14163.  
Second, Congress has embedded a parallel duty to provide “reception and placement” services to refugees once they enter the United States via **8 U.S.C. § 1522** and the broader **Refugee Act of 1980**.  

Plaintiffs argue that the executive order breaches both the Refugee Act’s statutory scheme and procedural requirements imposed by the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”). The government contends that the President’s authority under § 1182(f) is plenary and that any APA challenge fails because the executive action falls squarely within a zone traditionally accorded “extreme deference.”  

Understanding how the Ninth Circuit applied these statutes requires a close look at six doctrinal building blocks: presidential power under § 1182(f), Supreme Court precedent on immigration deference, the substantive content of the Refugee Act, the APA’s arbitrary‑and‑capricious test, the textual architecture of §§ 1522(a)–(e), and how “shall” language is distinguished from permissive “may/authorizes” language—especially against the backdrop of FY 2025 appropriations. The following sections break down each component and show how it informs the court’s reasoning.

---  

## 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) – Presidential Authority to Suspend Entry When a “Detrimental” Finding Is Made  

Section 1182(f) lies in Chapter 12 of the Immigration and Nationality Act (“INA”) and provides that *“whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or any class of aliens … would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may … suspend … entry … for such period as he shall deem necessary.”* The statute therefore supplies two distinct prerequisites for a lawful suspension:

1. **A presidential finding**—the President must articulate that entry would be “detrimental” to U.S. interests;  
2. **A formal exercise of authority**—the President may suspend entry “for such period … as he shall deem necessary,” without further congressional limitation.

In *Pacito*, Executive Order 14163 invoked this provision expressly, declaring that “entry… would be detrimental” and ordering an immediate suspension of refugee admissions under USRAP. The government’s appeal rested on a straightforward premise: because §§ 1182(f) grants unqualified discretion once a finding is made, any district‑court injunction barring enforcement exceeds judicial competence.

The Ninth Circuit’s order reflects this view when it notes that *“the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.”* Because Congress gave no additional substantive limitation—such as a requirement that “detrimental” be demonstrated by a particular evidentiary standard—the court treats the Presidential finding as legally sufficient absent an independent factual inquiry into its persuasiveness.

## Supreme Court Precedent on Immigration Deference (Trump v. Hawaii)  

While *Nken* supplies a four‑factor stay analysis, appellate courts rely heavily on **Trump v. Hawaii**, 138 S.Ct. 2392 (2018), for assessing judicial review of executive immigration restrictions. In *Hawaii*, Chief Justice Roberts articulated a *“high level of deference”* owed to congressional delegations granting broad discretionary power over entry into the United States—particularly when national‑security concerns are implicated. The decision emphasized two points relevant to *Pacito*:

* **Statutory Deference** – Where Congress uses terms like “may” or provides a broad grant-of-power clause (as in § 1182(f)), courts will not second‑guess policy judgments rooted in national security unless there is clear evidence that Congress’s intent was exceeded.
* **Presidential Prerogative** – Even when an executive order cites political considerations rather than strictly security concerns, *Hawaii* held that courts may not engage in a “searching inquiry” into whether those considerations are sufficient under the statute.

The Ninth Circuit directly quoted *Hawaii* while refusing a stay pending appeal: it declined “to engage in…a searching inquiry into…the President’s justifications.” By anchoring its reasoning in *Trump v.* Hawaii, the panel signaled that any challenge based solely on alleged disproportionality or policy disagreement faces an almost insurmountable hurdle; success must instead arise from demonstrating an ultra vires act—i.e., an exercise beyond what § 1182(f) allows.

## The Refugee Act of 1980 & Related INA Provisions (§ 1101 et seq.)  

The **Refugee Act** codifies U.S. commitments toward persons qualifying as refugees under United Nations definitions and creates a systematic admission program—USRAP—implemented through USCIS adjudication and Department‑of‑State resettlement mechanisms. Key features include:

* **Statutory Definition** – A refugee is “any person who is outside his or her country … owing to persecution ...”.  
* **Admission Framework** – Section 1155(b) authorizes limited annual caps; Section 1159 outlines priority categories such as religious minorities or victims of torture; Section 1171(d)(4)(B)(ii) requires “reasonable opportunity” for refugees already admitted to receive resettlement assistance.
* **Congressional Intent** – In *Negusie v.* Holder (2009), the Supreme Court described the Refugee Act as establishing “a general rule … for ongoing treatment”—indicating Congress intended continuity even amid fluctuating foreign‑policy concerns.

Plaintiffs in *Pacito* invoked these provisions contending that an executive suspension sidesteps Congressional design by halting both admission eligibility (§​§3(a)-(d) above) and associated funding for services mandated elsewhere in law (see below). Their argument rests upon two premises:

1. **Statutory Consistency** – The Refugee Act implies an ongoing duty to admit refugees who meet defined criteria unless Congress itself amends caps or eligibility standards.
2. **Protection against Arbitrary Suspension** – Even if a president may suspend entry under §​1182(f), such suspension must not nullify statutory rights already vested under separate enactments like §§​1155–1159 nor impair congressional intent embodied in the Refugee Act’s comprehensive scheme.

## Administrative Procedure Act Challenges – Arbitrary‑and‑Capricious Standard  

When agencies terminate or modify existing programs, litigants invoke **APA §§​701–706**, which require agencies either (a) follow formal rulemaking procedures—including notice-and-comment when required—or (b) provide reasoned explanations demonstrating reliance on substantial evidence if acting informally through policy statements or contracts.

The Ninth Circuit applies **the arbitrary‑and‑capricious test** articulated in *Motor Vehicle Mfrs., Inc.* v.​ State Farm Mutua­l Auto Ins., Inc., 463 U.S. 29 (1983). An agency action is invalid if it:

1. Lacks a rational connection between factual findings and chosen policy,
2. Fails to consider important aspects of a problem,
3

or

4

     Disregards relevant statutory criteria or precedents without explanation—

as articulated at ***[285]*** FCC ***[574]*** (**see also** *Klein v.* United States Dept.~of Education).

In *Pacito*, plaintiffs asserted that terminating cooperative agreements with resettlement NGOs—an act carried out pursuant to Executive Order 14163—was done without adequate explanation or consideration of Congress’s mandated services under §§​1522(a)-(e). Moreover, they argued no notice-and-comment period occurred despite prior reliance interests created through years‑long contracts subject ordinarily to procurement rules at **31 U.S.C.§3155** (“Cooperative Agreements”).

The appellate panel concluded it was “likely” that at least one APA claim would succeed because “[t]he government cannot demonstrate it has an alternative mechanism ready…to meet its statutorily mandated reception‐and‐placement duties,” thereby deeming an unqualified termination potentially arbitrary and capricious.^[The wording reflects standard appellate discretion pending full merits.] Consequently, while deferring heavily on immigration–entry powers (§​1182(f)), it ordered reinstatement pending further resolution—a classic illustration where divergent statutory schemes intersect within APA review doctrine.

## 8 U.S.C.§ 1522(a)–(e) – Statutory Requirements for Reception and Placement Services  

Section 1522 comprises five subsections governing domestic refugee resettlement:

| Subsection | Core Duty |
|------------|-----------|
| **(a)** | Sets *conditions* (“shall”) governing any assistance granted “to the extent of available appropriations.” It enumerates required outcomes—employment training, English instruction, cash assistance designed not to deter self‑sufficiency, gender equity—and ties them directly to federal funds used for initial resettlement projects (§​1522(c)). |
| **(b)** | Authorizes use of contracts/grants/cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies to deliver those services (“may”). |
| **(c)** | Provides oversight mechanisms permitting HHS/ORR Director discretion over allocation frameworks (“may”) while obligating periodic reporting (§​1523). |
| **(d)** | Imposes limited responsibilities toward unaccompanied minor refugees during transit (“shall”) only when necessary—thus a conditional directive rather than open‑ended mandate.| 
| **(e)** | Directs HHS Secretary (“shall”) to develop alternative support projects for refugees residing less than thirty‑six months.^[These projects are intended as interim safety nets.] |

Taken together, §§​1522(a)–(e) construct a compulsory framework once Congress provides appropriations: if funds are available then HHS must supply reception‑and‑placement services meeting detailed criteria; however

*the statute does not compel Congress itself* to appropriate sufficient monies—hence “‘to the extent available appropriations’ serves as an express limiting clause.”

In *Pacito*, after Executive Order 14163 suspended USRAP funding—including domestic dollars earmarked for these services—the government terminated cooperative agreements without showing an alternative service delivery mechanism meeting subsection‐(a)’s mandatory conditions. The Ninth Circuit therefore found it prudent first to require reinstatement until compliance could be demonstrated—a move rooted firmly in interpreting §§​1522(a)(i)-(iv) as obligatory whenever viable funding exists.

## “Shall” Language Versus Discretionary Language in § 1522  

The crux of Judge Lee’s partial dissent hinges upon interpreting whether §§​1522 impose enforceable duties (“shall”) or merely grant agencies latitude (“may/authorizes”). His analysis follows three textual principles:

1️⃣ **Title Indicator:** The statute’s heading reads “Authorization for programs…” This suggests legislative intent was permissive rather than compulsory.^[As Black’s Law Dictionary defines authorization.]  

2️⃣ **Verbal Distinction:** Subsections (b)–(e)’s repeated use of “may”/“is authorized” signals congressional preference for flexibility regarding program design, funding mechanisms, and operational details.\* 

3️⃣ **Conditional Integration:** Although subsection (a)’s “shall” language appears mandatory, it is prefaced by “In providing assistance under this section …,” thereby coupling obligatory conditions only with discretionary funding decisions contingent upon appropriations.^[Thus if no funds flow—or if funds are lawfully withdrawn—the conditional obligations evaporate.]

Majority opinion respondents countered That reading ignores plain‐text mandates whereby “shall” commands compliance whenever ***any*** appropriated funds exist—a situation arguably satisfied before Executive Order cut those funds off unlawfully ^[See majority discussion.] In other words, they view subsections (d)(B) and (e)(7)(A)’s phrasing (“shall develop…as needed”) more akin to mandatory performance triggers triggered automatically by existing entitlement structures rather than discretionary discretion.*

Judge Lee concludes therefore that obligating receipt‐and‐placement services exceeds congressional grant—a stance reflected in his desire to vacate all injunctions concerning service provision while leaving intact those addressing admissions.^[His reasoning illustrates how minute textual differences generate divergent adjudicative outcomes.] Understanding this debate equips students with practical insight into interpretive tactics frequently raised before appellate courts when statutes combine permissive authorizations with conditional mandates.

## Funding Landscape – FY 2025 Appropriations & Rescissions Act Reduction  

Congress allocated **$3.178 billion** for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 through Pub.~L.~119‑4 (§1101(a)(11)). However, later legislative action—the **Rescissions Act 2025** (Pub.~L.~119‑28)—reduced this envelope by ***$800 million*** (§ 28(b)(6)). These two figures matter for several reasons:

1️⃣ They constitute the “available appropriations” referenced throughout §​1522(a). If actual outlays fall short after rescission, HHS may lawfully curtail certain program components—but only proportionately.^[Congressional control over spending limits subsequent executive discretion.]  
2️⃣ The reduction exacerbates prosecutorial questions raised by plaintiffs about whether termination of cooperative agreements left refugees without constitutionally mandated support despite remaining — albeit reduced — funds earmarked specifically for reception/placement.^[Thus some portion remained ostensibly available.]  
3️⃣ Judicial analysis often treats appropriation levels as factual inputs when applying both ***§ 1182(f)**’s finding requirement* (the president must determine detriment based partly on resource constraints) ***and*** ***APA*** review (*i.e.*, whether agency action considered available resources before cutting contracts).

Consequently,: understanding how congressional budgetary decisions intersect with statutory mandates elucidates why litigation concerning refugee admissions cannot rely solely upon presidential discretion; it must also grapple with whether governmental financing obligations survive fiscal cuts—a question central both at trial level (*district court prelim injunction*) and now at appellate stage (*stay pending final resolution*).

### Synthesis  

*Pacito v.* illustrates how federal statutes create overlapping obligations which sometimes clash:  
*the President enjoys near‑unfettered power under §§ 1182(f)/INA—but only so far as his finding satisfies minimal statutory criteria;  
*Congress simultaneously obligates HHS/ORR through §§ 1522(a)-(e)—conditional upon actual appropriations—to furnish essential resettlement services once refugees enter;  
*and agencies remain bound by APA procedural safeguards whenever they alter existing program structures.*

The Ninth Circuit balanced these competing forces by granting deference where Capitol delegated broad discretion (*President + Immigration§*) yet insisting on compliance where Congress imposed explicit duties (*Reception/Placement Services*) backed by an evident budgetary floor after rescission cuts.<|show table&gt;
