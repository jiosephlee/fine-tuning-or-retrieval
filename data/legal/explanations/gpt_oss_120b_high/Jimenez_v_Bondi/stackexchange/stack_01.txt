Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What is the scope of appellate jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 for reviewing BIA asylum decisions?
Question:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit cites 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1) and (b)(9) as giving it jurisdiction to review the Board’s denial of asylum. I’m confused about how a "final order of removal" includes a discretionary grant of asylum, which is normally within the Attorney General’s sole discretion. Does every denial of asylum constitute a final order that can be appealed? And how does the court decide whether it should look at only the administrative record versus also consulting the immigration judge’s explanation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1) and § 1252(b)(9) the Courts of Appeals have **jurisdiction over any “final order of removal.”** In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit holds that a Board‑of‑Immigration‑Appeals (BIA) denial of asylum is such a final order because the denial leaves the alien **subject to removal** – even though asylum itself is a discretionary grant normally reserved to the Attorney General. Consequently, once the BIA’s decision becomes final (no pending motions for reconsideration or stays), it may be appealed under § 1252, and the appellate court may review **all aspects** of the underlying asylum claim in a single, consolidated proceeding.

---

### 1. What “final order of removal” means in this context  

The opinion cites two controlling authorities:

> “We generally have jurisdiction to review final orders of removal, which can include a consolidated review of asylum claims.”  
> *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (citing §§ 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)).  

Section 1252(a)(1) gives courts jurisdiction over *orders removing* an alien, while § 1252(b)(9) defines a *final order* as one “issued by the Board … with respect to removal.” The court treats a **BIA denial of asylum** as falling within this definition because such a denial **removes any statutory barrier to deportation** – the alien now has no lawful status and will be ordered removed under the INA’s general removal provisions (§ 1229a).  

Thus, although granting asylum is discretionary, *refusing* it produces a **determinative consequence:** the alien becomes removable. That consequence satisfies the statutory phrase “final order of removal,” giving appellate courts authority to review it.

---

### 2. Does *every* asylum denial trigger appellate jurisdiction?  

The court’s analysis implies two practical limits:

1. **Finality requirement** – The decision must be **final and unreviewed elsewhere** before federal courts can intervene (§ 1252(b)(9)). If the alien has filed a motion for reconsideration, asked for a stay pending litigation, or otherwise kept the BIA decision in flux, the order is not yet “final” and therefore not appealable under § 1252.
   
2. **Link to removal** – The denial must be **the reason why removal will occur**. In *Jimenez* the BIA affirmed an immigration‑judge finding that the petitioners were inadmissible and directed them back to Colombia; there was no other form of relief left open. When an applicant receives an alternative form of relief (e.g., withholding of removal or protection under CAT) that prevents removal, a pure asylum denial would *not* be “final” for purposes of § 1252 because another statutory avenue bars deportation.

Consequently, while most **pure denials** after an immigration judge’s ruling do become final orders subject to appeal, an exception exists when another protective remedy remains viable or when procedural wrangling keeps the decision from being final.

---

### 3. What portion of the record may the appellate court consider?  

Statutory text limits judicial review:

> “Our review ... is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order … is based.’”  
> *Jimenez*, 130 F.4th at 859 (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)).  

However, *Jimenez* also adopts prior Ninth‑Circuit precedent incorporated by this court:

> “We are **not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation** of those same grounds.”  
> *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d at 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

In practice this means:

* **Substantive factual findings** – reviewed under the *substantial‑evidence* standard using only what appears in the **administrative record** (the transcript, exhibits submitted at ICE/DOJ hearings, and any written decisions).
* **Interpretive material** – The immigration judge’s written reasoning may be read alongside that record as an explanatory aid because it is considered part of the same administrative dossier—not extraneous evidence outside the record.
* **Legal conclusions** – Reviewed de novo; again only relying on material present in the administrative file.

The court therefore balances strict adherence to § 1252(b)(4)(A) with flexibility permitted by *Uanreroro*, allowing it to glean context from the IJ’s statement without straying into new fact‑finding territory.

---

### 4. How does this shape appellate practice for future BIA asylum cases?  

In practical terms, *Jimenez* tells practitioners that:

| Issue | Take‑away |
|-------|-----------|
| **Jurisdiction** | A BIA denial that leaves an alien removable creates a *final order* under § 1252(b)(9); appeal may proceed even though asylum itself is discretionary. |
| **When not appealable** | If another protective form remains available or if procedural steps keep the decision non‑final, appellate jurisdiction does not attach yet. |
| **Scope of review** | The appellate court reviews factual determinations under substantial evidence using only what appears in the administrative record but may read any detailed IJ explanation attached to that record for clarification.^[See *Jimenez*, ¶ A–B.] |
| **Standard applied** | Legal questions are reviewed de novo; factual findings receive substantial‑evidence deference.^[See *Jimenez*, ¶ A.] |

Thus, while every *pure* denial after an immigration judge’s ruling typically constitutes a “final order,” appellate courts are bounded by both statutory language (§ 1252(b)(4)) and by precedent allowing limited consultation of related judicial explanations.^[Id.] This framework explains why *Jimenez* could deny petitioners’ appeal despite acknowledging that asylum relief rests within Attorney General discretion—it was precisely because their denied claim rendered them subject to removable status under INA §§ 1229a–1229b.

---

#### Bottom line

- **Scope:** § 1252 authorizes appellate review whenever a BIA decision denies asylum and thereby makes removal inevitable—i.e., when it produces a *final order*.
- **Not automatic:** The order must be final (no pending motions or alternative relief).
- **Record limits:** Review is confined to the administrative record; however, courts may read any accompanying immigration‑judge explanation because it forms part of that same record.^[Id.]
