Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the burden of proof differ between asylum and withholding of removal claims?
Question:
"Jimenez v. Bondi" says that because petitioners failed on their asylum claim they also fail on withholding of removal since it requires a higher standard. Can someone explain what evidentiary standards apply to each form of relief under INA § 1158(b) and § 115(b)(2)? Does withholding require proof of past persecution only, or also a higher likelihood threshold for future persecution?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the INA an asylum applicant must prove his or her claim **by a pre‑ponderance of the evidence** (the “lower” burden). A withholding‑of‑removal applicant must meet **the same substantive elements** but prove them **by “clear probability”** (the “higher” burden). Because the withholding standard is stricter, a failure to satisfy even the lower‑burden asylum requirements—particularly proof of past persecution and a nexus to a protected ground—automatically defeats a withholding claim. As the Tenth Circuit explained in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, “since petitioners fail to demonstrate…past persecution…or a well‑founded fear…they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.”¹

Below is a side‑by‑side breakdown of the two statutes and how *Jimenez* applies them.

---

### 1. Who bears the burden?

| Relief | Statutory source | Who carries the burden? |
|--------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **Asylum** | 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1) | **Applicant** – must prove he/she is a refugee. |
| **Withholding of removal** | 8 U.S.C. § 115(b)(2) | **Applicant** – must prove he/she meets the refugee definition *and* that removal would subject him/her to persecution. |

Both are “prima facie” burdens on the non‑citizen; the government does not have to produce evidence once the applicant meets his/her burden.

---

### 2. Evidentiary standards

| Relief | Standard in the statute | How courts phrase it |
|--------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| **Asylum** | “Preponderance of the evidence.” | The applicant’s evidence must be more convincing than contrary evidence. (See *Jimenez*, B.) |
| **Withholding** | “Higher evidentiary burden” — *clear probability* of future persecution. | The applicant must show it is “more likely than not” that he/she will be persecuted if removed, i.e., a clear and convincing likelihood (see *Jimenez*, Conclusion). |

The *Jimenez* court stressed that this “higher standard” makes withholding “more difficult to obtain” than asylum.

---

### 3. Substantive elements required

Both forms require satisfaction of the refugee definition in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A) — three prongs:

1. **Past persecution** (or its equivalent).  
2. **Nexus** – persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.  
3. **Perpetrator** – government or forces the government is unable or unwilling to control (or will not protect).

#### Asylum
* The applicant may rely either on **past persecution** (which creates a rebuttable presumption of future risk) **or on a well‑founded fear** of future persecution.  
* A “well‑founded fear” is measured at the pre‑ponderance level (see *Jimenez*, B.–D.).

#### Withholding
* The same three prongs apply, **but** each must be shown at the **clear‑probability** level (see *Jimenez*, Conclusion).  
* Even if there is no demonstrable past persecution, an applicant can still qualify by proving **future persecution will occur with clear probability**, together with internal relocation infeasibility and government inability/unwillingness (see *Jimenez*, E.–F.).  

Thus, unlike asylum, withholding does **not** rely solely on proof of past persecution; it demands an additional—higher—probability assessment for future harm.

---

### 4. Why failure on asylum defeats withholding in *Jimenez*

The Tenth Circuit’s reasoning was straightforward:

1. **Past‑persecution element:** Petitioners could not show that their threats and assaults rose to “persecution” under INA definitions (§ 1158(b)(1)). The court emphasized that “threats alone are insufficient” and that there was no physical or lasting harm capable of constituting persecution (see *Jimenez*, C.).  
2. **Nexus element:** Even assuming some harm occurred, petitioners failed to demonstrate that their political opinion was a *central* reason for it—rather, the court found motive was Jiménez’s personal conflict with “Don Rafa” (see *Jimenez*, D.).  
3. Because both elements were lacking at the **pre‑ponderance level**, petitioners could not even meet asylum’s lower threshold.
4. Since withholding requires at least as much proof—indeed more (“clear probability”)—the court concluded petitioners “necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding” (see *Jimenez*, Conclusion).

In short, once an applicant cannot satisfy any element required for asylum at its lower burden, he/she cannot satisfy it at an even higher burden required for withholding.

---

### 5. Bottom line for your study

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| **What evidentiary standard applies to an asylum claim?** | Pre‑ponderance of evidence; must prove either past persecution plus nexus (**or**) a well‑founded fear of future persecution at that same burden (INA § 1158(b)). |
| **What evidentiary standard applies to a withholding‑of‑removal claim?** | Same substantive elements as asylum **plus** proof by “clear probability” (a higher evidentiary bar) that removal would subject the applicant to persecution (INA § 115(b)(2)). |
| **Does withholding require proof of past persecution only?** | No. It can be grounded either in proven past persecution *or* in a clear‑probability prediction of future persecution—but whichever route is taken it must meet the higher burden and also satisfy internal relocation and government‐protection analyses (see *Jimenez*, E.–F.). |
| **Why did *Jiminez v.* say failure on asylum means failure on withholding?** | Because asylum’s lower‐burden test includes all three refugee prongs; failing them at the pre‑ponderance level means there is no basis left to satisfy those same prongs at an even stricter (“clear probability”) level required for withholding (see *Jiminez*, Conclusion). |

Understanding this hierarchy will help you see why courts treat withholding as a “higher bar” after an unsuccessful asylum claim—exactly as illustrated by *Jimenez v.* Bondi.
