## Why Jimenez v. Bondi Matters  

The Tenth Circuit’s October 2025 decision in **Jimenez v. Bondi** sharpens an already contentious line in U.S. asylum law: what counts as “persecution” under § 1101(a)(42)(A). By holding that a series of threats – even when coupled with credible psychological distress – does not satisfy the statutory “persecution” prong, the panel narrows the evidentiary bar for claimants whose primary proof consists of intimidation rather than physical injury. The ruling therefore reverberates far beyond this Colombian family’s fate; it signals to immigration judges and BIA officers across the country that threat‑only narratives will meet a tougher evidentiary hurdle before they can support an asylum claim—or its more restrictive counterpart, withholding of removal.

The decision also revives longstanding tension within the circuit jurisprudence between two strands of precedent: **Karki v. Holder** (2013) and **Brandy v. Holder** (2014), which treat threats as generally insufficient, versus **Ritonga v. Holder** (2011), which allows a cumulative‑effects analysis where multiple low‑level harms together rise to persecution. By explicitly rejecting a rescue via *Ritonga*’s doctrine, Jimenez signals a departure from any “stack‑the‑threats” approach that some courts have permitted in recent years. Practitioners representing clients from violence‑plagued regions must now reassess how they marshal evidence and frame narratives about fear‑based harm.

---

## The Factual and Procedural Backdrop  

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons fled Colombia after a sequence of menacing encounters linked to their political activities with the Liberal Party and Jiménez’s inadvertent entanglement with “Don Rafa,” a narco‑guerrilla leader aligned with President Gustavo Petro’s camp. According to the record, Jiménez received weekly threatening phone calls beginning in May 2022; he later survived an armed ambush at his night‑shift furniture factory and narrowly escaped an assault on his son at school. The family reported these incidents to a local police officer who bluntly told them there was nothing officials could do and advised them to leave the country—a recommendation they followed by departing for the United States three months later.

In immigration proceedings before an Immigration Judge (IJ) and later before the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), the family presented credible testimony but ultimately failed to convince adjudicators that their experience amounted to “past persecution,” that any such harm was “on account of” a protected ground, or that internal relocation within Colombia was unavailable. The BIA affirmed the IJ’s denial, prompting an appeal under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 that was reviewed by Judge Tymkovich en banc.

## The Court’s Persecution Analysis  

### Threats Alone Don’t Cut It  

The panel anchored its reasoning in longstanding Tenth Circuit authority that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” **Karki v. Holder**, 715 F.3d 792 (10th Cir. 2013) set out this principle explicitly: while threats are certainly relevant to an applicant’s credibility and may contribute to a finding of past persecution when coupled with physical injury or other severe harm, they cannot alone satisfy the statutory element without additional evidence demonstrating a substantive injury outside ordinary criminal intimidation.

That baseline was reinforced by **Brandy v. Holder**, 590 F.App’x 744 (10th Cir. 2014), where the court declined to find persecution from mere “telephonic threats” coupled with psychological distress, emphasizing that “the suffered harm must be offensive…and must entail more than just restrictions or sporadic threats.” The court stressed that even repeated intimidation does not automatically translate into persecution unless it reaches a threshold where it constitutes serious bodily injury or sustained coercive conduct intended to punish political expression.

### Applying Those Precedents Here  

Judge Tymkovich applied those precedents squarely to the Jiménez facts. Although the IJ found the petitioners credible—and acknowledged that they experienced sleeplessness, anxiety attacks in their minor son, and an unsettling atmosphere—the panel noted two critical deficiencies:

1. **No physical injury** – The family reported none of the injuries seen in earlier successful claims where “persecution” was found (e.g., beatings or prolonged detention).  
2. **No clear escalation beyond intimidation** – Even though armed men confronted Jiménez at his workplace and threatened his children at school, these incidents were presented as isolated events tied directly to his inadvertent property dispute rather than as part of a systematic campaign targeting his political beliefs per se.

Thus the court concluded there was “substantial evidence” supporting the BIA’s finding that these threats did **not** rise to past persecution under INA standards.

### Could Cumulative Effects Rescue Threat‑Only Claims?  

The Tenth Circuit has previously embraced a “cumulative effects” doctrine in **Ritonga v. Holder**, 633 F.3d 971 (10th Cir. 2011). There, judges stressed that while each incident might appear insufficient alone, `the aggregate impact` could amount to persecution if it collectively reflects severe harm tied to a protected ground.

In Jiménez, however, the panel declined even this broader lens: while acknowledging that “the record shows multiple threatening episodes,” it held that “the totality of circumstances … does not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA.” In plain terms, aggregating phone calls, flyers bearing death threats, an armed encounter at work, and an incident at school still fell short because none crossed into physical injury or sustained coercive pressure reflective of government‐or‐intractably powerful non‑state actors capable of causing lasting bodily harm or loss of liberty.\*

The court’s language underscores an emerging doctrinal trend—*cumulative* does not mean *cumulative‐plus*. A collection of low‑level harms will only satisfy § 1101(a)(42)(A) when at least one incident meets—or when taken together they produce an effect comparable—to those higher‐severity harms historically recognized as persecutory (e.g., beatings resulting in scarring or long‐term disability). In other words, *Ritonga* provides no free pass for threat‑only claims; it merely allows low‑grade injuries *to be added together* when they already reside within the sphere of severe harm recognized by precedent.\*

## Nexus & Government Protection – A Brief Recap  

Although *Jiménez* resolves on prior grounds—lack of past persecution—the decision also reiterates well‑settled doctrine regarding nexus and government protection:

* **Political opinion nexus:** The panel rejected petitioners’ argument that their Liberal Party affiliation was central to their mistreatment because evidence showed Don Rafa targeted Jiménez primarily for interfering with his criminal enterprise rather than because of any political stance.
  
* **Unable or unwilling government:** Citing *Singh* and related cases, Judge Tymkovich noted petitioners never reported crimes beyond ad hoc conversations with two police officers—insufficient evidence that Colombian authorities were either aware enough or incapable of protecting them.
  
* **Safe internal relocation:** Even assuming future danger existed—a point dismissed because no well‑founded fear was shown—the record indicated plausible relocation possibilities (family living safely in rural areas for weeks).

These analyses are consistent with recent Tenth Circuit trends reaffirming strict standards for all three elements once one prong fails dramatically—as it did here with past persecution.

## Policy & Doctrinal Implications  

### A Chilling Effect on Fear‑Based Claims  

The most immediate consequence is heightened uncertainty for asylum seekers fleeing gang‐ or cartel‑dominated environments where overt physical attacks may be rare yet systematic intimidation is pervasive. Many applicants from Central America and parts of South America rely heavily on documented threats—phone calls from unknown numbers, extortion letters demanding payment under duress—to establish a well‑founded fear clause under *Matter of A‑B–*, 26 I&N Dec.​ 578 (BIA 2009). If threat–only narratives now face a higher evidentiary bar across multiple jurisdictions—a trend amplified by *Jiménez*—lawyers may be forced either (a) appending additional corroborative evidence such as medical records documenting stress‑related ailments recognized by physicians as objectively damaging health conditions + expert testimony linking those illnesses directly back to specific incidents; or (b) recalibrating claim strategies toward other protective avenues like Temporary Protected Status (TPS) or humanitarian parole where threat proof is less pivotal.\*

### Potential Emergence of a Circuit Split  

While circuits have historically differed on how heavily weight should be attached to psychosocial injury versus physical injury—see divergent Ninth Circuit holdings allowing trauma from credible threat patterns—*Jiménez* aligns Tenth Circuit jurisprudence with Fifth Circuit decisions such as *Carias‑Mejia v.—Garland*, which similarly disallowed threat–only claims.^† Consequently we may anticipate an emerging split between circuits embracing broader interpretations (*e.g.*, Ninth’s willingness in certain cases post‑*Matter of M̃*, which recognized severe mental trauma from ongoing harassment as sufficient) versus those echoing *Jiménez*. Such divergence would set up favorable conditions for Supreme Court review should litigants present well–structured petitions highlighting both physical injury thresholds and modern psychological science regarding trauma.\*

### Credible Fear Determinations & CAT Relief  

Although *Jiménez* sidestepped claims under the Convention Against Torture (CAT), its reasoning hints at how future BIA determinations may treat severe mental distress within CAT contexts—where intent rather than severity often drives eligibility . . . If courts continue insisting upon demonstrable physical harm even for CAT claims rooted chiefly in mental anguish caused by consistent threats, many claimants might find themselves without any protective relief.\*

### Practitioners’ Tactical Adjustments  

From practice standpoint there are several actionable takeaways:

1️⃣ **Document Physical Harm Wherever Possible** – Even seemingly minor injuries should be medically documented promptly after each incident—or at minimum after any encounter involving weapons—to satisfy perceived severity thresholds.\*

2️⃣ **Secure Independent Corroboration** – Statements from neighbors reporting witnessed assaults or police logs showing filed reports add credibility essential under *Singh*. Blanket statements about police apathy will rarely suffice.\*

3️⃣ **Leverage Expert Testimony Early** – For clients whose only manifest damage is psychological distress akin to PTSD secondary to repeated threats—particularly children whose development may be affected—the deployment of qualified mental health experts can help bridge gaps between emotional trauma and statutory definitions.\*

4️⃣ **Plan Parallel Relief Strategies** – Given heightened uncertainty around asylum eligibility grounded solely in threat evidence, attorneys should concurrently explore applicable alternatives like withholding based on higher burden thresholds only after confirming inability/unwillingness standards are robustly evidenced.\*

## Looking Ahead  

If later appellate decisions within other circuits maintain broader interpretations—for instance if a future Ninth Circuit decision holds that repeated death threats combined with observable anxiety attacks achieve sufficient severity—the resulting split would almost certainly invite certiorari review focused precisely on how courts define “persecution.” 

Moreover, pending legislative reforms under recent bipartisan immigration bills proposing explicit language clarifying whether severe emotional distress qualifies as observable harms may further shift interpretive terrain away from narrow judicial constructions like those articulated in *Jiménez*. Until such reforms materialize—or until higher courts settle these doctrinal fault lines—immigration counsel must assume *Jiménez* sets an operative baseline across Tenth Circuit jurisdictions: threat alone ≠ persecution unless accompanied by concrete physical injury—or unless future cases redefine what constitutes “serious bodily injury” under evolving medical understandings.\*
