Chapter 2: # II Factual Background and Immigration Proceedings  

In **Jimenez v. Bondi**, the Tenth Circuit reviewed a highly fact‑laden asylum case involving a Colombian family who fled violent intimidation tied to both political activity and a local narco‑guerrilla network. Understanding how the factual narrative fits into the procedural posture of U.S. immigration law is essential for any student learning to dissect an appellate opinion. This chapter walks through each element of the record that the district court judge relied upon—including who the petitioners are, what threats they faced in Colombia, how they entered the United States without inspection, and how their claims moved through removal proceedings before reaching the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and ultimately this Court of Appeals.


## Petitioners’ Family Composition, Citizenship, and Party Affiliation  

The principal petitioner is **Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez**, a native‑born citizen of Colombia who holds a *provisional* law license there. He is married to **Ángela Parada Duarte**, also a Colombian citizen, and together they have **two minor sons** whose ages are not specified in the record but who were held in “rider‑respondent” status during removal proceedings—meaning they derived any relief solely from their father’s asylum application. All four family members share Colombian citizenship and have lived continuously in Bogotá until December 2022 when they departed for the United States.

Since **2012** each member has been an active member of the **Colombian Liberal Party**, Colombia’s historic centrist party that opposed President Gustavo Petro’s left‑leaning coalition in the 2022 presidential election. Their long‑standing affiliation establishes a collective political identity that later becomes relevant when assessing whether alleged persecution was “on account of political opinion,” one of the five protected grounds enumerated in § 1101(a)(42)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (“INA”).

## Political Canvassing During the 2022 Presidential Election  

During the heated run‑up to Colombia’s June 2022 presidential contest, Jiménez, Duarte, and other Liberal Party volunteers engaged in **door‑to‑door canvassing** throughout Bogotá neighborhoods on behalf of **Federico Gutierrez**, the Liberal candidate. The campaign strategy involved personal visits to solicit votes—a standard political activity under Colombian law—but one that placed them publicly on record as opponents of Petro’s coalition government.  

Their canvassing continued unabated even after **May 2022**, when threatening telephone calls began targeting Jiménez specifically because of his party work. The persistence of campaign activity despite escalating threats underscores both their political commitment and their exposure to potential retaliation—a factual backdrop against which later claims of political persecution are evaluated.

## Threatening Phone Calls Beginning May 2022 and Escalation After a Colleague’s Kidnapping/Murder  

The first documented intimidation took shape as **frequent threatening phone calls**—roughly once or twice weekly—directed at Jiménez from unknown callers who warned him about his Liberal Party involvement. The pattern intensified dramatically after an incident in early June 2022 when a fellow Liberal activist who had also received threats was **kidnapped and subsequently murdered** despite having reported those threats to local authorities (the record does not identify any perpetrator).

Rather than curtailing their political work, Jiménez and Duarte pressed forward with campaigning throughout June–July 2022. The escalation from anonymous calls to lethal violence against an associate heightened their sense of danger but nevertheless did not immediately alter their public political conduct—a fact later relevant when courts ask whether threat motives were primarily political or otherwise rooted.



## Jiménez’s Role as a Provisional Lawyer/Intern with the National Prosecutor’s Office  

In addition to his party activism, Jiménez held dual roles within Colombia’s legal system:

1️⃣ He was a **provisional lawyer**, meaning he had completed his legal studies but had yet to achieve full licensure—a status that permitted limited practice under supervision.  
2️⃣ He served as an **intern** with Colombia’s **National Prosecutor’s Office** (“Fiscalía General de la Nación”), assisting victims affected by domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes—a position placing him at direct contact with law enforcement officials tasked with investigating organized crime.

These roles placed him at points where he could encounter sensitive investigations concerning criminal networks—including those linked—by various witnesses—to politically aligned guerrilla groups operating illicit economies such as drug trafficking.


## Property Dispute Involving “Don Rafa” – Discovery of Narco‑Trafficking Operations at Client’s Property  

In June 2022 Jiménez accepted representation from a client named **María del Carmen San Bernardo**, who approached him about a rent dispute concerning her property on Bogotá’s third floor—a property she claimed she owned outright following a divorce settlement wherein her ex‑husband transferred title to her. The client alleged that her former husband—a tenant—refused to vacate despite being out‐of‑payment for over twelve months.

When Jiménez investigated personally he learned two critical facts:

* The tenant was indeed her ex‑husband **Rafael Augusto Montañez Lancheros**, known locally by his alias “**Don Rafa**.”  
* Don Rafa headed an armed group identified by police witnesses as **“narco‑trafficking guerrillas”** politically allied with President Petro’s administration—a coalition often described in Colombian media as part “paramilitary,” part “drug cartel.”

Further on‑site inspection revealed that portions of San Bernardo’s property had been **sealed by health officials** because it operated informally as a **day‑rental hub for migrants**, simultaneously serving as a base for drug trafficking operations, prostitution rings, kidnappings, and murders—a nexus between illicit commerce and violent crime known locally enough that police had already attempted multiple searches without success. During his visit a local police officer explicitly warned Jiménez **“to stay away from the property”** (“no se meta”). This warning hinted at both criminal danger tied directly to Don Rafa’s group **and** implicit collusion—or at least acquiescence—by authorities aware yet unwilling or unable—or too constrained—to intervene.



## Specific Incidents of Threats and Violence  

The escalating hostilities culminated in four discrete episodes documented during removal proceedings:

### Brochure Threat at the Furniture Factory  

Within weeks after discovering Don Rafa’s involvement in San Bernardo’s property dispute, employees at Jiménez’s family‐owned furniture factory received a printed flyer stating ominously: *“Death to snitches from Liberal Party.”* Although no physical harm followed this distribution, it marked an explicit public intimidation linking their political affiliation with lethal consequences.


### Gunpoint Intimidation at the Factory  

In July 2022—not long after receipt of the flyer—two men entered Jiménez’s night‑shift factory brandishing guns while demanding he leave *the neighborhood*. One assailant labeled him a “snitch” working for prosecutorial authorities (referencing his internship), accusing him specifically of facilitating recent “searches and seizures” at Don Rafa's property—a direct connection between his legal work against organized crime and personal retaliation.


### Assault on Factory Employee  

Two months later *the same pair* returned while both Jiménez and his wife were traveling out‐of‑town; this time they threatened an employee who happened upon them operating machinery inside after hours. The men demanded knowledge about Petérez (“Where are Pet­ez?”); upon being told silently that Peterson's family was traveling elsewhere they departed without resorting againto lethal force but leaving behind unmistakable threats (*“You will be killed if you appear again”*) reinforced by reference that *the order came directly from Don Rafa*.  


### Attempted Abduction at School  

A further chilling incident unfolded when a man assaulted young *son* James (name withheld) outside his school—grasping him around the shoulder before clamping his hand over his mouth while insisting he *“run to your dad.”* James escaped thanks chiefly to paternal intervention wherein Jiménez physically pushed back while sprinting toward their car—a direct attempt aimed at kidnapping or murder rather than mere intimidation.


Taken together these episodes evidentially demonstrate escalating physical threat dimensions—from nonviolent written warnings through overt armed intimidation—and reveal how hostile actors blended political animus against Liberal Party supporters with personal retribution linked directly back to concerns over Jiménez’ interference with Don Rafa’s criminal interests.


## Unsuccessful Attempts to Obtain Assistance from Colombian Police Officers  

When pressed by these incidents toward protection or redress:

* After witnessing assaults inside his factory he consulted one local police patrol officer who bluntly replied *“nothing can be done”* before disclosing “police officers themselves contribute problematically.”  
* When seeking help following threats he met again only one officer who simply advised *“you should leave”* rather than offering protective custody or initiating investigation into Don Rafa or associated weaponized groups affiliated with state actors.

Crucially no formal complaint was lodged through official channels—no written report aligning recreational grievance documentation wherein authorities would demonstrably act—or any request placed before Bogotá's public prosecutor office despite Jiménez’s internship there.%¹ The absence—or perceived futility—of state protection would become central when analyzing whether Colombia was *unable or unwilling* under INA standards.

## Decision to Flee Colombia and Arrival in the United States in December 2022  

Following sustained harassment—including physical gunpoint confrontations—and considering explicit police messages indicating governmental indifference if anything else would occur if they remained behind—as well as direct attempts targeting James—the family concluded their safety could no longer be ensured within Bogotá proper nor its immediate surroundings (**three weeks living temporarily with Jiménez’ mother in rural Bogotà countryside did not alleviate fear**). Consequently they resolved permanently exit Colombia 

They departed ***December 2022*** without ever obtaining lawful admission into America—i.e., none entered through any I‑94 inspection point nor received parole from an immigration officer—and therefore arrived *without inspection.* This uninspected entry placed them squarely within Section 212(a)(6)(C)(i) grounds relating specifically to unauthorized entry chosen historically trumped any tentative presence rights.


---

> **Pedagogical Note:** An uninspected arrival triggers automatic inadmissibility under INA §§​212(a)(6)(C)(i)&(ii). Such individuals are subject only via expedited removal procedures unless formally placed into regular removal hearings by DHS—the route ultimately taken here.



## Issuance of Individual Notices To Appear (NTAs) and Filing Of Asylum/Withholding Applications  

Within roughly **three months** after landing—in march–April 2023—the Department of Homeland Security issued separate **Individual Notices To Appear** (“NTAs”) against each family member charging them with removability pursuant exclusively-to INA provisions contracting unauthorized entry⁽¹⁾ These NTAs initiated formal removal proceedings before an immigration judge (IJ).

During ensuing master calendar hearings Petitioner —actually primarily James’ father —in August 2023 elected *to admit* all allegations embodied within each NTA (*a tactical admission employed often by respondents seeking procedural leverage*). On that same hearing each petitioner filed two mutually exclusive relief requests:

1️⃣ An application for **asylum** under §1158(b)(1) based upon fear stemming primarily from alleged persecution tied nominally ___to political opinion___ while   
2️⃣ A parallel request for **withholding Of Removal** (§1229b(a)) which does not require discretionary grant—the burden remains establishing past persecution plus government inability/unwillingness protections—but offers higher-order protection against mandatory return .   

Both petitions listed their two minor sons as **rider respondents/derivative beneficiaries** meaning any grant would automatically extend coverage without separate filings—an established procedural shorthand within ICE practice.’¹

### Immigration Judge’s Denial Reasoning – Lack Of Past Persecution And Nexus  

The IJ conducted evidentiary hearings spanning several weeks during which petitioners testified extensively about phone threats, gunpoint episodes & attempted school abduction—all corroborated by documentary evidence (brochures,*police statements*, press clippings). While acknowledging sincerity—and thus finding petitioner credibility sufficient—the IJ concluded *the cumulative facts failed statutory thresholds*:

* No confirmed act rose legally unto “persecution”; namely threats alone—even repeated threats—in isolation were insufficient per Ninth Circuit precedent adopted older Tenth decisions demonstrating *persecution must involve serious bodily injury or extreme psychological trauma brighter than mere intimidation*.  
* Even assuming some harms constituted persecution,*in absence convincingly plausible evidence that these harms were motivated predominantly by protected ground ("political opinion") rather than personal vendetta over Juan​̃ego's legal entanglements involving Don Rafa —the nexus fell short.*  
* Internal relocation ***was deemed reasonably feasible*** because relatives continued safely residing across various regions including rural Bogotà countryside following an intermittent relocation period—contrasting petitioner willingness vs actual impediment.*  
* Finally regarding government protection ***the record lacked concrete proof*** indicating any genuine attempt by public officials—including Bogotá police officers—to intervene or protect petitioners—it instead reflected individual officers’ dismissive remarks absent systemic demonstration home ​that Government was unable/unwilling.*

Consequently all three statutory prongs essential for both asylum (*discretionary*)and withholding (*mandatory*) failed → grant denied across board.
