Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # III. Legal Framework Governing Asylum, Withholding of Removal, and Review Standards  

The court’s analysis in **Jimenez v. Bondi**, 2025 WL … (10th Cir.), rests on a tightly defined statutory scheme and a body of Ninth‑Circuit precedent that sets out the elements a non‑citizen must prove and the evidentiary standards the reviewing courts apply.  This chapter isolates those rules—*the refugee definition*, *the three principal forms of humanitarian relief* (asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture), *the individual elements* such as past persecution, nexus and government protection, *the internal‑relocation prerequisite*, and finally *the standards of appellate review*.  By learning each component separately you will be able to see how the Court assembled them in Jimenez when it concluded that Petitioners failed to meet any relief threshold.



## “Refugee” Definition Under INA § 1101(a)(42)(A)

The cornerstone of both asylum and withholding analyses is the statutory definition of “refugee.”  

> A **refugee** is any person who is outside any country of such person’s nationality **or** who is unable or unwilling to return to that country because… he or she has suffered **persecution** on account of **race**, **religion**, **nationality**, **membership in a particular social group**, or **political opinion**  
> *(see INA § 1101(a)(42)(A); *Niang v. Gonzales*, 422 F.3d at 1194).*  

The five protected grounds are exhaustive; an applicant must tie any harm claimed to one—or more—of them.  The definition is deliberately broad (“being outside any country”) so that a noncitizen who has fled his home country qualifies at once if he meets the persecution prong.*  

In *Jimenez* the district court identified Petitioners’ nationality (Colombian) and noted their political activity with the Liberal Party—but later held they failed either to establish actual past persecution or a nexus between that harm and their political opinions.



## “Asylum” as Discretionary Relief – Burden by Preponderance  

Statutory authority gives the Attorney General **discretion** to grant asylum once an applicant demonstrates he meets the refugee criteria.*  

> The applicant bears the **burden** of proving “by a preponderance of the evidence” that he is a refugee.*  
> *(see 8 U.S.C._§1158(b)(1); *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.​4th at 859).*  

Because asylum is discretionary—not mandatory—the government may deny relief even when an applicant satisfies all elements, though denial must still be grounded in factual findings supported by substantial evidence (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.​3d at 645).  

In practical terms the non‑citizen must show three things by more than a fifty percent likelihood:
1️⃣ The existence of past persecution  
2️⃣ That such persecution was on account of a protected ground  
3️⃣ That either he cannot obtain adequate governmental protection or he cannot safely relocate within his country.*   

If any prong fails—as it did for Petitioners—the discretion remains unexercised.



## “Withholding of Removal” – Higher Evidentiary Burden  

Unlike asylum’s discretionary nature, **withholding of removal** is a mandatory bar against deportation once its narrow statutory prerequisites are satisfied.*

> The statutory provision bars removal when an alien can demonstrate—that **by clear evidence**—that his life or freedom would be threatened on account of one of the protected grounds if he were returned.*  
> *(see INA § 1159(b); *Niang* at 1194–95).*  

The key distinction lies not only in dispensability (asylum can be denied despite eligibility) but also in an elevated evidentiary threshold often described as “clear” or “convincing” proof—higher than the mere preponderance required for asylum.* In *Jimenez* the Court emphasized that Petitioners’ inability even to establish past persecution meant they automatically failed the higher bars imposed by withholding.



## Convention Against Torture Claim – Elements (Not Reviewed)

Although Petitioners’ claim under the Convention Against Torture (“CAT”) was **not** subject to appellate review here, its statutory framework often surfaces alongside asylum arguments because it provides an alternative form‐of‐relief independent of protected‐ground analysis.*

> An alien may obtain CAT protection if he establishes—by a **preponderance**—that it is “more likely than not” that he would be tortured if removed.*  
> *(see INA §§1231A(b); *Uanreroro* discussion on procedural posture).*  

Because CAT does not require proof of a protected ground nor past persecution, it sometimes offers a lifeline when those elements are weak—but only if credible torture risk exists—a point Petitioners could not make according to both BIA and this Court.



## Past Persecution Element  

Both asylum and withholding hinge on **past persecution**—the notion that an applicant has already suffered harms qualifying as “persecution.”  

The Ninth Circuit has articulated two intertwined components:
*the factual element* – conduct constituting “persecution,” i.e., severe suffering inflicted because‑of=.  
*the legal element* – establishing that conduct satisfies INA’s protective purpose.*

Key jurisprudence clarifies what counts:

* A finding must involve more than mere threats or routine police encounters.*  
 *(see *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.​3d at 893.)*  

* Multiple low‑level incidents can combine into a single finding when their cumulative effect meets the severity threshold.*  
 *(see *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.​3d at 975.)*  

* Threats alone are generally insufficient without accompanying violence or serious injury.*  
 *(see *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.​3d at 805.)*  

Moreover, whether an act qualifies as “persecution” depends on who commits it: either state actors or private actors whom the state **cannot or will not control** (*Niang*). In *Jimenez*, despite many threats—including gunpoint confrontations—the Court concluded there was no substantial evidence that such harms rose above simple intimidation or isolated intimidation episodes into constitutional‐level persecution.



## Nexus Requirement – Political Opinion Must Be Central  

Even assuming an incident qualifies as persecution, an applicant must link it **nexus‑wise** to oneofthe protected grounds.*

> For political opinion claims “the political opinion must be ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.”  
> *(see *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.​3d at 646; *Dallakoti​ v​.Holder*, 619 F.​3d at​1369.)*

The jurisprudential test asks whether political motivation was merely incidental—or whether it was pivotal yet possibly mingled with other motives (“mixed motives”). The Court holds:
1️⃣ If another non‑protected motive dominates – claim fails (*Yuk​ v​.Ashcroft*, 355​F​​/.3d at​1236).  
2️⃣ If persecutor’s primary grievance targets a protected ground—even amid secondary motives – claim may succeed (*O.C.V​.v​.Bondi*,‍2025 WL…).  

In *Jimenez*, evidence showed threats escalated only after Mr.–Jiménez interfered with Don Rafa’s property dispute—a personal vendetta unrelated directly to party politics—so political belief was deemed peripheral (“tangential”). Accordingly there was insufficient nexus.



## Unable‑or‑Unwilling Government Protection Analysis  

Even when an applicant proves past political‐grounded persecution, relief hinges on whether his home government **cannot or will not protect him**.*

The Ninth Circuit applies two prongs:  

1️⃣ **Government’s ability** – Whether it has effective control over persecutors (**or can stop them**).   
2️⃣ **Government’s willingness** – Whether it takes reasonable steps (**or declines protection**) after notice.*

Key guidance comes from *Singh*:  
> The analysis “considers … whether … (1) the government stopped or tried to stop … , and (2) offered protection such as protective custody.” *(see Singh†91).*  

A crucial evidentiary factor is whether petitioner **reportedly sought help** from authorities—a failure undermines claims unless petitioner shows reporting would have been futile or dangerous (*Aviles‑Gonzalez​​·v​.Garland*, 2024 WL…). Courts also demand evidence indicating systemic failure rather than isolated police inertia (*Osorio‑Morales​·v​.Garland*, 72 F​·4th at​743).  

In Jimenez’s factual matrix:
*Petitioners spoke only casually with two low‑level officers who told them merely “to leave,” providing no indication governmental policy against their persecutors.*   
Thus no substantial evidence proved Colombian officials were unable—or unwilling—to shield them from Don Rafa’s criminal network.



## Internal Relocation Doctrine  

Before granting asylum—or even before denying it—the government may rebut an applicant’s fear by demonstrating that safe internal relocation is feasible.*

> Under regulation **8 C.F.R.§1208.13(b)(1)(i)** an applicant bears the burden “to show that internal relocation is unavailable.” *(cited throughout adjudicative decisions).*  

Courts assess feasibility based on factors such as family ties elsewhere in the country, economic resources available for moving, comparable safety records among regions considered safe versus dangerous regions—the totality forming an objective evaluation (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 645). 

In ***Jimenez*** petitioners simply pointed out they spent three weeks living safely with relatives outside Bogotá prior to fleeing abroad—a factular demonstration that relocation within Colombia was practicable enough for dismissal even absent deeper hardship inquiries.


## Standard of Review – Substantial Evidence for Fact Findings & De Novo for Law Questions  

When an agency decision enters appellate scrutiny via **§1252(b)(4)(A)** (the BIA’s final order), courts follow bifurcated standards:

*For questions **of fact**,* uphold agency determinations unless unsupported by "**substantial evidence**"—meaning there must be enough relevant record material that a reasonable adjudicator could accept it as adequate proof.*   
*(Rivera‑Barrientos* at ***645***).*   This deference prevents Courts from reweighing testimony submitted before immigration judges.*

*For questions **of law**, including statutory construction or applicability,* review occurs "**de novo**"—appellate courts analyze without deference.*   
*(Singh†859).*   

Consequently in *Jimenez*, factual findings concerning threats’ severity, nexus relevance and governmental response were upheld because substantial evidence existed; however any residual legal interpretations—such as whether threats qualify legally as “persecution”—were subject merely insofar as precedent authorized those determinations *de novo*.  


## Scope Of Appellate Review Under §1252(b)(4)(A) – Reliance On The Administrative Record  

Statutory language limits federal district-court jurisdiction over immigration matters importably:
> “[T]he court shall…review only … ‘the administrative record on which … an order…is based.’” *(see ***8 U.S.C.§ 1252(b)(4)(A)**).*   

Thus appellate courts cannot consider new testimony nor extrinsic evidence absent from proceedings below—that limitation aligns precisely with Supreme Court precedent emphasizing due process protections remain anchored prior‑record adjudication (*Singh†857).*

Nevertheless judges may examine explanatory memoranda accompanying decisions—for instance this Court freely consulted both BIA’s decision memorandum ***and*** Immigration Judge’s more thorough explanatory record pursuant to ***Uanreroro v．Gonzales***,* confirming BIA rulings are valid even when initially rendered through single-member affirmation.*    

Consequently Jimenez’s ultimate outcome rests squarely upon what existed inside the administrative file—not upon fresh investigatory findings {*but solely upon what record revealed during lower‑level adjudication}.*

### Synthesis

Understanding difficult immigration opinions begins by compartmentalizing each statutory building block:
1️⃣ Define who qualifies as a refugee (§1101).  
2️⃣ Apply distinct procedural tracks—discretionary asylum versus mandatory withholding—and note differing burdens (*preponderance vs clear/evidentiary*. )  
3️⃣ Identify core components—past persecution (§1158), nexus (§1158–b(ii)), inability/unwillingness (§1159)—and test them against established Ninth Circuit authority (*Wiransane*, *Ritonga*, *Karki*, *Dallakoti*, *Yuk*, *Aviles‑Gonzalez*).  
4️⃣ Address ancillary issues such as CAT eligibility (**more likely than not**) and internal relocation (**safe haven viable?**) when applicable.
5️⃣ Finally remember procedural posture governs how much deference appellate courts extend—the combination hinges upon §§1252(a)–(b) coupled with substantive standards like substantial‑evidence review (*Rivera–Barrientos*) versus de novo legal scrutiny (*Singh*).

By mastering these discrete concepts you will be equipped to dissect any future decision—including ***Jimenez v Bondi***—and see precisely why courts accept or reject claims based upon what applicants proved within those rigid confines._references_
