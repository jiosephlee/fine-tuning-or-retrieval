# I. Introduction and Procedural Overview  

In order to read any appellate decision effectively, a student must first locate the case within its jurisdictional framework, identify the relief that was contested, and trace the procedural steps that carried the dispute from an immigration judge (IJ) through the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) to the federal circuit court.  *Jimenez v. Bondi* provides a textbook illustration of how a Tenth Circuit panel evaluates a petition for review under **8 U.S.C. § 1252** when the underlying relief at issue is asylum and withholding of removal.  The following sections break down each element of the procedural posture so that you can see how the court’s analysis is bounded by statute and precedent.

## Case Citation, Docket Number, and Deciding Judge  

The opinion appears as **Jimenez v. Bondi**, No. 24‑9573 (10th Cir. Oct. 7 2025), authored by **Circuit Judge Timothy Tymkovich**.  The citation tells us three things at a glance: (1) *Jimenez* is the plaintiff‑appellant (the Colombian natives seeking protection), *Bondi* is the respondent—chiefly an official of the Department of Homeland Security who issued the removal order; (2) “No. 24‑9573” identifies the docket number assigned by the Tenth Circuit clerk’s office, which you will use when locating briefs or oral argument transcripts; and (3) “Oct. 7 2025” marks the date the panel issued its decision.  Knowing these identifiers permits a researcher to retrieve both the opinion itself and any subsequent citations it may receive.

## Relief Sought: Asylum and Withholding of Removal (CAT Claim Excluded)  

Petitioners asked two forms of discretionary relief under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA):  

1. **Affirmative asylum** under **8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)**—a grant that would allow them to remain in the United States as refugees because they have suffered past persecution or have a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground.  

2. **Withholding of removal** under **8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3)**—a mandatory form of protection that bars removal to a country where an individual would face persecution on a protected ground but does not confer work authorization or a path to permanent residence.  

Both claims hinge on showing past persecution or a well‑founded fear thereof, plus proof that the Colombian government is unable or unwilling to protect them.  Petitioners also filed an application for protection under the **Convention Against Torture (CAT)**, but they expressly limited their appellate challenge to the asylum and withholding determinations; consequently, the CAT claim does not appear in this opinion or affect its reasoning.

## Immigration Judge’s Denial and BIA’s Affir­mation  

After being served with individual Notices to Appear shortly after entering the United States without inspection or parole, petitioners appeared before an IJ in August 2023.  The IJ found them **credible** yet concluded that their evidence failed on four essential elements:  

* no past persecution sufficient to satisfy the INA definition;  
* no nexus between any alleged harm and a protected ground;  
* internal relocation within Colombia was reasonably available; and  
* the Colombian government was neither unable nor unwilling to protect them from further harm.  

Because all four prongs were missing, the IJ denied both asylum and withholding relief and ordered removal to Colombia.  Petitioners appealed directly to the BIA under **8 U.S.C. § 1229(a)(1)** (“Filing an appeal from an IJ’s decision”).  The BIA performed a de novo review of factual findings but affirmed both substantive denials without identifying any error in applying INA standards or in weighing credibility determinations.

The BIA’s decision constitutes a **final order of removal**—the type of agency action that triggers statutory authority for judicial review in federal courts.

## Petition for Review Under 8 U.S.C. §§1252(a)(1)–(b)(9)  

Under **8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1)**, any alien who has been ordered removed may seek judicial review by filing a petition for review in a U.S. Court of Appeals “*when an applicant has been denied relief*.”  Section **§ 1252(b)(9)** specifies that such a petition may be filed “*against any final order…or decision…of…the Board*.”  Accordingly, petitioners filed a petition for review on October 7 2025 asking that this Court overturn both the asylum denial and withholding denial.

A petition for review differs from an appeal under **§ 1229** in two key respects:

1️⃣ It limits judicial scrutiny to **the administrative record** compiled before the BIA—including transcripts from hearings before both the IJ and BIA but not new evidence or testimony absent from that record (§ 1252(b)(4)(A)).  

2️⃣ It places all substantive questions—whether petitioner proved past persecution or established nexus—within *the purview* of appellate courts because those are deemed “legal conclusions” subject either to de novo review or mixed standards depending on whether they are pure legal questions or mixed fact‑law determinations.

Because petitioner’s brief focused exclusively on those two grounds—the asylum claim and withholding—this Court’s jurisdiction extended only as far as reviewing whether substantial evidence supported those specific findings.

## Limits on Appellate Review – Focus on Factual Findings and Legal Conclusions  

The Tenth Circuit has articulated two distinct standards when reviewing BIA decisions:

* **Substantial‑evidence standard** for factual findings — A finding is “conclusive unless…any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled…to reach a contrary conclusion.” (*Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 645).  This high bar protects agency discretion when credibility determinations or underlying facts are at issue.

* **De novo standard** for pure legal conclusions — When an issue calls solely upon statutory interpretation (e.g., whether an act qualifies as “persecution” under § 1101(a)(42)(A)), courts give no deference: they independently examine congressional text, precedent, and regulatory construction.

The Tenth Circuit also allows agencies’ explanatory memoranda — such as those attached by an IJ — to be consulted as persuasive material (*Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d at 1204), though they do not bind appellate judges if contrary substantial evidence exists.

In *Jimenez*, because petitioners framed their arguments around whether their alleged harms rose to “past persecution,” whether there existed a requisite nexus, and whether internal relocation was feasible—all fact‑intensive inquiries—the panel applied substantial‑evidence scrutiny when evaluating how lower adjudicators weighed credibility, threat severity, and relational motives behind their persecutors.

Conversely, when interpreting statutory language—such as what constitutes “political opinion” sufficient for a nexus—the court reviewed those points de novo before applying them back onto the record.

Thus, understanding which standard applies to each issue prevents students from over‑estimating either agency deference or judicial power at this stage of appellate review.

## Relevance of *Jimenez v. Bondi* for Future Asylum Appeals in the Tenth Circuit  

Although *Jimenez* resolves only this set of facts, it reinforces several doctrinal touchstones that will guide upcoming cases:

1️⃣ **Threats alone rarely satisfy “past persecution.”** The court reiterated prior Ninth Circuit holdings requiring more than sporadic threats—cumulative physical injury or sustained oppression is typically required (*Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d at 805).

2️⃣ **Nexus must be central rather than peripheral**—Political motive cannot be merely incidental where another factor—here Jimenez’s professional conflict with “Don Rafa”—drives hostility (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, supra). Future appellants must isolate documentary or testimonial evidence showing protected‑ground motive as primary rather than tangential.

3️⃣ **Government inability/unwillingness hinges on concrete reporting efforts**—Having spoken only with two low‑level officers without filing formal complaints left petitioners with insufficient proof that Colombia would—or could—not protect them (*Aviles‑Gonzalez*, No. 23‑9547).

4️⃣ **Internal relocation remains viable unless demonstrably unsafe**—Even short stays outside one’s primary residence can rebut claims that relocation is impossible (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, supra). Petitioners’ three‑week stay with family proved internal relocation feasible enough to defeat their fear argument.

By applying these precedents consistently across factual contexts, lower tribunals within Colorado, Kansas, New Mexico, Oklahoma, Utah, Wyoming—and even immigration courts elsewhere looking at Tenth Circuit authority—will likely follow *Jimenez*’s analytical template when confronting similar fact patterns involving threats tied to criminal enterprises rather than pure political expression.

In sum, this opening chapter supplies all procedural scaffolding you need before delving into *Jimenez v.’s* substantive sections on past persecution, nexus analysis, government protection duty, and safe relocation considerations—all critical components every aspiring immigration practitioner must master when reading Tenth Circuit asylum jurisprudence.

# II Factual Background and Immigration Proceedings  

In **Jimenez v. Bondi**, the Tenth Circuit reviewed a highly fact‑laden asylum case involving a Colombian family who fled violent intimidation tied to both political activity and a local narco‑guerrilla network. Understanding how the factual narrative fits into the procedural posture of U.S. immigration law is essential for any student learning to dissect an appellate opinion. This chapter walks through each element of the record that the district court judge relied upon—including who the petitioners are, what threats they faced in Colombia, how they entered the United States without inspection, and how their claims moved through removal proceedings before reaching the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and ultimately this Court of Appeals.


## Petitioners’ Family Composition, Citizenship, and Party Affiliation  

The principal petitioner is **Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez**, a native‑born citizen of Colombia who holds a *provisional* law license there. He is married to **Ángela Parada Duarte**, also a Colombian citizen, and together they have **two minor sons** whose ages are not specified in the record but who were held in “rider‑respondent” status during removal proceedings—meaning they derived any relief solely from their father’s asylum application. All four family members share Colombian citizenship and have lived continuously in Bogotá until December 2022 when they departed for the United States.

Since **2012** each member has been an active member of the **Colombian Liberal Party**, Colombia’s historic centrist party that opposed President Gustavo Petro’s left‑leaning coalition in the 2022 presidential election. Their long‑standing affiliation establishes a collective political identity that later becomes relevant when assessing whether alleged persecution was “on account of political opinion,” one of the five protected grounds enumerated in § 1101(a)(42)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (“INA”).

## Political Canvassing During the 2022 Presidential Election  

During the heated run‑up to Colombia’s June 2022 presidential contest, Jiménez, Duarte, and other Liberal Party volunteers engaged in **door‑to‑door canvassing** throughout Bogotá neighborhoods on behalf of **Federico Gutierrez**, the Liberal candidate. The campaign strategy involved personal visits to solicit votes—a standard political activity under Colombian law—but one that placed them publicly on record as opponents of Petro’s coalition government.  

Their canvassing continued unabated even after **May 2022**, when threatening telephone calls began targeting Jiménez specifically because of his party work. The persistence of campaign activity despite escalating threats underscores both their political commitment and their exposure to potential retaliation—a factual backdrop against which later claims of political persecution are evaluated.

## Threatening Phone Calls Beginning May 2022 and Escalation After a Colleague’s Kidnapping/Murder  

The first documented intimidation took shape as **frequent threatening phone calls**—roughly once or twice weekly—directed at Jiménez from unknown callers who warned him about his Liberal Party involvement. The pattern intensified dramatically after an incident in early June 2022 when a fellow Liberal activist who had also received threats was **kidnapped and subsequently murdered** despite having reported those threats to local authorities (the record does not identify any perpetrator).

Rather than curtailing their political work, Jiménez and Duarte pressed forward with campaigning throughout June–July 2022. The escalation from anonymous calls to lethal violence against an associate heightened their sense of danger but nevertheless did not immediately alter their public political conduct—a fact later relevant when courts ask whether threat motives were primarily political or otherwise rooted.



## Jiménez’s Role as a Provisional Lawyer/Intern with the National Prosecutor’s Office  

In addition to his party activism, Jiménez held dual roles within Colombia’s legal system:

1️⃣ He was a **provisional lawyer**, meaning he had completed his legal studies but had yet to achieve full licensure—a status that permitted limited practice under supervision.  
2️⃣ He served as an **intern** with Colombia’s **National Prosecutor’s Office** (“Fiscalía General de la Nación”), assisting victims affected by domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes—a position placing him at direct contact with law enforcement officials tasked with investigating organized crime.

These roles placed him at points where he could encounter sensitive investigations concerning criminal networks—including those linked—by various witnesses—to politically aligned guerrilla groups operating illicit economies such as drug trafficking.


## Property Dispute Involving “Don Rafa” – Discovery of Narco‑Trafficking Operations at Client’s Property  

In June 2022 Jiménez accepted representation from a client named **María del Carmen San Bernardo**, who approached him about a rent dispute concerning her property on Bogotá’s third floor—a property she claimed she owned outright following a divorce settlement wherein her ex‑husband transferred title to her. The client alleged that her former husband—a tenant—refused to vacate despite being out‐of‑payment for over twelve months.

When Jiménez investigated personally he learned two critical facts:

* The tenant was indeed her ex‑husband **Rafael Augusto Montañez Lancheros**, known locally by his alias “**Don Rafa**.”  
* Don Rafa headed an armed group identified by police witnesses as **“narco‑trafficking guerrillas”** politically allied with President Petro’s administration—a coalition often described in Colombian media as part “paramilitary,” part “drug cartel.”

Further on‑site inspection revealed that portions of San Bernardo’s property had been **sealed by health officials** because it operated informally as a **day‑rental hub for migrants**, simultaneously serving as a base for drug trafficking operations, prostitution rings, kidnappings, and murders—a nexus between illicit commerce and violent crime known locally enough that police had already attempted multiple searches without success. During his visit a local police officer explicitly warned Jiménez **“to stay away from the property”** (“no se meta”). This warning hinted at both criminal danger tied directly to Don Rafa’s group **and** implicit collusion—or at least acquiescence—by authorities aware yet unwilling or unable—or too constrained—to intervene.



## Specific Incidents of Threats and Violence  

The escalating hostilities culminated in four discrete episodes documented during removal proceedings:

### Brochure Threat at the Furniture Factory  

Within weeks after discovering Don Rafa’s involvement in San Bernardo’s property dispute, employees at Jiménez’s family‐owned furniture factory received a printed flyer stating ominously: *“Death to snitches from Liberal Party.”* Although no physical harm followed this distribution, it marked an explicit public intimidation linking their political affiliation with lethal consequences.


### Gunpoint Intimidation at the Factory  

In July 2022—not long after receipt of the flyer—two men entered Jiménez’s night‑shift factory brandishing guns while demanding he leave *the neighborhood*. One assailant labeled him a “snitch” working for prosecutorial authorities (referencing his internship), accusing him specifically of facilitating recent “searches and seizures” at Don Rafa's property—a direct connection between his legal work against organized crime and personal retaliation.


### Assault on Factory Employee  

Two months later *the same pair* returned while both Jiménez and his wife were traveling out‐of‑town; this time they threatened an employee who happened upon them operating machinery inside after hours. The men demanded knowledge about Petérez (“Where are Pet­ez?”); upon being told silently that Peterson's family was traveling elsewhere they departed without resorting againto lethal force but leaving behind unmistakable threats (*“You will be killed if you appear again”*) reinforced by reference that *the order came directly from Don Rafa*.  


### Attempted Abduction at School  

A further chilling incident unfolded when a man assaulted young *son* James (name withheld) outside his school—grasping him around the shoulder before clamping his hand over his mouth while insisting he *“run to your dad.”* James escaped thanks chiefly to paternal intervention wherein Jiménez physically pushed back while sprinting toward their car—a direct attempt aimed at kidnapping or murder rather than mere intimidation.


Taken together these episodes evidentially demonstrate escalating physical threat dimensions—from nonviolent written warnings through overt armed intimidation—and reveal how hostile actors blended political animus against Liberal Party supporters with personal retribution linked directly back to concerns over Jiménez’ interference with Don Rafa’s criminal interests.


## Unsuccessful Attempts to Obtain Assistance from Colombian Police Officers  

When pressed by these incidents toward protection or redress:

* After witnessing assaults inside his factory he consulted one local police patrol officer who bluntly replied *“nothing can be done”* before disclosing “police officers themselves contribute problematically.”  
* When seeking help following threats he met again only one officer who simply advised *“you should leave”* rather than offering protective custody or initiating investigation into Don Rafa or associated weaponized groups affiliated with state actors.

Crucially no formal complaint was lodged through official channels—no written report aligning recreational grievance documentation wherein authorities would demonstrably act—or any request placed before Bogotá's public prosecutor office despite Jiménez’s internship there.%¹ The absence—or perceived futility—of state protection would become central when analyzing whether Colombia was *unable or unwilling* under INA standards.

## Decision to Flee Colombia and Arrival in the United States in December 2022  

Following sustained harassment—including physical gunpoint confrontations—and considering explicit police messages indicating governmental indifference if anything else would occur if they remained behind—as well as direct attempts targeting James—the family concluded their safety could no longer be ensured within Bogotá proper nor its immediate surroundings (**three weeks living temporarily with Jiménez’ mother in rural Bogotà countryside did not alleviate fear**). Consequently they resolved permanently exit Colombia 

They departed ***December 2022*** without ever obtaining lawful admission into America—i.e., none entered through any I‑94 inspection point nor received parole from an immigration officer—and therefore arrived *without inspection.* This uninspected entry placed them squarely within Section 212(a)(6)(C)(i) grounds relating specifically to unauthorized entry chosen historically trumped any tentative presence rights.

## Issuance of Individual Notices To Appear (NTAs) and Filing Of Asylum/Withholding Applications  

Within roughly **three months** after landing—in march–April 2023—the Department of Homeland Security issued separate **Individual Notices To Appear** (“NTAs”) against each family member charging them with removability pursuant exclusively-to INA provisions contracting unauthorized entry⁽¹⁾ These NTAs initiated formal removal proceedings before an immigration judge (IJ).

During ensuing master calendar hearings Petitioner —actually primarily James’ father —in August 2023 elected *to admit* all allegations embodied within each NTA (*a tactical admission employed often by respondents seeking procedural leverage*). On that same hearing each petitioner filed two mutually exclusive relief requests:

1️⃣ An application for **asylum** under §1158(b)(1) based upon fear stemming primarily from alleged persecution tied nominally ___to political opinion___ while   
2️⃣ A parallel request for **withholding Of Removal** (§1229b(a)) which does not require discretionary grant—the burden remains establishing past persecution plus government inability/unwillingness protections—but offers higher-order protection against mandatory return .   

Both petitions listed their two minor sons as **rider respondents/derivative beneficiaries** meaning any grant would automatically extend coverage without separate filings—an established procedural shorthand within ICE practice.’¹

# III. Legal Framework Governing Asylum, Withholding of Removal, and Review Standards  

The court’s analysis in **Jimenez v. Bondi**, 2025 WL … (10th Cir.), rests on a tightly defined statutory scheme and a body of Ninth‑Circuit precedent that sets out the elements a non‑citizen must prove and the evidentiary standards the reviewing courts apply.  This chapter isolates those rules—*the refugee definition*, *the three principal forms of humanitarian relief* (asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture), *the individual elements* such as past persecution, nexus and government protection, *the internal‑relocation prerequisite*, and finally *the standards of appellate review*.  By learning each component separately you will be able to see how the Court assembled them in Jimenez when it concluded that Petitioners failed to meet any relief threshold.



## “Refugee” Definition Under INA § 1101(a)(42)(A)

The cornerstone of both asylum and withholding analyses is the statutory definition of “refugee.”  

> A **refugee** is any person who is outside any country of such person’s nationality **or** who is unable or unwilling to return to that country because… he or she has suffered **persecution** on account of **race**, **religion**, **nationality**, **membership in a particular social group**, or **political opinion**  
> *(see INA § 1101(a)(42)(A); *Niang v. Gonzales*, 422 F.3d at 1194).*  

The five protected grounds are exhaustive; an applicant must tie any harm claimed to one—or more—of them.  The definition is deliberately broad (“being outside any country”) so that a noncitizen who has fled his home country qualifies at once if he meets the persecution prong.*  

In *Jimenez* the district court identified Petitioners’ nationality (Colombian) and noted their political activity with the Liberal Party—but later held they failed either to establish actual past persecution or a nexus between that harm and their political opinions.



## “Asylum” as Discretionary Relief – Burden by Preponderance  

Statutory authority gives the Attorney General **discretion** to grant asylum once an applicant demonstrates he meets the refugee criteria.*  

> The applicant bears the **burden** of proving “by a preponderance of the evidence” that he is a refugee.*  
> *(see 8 U.S.C._§1158(b)(1); *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.​4th at 859).*  

Because asylum is discretionary—not mandatory—the government may deny relief even when an applicant satisfies all elements, though denial must still be grounded in factual findings supported by substantial evidence (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.​3d at 645).  

In practical terms the non‑citizen must show three things by more than a fifty percent likelihood:
1️⃣ The existence of past persecution  
2️⃣ That such persecution was on account of a protected ground  
3️⃣ That either he cannot obtain adequate governmental protection or he cannot safely relocate within his country.*   

If any prong fails—as it did for Petitioners—the discretion remains unexercised.



## “Withholding of Removal” – Higher Evidentiary Burden  

Unlike asylum’s discretionary nature, **withholding of removal** is a mandatory bar against deportation once its narrow statutory prerequisites are satisfied.*

> The statutory provision bars removal when an alien can demonstrate—that **by clear evidence**—that his life or freedom would be threatened on account of one of the protected grounds if he were returned.*  
> *(see INA § 1159(b); *Niang* at 1194–95).*  

The key distinction lies not only in dispensability (asylum can be denied despite eligibility) but also in an elevated evidentiary threshold often described as “clear” or “convincing” proof—higher than the mere preponderance required for asylum.* In *Jimenez* the Court emphasized that Petitioners’ inability even to establish past persecution meant they automatically failed the higher bars imposed by withholding.



## Convention Against Torture Claim – Elements (Not Reviewed)

Although Petitioners’ claim under the Convention Against Torture (“CAT”) was **not** subject to appellate review here, its statutory framework often surfaces alongside asylum arguments because it provides an alternative form‐of‐relief independent of protected‐ground analysis.*

> An alien may obtain CAT protection if he establishes—by a **preponderance**—that it is “more likely than not” that he would be tortured if removed.*  
> *(see INA §§1231A(b); *Uanreroro* discussion on procedural posture).*  

Because CAT does not require proof of a protected ground nor past persecution, it sometimes offers a lifeline when those elements are weak—but only if credible torture risk exists—a point Petitioners could not make according to both BIA and this Court.



## Past Persecution Element  

Both asylum and withholding hinge on **past persecution**—the notion that an applicant has already suffered harms qualifying as “persecution.”  

The Ninth Circuit has articulated two intertwined components:
*the factual element* – conduct constituting “persecution,” i.e., severe suffering inflicted because‑of=.  
*the legal element* – establishing that conduct satisfies INA’s protective purpose.*

Key jurisprudence clarifies what counts:

* A finding must involve more than mere threats or routine police encounters.*  
 *(see *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.​3d at 893.)*  

* Multiple low‑level incidents can combine into a single finding when their cumulative effect meets the severity threshold.*  
 *(see *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.​3d at 975.)*  

* Threats alone are generally insufficient without accompanying violence or serious injury.*  
 *(see *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.​3d at 805.)*  

Moreover, whether an act qualifies as “persecution” depends on who commits it: either state actors or private actors whom the state **cannot or will not control** (*Niang*). In *Jimenez*, despite many threats—including gunpoint confrontations—the Court concluded there was no substantial evidence that such harms rose above simple intimidation or isolated intimidation episodes into constitutional‐level persecution.



## Nexus Requirement – Political Opinion Must Be Central  

Even assuming an incident qualifies as persecution, an applicant must link it **nexus‑wise** to oneofthe protected grounds.*

> For political opinion claims “the political opinion must be ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.”  
> *(see *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.​3d at 646; *Dallakoti​ v​.Holder*, 619 F.​3d at​1369.)*

The jurisprudential test asks whether political motivation was merely incidental—or whether it was pivotal yet possibly mingled with other motives (“mixed motives”). The Court holds:
1️⃣ If another non‑protected motive dominates – claim fails (*Yuk​ v​.Ashcroft*, 355​F​​/.3d at​1236).  
2️⃣ If persecutor’s primary grievance targets a protected ground—even amid secondary motives – claim may succeed (*O.C.V​.v​.Bondi*,‍2025 WL…).  

In *Jimenez*, evidence showed threats escalated only after Mr.–Jiménez interfered with Don Rafa’s property dispute—a personal vendetta unrelated directly to party politics—so political belief was deemed peripheral (“tangential”). Accordingly there was insufficient nexus.



## Unable‑or‑Unwilling Government Protection Analysis  

Even when an applicant proves past political‐grounded persecution, relief hinges on whether his home government **cannot or will not protect him**.*

The Ninth Circuit applies two prongs:  

1️⃣ **Government’s ability** – Whether it has effective control over persecutors (**or can stop them**).   
2️⃣ **Government’s willingness** – Whether it takes reasonable steps (**or declines protection**) after notice.*

Key guidance comes from *Singh*:  
> The analysis “considers … whether … (1) the government stopped or tried to stop … , and (2) offered protection such as protective custody.” *(see Singh†91).*  

A crucial evidentiary factor is whether petitioner **reportedly sought help** from authorities—a failure undermines claims unless petitioner shows reporting would have been futile or dangerous (*Aviles‑Gonzalez​​·v​.Garland*, 2024 WL…). Courts also demand evidence indicating systemic failure rather than isolated police inertia (*Osorio‑Morales​·v​.Garland*, 72 F​·4th at​743).  

In Jimenez’s factual matrix:
*Petitioners spoke only casually with two low‑level officers who told them merely “to leave,” providing no indication governmental policy against their persecutors.*   
Thus no substantial evidence proved Colombian officials were unable—or unwilling—to shield them from Don Rafa’s criminal network.



## Internal Relocation Doctrine  

Before granting asylum—or even before denying it—the government may rebut an applicant’s fear by demonstrating that safe internal relocation is feasible.*

> Under regulation **8 C.F.R.§1208.13(b)(1)(i)** an applicant bears the burden “to show that internal relocation is unavailable.” *(cited throughout adjudicative decisions).*  

Courts assess feasibility based on factors such as family ties elsewhere in the country, economic resources available for moving, comparable safety records among regions considered safe versus dangerous regions—the totality forming an objective evaluation (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 645). 

In ***Jimenez*** petitioners simply pointed out they spent three weeks living safely with relatives outside Bogotá prior to fleeing abroad—a factular demonstration that relocation within Colombia was practicable enough for dismissal even absent deeper hardship inquiries.


## Standard of Review – Substantial Evidence for Fact Findings & De Novo for Law Questions  

When an agency decision enters appellate scrutiny via **§1252(b)(4)(A)** (the BIA’s final order), courts follow bifurcated standards:

*For questions **of fact**,* uphold agency determinations unless unsupported by "**substantial evidence**"—meaning there must be enough relevant record material that a reasonable adjudicator could accept it as adequate proof.*   
*(Rivera‑Barrientos* at ***645***).*   This deference prevents Courts from reweighing testimony submitted before immigration judges.*

*For questions **of law**, including statutory construction or applicability,* review occurs "**de novo**"—appellate courts analyze without deference.*   
*(Singh†859).*   

Consequently in *Jimenez*, factual findings concerning threats’ severity, nexus relevance and governmental response were upheld because substantial evidence existed; however any residual legal interpretations—such as whether threats qualify legally as “persecution”—were subject merely insofar as precedent authorized those determinations *de novo*.  


## Scope Of Appellate Review Under §1252(b)(4)(A) – Reliance On The Administrative Record  

Statutory language limits federal district-court jurisdiction over immigration matters importably:
> “[T]he court shall…review only … ‘the administrative record on which … an order…is based.’” *(see ***8 U.S.C.§ 1252(b)(4)(A)**).*   

Thus appellate courts cannot consider new testimony nor extrinsic evidence absent from proceedings below—that limitation aligns precisely with Supreme Court precedent emphasizing due process protections remain anchored prior‑record adjudication (*Singh†857).*

Nevertheless judges may examine explanatory memoranda accompanying decisions—for instance this Court freely consulted both BIA’s decision memorandum ***and*** Immigration Judge’s more thorough explanatory record pursuant to ***Uanreroro v．Gonzales***,* confirming BIA rulings are valid even when initially rendered through single-member affirmation.*    

Consequently Jimenez’s ultimate outcome rests squarely upon what existed inside the administrative file—not upon fresh investigatory findings {*but solely upon what record revealed during lower‑level adjudication}.*
