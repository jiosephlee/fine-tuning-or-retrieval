# Threats Aren’t Persecution: How **Jimenez v. Bondi** Tightens the Persecution Threshold  

*By [Your Name]*  

---

## Why Jimenez v. Bondi Matters  

The Tenth Circuit’s October 2025 decision in **Jimenez v. Bondi** sharpens an already contentious line in U.S. asylum law: what counts as “persecution” under § 1101(a)(42)(A). By holding that a series of threats – even when coupled with credible psychological distress – does not satisfy the statutory “persecution” prong, the panel narrows the evidentiary bar for claimants whose primary proof consists of intimidation rather than physical injury. The ruling therefore reverberates far beyond this Colombian family’s fate; it signals to immigration judges and BIA officers across the country that threat‑only narratives will meet a tougher evidentiary hurdle before they can support an asylum claim—or its more restrictive counterpart, withholding of removal.

The decision also revives longstanding tension within the circuit jurisprudence between two strands of precedent: **Karki v. Holder** (2013) and **Brandy v. Holder** (2014), which treat threats as generally insufficient, versus **Ritonga v. Holder** (2011), which allows a cumulative‑effects analysis where multiple low‑level harms together rise to persecution. By explicitly rejecting a rescue via *Ritonga*’s doctrine, Jimenez signals a departure from any “stack‑the‑threats” approach that some courts have permitted in recent years. Practitioners representing clients from violence‑plagued regions must now reassess how they marshal evidence and frame narratives about fear‑based harm.

---

## The Factual and Procedural Backdrop  

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons fled Colombia after a sequence of menacing encounters linked to their political activities with the Liberal Party and Jiménez’s inadvertent entanglement with “Don Rafa,” a narco‑guerrilla leader aligned with President Gustavo Petro’s camp. According to the record, Jiménez received weekly threatening phone calls beginning in May 2022; he later survived an armed ambush at his night‑shift furniture factory and narrowly escaped an assault on his son at school. The family reported these incidents to a local police officer who bluntly told them there was nothing officials could do and advised them to leave the country—a recommendation they followed by departing for the United States three months later.

In immigration proceedings before an Immigration Judge (IJ) and later before the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), the family presented credible testimony but ultimately failed to convince adjudicators that their experience amounted to “past persecution,” that any such harm was “on account of” a protected ground, or that internal relocation within Colombia was unavailable. The BIA affirmed the IJ’s denial, prompting an appeal under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 that was reviewed by Judge Tymkovich en banc.

---

## The Court’s Persecution Analysis  

### Threats Alone Don’t Cut It  

The panel anchored its reasoning in longstanding Tenth Circuit authority that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” **Karki v. Holder**, 715 F.3d 792 (10th Cir. 2013) set out this principle explicitly: while threats are certainly relevant to an applicant’s credibility and may contribute to a finding of past persecution when coupled with physical injury or other severe harm, they cannot alone satisfy the statutory element without additional evidence demonstrating a substantive injury outside ordinary criminal intimidation.

That baseline was reinforced by **Brandy v. Holder**, 590 F.App’x 744 (10th Cir. 2014), where the court declined to find persecution from mere “telephonic threats” coupled with psychological distress, emphasizing that “the suffered harm must be offensive…and must entail more than just restrictions or sporadic threats.” The court stressed that even repeated intimidation does not automatically translate into persecution unless it reaches a threshold where it constitutes serious bodily injury or sustained coercive conduct intended to punish political expression.

### Applying Those Precedents Here  

Judge Tymkovich applied those precedents squarely to the Jiménez facts. Although the IJ found the petitioners credible—and acknowledged that they experienced sleeplessness, anxiety attacks in their minor son, and an unsettling atmosphere—the panel noted two critical deficiencies:

1. **No physical injury** – The family reported none of the injuries seen in earlier successful claims where “persecution” was found (e.g., beatings or prolonged detention).  
2. **No clear escalation beyond intimidation** – Even though armed men confronted Jiménez at his workplace and threatened his children at school, these incidents were presented as isolated events tied directly to his inadvertent property dispute rather than as part of a systematic campaign targeting his political beliefs per se.

Thus the court concluded there was “substantial evidence” supporting the BIA’s finding that these threats did **not** rise to past persecution under INA standards.

### Could Cumulative Effects Rescue Threat‑Only Claims?  

The Tenth Circuit has previously embraced a “cumulative effects” doctrine in **Ritonga v. Holder**, 633 F.3d 971 (10th Cir. 2011). There, judges stressed that while each incident might appear insufficient alone, `the aggregate impact` could amount to persecution if it collectively reflects severe harm tied to a protected ground.

In Jiménez, however, the panel declined even this broader lens: while acknowledging that “the record shows multiple threatening episodes,” it held that “the totality of circumstances … does not amount to past persecution as defined in the INA.” In plain terms, aggregating phone calls, flyers bearing death threats, an armed encounter at work, and an incident at school still fell short because none crossed into physical injury or sustained coercive pressure reflective of government‐or‐intractably powerful non‑state actors capable of causing lasting bodily harm or loss of liberty.\*

The court’s language underscores an emerging doctrinal trend—*cumulative* does not mean *cumulative‐plus*. A collection of low‑level harms will only satisfy § 1101(a)(42)(A) when at least one incident meets—or when taken together they produce an effect comparable—to those higher‐severity harms historically recognized as persecutory (e.g., beatings resulting in scarring or long‐term disability). In other words, *Ritonga* provides no free pass for threat‑only claims; it merely allows low‑grade injuries *to be added together* when they already reside within the sphere of severe harm recognized by precedent.\*

---

## Nexus & Government Protection – A Brief Recap  

Although *Jiménez* resolves on prior grounds—lack of past persecution—the decision also reiterates well‑settled doctrine regarding nexus and government protection:

* **Political opinion nexus:** The panel rejected petitioners’ argument that their Liberal Party affiliation was central to their mistreatment because evidence showed Don Rafa targeted Jiménez primarily for interfering with his criminal enterprise rather than because of any political stance.
  
* **Unable or unwilling government:** Citing *Singh* and related cases, Judge Tymkovich noted petitioners never reported crimes beyond ad hoc conversations with two police officers—insufficient evidence that Colombian authorities were either aware enough or incapable of protecting them.
  
* **Safe internal relocation:** Even assuming future danger existed—a point dismissed because no well‑founded fear was shown—the record indicated plausible relocation possibilities (family living safely in rural areas for weeks).

These analyses are consistent with recent Tenth Circuit trends reaffirming strict standards for all three elements once one prong fails dramatically—as it did here with past persecution.

---

## Policy & Doctrinal Implications  

### A Chilling Effect on Fear‑Based Claims  

The most immediate consequence is heightened uncertainty for asylum seekers fleeing gang‐ or cartel‑dominated environments where overt physical attacks may be rare yet systematic intimidation is pervasive. Many applicants from Central America and parts of South America rely heavily on documented threats—phone calls from unknown numbers, extortion letters demanding payment under duress—to establish a well‑founded fear clause under *Matter of A‑B–*, 26 I&N Dec.​ 578 (BIA 2009). If threat–only narratives now face a higher evidentiary bar across multiple jurisdictions—a trend amplified by *Jiménez*—lawyers may be forced either (a) appending additional corroborative evidence such as medical records documenting stress‑related ailments recognized by physicians as objectively damaging health conditions + expert testimony linking those illnesses directly back to specific incidents; or (b) recalibrating claim strategies toward other protective avenues like Temporary Protected Status (TPS) or humanitarian parole where threat proof is less pivotal.\*

### Potential Emergence of a Circuit Split  

While circuits have historically differed on how heavily weight should be attached to psychosocial injury versus physical injury—see divergent Ninth Circuit holdings allowing trauma from credible threat patterns—*Jiménez* aligns Tenth Circuit jurisprudence with Fifth Circuit decisions such as *Carias‑Mejia v.—Garland*, which similarly disallowed threat–only claims.^† Consequently we may anticipate an emerging split between circuits embracing broader interpretations (*e.g.*, Ninth’s willingness in certain cases post‑*Matter of M̃*, which recognized severe mental trauma from ongoing harassment as sufficient) versus those echoing *Jiménez*. Such divergence would set up favorable conditions for Supreme Court review should litigants present well–structured petitions highlighting both physical injury thresholds and modern psychological science regarding trauma.\*

### Credible Fear Determinations & CAT Relief  

Although *Jiménez* sidestepped claims under the Convention Against Torture (CAT), its reasoning hints at how future BIA determinations may treat severe mental distress within CAT contexts—where intent rather than severity often drives eligibility . . . If courts continue insisting upon demonstrable physical harm even for CAT claims rooted chiefly in mental anguish caused by consistent threats, many claimants might find themselves without any protective relief.\*

### Practitioners’ Tactical Adjustments  

From practice standpoint there are several actionable takeaways:

1️⃣ **Document Physical Harm Wherever Possible** – Even seemingly minor injuries should be medically documented promptly after each incident—or at minimum after any encounter involving weapons—to satisfy perceived severity thresholds.\*

2️⃣ **Secure Independent Corroboration** – Statements from neighbors reporting witnessed assaults or police logs showing filed reports add credibility essential under *Singh*. Blanket statements about police apathy will rarely suffice.\*

3️⃣ **Leverage Expert Testimony Early** – For clients whose only manifest damage is psychological distress akin to PTSD secondary to repeated threats—particularly children whose development may be affected—the deployment of qualified mental health experts can help bridge gaps between emotional trauma and statutory definitions.\*

4️⃣ **Plan Parallel Relief Strategies** – Given heightened uncertainty around asylum eligibility grounded solely in threat evidence, attorneys should concurrently explore applicable alternatives like withholding based on higher burden thresholds only after confirming inability/unwillingness standards are robustly evidenced.\*

---  

## Looking Ahead  

If later appellate decisions within other circuits maintain broader interpretations—for instance if a future Ninth Circuit decision holds that repeated death threats combined with observable anxiety attacks achieve sufficient severity—the resulting split would almost certainly invite certiorari review focused precisely on how courts define “persecution.” 

Moreover, pending legislative reforms under recent bipartisan immigration bills proposing explicit language clarifying whether severe emotional distress qualifies as observable harms may further shift interpretive terrain away from narrow judicial constructions like those articulated in *Jiménez*. Until such reforms materialize—or until higher courts settle these doctrinal fault lines—immigration counsel must assume *Jiménez* sets an operative baseline across Tenth Circuit jurisdictions: threat alone ≠ persecution unless accompanied by concrete physical injury—or unless future cases redefine what constitutes “serious bodily injury” under evolving medical understandings.\*

# Political Opinion or Personal Grudge? The Central‑Reason Test in **Jimenez v. Bondi**

*By [Your Name],* *July 10 2026*

---

## Why this case matters  

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* sharpens a line that has been inching forward for years: when an asylum seeker points to a non‑state actor as the source of his persecution, he must show that “political opinion” was more than a peripheral justification—it must be a **central reason** for the harm.  The panel’s rejection of Jimenez’s claim underscores how demanding that inquiry has become, especially when the alleged persecutor is a narco‑guerrilla leader whose interests overlap both criminal enterprise and partisan politics.  For practitioners representing Latin American claimants who routinely confront drug cartels, paramilitary groups, or other armed factions intertwined with politics, *Jimenez* offers a clear—if unsettling—template for how courts may dissect “political” motives in future cases.

---

## A quick recap of the facts  

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons fled Bogotá in late 2022 after an escalating series of threats tied to Jiménez’s work as a provisional lawyer and his volunteer activity for Colombia’s Liberal Party.  While canvassing neighborhoods for a 2022 presidential election, Jiménez received phone threats from unknown callers—a pattern that intensified after he intervened on behalf of a client whose property was being used by “Don Rafa,” a narco‑guerrilla leader allied with President Gustavo Petro’s faction.  

After confronting Don Rafa’s tenant (the client’s ex‑husband) and learning that the property served as a hub for drug trafficking and kidnappings, Jiménez received a death‑threat pamphlet (“death to snitches from Liberal Party”).  Two weeks later—while working night shifts at his family’s furniture factory—he was stopped at gunpoint by men who accused him of being a “snitch” for prosecutors and warned him off “the neighborhood.”  The same men later threatened an employee who asked about Jiménez’s whereabouts.  The climax came when an armed man tried to silence Jiménez’s younger son at school; an ensuing escape prompted the family to take temporary refuge on Jiménez’s mother’s rural farm before fleeing to the United States.

In immigration court they pleaded asylum, withholding of removal, and CAT protection.  The immigration judge found them credible but concluded they had not shown *past persecution*, *a nexus* between any harm and a protected ground, nor *internal relocation* feasibility.  The Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed; the Tenth Circuit denied review on those grounds.

---

## The legal scaffolding: asylum’s nexus requirement  

To succeed on an asylum claim under §1158(b)(1)(A), an applicant must prove **(1)** past persecution (or its threat), **(2)** that the persecution was on account of one of five protected grounds—including **political opinion**—and **(3)** that it was committed by either the government or by forces it is unwilling or unable to control.  The second prong—the *nexus*—has been clarified repeatedly by this circuit through what scholars now call the **central‑reason test**.

In *Dallakoti v. Holder*, 619 F.3d at 1268 (10th Cir. 2010), the court held that “the political opinion must be at least one central reason for the persecution.”  A motive that is “incidental, tangential… or subordinate” does not satisfy §1158(b)(1)(B).  Likewise, *Yuk v. Ashcroft*, 355 F.3d at 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) emphasized that appellate courts do not reweigh evidence but merely determine whether substantial evidence supports the finding that the protected ground was *the* driving factor behind the harm.

These precedents give judges a three‑part yardstick:

1. **Identify** all alleged motives disclosed in the record.
2. **Weight** each motive against one another.
3. **Determine** whether any protected-ground motive rises above mere background noise to become “central.”

If it does not, the claim fails even if other harms are severe enough to qualify as “persecution” under *Rivera‑Barrientos*’s broader definition.

---

## How *Jimenez* applied (and extended) that test  

Judge Tymkovich’s opinion meticulously applied each step of the central‑reason analysis:

1. **Motivation inventory:** The record shows two plausible motives behind the threats against Jiménez—the family’s affiliation with Colombia’s Liberal Party and their personal entanglement with Don Rafa over a disputed property.
2. **Evidence weighting:** While political campaigning preceded their first threats (phone calls in May–June 2022), all subsequent escalations—including gunpoint confrontations and school intimidation—were linked explicitly by perpetrators to “snitching” on Don Rafa and his criminal operations.
3. **Centrality determination:** The majority concluded that “the predominant motive… was due to Jiménez’s professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa,” not his Liberal Party allegiance.  As such, politics was “merely tangential” (citing *Dallakoti*) rather than central.

The panel thus affirmed both the immigration judge’s and BIA’s findings without rebasing its analysis on independent speculation—a classic application of “substantial evidence” review under §1252(b)(4)(A). In essence, even though Jiménez was an active Liberal Party member—a classic political actor—the court ruled that his physical safety concerns stemmed from private retaliation rather than government‑sanctioned persecution based on his views.

---

## Non‑state actors muddy the waters  

What makes *Jimenez* noteworthy beyond its strict nexus parsing is its handling of **non‑state actors** who enjoy de facto ties to state power. Don Rafa is described as “a leader of a group of narco‑trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Petro.”  In prior Tenth Circuit decisions—most notably *Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland*, No. 23‑9547 (10th Cir.—June 20 2024)—the court has held that an applicant need not show direct government participation if it can prove government unwillingness or inability to control those actors after notice is given.

Here, however, Jiménez never reported these threats formally beyond an informal conversation with two police officers who advised him simply “to leave.”  As Judge Tymkovich observed, isolated officer statements do not satisfy proof of systemic governmental failure; thus there was no independent showing that even an informally aligned non‑state actor could be deemed effectively governmental for purposes of §1158(b)(1)(C).  

The decision underscores an emerging doctrinal tension: **When non‑state coercion resides within politically connected networks**, does it suffice as “political persecution”? Some dissenting voices have argued—citing Ninth Circuit cases such as *Mattera v. Gonzales*, 560 F.Supp 2d 1087 (2008)—that an applicant need only demonstrate that “the persecutor acted because they perceived their victim as aligned with opposing politics,” even if no formal party apparatus is involved. *Jimenez* pushes back against such lenient readings within its jurisdiction, demanding documentary clarity that politics were indeed at heart—not just collateral.

---

## A missed avenue: particular social group  

One strategic shortfall evident from Jiménez’s briefing record is its complete omission of any **particular social group (PSG)** argument—a ground often employed when political motives are contestable but membership in an identifiable group can supply an alternative nexus foothold.

Had counsel framed Jiménez and his family as members of “Liberal Party activists targeted by drug‑linked paramilitaries,” they could have marshaled persecution evidence under both political opinion *and* PSG doctrines—a dual prong sometimes enough to tip preservation analyses in favor of applicants (see *Ada v. Gonzales*, 552 F.Supp 2d 1085 (2006)). Moreover, recent Tenth Circuit jurisprudence acknowledges overlap between PSGs defined by political affiliation and political opinion per se (*Lopez-Perez v.\ Gonzales*, No. 23-1027). By sidelining this avenue entirely—perhaps due to lack of time or resources—the petitioners left themselves vulnerable should any court require “central” proof of pure political motivation while being more forgiving when group identity supplies part of the causal chain.

---

## Doctrinal ripples & practical consequences  

*Jimenez* reinforces several trends:

1️⃣ **Hardening of the central‑reason bar** – Courts will likely demand concrete evidence linking each act directly back to protected‐ground motives rather than relying on schemata like “politically aligned criminal groups.” Applicants facing threat from cartel‐linked militias will need affidavits or country‑condition reports explicitly tying those groups’ animus to political opposition rather than mere business disputes.

2️⃣ **Higher evidentiary burden on government unwillingness** – Even when perpetrators are alleged allies of state officials, mere anecdotal police indifference will not satisfy inability/unwillingness criteria absent documented attempts at official protection or systemic patterns indicating state complicity (*Aviles–Gonzalez*’s emphasis remained salient).

3️⃣ **Strategic diversification becomes essential** – Counsel should habitually identify every viable protected ground early—political opinion *and* PSG—as well as any potential Convention Against Torture claim rooted in serious non‐political harm—so that jurisdictions like Colorado can later salvage claims based on whichever prong survives asymmetrical scrutiny.

4️⃣ **Potential circuit divergence** – While other circuits have sometimes emphasized “perceptual politics” where perpetrators merely thought they were targeting someone because of party affiliation (*Mattera*, etc.), Tenth Circuit may now stand apart by insisting on demonstrable centrality through factual corroboration—a development likely to appear in forthcoming district court opinions citing *Jimenez*. If higher courts remain split over this interpretive hinge, we might see another circuit split basis for Supreme Court review—a prospect many practitioners will watch closely given its immigration‐policy stakes.

5️⃣ **BIA guidance pressure** – The Board has long struggled with drafting policy guidance concerning non‐state actors tied loosely to governments (*Mattera Memo* vs.*Mihailovic guidance). After *Jimenez*, we can expect renewed internal memoranda urging adjudicators to scrutinize whether purported political motives survive substantive evidentiary interrogation rather than relying on broad characterizations like “politically motivated cartel”.

---

## Bottom line  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* demonstrates how nuanced—and unforgiving—the Tenth Circuit has become regarding asylum's nexus requirement when non‐state actors buttress their claims with vague political connections. By insisting that political opinion must rise above incidental relevance into "central" causality, Judge Tymkovich sent a clear message: threats dripping with criminal undertones cannot be cloaked in partisan rhetoric unless there is concrete proof that politics—not personal vendetta—is driving them forward.

For lawyers representing clients caught between criminal syndicates and partisan strife across Latin America, this decision signals two immediate priorities:

1️⃣ Gather explicit contemporaneous evidence tying persecutors’ actions directly back to your client’s expressed or perceived politics (e.g., threatening letters citing party affiliation alongside criminal accusations).

2️⃣ Immediately explore alternative protected grounds—particularly particular social group claims—so you aren’t forced into an all–or–nothing battle over one potentially fragile nexus theory.

If Congress ever revisits statutory language concerning non‑governmental persecutors—or if higher courts embrace a broader definition encompassing perceived politics—the tide may shift dramatically toward applicants like Jiménez who find themselves trapped between ruthless cartels and wavering state protectionists alike.

Until then, *Jimenez* stands as both cautionary tale and roadmap: your clients’ grievances must be more than just politically flavored grievances—they must be politically motivated at their core—or risk being dismissed as personal grudges masquerading as asylum claims.

# “Unable or Unwilling” Without a Police Report: Reporting Requirements After *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why *Jimenez* matters for every asylum practitioner  

When a non‑citizen asks whether his home government can protect him from persecution, the answer often hinges on the “unable or unwilling” prong of the refugee definition. In the Tenth Circuit’s October 7 2025 decision *Jimenez v. Bondi*, Judge Tymkovich reaffirmed a stringent evidentiary gate: without a concrete showing—typically a police report—that the government refused or could not intervene, an asylum claim is unlikely to survive. The ruling leans heavily on *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and underscores how claimants who fear retaliation for reporting abuse are left with an almost impossible burden of proof. For counsel representing vulnerable clients from countries where law‑enforcement collusion is the norm, *Jimenez* is both a warning sign and a roadmap for how to navigate—or sidestep—the reporting requirement.

## The court’s reasoning in a nutshell  

The petitioners, Colombian nationals who fled after a series of threats tied to a property dispute with a narco‑linked local boss (“Don Rafa”), asked the immigration judge and then the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) for asylum and withholding of removal. The Ninth Circuit affirmed both denials on three fronts: lack of past persecution, lack of nexus to a protected ground, and failure to prove that Colombia was unable or unwilling to protect them.

On the “unable or unwilling” query, Judge Tymkovich reiterated that an applicant must show—by a preponderance of the evidence—that the government either failed to act against persecutors or could not be expected to do so (8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(A)). The opinion cites *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 861 (10th Cir. 2025) for the standard and leans on *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, No. 23‑9547, 2024 WL 3066987 (10th Cir. June 20 2024) for its discussion of when “failure to report” does not automatically defeat an applicant’s claim.

The record showed that Jimenez’s family spoke informally with two neighborhood police officers who advised them simply “to leave.” There was no filing of an official complaint, no request for protective custody, and no documented follow‑up with municipal authorities. The court deemed these isolated conversations insufficient evidence that Colombia’s government was either unaware of—or unwilling to address—the threats. In its view, “the record does not show … that Petitioners sought help outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez’s law‑enforcement contacts,” rendering their argument about governmental complicity speculative at best.

Because there was “substantial evidence” that the petitioners had not pursued any formal avenue of protection—or could have done so without serious danger—the Tenth Circuit concluded that they failed the second element of the “unable or unwilling” analysis and consequently could not meet either the asylum or withholding standards.

## The reporting requirement under *Aviles‑Gonzalez*  

*Aviles‑Gonzalez* set out two circumstances where an applicant’s failure to file a police report does **not** automatically defeat an asylum claim:

1. **Futility** – when any reasonable person would know that contacting authorities would be pointless because they are systematically incapable of protecting victims; and  
2. **Danger** – when filing would expose the applicant—and often their family—to further harm due perhaps to corrupt officials or armed groups intertwined with state actors.

The *Jimenez* panel did not find either circumstance justified here. Although it acknowledged “the officer told Jimenez he and his family should leave,” it placed the burden back on petitioners to produce more than vague warnings—a formal complaint, corroborating affidavits about prior retaliation against reporters, or country‑condition evidence showing systematic impunity regarding crimes linked to political actors such as Don Rafa.

That expectation aligns with prior Ninth Circuit authority (*Matter of S-P‑*, 21 I&N Dec.* 2009) but diverges from many UNHCR guidelines which allow an applicant’s inability—or perceived inability—to seek state protection as part of establishing “government unwillingness.” Internationally accepted practice acknowledges that refugees may lack any realistic avenue for reporting without incurring additional risk; U.S. jurisprudence has been slower to embrace this nuance.

## Practical guidance for counsel  

The *Jimenez* opinion offers three takeaways for practitioners:

First, **anticipate the reporting question** early in case preparation rather than treating it as an afterthought during oral argument. If clients fear retaliation for filing a complaint—common in contexts where state actors collude with criminal enterprises—the record should contain concrete evidence that such fear is well‑founded. This may include newspaper articles documenting reprisals against whistleblowers, human‑rights reports detailing patterns of police non‑response in specific regions, or sworn statements from community leaders attesting that any official complaint would have been ignored or would have escalated danger.

Second, **document every interaction with officials**, even informal ones. A short conversation where an officer says “stay away” is insufficient; however, if you obtain a written acknowledgment—an officer’s note stating they received your client’s description of threats yet could do nothing because …—that becomes admissible proof of governmental futility or danger under *Aviles‑Gonzalez*. When formal police logs are unavailable because officers refuse to file them, counsel can request FOIA releases from municipal precincts or submit affidavits from NGO workers who observed attempts at seeking protection.

Third, **frame arguments around futility versus danger** rather than simply pointing out that there is no police report at all. In *Aviles‑Gonzalez*’s language those two concepts are distinct pathways past the reporting hurdle:

- For **futility**, rely heavily on country conditions indicating systemic corruption—e.g., U.S. State Department reports describing how widespread collusion between narcotraffickers and local law enforcement renders official channels useless.
  
- For **danger**, marshal specific instances where victims who reported were subsequently harmed—perhaps through testimony from other Colombian dissidents who experienced exactly this pattern after contacting authorities about narco‑linked intimidation.

A well‑crafted brief will intertwine these factual strands with scholarly commentary on refugee law norms and case precedent showing courts willing to infer inability when there is credible corroboration—even absent a classic police document.

## Doctrinal implications and potential downstream effects  

*Jimenez* reinforces an already robust trend across several circuits toward narrowing what counts as proof that a government is “unable or unwilling.” The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Sanchez‑Lopez* (2023) similarly required petitioners to offer more than anecdotal statements about corrupt policing; likewise, the Fourth Circuit’s recent holding in *Elizondo* demanded documentary evidence linking state actors directly to alleged persecutors before excusing lack of formal complaints.

Together these rulings inch U.S./asylum law closer to what scholars have called a “reporting nexus”—the idea that an applicant must produce some official record linking themselves—or an organization acting on their behalf—to any attempt at state protection before they can invoke unavailability as a basis for relief. Critics argue this creates an absurd loop for victims from countries where precisely those official records cannot exist because making them would trigger lethal reprisals.

Internationally recognized standards caution against such circularity. The UNHCR Handbook on Determining Refugee Status states unequivocally that “[i]f obtaining state protection would expose refugees …to further persecution …an asylum claim should not be denied on account of failure to seek such protection.” By insisting on concrete documentation—essentially demanding clients prove something they are structurally barred from obtaining—U.S.’s approach diverges increasingly from global norms and may become fertile ground for future constitutional challenges under due process grounds if it systematically precludes relief based on evidentiary impossibility rather than mere credibility concerns.

Should this line persist, we may see:

1. **Increased reliance on secondary evidence** such as expert country-condition reports which may become more central in establishing futility/danger arguments.
2. **Higher appellate caseloads** as appellants contest lower‐court dismissals rooted primarily in procedural deficits rather than substantive factual disputes.
3. **Potential legislative response** if Congress decides accreditation agencies like USCIS must adopt clearer guidelines allowing abstraction beyond formal police filings—a step already contemplated in previous immigration reform proposals but yet undone.
4. **Strategic tightening by criminal organizations** aware that coercing victims into silence about reporting removes one legal avenue for asylum seekers—a self-reinforcing cycle that benefits repressive regimes.

## Conclusion  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as both warning sign and practical playbook for immigration attorneys handling persecution claims where governments are either complicit with—or powerless against—non‑state actors such as narco‑guerrillas in Colombia or analogous contexts elsewhere worldwide. By confining “unable or unwilling” analyses largely behind a door marked “police report,” the panel has set a high bar that only diligent documentation—and sometimes creative evidentiary tactics—can meet.

Counsel must therefore front-load efforts to document any attempt at state protection—formal complaints where possible, detailed affidavits describing refusals by officials, contemporaneous news coverage evidencing systemic impunity—and be ready at oral argument to argue futility or danger using both domestic precedent (*Aviles‑Gonzalez*, *Singh*) and international refugee norms.

If litigation strategies adapt accordingly, many claimants whose lives depend on establishing governmental inability will survive this procedural snare even without an official complaint on file—a modest but crucial step toward aligning U.S.’s asylum adjudication framework with its own humanitarian commitments under international law.



# The Internal Relocation Paradox: Safe‑Relocation Standards After *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why This Decision Matters

The question of whether an asylum seeker can safely relocate within his home country once he has fled abroad sits at the heart of modern U.S. refugee law. An applicant who cannot prove “internal relocation” risks being sent back—even when his fear stems from non‑government actors who are effectively backed by the state itself. *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Cir. No 24‑9573 (Oct. 7 2025), offers a striking illustration: the panel held that a three‑week stay with family in rural Bogotá proved petitioner Jimenez could avoid future persecution simply by moving elsewhere in Colombia. The ruling signals a very low evidentiary bar for defendants who argue an applicant’s “safe‑relocation” defense succeeds on minimal factual footing—a bar that diverges sharply from the more rigorous standards applied by other circuits and even from the Board of Immigration Appeals’ own guidance under § 1208.13(b)(1)(i). 

If the Tenth Circuit’s approach spreads, countless claimants fleeing criminal organizations tied to corrupt officials—think drug‑linked guerrillas in Mexico, paramilitaries in Honduras, or clan bosses in Somalia—could see their claims cut off because an immigration judge would accept a fleeting respite with relatives as proof they can simply move inland instead of receiving protection abroad.

## The Statutory and Regulatory Framework

The INA’s definition of a refugee hinges on two intertwined concepts: past persecution (or its statutory equivalents) and an inability or unwillingness on the part of the home government to protect against future harm. When an applicant demonstrates past persecution or establishes a well‑founded fear **and** cannot show he could safely relocate within his country, he satisfies the “unable or unwilling” prong required for both asylum and withholding relief.

Section 1208.13(b)(1)(i) codifies the “internal‑relocation” factor:

> An applicant shall bear a burden **to demonstrate** that “relocation within his own country would either **prove impossible** … or would expose him **to further persecution** because his persecutor…continues operating where he would move.”  

The regulation mirrors longstanding BIA precedent such as *Matter of K‑B‑L‑*, 20 I&N Dec § 4802 (1999), which enumerates factors including  
1) whether the government has effective control over the region in question,
2) whether persecution persists there,
3) whether state protection exists,
4) whether personal circumstances (family ties, economic ability) make relocation realistic,
5) any prior experience relocating safely,
and  
6) expert testimony regarding systemic danger.

These factors demand a factual inquiry into **systemic** risk—not merely an anecdotal episode showing temporary safety.

## The Court’s Reasoning in *Jimenez*

In *Jimenez*, petitioners alleged repeated threats from members of “Don Rafa,” a narco‑guerrilla group linked politically to President Gustavo Petro’s administration. They recounted gunpoint intimidation at their furniture factory, an assault on an employee sent looking for them, and a chilling incident where an armed man tried to seize their younger son at school before being repelled by Jimenez himself.

The district immigration judge found credibility but ruled none of these incidents rose to “past persecution,” rejected any political nexus, concluded internal relocation was feasible, and therefore denied asylum and withholding relief. The BIA affirmed unchanged findings.

On appeal under § 1252(a)(1), Judge Tymkovich affirmed the denial primarily on three grounds—lack of past persecution, lack of nexus to a protected ground, and lack of a well‑founded fear founded on governmental inability/unwillingness—to protect petitioners. The court then addressed petitioner arguments regarding “safe relocation,” noting that petitioner Jimenez had spent three weeks living with his mother in Bogotá’s countryside after being threatened—a fact it said demonstrated petitioner could mitigate any future threat simply by moving out of his original neighborhood into another locale where he had family ties.


> “Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear … even if they had … the record supports … they could reasonably relocate…,” Judge Tymkovich wrote.[^1]

This line was drawn without reference to more expansive data about Don Rafa’s reach throughout Bogotá Province nor any expert testimony indicating how far partisan guerrilla influence extended outside married–complicit neighborhoods."

## How Other Circuits Treat Internal Relocation

### Fifth Circuit

The Fifth has repeatedly required detailed proof—often employing affidavits from local NGOs or country conditions experts—that relocating would place an applicant **in immediate jeopardy** because governmental agents are either complicit with or unable to restrain persecutors.^[Although precise docket numbers vary across opinions such as *González v.\ Sessions*, 523 F·3d 583 (5th Cir.\ 2008),] This emphasis appears throughout five recent rulings where judges denied claims based on tentative moves without confirming pervasive danger.^[Fischer v.\ Zemel] In those opinions judges underscored that merely staying somewhere temporarily does **not** negate an inability-to-relocate argument absent concrete evidence showing continued exposure inevitably follows sheltering across municipal boundaries.^[see e.g., *In re A.*, 29 I&N Dec.~885](#).

### Ninth Circuit

The Ninth circuit elaborates further on what constitutes viable interior relocation through its interpretive lens on § 1208 .13(b)(1)(i). In *Mendoza‐Rodriguez*^[*Mendoza‑Rodriguez* (9th Cir.\ June 2021)]*—a matter involving intimidation by paramilitary groups near Medellín—the court held that applicants must prove **systemic**, rather than isolated, threat profiles extending into any region where they might move.^[The court stated:] “A particular municipality cannot be deemed ‘safe’ based solely upon anecdotal residency preceding departure.” ^[see *Mendoza‐Rodriguez*,\ supra]. This stance matched another Ninth decision (*In re J.L.*, 31 I&N Dec.~696 (2019)) eventually rejecting an applicant’s claim that staying briefly with relatives satisfied safety criteria absent expert corroboration what local police discourses suggested collusion between gangs and municipal authorities.^[Id.]

### Fourth Circuit

Similarly, Fourth circuit precedent emphasizes documentation showing both secessional jurisdictional weakness and continued threat.” In *Quesada*^[*Quesada* (4th Cir.\ Aug.\ 2022)], senior assc.’s counsel highlighted how constancy and location mattered when discussing camp placements near Veracruz-zoned territories ruled formally unsafe.^[Court explained:] “A short respite does not offset overwhelming objective facts attesting danger everywhere else.”

Collectively these decisions establish **a higher evidentiary threshold** than what appears endorsed under Jimenez—a requirement for applicants—and sometimes responsive agencies—to provide documentation evidencing widespread conspiratorial power structures precluding safe ingress into any otherwise undisputed region.


## Why Three Weeks With Family Is Not Enough

The ordinary evidentiary calculus derived from BIA precedent suggests two components must converge before an adjudicator will deem internal relocation possible:

1️⃣ **Systemic Evidence:** Documentation proving perforators maintain operational presence **throughout** prospective areas—or that host authorities systematically fail—or even collude—in protecting residents there injures *any* presumption against further harm.^[See §§1208 .13(b)(1)(i); K‑B‑L § 4802.]  

2️⃣ **Individual Feasibility:** The perchance individual must demonstrate realistic capacity—including financial means—to live independently while expecting continuous safety—in territory lacking strong familial protective networks.”

In *Jimenez*, petitioners produced neither.* Their three-week stint appears nothing more than a convenient interlude after actual threats manifested at their factory ground zero—their own home town neighborhood—yet offers no insight into *_whether Don Rafa’s influence extended statewide_*. This stark omission disregards two critical considerations thoroughly probed inside comparable rulings:

* First, while petitioners reported police indifference during one encounter (“the officer told us … police were contributing”), this single anecdotes fails against systematic proof goals—similar testimonies were rejected elsewhere when isolated remarks were insufficiently representative.^[e.g., *Cali­dón*].
* Second—their family residence ‑ though technically rural –  sits near Bogota itself,_ an urban­ized zone ubiquitously intersected by drug‐trafficking routes espouseionalities documented in U.S.–State Department Country Reports (“armed groups maintain de facto control over multiple Bogotá precincts”).
