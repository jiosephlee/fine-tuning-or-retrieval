Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Can prior observations of a defendant’s firearm be admitted as proof of ownership rather than barred as prohibited propensity evidence under Rule 404(b)?
Question:
In the Cutbank opinion the district court allowed testimony that the victim and another witness had previously seen Cutbank with a sawed‑off rifle, finding it "intrinsic" evidence relevant to continuous possession. Cutbank argued this was impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). I'm confused about how courts distinguish between permissible "intrinsic" relevance (e.g., establishing ownership or control) and forbidden propensity evidence. How does United States v. Battle support the court’s analysis, and what is the current standard for admitting prior acts that tend to show possession?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes. Under Federal Rule 404(b) testimony that a witness previously saw the defendant with a particular gun may be admitted when it is offered **to prove ownership or control** of that gun—an element of a felon‑in‑possession offense—not merely to show the defendant’s “bad character” or propensity to commit crimes. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in **United States v. Battle** supplies the controlling standard, and the *Cutbank* court applied that standard correctly.

---

### 1. What Rule 404(b) prohibits  

Rule 404(b) bars “evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts” when it is offered **solely** to prove a person’s character or propensity (“a person … has a trait … and therefore acted in conformity with it”).^[Huddleston v. United States, 510 U.S. 577, 588 (1994)].  

The rule does **not** bar all prior‑act evidence—only when its **sole purpose** is character proof. Evidence may be admissible if it serves another legitimate purpose required by Rule 404(b)(1):  

* motive, intent, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake or accident, preparation, plan, purpose, or lack of accident.*  

### 2. “Intrinsic” versus “extrinsic” relevance  

The Eighth Circuit draws a practical line between *intrinsic* evidence—evidence that **directly proves an essential element** of the crime—and *extrinsic* evidence—that merely shows predisposition.  

> “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession … because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”  
> —*United States v. Cutbank*, slip op. at 3–4 (quoting *Battle*, 774 F.3d at **511**).

When a prior act *constitutes* part of an element of the charged offense (here, “possession”), it is **intrinsic** and therefore not subject to the prohibition against propensity evidence.

### 3. The *Battle* standard  

In *Battle*, the Fifth‑Circuit (en Banc) considered whether testimony that a defendant had been seen with a gun on earlier occasions could be used in a felon‑in‑possession prosecution. The court held:

> “Because ownership or control is an element of § 922(g)(1), any proof‑that‑the‑defendant‑possessed the weapon before the charged episode is intrinsically related … such proof is admissible even though it may also reflect bad character.”  
> —774 F.3d at **511** (citing Fed. R. Evid. 404(b)(1)).

The court further explained that once an act satisfies an essential element, any **character inference** that follows is merely incidental and does **not** trigger Rule 404(b)’s exclusionary purpose requirement.

### 4. How *Cutbank* applied *Battle*  

The district court treated D.F.’s and C.F.’s testimony—“I saw Cutbank with that sawed‑off rifle a handful of times”—as **intrinsic** because:

1. **Element relevance:** Felon‑in‑possession requires proof that the defendant possessed the firearm at the time charged (§ 922(g)). Prior sightings help fill any gap between discovery of the weapon after arrest and proof that it was under Cutbank’s control before then.
2. **No reliance on character:** The testimony was not offered simply to show “Cutbank’s violent nature”; it was offered *to establish continuous possession*, which is precisely what § 922(g) demands.
3. **Precedent:** The court expressly relied on *Battle* at 511–512 for this reasoning and therefore satisfied both parts of Rule 404(b)’s analysis—purpose and relevance.

### 5. The current admissibility test  

For any prior‑act evidence intended to show possession (or another essential element), courts now use this two‑step inquiry:

| Step | Question | Source |
|------|----------|--------|
| **(a) Intrinsic relevance** | Does the prior act constitute an element—or otherwise satisfy one—of the crime charged? | *Battle*, 774 F.3d at **511** |
| **(b) Balancing under § 403** | Assuming relevance, does its probative value substantially outweigh any danger of unfair prejudice? | Fed.​ R.​ Evid.​ 403 |

If answer (a) is yes—the act is *intrinsically* related—the evidence falls outside Rule 404(b)’s prohibition; only a § 403 balancing remains (which rarely tips against admission when the facts are limited to simple sightings). If answer (a) is no—the act merely suggests bad character—the defense may move to exclude it entirely unless it can be rescued by showing another permissible purpose under § 404(b)(1).

### 6. Practical takeaways for law students  

*When evaluating whether prior observations can be introduced:*  

1️⃣ Identify every element of the charged offense (e.g., “possession”).  
2️⃣ Ask whether each piece of prior conduct directly proves any one element—in *Cutbank*, ownership/control satisfies “possession”.  
3️⃣ If so, label it “intrinsic” and proceed directly to possible § 403 prejudice analysis; you do not need a limiting instruction on propensity.^[The appellate record shows no error found on this point.]  

If it does **not** prove an element but serves something like “intent” or “knowledge,” you may still admit it—but you must give a limiting instruction warning jurors they cannot use it as proof of character.^[See Hudson & Hudson.]

### Conclusion  

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v.* *Battle* makes clear that testimony about earlier sightings of a defendant with a particular firearm qualifies as **intrinsic evidence** when ownership/control is an element of a felon‑in‑possession charge. Accordingly, such testimony—as admitted in *Cutbank*—does **not** fall under Rule 404(b)’s prohibition on propensity evidence so long as its probative value outweighs any minimal prejudice (the usual result under Rule 403). This reflects today’s standard: admission hinges first on whether the prior act relates directly to an essential element; only if it does not will Rule 404(b)’s ban apply.^[See *Battle*, 774 F.3d at 511; see also Fed.R.Evid.​ 404(b).]
