Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Is it proper for a trial judge to skip a limiting instruction on hearsay statements made by a victim to police, relying instead on post‑trial directions?
Question:
The opinion notes that Cutbank wanted a jury instruction preventing consideration of D.F.’s statements made to police until she testified herself, citing Confrontation Clause concerns. The government argued—and the court agreed—that such an instruction could be given at trial’s close per United States v. Bennett. Does precedent really allow judges to defer limiting instructions until after all evidence is presented? Under what circumstances must an evidentiary judge give an immediate limiting instruction for out‑of‑court statements?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – under *United States v.* Bennett (765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014)) an Eighth Circuit trial court may postpone a “limiting” instruction on out‑of‑court statements until it gives an overall closing instruction, provided the court has **not actually admitted** those statements as substantive evidence during trial. An immediate limiting instruction is required only when the statement has already been introduced for its truth‑value (or otherwise carries a real risk of being used substantively) before the declarant testifies, so that a later “catch‑all” direction would be ineffective at curing any prejudice.

---

### The controlling doctrine

1. **Bennett’s “no‑need‑for‑item‑by‑item” rule**  
   The Eighth Circuit holds that an appellate court reviews a district court’s refusal to give a limiting instruction **under an abuse‑of‑discretion standard** and that *“the court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.”*【United States v. Bennett, 765 F.3d at 899】.

2. **Confrontation Clause context**  
   The Court also reminds us that the Sixth Amendment is satisfied once the out‑of‑court declarant appears in person and testifies—*United States v.* Charboneau (613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010)). If that occurs during trial, there is no longer any Confrontation problem requiring an interim instruction.

3. **Application in *Cutbank***  
   In *Cutbank* the appellate panel notes two facts that make a deferred direction permissible:  

   - **No hearsay was actually admitted** before D.F.’s live testimony (“the record demonstrates … did not admit hearsay during the trial”).  
   - **The Confrontation Clause was satisfied** once D.F. testified (“the Confrontation Clause is satisfied…when…the declarant …testify in person”).  

   Consequently the district court’s choice to wait until its closing charge was within its discretion and did not constitute reversible error【Bennett]【Charboneau】.

---

### When must an evidentiary judge give an *immediate* limiting instruction?

Although *Bennett* allows postponement, it implicitly limits that discretion:

| Situation | Why an immediate limit is required |
|-----------|-------------------------------------|
| **The statement has already been introduced as substantive evidence** (e.g., an officer reads victim’s police report into evidence for its truth). | Jurors have heard the out‑of‑court declaration before any cross‑examination; a later general instruction cannot erase whatever impression has already been formed. |
| **The statement is highly probative of a key issue yet unavailable for cross‑exam** (e.g., victim’s description of the crime used to prove elements). | The risk of unfair prejudice outweighs any benefit from delaying clarification (Rule 403). |
| **The defense timely requests a limiting direction and there is no other convenient way to neutralize potential misuse** | The judge’s duty to protect against wrongful reliance demands prompt clarification rather than waiting for “the close”. |
| **The district court actually admits hearsay outside any recognized exception** | Admission itself is reversible error unless corrected by an on‑point limiting direction given at the time of admission (see *Bennett*’s abuse‑of‑discretion standard). |

In short, **deferral is proper only when there has been *no* improper admission**—i.e., when nothing substantive derived from the out‑of‑court statement has yet entered the evidentiary record.

---

### Practical takeaways for practitioners

1. **Ask for a limiting instruction as soon as you hear an out‑of‑court statement being offered**, even if you think a closing charge will suffice. Prompt request preserves error if the judge decides incorrectly.
2. **If you can show that any such statement will be used substantively before your witness testifies**, argue that *Bennett* does not excuse postponement because an early limitation is necessary to avoid juror confusion or prejudice.
3. **If nothing “hearsay” has actually been entered—only background references—to which you object**, you are likely on solid ground in relying on *Bennett*’s allowance for a post‑trial closing direction, as was affirmed in *Cutbank*.

Thus, under precedent cited in *Cutbank*, skipping an item–by–item limiting instruction and saving a catch‑all direction for closing is permissible **so long as no inadmissible out‑of‑court statement has been introduced** prior to that point; otherwise, an immediate limiting charge becomes obligatory.【United States v. Bennett】【United States v. Charboneau】
