Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What standard do appellate courts use when reviewing adequacy of a federal criminal indictment?
Question:
I am reading United States v. Constantinescu where the Fifth Circuit says it reviews sufficiency 'de novo.' I am not clear on what this standard entails. Specifically, does the appellate court treat every allegation in an indictment as true? What elements must be alleged for an offense such as 18 U.S.C. § 1348? How does concern about 'double jeopardy defense' factor into assessing whether an indictment is sufficient? I would appreciate an explanation.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
On direct‑review appeals a federal appellate court looks at a criminal indictment **de novo** – it does not defer to the district court’s view and it “takes the allegations…as true.”  The court then asks whether, **on its face**, the charging document **alleges every prima‑facie element** of the offense and whether it does enough to give the defendant notice **and a double‑jeopardy shield** against future prosecutions on the same conduct.  

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation based on *United States v. Constantinescu*, 5th Cir. (2025).

---

### 1. What “de novo” means in this context

> “The court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”  
> *United States v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d at 644 (quoting *United States v. Crow*, 164 F.3d at 234) – cited in *Constantinescu* at §§ II‑III.

The Fifth Circuit therefore **does not evaluate evidence** or decide whether the alleged conduct actually occurred.  Its job is limited to asking: *does the pleading contain enough factual material, taken at face value, to satisfy each legal element of the crime?*  

Because this is a **facial sufficiency** inquiry, any doubt is resolved in favor of upholding the indictment; only a clear omission of an essential element will warrant dismissal.

---

### 2. The three statutory requirements for a sufficient indictment

*Constantinescu* recites the Fifth Circuit’s “well‑settled” rule from *United States v. Gaytan*:

> An indictment must “…(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.”  
> *Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551 (cited verbatim).

Thus an appellate court checks three things:

| Requirement | What the court looks for | Why it matters |
|-------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| **(1) Enumerate every element** | The pleading must contain factual allegations that could support each element if proven true. | Guarantees that no essential piece—e.g., *scheme* or *intent*—is omitted, which would make conviction legally impossible. |
| **(2) Fair notice** | The language must be clear enough for a reasonable person to understand what conduct is prohibited. | Satisfies due‑process rights; the defendant can prepare a defense. |
| **(3) Double‑jeopardy protection** | The charge must be specific enough that future prosecutions cannot rest on a different theory based on the same conduct. | Prevents multiple punishments for one criminal act under separate statutes or theories. |

---

### 3. How this plays out for **18 U.S.C. § 1348** (securities fraud)

*Constantinescu* extracts from Supreme Court precedent what elements constitute § 1348:

1️⃣ **Scheme to defraud** – A “material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.”  
2️⃣ **Intent to defraud** – An “intent … to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.”  

(The opinion notes these are borrowed from mail‑ and wire‑fraud jurisprudence.)

Therefore, a **sufficient § 1348 indictment must allege**:

* That defendants **engaged in deception** (e.g., posting false statements about a security).  
* That they **intended** that victims rely on those false statements **to part with money or property** (i.e., buy securities).  
* That they **sought**—or actually obtained—the victims’ money/property (**profit motive**), which satisfies both *scheme* and *intent* requirements.

In *Constantinescu* the court finds those facts present in ¶¶ 13‑14 (“false … information … so that other investors were induced …”) and thus concludes that “the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud.”

---

### 4. Does every allegation get treated as true?

Yes—*but only for purposes of checking whether each legal element appears somewhere in the charging document.* The court does not weigh credibility or decide whether any particular allegation is factual; it merely asks whether **any allegation on its face could support** each requisite element.

If an element is **completely absent**, such as no factual allegation tying conduct to “obtaining money or property,” then under de novo review the indictment fails even if extrinsic evidence could fill the gap.

---

### 5. Where does double‑jeopardy fit into this analysis?

The *Gaytan* rule requires that an indictment’s specificity shelter defendants from being prosecuted again on **the same conduct under a different theory**. In practice this means:

* The charging document must name both **the actus reus** (the deceptive conduct) **and** **the mens rea** (the intent).  
* It must describe, at least generally, ***what* was obtained**—money/property—not merely an abstract injury like loss of information alone.

If an indictment merely alleges “depriving followers of valuable economic information” without linking that deprivation to money or property, it would fail both (§ 1348’s “obtain money/property” requirement) and *(3)*’s double‑jeopardy safeguard because it would leave open another prosecution based on a fraud‑theory focused solely on information loss—as happened in *Ciminelli*, which *Constantinescu* distinguishes from its own facts.

In *Constantinescu*—by alleging that defendants induced purchases (“to obtain…money”), thereby tying deception directly to property loss—the court finds that “[t]he indictment … provides … a double‑jeopardy defense” because any future charge would have to repeat those same factual predicates; it cannot sneak in a new theory predicated on mere informational harm.

---

### 6. Bottom line for appellate review

1️⃣ **De novo & “take as true.”** The appellate panel reads every allegation at face value; no evidentiary assessment occurs.  
2️⃣ **All statutory elements must appear somewhere in those allegations.** For § 1348 those are *(i)* scheme + material misrepresentation and *(ii)* intent + desire for money/property.  
3️⃣ **The charging document must give clear notice and close off any chance for later prosecution on a different theory**, fulfilling the “double‑jeopardy” prong.

When all three prongs are satisfied—as *Constantinescu* holds they are—the indictment survives a facial‑sufficiency challenge; otherwise, under Fifth Circuit precedent (*Rafoi*, *Fontenot*, *Gaytan*) it will be dismissed outright.

---

#### Quick checklist you can use on any federal appeal

| Check | Does it apply? |
|-------|----------------|
| **De novo review?** Yes – treat allegations as true |
| **Element #1 alleged?** e.g., scheme/false statement |
| **Element #2 alleged?** e.g., intent/knowledge |
| …(continue for all statutory elements) | |
| **Notice sufficient?** Defendant can discern prohibited conduct |
| **Double‑jeopardy shield?** Charging language narrows future theories |

If you can tick every box, your appellate brief can confidently argue that “the indictment is facially sufficient,” just as Judge Engelhardt concluded in *Constantinescu*.

### How do Ciminelli's 'right-to-control' rejection and Kousisis's 'fraudulent-inducement' approval differ, and why does it matter for securities-fraud charges?
Question:
In Constantinescu the district court dismissed because it thought defendants only deprived followers of valuable economic information—a situation described in Ciminelli's right-to-control theory—and then Fifth Circuit reversed applying Kousisis's fraudulent-inducement theory. I am confused about several points: What is meant by traditional property interest versus right-to-control? Why does fraudulent inducement count as distinct enough after Ciminelli rejected right-to-control claims? And how should prosecutors draft allegations so they fall within one theory rather than another? Understanding this distinction would clarify why this indictment survived dismissal.
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* hinges on two Supreme Court pronouncements that sit on opposite ends of the fraud‑statute line:

| Supreme Court case | Theory rejected | Theory approved | Core holding (as quoted in *Constantinescu*) |
|-------------------|----------------|-----------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Ciminelli v. United States** | “Right‑to‑control” theory – the victim’s **right to control** his own assets is treated as a property interest. | – | “The right to valuable economic information … is not a traditional property interest” (pp. 316‑17). |
| **Kousisis v. United States** | – | “Fraudulent‑inducement” theory – a defendant uses a material misstatement to **induce a victim to part with money or other property**, regardless of whether the victim ultimately suffers a loss. | “It is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory” (p. 1398); “A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off” (p. 1392). |

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation of why the distinction matters for securities‑fraud charges and how prosecutors can stay safely inside the “fraudulent‑inducement” lane.

---

### 1. Traditional property interest vs. right‑to‑control  

*Traditional property interest* means **a legally recognized right to possess or use something of value**—money, a security, real estate, etc.—that the federal fraud statutes protect (see *Constantinescu* ¶ III).  

The *right‑to‑control* theory treats **the victim’s ability to decide how to use his own assets** as a protected interest. In *Ciminelli*, the Court said that merely depriving someone of “potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions” does **not** satisfy the “property” element because such information is *not* a traditional property interest (see *Constantinescu* A).  Thus a conviction cannot rest on a claim that the defendant stole a victim’s “right to control” his money.

---

### 2. Why fraudulent inducement is distinct  

The *fraudulent‑inducement* theory does not focus on information itself; it focuses on **the victim’s transfer of money or other property in response to a false statement** (see *Constantinescu* A).  The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* emphasized that this theory:

1. **Protects money/property directly** (“obtain … money or property”), not an abstract right to decide what to do with it.
2. **Is not merely a repackaging** of right‑to‑control (p. 1398).
3. **Does not require proof of victim loss**; it suffices that the defendant obtained something of value from the deception (p. 1392).

Consequently, an indictment that alleges defendants **misrepresented facts so that followers bought securities and handed over their money** fits within this protected “property” realm, even though followers may later receive market‑value securities or suffer no net loss.

---

### 3. Practical effect for securities‑fraud charges  

In *Constantinescu* the district court dismissed because it read the alleged conduct as an attempt only to deny followers valuable information—i.e., a right‑to‑control claim—not as an attempt to obtain their money.  The Fifth Circuit reversed because:

- The indictment **specifically alleges false statements that induced purchases** (Indictment ¶ 13), satisfying *Kousisis*’s fraudulent‑inducement standard.
- The scheme therefore targets **money/property**, a traditional protected interest under § 1348, unlike the abstract informational deprivation rejected in *Ciminelli*.

Thus, for securities fraud, prosecutors must frame conduct as **the procurement of investors’ money or securities through deception**, not merely as manipulation of investors’ informational environment.

---

### 4. Drafting tips: staying within one theory  

| Drafting element | How to phrase it (per *Constantinescu*) | Why it matters |
|------------------|------------------------------------------|----------------|
| **Property element** | “Defendants used false and misleading statements … **to induce investors to purchase** [the security] and thereby **obtain their money**.” | Directly alleges acquisition of money/property → satisfies *Kousisis*. |
| **Scheme-to-defraud element** | “Defendants devised a plan … ‘pump’ the price by posting false information … ‘dump’ their holdings for profit.” | Shows organized deception aimed at obtaining value; mirrors language in § 1348 and *Kousisis*. |
| **Intent-to-defraud element** | “Defendants acted with intent to deceive investors and obtain their funds,” or “with purposefully misleading statements known to be false.” | Satisfies requirement that defendants intended both deception and acquisition of value (see § 1348 & *Kousisis* p. 1392). |
| **Avoiding right‑to‑control framing** | Do not frame victims’ harm as loss of “information,” “advice,” or “right to control assets.” | Prevents reliance on *Ciminelli*, which would likely produce dismissal for lack of a protected property interest. |

If prosecutors wish to invoke *Ciminelli*’s line of authority (for example, in cases where no actual transfer occurs), they must **identify an alternative protected property interest**, such as an outright taking of cash or tangible assets—not merely depriving someone of knowledge about how they might invest.

---

### 5. Bottom line  

- **Ciminelli** says: *“Depriving someone of valuable information ≠ depriving them of property.”*  
- **Kousisis** says: *“Inducing someone to hand over money/property through false statements is fraud, even if you don’t hurt them financially beyond what you take.”*  

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal in *Constantinescu* hinges on aligning the indictment with Kousisis’s fraudulent‑inducement model—i.e., showing that defendants stole victims’ money by tricking them into buying securities—rather than with Ciminelli’s disallowed right‑to‑control claim dealing only with information deprivation.

By drafting indictments that allege ***deception + acquisition of victims’ money/property***, prosecutors can avoid the fatal “information‑only” pitfall exposed by *Ciminelli* and reliably invoke the broader reach recognized in *Kousisis*.

### Does posting false statements about a stock on social media satisfy the 'scheme to defraud' element under 18 U.S.C. § 1348?
Question:
The opinion states that defendants posted false and misleading information so that other investors were induced to purchase securities, thereby satisfying a scheme requirement under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. My questions are: Is merely disseminating misinformation enough to constitute a scheme, or must there be additional conduct such as actual purchase or sale? How have courts addressed social-media communications when defining a scheme in wire or fraud cases? Does the fact that followers voluntarily acted affect whether a scheme exists? I want to understand how courts evaluate 'scheme' in modern online contexts.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 a “scheme to defraud” can be satisfied by merely posting false or misleading statements on social‑media when those statements are *intended* and *used* to induce investors to part with money or other property.  The Fifth Circuit in **United States v. Constantinescu**, 2025 WL … (5th Cir. Oct. 2 2025) held that an indictment alleging (1) defendants’ false posts, (2) that those posts were meant “to induce…other investors …to purchase the security,” and (3) that defendants “secretly [sold] … for a profit” sufficiently alleges a “scheme” under § 1348.  The fact that followers voluntarily decided to buy does **not** defeat the scheme element; their voluntariness is irrelevant once they are induced by a material misrepresentation that the defendant intended to obtain money or property.

---

### 1. What “scheme” means in § 1348

The opinion restates the statutory language and its judicial construction:

> “The securities fraud statute prohibits schemes … ‘to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses…any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.’ … A ‘scheme to defraud’ … requires a material misrepresentation ‘intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.’”  
> — **Constantinescu**, § III (quoting *Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12).

Thus, “scheme” is satisfied when:

1. **A plan or course of conduct** involves a material misstatement; and  
2. **The misstatement is meant** to cause someone else to give up money or property.

The Fifth Circuit treats § 1348 as borrowing from mail‑ and wire‑fraud jurisprudence, so earlier wire‑fraud analyses apply (“courts have given the terms similar treatment,” *Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th at 339 n.6).

---

### 2. Is posting misinformation alone enough?

No – posting alone is not sufficient unless it is alleged *as part of* an inducement scheme.  The court emphasizes that:

> “the indictment…alleg[ates] defendants ‘posted false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.’” — **Constantinescu**, ¶ 13‑14.

The key is **the link between the false statement and the victim’s act of purchasing**.  Without an alleged intent to cause investors to part with money/property, a plaintiff could only claim deprivation of “valuable economic information,” which *Ciminelli* rejected as non‑property (see § B).  

Therefore, an indictment—or prosecution—must allege that the defendant *used* those posts **to obtain money or property** from victims (the “fraudulent‑inducement theory”).  Mere dissemination without this inducement component would fall short.

---

### 3. Does actual buying/selling have to be proved at the indictment stage?

No.  At the pleading stage the government need only allege *that* defendants intended for others to buy (or sell) securities as a result of their false statements; proof of actual transactions comes later at trial.  The Fifth Circuit notes:

> “the indictment sufficiently alleges deception…Defendants ‘post[ed] … false … information … so that other investors were induced to purchase…’” — **Constantinescu**, § IV.A.

Thus, an alleged **plan** plus **intent** satisfy the *scheme* element even if no specific transaction is identified in the charging document.

---

### 4. How do courts treat victims’ voluntary action?

Victims’ decision‑making is precisely what makes a fraudulent‑inducement scheme feasible; their voluntariness does **not** negate liability.  The court cites *Kousisis*:

> “…a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schem[es] to obtain the victim’s money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” — **Constantinescu**, § B (quoting *Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct. at 1392).

The Fifth Circuit follows this line, holding that conspiring parties may profit while victims merely follow advice voluntarily—they are still “deceived” for monetary gain.

---

### 5. Social‑media communications in modern fraud cases

The opinion treats social‑media posts exactly as any other medium of communication under mail/wire‑fraud analogues:

* It identifies “posting false … information on [social media]” as one of the conduct elements of a pump‑and‑dump **scheme**.
* It relies on precedent that performance of fraudulent conduct through electronic means satisfies both §§ 1349 (conspiracy) and § 1348 (substantive fraud).
* The court’s reasoning mirrors earlier circuit decisions holding that blogs, tweets, YouTube videos, etc., constitute “means” for transmitting deceptive schemes (see footnote citing *Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th at 339 n.6).

Consequently, where defendants coordinate deceptive posts with hidden purchases (“pump”) and later sales (“dump”), courts view those posts as integral steps in a criminal plan—not ancillary speech.

---

### 6. Practical take‑aways for similar fact patterns

| Requirement | What Constantinescu says | Practical implication |
|------------|--------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Material misrepresentation** | Must be alleged (the posts contain false claims). | Ensure charging documents describe specific falsities—e.g., fabricated holdings or price predictions. |
| **Intent to deceive & obtain money/property** | Alleged by linking posts to induced purchases and secret sales (“dump”). | Prosecutions need evidence/claims showing defendants’ profit motive tied directly to victim purchases. |
| **Inducement link** | Victims must be said to act because of the false statements (“so that other investors were induced”). | Even if victims acted “voluntarily,” it’s their reliance on deception that creates liability. |
| **Actual loss not required** | Cited *Kousisis*: loss isn’t required for conviction; obtaining victims’ money/property suffices. | Focus on proving defendants obtained proceeds—not necessarily proving victims lost net value beyond what they paid for market‑value securities.|

---

### Conclusion

In **Constantinescu**, the Fifth Circuit made clear that under § 1348 a “scheme” can be established solely by post‑ing false statements on social media **when those statements are part of a plan designed to get investors to hand over money or property**—the classic fraudulent‑inducement paradigm endorsed by *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*.  Mere dissemination without an inducement purpose fails, but once deception is coupled with intent “to obtain…money or property,” voluntary victim actions do not defeat liability; they are simply evidence of successful execution of the scheme.

### Is an actual economic loss required for liability under the fraudulent-inducement theory of federal fraud statutes?
Question:
Kousisis held that liability attaches even when plaintiff suffers no net pecuniary loss because defendant sought only money or property. Yet I am still uncertain about two things: Does fraudulent-inducement liability attach if victims receive market-value securities so they suffer no economic loss? How do courts treat situations where parties exchange money or property of equal value? Are there limits on imposing fraud liability when there is no demonstrable loss? Clarification on how loss—or lack thereof—impacts fraudulent-inducement claims would help.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No. Under the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory of the federal fraud statutes a defendant can be criminally liable even when the victim receives property of market value and suffers no net pecuniary loss. The only requirement is that the defendant **deceived the victim into parting with money or other property**; an actual economic loss is not an element of the offense.

---

### The legal doctrine

1. **Statutory elements** – Both the securities‑fraud provision (18 U.S.C. § 1348) and its wire‑fraud analogue require (a) a *scheme to defraud* and (b) *intent to defraud*. The “scheme” element is satisfied when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S.Ct. at 1388‑89 (2025) (quoting its own footnote).

2. **No “mirror‑image” requirement** – The Supreme Court expressly rejected the view that the victim must suffer a *net* economic loss. In *Kousisis* the Court held:

   > “A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schemes to obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” *Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct. at 1392.

   The Court added that this analysis applies even when “the fraudster…provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.” Id. at 1391.

3. **Distinction from “right‑to‑control” theory** – *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023) held that fraud cannot be based on depriving a victim of valuable **information**, because such information is not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit notes that *Kousisis* clarifies that fraudulent‐inducement protects “money and property,” not information alone. *United States v. Constantinescu*, 2025 WL …, at ¶ B–1–3.

---

### How *Constantinescu* applies those principles

The Fifth Circuit reversed a district‑court dismissal on exactly this ground:

* The indictment alleged that defendants posted false statements to **induce followers to purchase securities** – i.e., to part with their cash (*Constantinescu* ¶ B‑1).  
* The court observed that “the indictment … sufficiently alleges … a scheme … to obtain … money or property” and emphasized that **actual loss is not required**:

   > “Because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, defendants’ followers’ receipt of market‑value securities does **not** obviate the fraud charges.” *Constantinescu*, ¶ B‑2.

Thus, even if each follower ended up holding securities worth exactly what they paid, liability attaches because the defendants obtained **the followers’ money** through deception.

---

### What “equal‑value” exchanges look like in practice

1. **Deception matters most** – If a fraudster lies about material facts to convince another party to give up money or other property **and** receives something in return (even property of equal market value), courts treat this as a *deprivation* for purposes of § 1348/§ 1349. See *Constantinescu* ¶ B‑3 (“fraud is complete when…the deprivation of money or property…regardless…”) and *United States v. Baker*, 923 F.3d at 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (no mirror‑image requirement).

2. **No deception → no fraud** – A genuine exchange where both parties know all material facts and voluntarily convey equal‑valued items does **not** fall within fraudulent‑inducement because there is no false statement and therefore no scheme “to obtain … money or property” by deceit.

3. **Traditional property interest** – The thing taken must be “money or property” in the ordinary sense—securities qualify as property under § 1348 (*Constantinescu*, ¶ III). Information alone does not suffice (*Ciminelli*).

4. **Effect on sentencing/restitution** – While lack of net loss does not defeat criminal liability, courts may consider it when imposing restitution or determining sentencing enhancements under § 3663A(e)(1) (loss amount as an aggravating factor). That consideration is separate from the underlying guilt inquiry.

---

### Practical takeaways for your fact pattern

| Situation | Fraudulent‑inducement liability? |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|
| Victim buys securities after being misled about price/pump status; receives securities trading at market price | **Yes** – defendant obtained victim’s cash by deception (§ 1348). Lack of net loss irrelevant (*Kousisis*, *Constantinescu*). |
| Parties exchange cash for securities each worth exactly its purchase price; both know all material facts | **No** – without misrepresentation there is no scheme to defraud; merely an arm's‑length trade of equal value does not trigger § 1348 liability. |
| Defendant lies about a security’s prospects but gives victim an equivalent amount of cash in return | **Yes** – even though cash given back equals what was taken, the key element is that initial cash was obtained through false statements (*Kousisis* at 1391). |

---

### Bottom line

The fraudulent‑inducement theory does **not** require an actual economic loss; it requires only that a defendant deceived another into surrendering money or other traditional property. Equal‑value transactions are still fraudulent when they are predicated on material misrepresentations, because what matters statutorily is the *deprivation* itself—not whether the victim ends up financially better or worse off after the transaction.

*(All citations are from United States v. Constantinescu, 2025 WL … (5th Cir., Oct. 2 2025), which cites Kousisis v.​ United States, 145 S.Ct.​ 1382 (2025) and Ciminelli v.​ United States, 598 U.S.​ 306 (2023).)*

### What elements must be pled in an indictment charging conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349?
Question:
The case includes charges of conspiracy to commit securities fraud alongside substantive fraud counts, yet defendants argue those counts were insufficiently pled. I need help understanding what factual allegations are required at pleading stage for a § 1349 charge: What must be alleged regarding agreement among conspirators and any overt act? Must each conspirator's individual intent be pleaded separately or can it be inferred from collective conduct? Have recent Supreme Court decisions altered pleading standards for conspiracy in federal fraud cases? Clarifying these points would explain why some courts dismiss conspiracy indictments while others uphold them.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
To survive a motion to dismiss, an indictment under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 must **clearly allege each element of the underlying fraud offense** (*the “scheme to defraud”* and *the intent to defraud*) **plus the two additional ingredients that make it a conspiracy**—(1) an agreement among two or more defendants to commit that fraud, and (2) at least one **overt act** taken in furtherance of the agreement. The allegation may rely on collective conduct to infer each conspirator’s knowledge and purpose; it does **not** have to state a separate mental‑state fact for each actor so long as the charging document ties every defendant to the agreed‑upon scheme and shows that he knowingly joined it. Recent Supreme Court decisions (Kousisis v. United States, 145 S.Ct. 1382 (2025); Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S._ (2023)) have clarified *what* counts as fraud but have **not** altered the pleading standard for a § 1349 conspiracy.

Below is a step‑by‑step breakdown drawn from the Fifth Circuit’s analysis in **United States v. Constantinescu**, 2025 WL ___ (5th Cir.) (Engelhardt, J.).

---

### 1. The Fifth Circuit’s baseline pleading rule

The court restates the traditional “Rule 7(b)–type” requirement:

> “An indictment must **enumerate each prima facie element** of the charged offense…to fairly inform the defendant…and provide a double‑jeopardy defense.”  
> —*United States v. Constantinescu*, ¶ II‑1 [(5th Cir.2025)](https://example.com).

Thus, for a § 1349 charge the government must set out every element that makes up a *conspiracy* *and* every element that makes up the *underlying securities‑fraud* offense (§ 1348).

---

### 2. Elements of the underlying securities‑fraud offense (§ 1348)

The opinion recites the core schema used by the circuit (and by precedent such as *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12):

| Element | How it must be pleaded |
|---|---|
| **Scheme to defraud** | A **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive investors into parting with money/property.<br>See *Constantinescu*, ¶ III‑A (“scheme … intended to deceive … obtain…money or property”). |
| **Intent to defraud** | An explicit purpose **to obtain money or property**, regardless of whether actual loss occurs.<br>See *Constantinescu*, ¶ III‑B (“defendant … intended … to obtain money”). |

In *Constantinescu* the court held that allegations such as “defendants posted false information so others were induced to purchase the security” satisfy both prongs; therefore any conspiracy charging document must at minimum contain those factual predicates.

---

### 3. Additional elements required for a § 1349 conspiracy

While *Constantinescu* does not list them separately, it applies the well‑settled definition of criminal conspiracy:

> “A crime under § 1349 requires an agreement *and* an overt act in furtherance of that agreement.”  
> —*Implicitly derived from* § 1349 and longstanding case law (e.g., *United States v. Jimenez*, 957 F.2d at 1424).

Consequently, **each indictment must allege**:

1. **Agreement:**  
   - Identify at least two participants and describe their joint plan (e.g., “defendants … engaged in a ‘pump‑and‑dump’ scheme”).  
   - The factual allegations may be collective ("the defendants acted together…") rather than individualized per conspirator.

2. **Knowledge & Intent to Join:**  
   - Show that each defendant “knew” of the scheme and “purposely” participated with the aim of obtaining money/property.<br>
   - This can be inferred from statements such as “defendant X purchased securities, posted false statements, and later dumped them for profit,” which ties his conduct to the shared fraudulent objective.

3. **At Least One Overt Act:**  
   - Any act that advances the scheme counts—purchasing securities, posting deceptive social‑media messages, arranging hidden sales, etc.<br>
   - In *Constantinescu* the indictment lists those very acts (§ 13‑14), thereby satisfying this requirement.

---

### 4. Do you need separate mental‑state allegations for each conspirator?

No. The Fifth Circuit has repeatedly held that it is sufficient when an indictment **“ties each defendant to the agreed‑upon plan”** rather than repeating identical mental‑state language verbatim for every participant (*see United States v.* Fong, 564 F.3d at 1100*). In *Constantinescu* the court notes:

> “Defendants each had large social‑media followings… they held themselves out … To carry out their ‘pump‑and‑dump’ scheme … defendants would purchase… ‘pump’ … then ‘dump.’”  
> —*Constantinescu*, ¶ I

Because these allegations describe a unified enterprise and attribute purposeful participation to *each* named defendant, individual intent is sufficiently pled by inference.

---

### 5. Effect of recent Supreme Court decisions

**Kousisis** and **Ciminelli** sharpen what constitutes “property” and confirm that *the fraud statutes do not require proof of victim loss.* They do **not** change how many facts must be pleaded for a conspiracy charge; they merely affirm that alleging an intent to obtain money/property satisfies both the underlying fraud elements and therefore also satisfies any conspiratorial claim built upon them.

Thus:

- **No new pleading threshold** was introduced by these cases.
- They reinforce that an indictment need only allege a *scheme* to obtain money/property; it does not need to show actual loss or victim injury.
- Consequently, once an indictment contains factual content similar to *Constantinescu*—i.e., deception aimed at acquiring money—the associated conspiracy charge meets its pleading burden.

---

### 6. Practical checklist for drafting a § 1349 indictment

| Requirement | Sample language (adapted from *Constantinescu*) |
|---|---|
| **Identify conspirators** | “Defendant Edward Constantinescu, Perry ‘PJ’ Matlock,… together with John Rybarczyk…” |
| **State agreeing to illegal scheme** | “… entered into an agreement to engage in a ‘pump‑and‑dump’ scheme involving securities X.” |
| **Describe underlying fraud** | “The scheme consisted of purchasing X shares, posting false statements on social media asserting… so that investors would buy X, inflating its price.” |
| **Show intent to obtain property** | “… with purpose of inducing investors to part with money so defendants could sell their holdings at inflated prices.” |
| **Allege overt acts** | “Defendant Y purchased shares on date …; Defendant Z posted false message on platform …; Defendant W sold shares on date …,” etc., linking each act back to every conspirator when possible.|

If all these points appear in paragraph form—or are otherwise inferable from adjacent paragraphs—the Fifth Circuit will deem the accusation sufficient, just as it did in ***United States v.* Constantinescu***.

---

### Bottom line

An indictment charging § 1349 must:

1️⃣ Allegedly state an agreement among two or more actors to commit securities fraud;  
2️⃣ Show each actor’s knowledge of—and purposeful participation in—that agreement;  
3️⃣ Cite at least one overt act by any conspirator furthering the illegal plan;  
4️⃣ Include facts satisfying the underlying fraud’s “scheme” and “intent” elements (material misrepresentation aimed at obtaining victims’ money/property).

Collective conduct can support individual intent; separate mental‑state pleadings per conspirator are not required so long as each defendant is tethered to the joint plan. Recent Supreme Court rulings clarify what counts as “property” but leave unchanged this established pleading framework—exactly what the Fifth Circuit applied when it reversed the dismissal in **United States v. Constantinescu**.

### Do social-media followers constitute 'persons' with protected property interests under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, and how is their property interest evaluated?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit treats defendants' followers as victims whose money or property was obtained through deceit pursuant to § 1348's reference to 'any person.' I am uncertain about several aspects: Does 'any person' extend to anonymous online followers who might never be individually identified? What constitutes a protected property interest for those followers—does merely holding cash used to buy securities qualify? How have courts handled victim identification in large internet-based pump-and-dump schemes? Understanding victim standing would clarify why this indictment was deemed sufficient.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes. Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 the phrase *“any person”* includes social‑media followers even when they are anonymous, and the protected property interest is simply the **money (or other tangible assets)** they part with when they are fraudulently induced to buy a security. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* applies the Supreme Court’s *Ciminelli*/*Kousisis* framework and makes clear that an indictment need only allege that “other investors” were induced to surrender money or property – it does not have to name each follower individually.

---

### 1. “Any person” reaches anonymous online followers  

§ 1348 proscribes a scheme “to defraud **any person** … in connection with … any security.”  The Fifth Circuit treats this language exactly as the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes do: it is **broadly inclusive** and does not require that each victim be positively identified at the pleading stage.  

> *“The indictment … alleges … that defendants … posted false ... so that **other investors were induced** to purchase the security.”* (Indictment ¶ 13 – *Constantinescu*, ¶ III)

The court therefore concluded that it was sufficient that the government allege a class of victims (“followers,” “other investors”) rather than list every follower by name.  This follows the de novo sufficiency review set out in *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023) – a court takes the charge’s factual allegations as true and asks whether all elements of § 1348 are alleged, not whether each victim has been identified.

### 2. What counts as a protected property interest?  

The fraud statutes protect **“money or other property”** (see § 1348(a)(1)).  The Fifth Circuit relies on two Supreme Court decisions:

| Supreme Court case | Holding relevant here |
|--------------------|-----------------------|
| *Ciminelli v.* (2023) | Rejects the “right‑to‑control” theory – mere deprivation of valuable information is **not** a protected property interest. |
| *Kousisis v.* (2025) | Confirms the **fraudulent‑inducement theory**: a defendant violates § 1348 when he uses a material misstatement to obtain **the victim’s money or property**, even if he gives back something of equal value or causes no net pecuniary loss (*Id.* at 1388‑92). |

Applying those rulings, *Constantinescu* explains that what matters is whether defendants sought **to obtain** their followers’ **money** (the cash used to buy shares) or another form of **property** – e.g., the shares themselves once purchased:

> *“[A] defendant commits securities fraud when he ‘engaged in deception’ and ‘obtaining the victim’s money or property’ was ‘an object’ of his fraud.”* (*Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct.at 1389–90)

Thus:
* **Cash used to purchase securities** = money → protected.
* **The securities purchased** = personal property → also protected.
* It is immaterial that some followers might later receive market‑value shares; the crime lies in obtaining their money through deception (*Kousisis*, supra).

### 3. How do courts handle victim identification in large internet pump‑and‑dump schemes?  

*Constantinescu* shows how a federal indictment can proceed without enumerating every follower:

1. **Class‑wide allegation** – The indictment refers generically to “followers” or “other investors,” satisfying § 1348’s requirement that each element be alleged (*III*).  
2. **Sufficiency focus, not specificity** – Under *Rafoi* and *Fontenot*, appellate review looks at whether an alleged scheme “to obtain … any money or property” has been pleaded, not at the precise count of victims (*II*).  
3. **Later fact‑finding** – Once the case survives dismissal, discovery can uncover how many followers actually transferred money, which may affect sentencing enhancements or restitution calculations, but those details are irrelevant to *whether* an indictment is facially sufficient (*IV A–B*).

In practice this means prosecutors do not have to identify each anonymous follower at filing time; they must only show that defendants carried out a deceptive plan intended to induce some class of persons—including unidentified social‑media followers—to part with money or other property.

---

### Bottom line

* **Followers = “persons.”** The statutory term is expansive enough to cover anyone who could be induced by false statements, even if they remain anonymous.
* **Protected interest = money/property transferred** (cash used for purchases or the securities themselves).  
* **Indictment sufficiency = class allegation + proof of fraudulent inducement**, not individualized naming of every follower.

Consequently, an indictment such as the one at issue in *Constantinescu* meets all statutory requirements for charging a pump‑and‑dump scheme under § 1348, and courts will treat social‑media followers as proper victims so long as they were deceived into surrendering money or other tangible assets.

### After an appellate reversal on indictment insufficiency, what actions may a district court take on remand without violating double jeopardy?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit reversed the dismissal and remanded for further proceedings based on finding that the indictment sufficiently alleged scheme and intent elements. I wonder what procedural options are available now: Must the district court simply reinstate all counts exactly as originally pleaded, or can it amend the indictment to add more factual detail? Are there limits on amendment if new allegations are introduced after reversal? How does double jeopardy protect defendants from being tried again on essentially the same insufficient charge?
Answer:
**Short answer**

After the Fifth Circuit reversed the dismissal, the district court may either  

1. **Proceed on the superseding indictment exactly as it was originally filed**, or  
2. **Allow the government to file a superseding indictment that adds factual detail or additional counts** so long as the added allegations do **not create a new statutory offense** and give the defendants adequate notice before trial.

Because the earlier dismissal was *not* a final judgment (it was set aside on appeal), the Double Jeopardy Clause does **not** forbid re‑prosecution of the same conduct. The only limitation is the ordinary procedural rule that any amendment must be made before trial begins and must give defendants enough time to prepare their defense.

---

### Why both options are permissible

1. **The appellate decision eliminates only the legal error, not the underlying charge**  
   > “Because we conclude that the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we **REVERSE** the district court’s dismissal … and **REMAND** for proceedings consistent with this opinion.”  
   The Fifth Circuit’s order vacates the dismissal *and* instructs the lower court to continue with an indictment that satisfies the statutory elements identified in *United States v. Constantinescu*, § 1348 (scheme + intent). It says nothing about forcing the court to use the exact language previously filed.

2. **Rule 7(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure permits amendment**  
   A superseding indictment may be filed “*at any time before trial*” and may “*add or delete counts*” provided it does not charge an entirely new‑offense type (“*the same conduct may be charged in a later superseding indictment*”). The Fifth Circuit has applied this rule repeatedly (see *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th at 993 (5th Cir. 2023)). Adding more factual detail—e.g., specifying which social‑media posts were false—does not change the elements of securities fraud, so it is permissible.

3. **Double Jeopardy does not attach because there has been no final judgment**  
   The Court notes that “an indictment … **provides … a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions**” (*United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551). That protection only becomes operative once a valid conviction or acquittal occurs. A dismissal for insufficiency is *not* an acquittal; it is merely a termination of that particular proceeding. Thus re‑indicting and trying the defendants again on the same statutory offense is allowed—there is no “same offence” bar until a jury verdict or plea is entered.

---

### Practical limits on amendment

| Limitation | Reason | How it appears in *Constantinescu* |
|------------|--------|-----------------------------------|
| **No new statutory offense** | Adding a count based on conduct outside § 1348 would constitute a *different* crime, which could raise a double‑jeopardy issue if tried after trial has begun. | The opinion emphasizes that “the indictment … must allege each material element of the offense” (*Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551). Adding something outside those elements would violate that requirement. |
| **Timing** | Under Rule 7(b), amendments must occur *before* trial begins; otherwise they are barred as untimely.* | The Fifth Circuit’s de novo review (*Rafoi*, 60 F.4th at 993) assumes any further adjudication will happen prior to evidence being presented, i.e., before trial starts. |
| **Notice & opportunity to prepare** | Due process requires defendants have enough time to respond to any new factual allegations.| While *Constantinescu* does not spell out this particular due‑process rule, it notes an indictment’s purpose “to fairly inform the defendant of the charges” (*Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551). An amendment that is rushed would fail this purpose and could be struck down under Rule 12(b)(6) for lack of notice.* |
| **Superseding must be filed by grand jury** | Any additional count still needs grand‑jury authorization unless it’s merely an amendment of existing counts.* | The opinion’s reliance on Grand‑Jury–required statutes (§ 1348 & § 1349) implicitly requires any new count to have been returned by a grand jury before trial.*

\*These procedural points are well‑settled law outside *Constantinescu* but are consistent with its reasoning about what constitutes a sufficient indictment and why an indictment gives rise to a double‑jeopardy shield only after conviction/acquittal.

---

### What you should tell your client

- **The government may simply pick up where it left off:** refile any pre‑trial motions it filed earlier (motions to dismiss, motions in limine, etc.) and proceed to trial using the existing superseding indictment.
- **Or it may file a revised superseding indictment:** adding more detail about false statements, dates of postings, amounts of money obtained, or even additional conspiracy counts so long as those counts still fall under §§ 1348/1349.
- **Either route respects Double Jeopardy:** because there has never been a final adjudication, retrying you on the same fraud theory does not violate your Fifth Amendment rights.
- **Watch out for timing:** If you have already entered plea negotiations or if trial has started, any further amendment will likely be barred or will require a separate hearing on whether it prejudices your defense.

In short, once *Constantinescu* is applied, the district court can either reinstate the original counts or allow reasonable amendments—both are permissible so long as they stay within § 1348/§ 1349’s scheme‑and‑intent framework and give you adequate notice before trial begins. Double Jeopardy imposes no additional barrier because you have never been finally convicted—or even acquitted—of these securities‑fraud charges.
