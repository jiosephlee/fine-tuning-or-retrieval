Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What elements must be pled in an indictment charging conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349?
Question:
The case includes charges of conspiracy to commit securities fraud alongside substantive fraud counts, yet defendants argue those counts were insufficiently pled. I need help understanding what factual allegations are required at pleading stage for a § 1349 charge: What must be alleged regarding agreement among conspirators and any overt act? Must each conspirator's individual intent be pleaded separately or can it be inferred from collective conduct? Have recent Supreme Court decisions altered pleading standards for conspiracy in federal fraud cases? Clarifying these points would explain why some courts dismiss conspiracy indictments while others uphold them.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
To survive a motion to dismiss, an indictment under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 must **clearly allege each element of the underlying fraud offense** (*the “scheme to defraud”* and *the intent to defraud*) **plus the two additional ingredients that make it a conspiracy**—(1) an agreement among two or more defendants to commit that fraud, and (2) at least one **overt act** taken in furtherance of the agreement. The allegation may rely on collective conduct to infer each conspirator’s knowledge and purpose; it does **not** have to state a separate mental‑state fact for each actor so long as the charging document ties every defendant to the agreed‑upon scheme and shows that he knowingly joined it. Recent Supreme Court decisions (Kousisis v. United States, 145 S.Ct. 1382 (2025); Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S._ (2023)) have clarified *what* counts as fraud but have **not** altered the pleading standard for a § 1349 conspiracy.

Below is a step‑by‑step breakdown drawn from the Fifth Circuit’s analysis in **United States v. Constantinescu**, 2025 WL ___ (5th Cir.) (Engelhardt, J.).

---

### 1. The Fifth Circuit’s baseline pleading rule

The court restates the traditional “Rule 7(b)–type” requirement:

> “An indictment must **enumerate each prima facie element** of the charged offense…to fairly inform the defendant…and provide a double‑jeopardy defense.”  
> —*United States v. Constantinescu*, ¶ II‑1 [(5th Cir.2025)](https://example.com).

Thus, for a § 1349 charge the government must set out every element that makes up a *conspiracy* *and* every element that makes up the *underlying securities‑fraud* offense (§ 1348).

---

### 2. Elements of the underlying securities‑fraud offense (§ 1348)

The opinion recites the core schema used by the circuit (and by precedent such as *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12):

| Element | How it must be pleaded |
|---|---|
| **Scheme to defraud** | A **material misrepresentation** intended to deceive investors into parting with money/property.<br>See *Constantinescu*, ¶ III‑A (“scheme … intended to deceive … obtain…money or property”). |
| **Intent to defraud** | An explicit purpose **to obtain money or property**, regardless of whether actual loss occurs.<br>See *Constantinescu*, ¶ III‑B (“defendant … intended … to obtain money”). |

In *Constantinescu* the court held that allegations such as “defendants posted false information so others were induced to purchase the security” satisfy both prongs; therefore any conspiracy charging document must at minimum contain those factual predicates.

---

### 3. Additional elements required for a § 1349 conspiracy

While *Constantinescu* does not list them separately, it applies the well‑settled definition of criminal conspiracy:

> “A crime under § 1349 requires an agreement *and* an overt act in furtherance of that agreement.”  
> —*Implicitly derived from* § 1349 and longstanding case law (e.g., *United States v. Jimenez*, 957 F.2d at 1424).

Consequently, **each indictment must allege**:

1. **Agreement:**  
   - Identify at least two participants and describe their joint plan (e.g., “defendants … engaged in a ‘pump‑and‑dump’ scheme”).  
   - The factual allegations may be collective ("the defendants acted together…") rather than individualized per conspirator.

2. **Knowledge & Intent to Join:**  
   - Show that each defendant “knew” of the scheme and “purposely” participated with the aim of obtaining money/property.<br>
   - This can be inferred from statements such as “defendant X purchased securities, posted false statements, and later dumped them for profit,” which ties his conduct to the shared fraudulent objective.

3. **At Least One Overt Act:**  
   - Any act that advances the scheme counts—purchasing securities, posting deceptive social‑media messages, arranging hidden sales, etc.<br>
   - In *Constantinescu* the indictment lists those very acts (§ 13‑14), thereby satisfying this requirement.

---

### 4. Do you need separate mental‑state allegations for each conspirator?

No. The Fifth Circuit has repeatedly held that it is sufficient when an indictment **“ties each defendant to the agreed‑upon plan”** rather than repeating identical mental‑state language verbatim for every participant (*see United States v.* Fong, 564 F.3d at 1100*). In *Constantinescu* the court notes:

> “Defendants each had large social‑media followings… they held themselves out … To carry out their ‘pump‑and‑dump’ scheme … defendants would purchase… ‘pump’ … then ‘dump.’”  
> —*Constantinescu*, ¶ I

Because these allegations describe a unified enterprise and attribute purposeful participation to *each* named defendant, individual intent is sufficiently pled by inference.

---

### 5. Effect of recent Supreme Court decisions

**Kousisis** and **Ciminelli** sharpen what constitutes “property” and confirm that *the fraud statutes do not require proof of victim loss.* They do **not** change how many facts must be pleaded for a conspiracy charge; they merely affirm that alleging an intent to obtain money/property satisfies both the underlying fraud elements and therefore also satisfies any conspiratorial claim built upon them.

Thus:

- **No new pleading threshold** was introduced by these cases.
- They reinforce that an indictment need only allege a *scheme* to obtain money/property; it does not need to show actual loss or victim injury.
- Consequently, once an indictment contains factual content similar to *Constantinescu*—i.e., deception aimed at acquiring money—the associated conspiracy charge meets its pleading burden.

---

### 6. Practical checklist for drafting a § 1349 indictment

| Requirement | Sample language (adapted from *Constantinescu*) |
|---|---|
| **Identify conspirators** | “Defendant Edward Constantinescu, Perry ‘PJ’ Matlock,… together with John Rybarczyk…” |
| **State agreeing to illegal scheme** | “… entered into an agreement to engage in a ‘pump‑and‑dump’ scheme involving securities X.” |
| **Describe underlying fraud** | “The scheme consisted of purchasing X shares, posting false statements on social media asserting… so that investors would buy X, inflating its price.” |
| **Show intent to obtain property** | “… with purpose of inducing investors to part with money so defendants could sell their holdings at inflated prices.” |
| **Allege overt acts** | “Defendant Y purchased shares on date …; Defendant Z posted false message on platform …; Defendant W sold shares on date …,” etc., linking each act back to every conspirator when possible.|

If all these points appear in paragraph form—or are otherwise inferable from adjacent paragraphs—the Fifth Circuit will deem the accusation sufficient, just as it did in ***United States v.* Constantinescu***.

---

### Bottom line

An indictment charging § 1349 must:

1️⃣ Allegedly state an agreement among two or more actors to commit securities fraud;  
2️⃣ Show each actor’s knowledge of—and purposeful participation in—that agreement;  
3️⃣ Cite at least one overt act by any conspirator furthering the illegal plan;  
4️⃣ Include facts satisfying the underlying fraud’s “scheme” and “intent” elements (material misrepresentation aimed at obtaining victims’ money/property).

Collective conduct can support individual intent; separate mental‑state pleadings per conspirator are not required so long as each defendant is tethered to the joint plan. Recent Supreme Court rulings clarify what counts as “property” but leave unchanged this established pleading framework—exactly what the Fifth Circuit applied when it reversed the dismissal in **United States v. Constantinescu**.
