Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Is an actual economic loss required for liability under the fraudulent-inducement theory of federal fraud statutes?
Question:
Kousisis held that liability attaches even when plaintiff suffers no net pecuniary loss because defendant sought only money or property. Yet I am still uncertain about two things: Does fraudulent-inducement liability attach if victims receive market-value securities so they suffer no economic loss? How do courts treat situations where parties exchange money or property of equal value? Are there limits on imposing fraud liability when there is no demonstrable loss? Clarification on how loss—or lack thereof—impacts fraudulent-inducement claims would help.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No. Under the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory of the federal fraud statutes a defendant can be criminally liable even when the victim receives property of market value and suffers no net pecuniary loss. The only requirement is that the defendant **deceived the victim into parting with money or other property**; an actual economic loss is not an element of the offense.

---

### The legal doctrine

1. **Statutory elements** – Both the securities‑fraud provision (18 U.S.C. § 1348) and its wire‑fraud analogue require (a) a *scheme to defraud* and (b) *intent to defraud*. The “scheme” element is satisfied when a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S.Ct. at 1388‑89 (2025) (quoting its own footnote).

2. **No “mirror‑image” requirement** – The Supreme Court expressly rejected the view that the victim must suffer a *net* economic loss. In *Kousisis* the Court held:

   > “A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he ‘schemes to obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” *Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct. at 1392.

   The Court added that this analysis applies even when “the fraudster…provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.” Id. at 1391.

3. **Distinction from “right‑to‑control” theory** – *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023) held that fraud cannot be based on depriving a victim of valuable **information**, because such information is not a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit notes that *Kousisis* clarifies that fraudulent‐inducement protects “money and property,” not information alone. *United States v. Constantinescu*, 2025 WL …, at ¶ B–1–3.

---

### How *Constantinescu* applies those principles

The Fifth Circuit reversed a district‑court dismissal on exactly this ground:

* The indictment alleged that defendants posted false statements to **induce followers to purchase securities** – i.e., to part with their cash (*Constantinescu* ¶ B‑1).  
* The court observed that “the indictment … sufficiently alleges … a scheme … to obtain … money or property” and emphasized that **actual loss is not required**:

   > “Because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, defendants’ followers’ receipt of market‑value securities does **not** obviate the fraud charges.” *Constantinescu*, ¶ B‑2.

Thus, even if each follower ended up holding securities worth exactly what they paid, liability attaches because the defendants obtained **the followers’ money** through deception.

---

### What “equal‑value” exchanges look like in practice

1. **Deception matters most** – If a fraudster lies about material facts to convince another party to give up money or other property **and** receives something in return (even property of equal market value), courts treat this as a *deprivation* for purposes of § 1348/§ 1349. See *Constantinescu* ¶ B‑3 (“fraud is complete when…the deprivation of money or property…regardless…”) and *United States v. Baker*, 923 F.3d at 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019) (no mirror‑image requirement).

2. **No deception → no fraud** – A genuine exchange where both parties know all material facts and voluntarily convey equal‑valued items does **not** fall within fraudulent‑inducement because there is no false statement and therefore no scheme “to obtain … money or property” by deceit.

3. **Traditional property interest** – The thing taken must be “money or property” in the ordinary sense—securities qualify as property under § 1348 (*Constantinescu*, ¶ III). Information alone does not suffice (*Ciminelli*).

4. **Effect on sentencing/restitution** – While lack of net loss does not defeat criminal liability, courts may consider it when imposing restitution or determining sentencing enhancements under § 3663A(e)(1) (loss amount as an aggravating factor). That consideration is separate from the underlying guilt inquiry.

---

### Practical takeaways for your fact pattern

| Situation | Fraudulent‑inducement liability? |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|
| Victim buys securities after being misled about price/pump status; receives securities trading at market price | **Yes** – defendant obtained victim’s cash by deception (§ 1348). Lack of net loss irrelevant (*Kousisis*, *Constantinescu*). |
| Parties exchange cash for securities each worth exactly its purchase price; both know all material facts | **No** – without misrepresentation there is no scheme to defraud; merely an arm's‑length trade of equal value does not trigger § 1348 liability. |
| Defendant lies about a security’s prospects but gives victim an equivalent amount of cash in return | **Yes** – even though cash given back equals what was taken, the key element is that initial cash was obtained through false statements (*Kousisis* at 1391). |

---

### Bottom line

The fraudulent‑inducement theory does **not** require an actual economic loss; it requires only that a defendant deceived another into surrendering money or other traditional property. Equal‑value transactions are still fraudulent when they are predicated on material misrepresentations, because what matters statutorily is the *deprivation* itself—not whether the victim ends up financially better or worse off after the transaction.

*(All citations are from United States v. Constantinescu, 2025 WL … (5th Cir., Oct. 2 2025), which cites Kousisis v.​ United States, 145 S.Ct.​ 1382 (2025) and Ciminelli v.​ United States, 598 U.S.​ 306 (2023).)*
