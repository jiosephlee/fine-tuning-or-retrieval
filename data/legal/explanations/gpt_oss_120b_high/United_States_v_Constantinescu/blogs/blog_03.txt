Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Pleading Fraud: De Novo Review versus Rule 8(b) – Lessons from *United States v. Constantinescu*

## Why *Constantinescu* Matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is already being cited in forums ranging from securities‑law conferences to DOJ training seminars. At its core, the opinion clarifies how a federal indictment must satisfy the “scheme‑to‑defraud” and “intent‑to‑defraud” elements of the securities‑fraud statutes when the alleged conduct consists mainly of false social‑media hype rather than overt theft of cash or securities.  

For criminal prosecutors, the ruling offers a concrete roadmap for surviving early dismissal motions that hinge on whether an indictment “states an offense.” For defendants—and their counsel—it underscores that merely arguing victims were left with market‑value securities (or that they were deprived only of “information”) will not usually defeat a fraud charge once the government has alleged fraudulent inducement of a purchase. In short, *Constantinescu* reaffirms that the federal fraud statutes protect money or property obtained from victims, even when victims receive something “of equal value” in return.

## The Indictment‑Sufficiency Standard Under Rule 8(b)  

Rule 8(b) commands federal criminal pleadings to contain “a short and plain statement … sufficient to give notice … of the nature ... charges.” Courts apply this rule **de novo**—they read the indictment as true and ask whether each statutory element is alleged on its face (see *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th at 993 (5th Cir. 2023)). The Fifth Circuit reiterated that approach in *Constantinescu*: it examined whether the superseding indictment alleged (1) a material misrepresentation; (2) a scheme to use that misrepresentation to obtain money or property; and (3) an intent embodying both deception and an objective to obtain something of value.

The district court had dismissed on a novel theory—that because the alleged victims received securities at market price they suffered no “property loss,” the indictment failed to allege a protected property interest under §1348. The appellate court rejected that premise outright, noting that Rule 8(b)’s purpose is not to ensure proof of actual loss at the pleading stage but merely to ensure that each element—including deprivation of money or property—is **alleged**.  

In practice, this means prosecutors need not couch their allegations in terms of calculated victim losses; they must simply assert that defendants induced victims to part with money (or other property) by means of false statements, and that they intended to obtain that money (or property). The *Constantinescu* opinion shows how precisely those allegations can be phrased without running afoul of Rule 8(b).

## Distinguishing “Fraudulent‑Inducement” From the Rejected Right‑to‑Control Theory  

The Supreme Court’s recent decisions provide the doctrinal backdrop for the Fifth Circuit’s analysis:

* **Ciminelli v. United States** (2025) held that fraud statutes protect traditional property interests—not merely “the right to valuable economic information.” The Court struck down convictions based on a “right‑to‑control” theory where defendants were accused solely of withholding information that would have helped victims make better decisions.
  
* **Kousisis v. United States** (2025) clarified that a “fraudulent‑inducement” theory—where a defendant uses a material misstatement to persuade a victim into a transaction involving money or property—does **not** require proof that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.

*Constantinescu* applies these precedents squarely. The indictment does not allege any “right‑to‑control” claim; instead it says defendants posted false statements designed expressly to **induce** followers to buy shares, thereby transferring cash from followers to defendants’ accounts before they dumped their positions at inflated prices. By anchoring its analysis in *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit confirmed that once an inducement leads victims to surrender money or property—even if they later receive something else in return—the statutory injury requirement is met.

## Intent To Defraud Is Satisfied By Profit Motive  

Defendants’ motion also argued that an intent‐to‐defraud requires an intention **to harm** victims rather than merely an intention **to profit**. The Supreme Court has consistently rejected this narrower reading. In *Kousisis*, Justice Sotomayor emphasized that fraud occurs when a defendant seeks “to obtain” another’s money or property *regardless* of any desire to injure the victim’s bottom line.

The Fifth Circuit echoed this language: it noted that the indictment alleges defendants bought securities first, then posted misleading claims so followers would buy them too—ultimately allowing defendants “to profit.” That profit motive illustrates an intent **to obtain** money and therefore satisfies both prongs of §1348’s intent requirement as articulated in *Evans* (5th Cir. 2018). Importantly, actual economic loss was unnecessary for sufficiency; prosecutors need only demonstrate an objective—on their face—to acquire something of value from another party via deception.

## How Criminal Pleading Differs From Civil Fraud Pleadings  

In criminal cases, Rule 8(b)’s low threshold contrasts sharply with civilian litigation’s heightened pleading standards under Rule 9(b), which demands more specific factual detail when alleging fraud (“the circumstances constituting fraud … shall be stated with particularity”). Civil plaintiffs must describe who made which false statements, what specific information was conveyed, how it was material, and what precise loss resulted—all *before* discovery begins.

The *Constantinescu* opinion underscores why such granular specificity is unnecessary in criminal indictments:

1. **Focus on statutory elements**, not loss quantification  
   An indictment must simply allege deception intended **and** resulting in acquisition of money/property—not measure each victim’s damage.
2. **Narrative sufficiency over evidentiary detail**  
   A short paragraph describing false posts intended to trigger purchases adequately signals notice because it enumerates every essential element.
3. **De novo legal review protects against overly granular demands**  
   Since appellate courts evaluate sufficiency without reference to evidence outside the complaint, any plausibly alleged scheme will survive unless it omits one required element entirely.

That does not mean prosecutors can write vague indictments (“defendant committed fraud”). They still need enough factual granularity—e.g., who posted what statements about which security at roughly what time—to give defendants fair notice and satisfy Rule 8(b)’s notice requirement (*Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551). But they do not have to trace exact dollar losses or show how many followers bought shares at inflated prices—a level more reminiscent of civil pleadings than criminal ones.

## Practical Takeaways for Prosecutors  

Guidance distilled from *Constantinescu*:

| What Drafting Requires | Why It Matters |
|------------------------|----------------|
| **Identify each defendant’s deceptive conduct** – e.g., specific social‑media posts containing false price predictions or fabricated personal holdings | Demonstrates material misrepresentation & satisfies element one |
| **Tie those statements directly to solicitations that cause followers to transfer money or other property** – phrase as “induced purchasers,” “prompted buyers,” etc.| Establishes fraudulent‑inducement theory under §1348 |
| **State clearly you seek acquisition of victim money/property**, not merely control over their decision‐making | Avoids right‑to‑control pitfall highlighted in *Ciminelli* |
| **Assert profit motive or expected gain**, even if you do not allege actual victim loss | Satisfies intent‐to‐defraud per *Kousisis* |
| **Include any substantial allegation about volume/scale** – e.g., total profit $114 million | Gives defendants fair notice while keeping banality low enough for rule compliance |

By threading these elements into each count, prosecutors give themselves maximal protection against Rule 8(b) attacks while staying within constitutional limits—no need for detailed forensic accounting absent discovery.

## Doctrinal Ripple Effects  

1. **Broader Reach Into Influencer Markets**  
   With influencers increasingly touting stocks, cryptocurrency tokens, NFTs or even real estate projects on platforms like TikTok or X, courts now have clear authority—via *Constantinescu*—to treat exaggerated hype as actionable fraudulent inducement so long as there is an underlying transfer worth $ value.
   
2. **Potential Convergence With Civil Enforcement**  
   While civil plaintiffs still face Rule 9(b)’s specificity hurdle, regulatory agencies may lean more heavily on criminal indictments as evidentiary scaffolding when pursuing private actions later under §10(b). A robust criminal charge can accelerate settlement negotiations even absent proven monetary loss.
   
3. **Limits Remain On “Information‑Only” Schemes**  
   Defendants wrapping schemes around pure misinformation without an accompanying solicitation will still fall prey under *Ciminelli*. Prosecutors should thus avoid framing every deceptive statement as fraudulent inducement if no monetary transaction follows.
   
4. **Future Jurisprudence May Refine ‘Money or Property’ Scope**  
   Some commentators fear an expansion toward non‑tangible assets like data vouchers or loyalty points could stretch ‘property’ too far—but current precedent anchors protection firmly on transferrable economic value (*Kousisis*, *Evans*).

## Bottom Line  

*United States v. Constantinescu* exemplifies how federal courts apply—and arguably reaffirm—the de novo review mandated by Rule 8(b). By leaning heavily on Supreme Court guidance from *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit demonstrated that an indictment need only allege deception aimed at obtaining money or property; proving actual loss is unnecessary at this stage. 

For practitioners drafting securities-fraud indictments—or any fraud charge where inducement drives victim transfers—the lesson is straightforward: focus your narrative plumbing on who said what, who was induced to part with cash or another asset, and why your client expected financial gain from those transfers. When you do so concisely yet fully—to satisfy both statutory notice requirements and the de novo scrutiny embodied in Rule 8(b)—your complaint is far more likely than today’s revived district-court opinions deemingly flippant claims—that it will survive early dismissal attacks whenever you bring it before a federal grand jury again.
