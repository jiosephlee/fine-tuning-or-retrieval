Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1 – Factual Synopsis and Procedural Posture  

This introductory chapter isolates the “who,” “what,” and “how” of *United States v. Constantinescu* before turning to the procedural steps that carried the case from a grand‑jury indictment through a district‑court dismissal and onto the Fifth Circuit on appeal. Understanding these facts and the procedural landscape is essential for any student who wishes to follow the Court’s legal analysis in later sections.

---

## The Alleged “Pump‑and‑Dump” Scheme Executed via Social‑Media Platforms  

The government’s case rests on a classic “pump‑and‑dump” fraud adapted to today’s digital environment. According to the superseding indictment, each defendant purchased shares of one or more securities and then used their considerable followings on platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, and Twitter to disseminate false or misleading statements about those securities. The postings alleged favorable trading positions, projected steep price appreciation, or otherwise mischaracterized the value of the securities.  

By presenting these deceptive endorsements as trustworthy advice from experienced traders, the defendants allegedly induced their followers—ordinary retail investors—to buy the same securities. The influx of follower purchases created artificial demand and elevated market prices (“the pump”). Once the price was sufficiently inflated, the defendants allegedly sold their own holdings at a profit (“the dump”), leaving their followers holding securities whose market value subsequently collapsed when truthful information entered the market or when trading volume normalized.  

The indictment frames these conduct elements as a coordinated scheme: (1) acquisition of securities by the defendants, (2) dissemination of material falsehoods via social media, (3) inducement of followers to purchase those securities, and (4) subsequent off‑market sale by the defendants at an artificial price peak.

---

## Defendants’ Roles, Follower Bases, and Purported $114 Million Profit  

Eight individuals are named in the indictment:

1. **Edward Constantinescu**  
2. **Perry “PJ” Matlock**  
3. **John Rybarczyk**  
4. **Gary Deel**  
5. **Stefan Hrvatin**  
6. **Tom Cooperman**  
7. **Mitchell Hennessey**  
8. **Daniel Knight**

Each is described as having “large social‑media followings across various platforms” and as presenting themselves publicly as successful stock traders who routinely share real‑time trade updates with their audiences. The government alleges that together they orchestrated a concerted effort that yielded **$114 million** in net profits from the described pump‑and‑dump operations—profits derived largely from the off‑market sales executed after prices were artificially heightened by followers’ purchases.

During the early stage of litigation, **Daniel Knight** entered a guilty plea to one or more counts related to the scheme; his plea removed him from subsequent motions challenging the sufficiency of the indictment. The remaining seven defendants filed a joint motion seeking dismissal on the ground that the complaint failed to allege a cognizable property loss under federal fraud statutes.

---

## Statutory Counts Charged – Conspiracy Under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and Securities Fraud Under §§ 1348 & 2  

The government charged two distinct federal offenses:

* **Conspiracy to Commit Securities Fraud**, 18 U.S.C. § 1349.  
  This statute makes it unlawful for two or more persons “to conspire” either (a) *to commit* any offense defined in § 1341–§ 1350 (including securities fraud), or (b) *to aid* any person in committing such an offense. The essential elements are an agreement among conspirators plus an overt act in furtherance of the illegal objective.

* **Securities Fraud**, principally under **18 U.S.C. § 1348** (“fraudulent acts … in connection with any security”) together with **§ 2** which provides criminal penalties for violations of § 1348/§ 1349 themselves (“any person who knowingly violates… shall be fined…or imprisoned…”). Section 1348 mirrors language found in common wire‑fraud provisions and requires proof that a defendant engaged in a “scheme…to defraud…any person…in connection with…any security,” accompanied by intent “to obtain…money or property” through false pretenses.

Together these statutes target both collective planning (the conspiracy count) and individual conduct (the substantive fraud count) stemming from misleading communications about securities.

---

## Key Provisions of the Superseding Indictment (¶¶ 13–14)  

Paragraphs 13–14 form the factual core upon which both counts rest:

*Paragraph 13* alleges that each defendant *“purchased a security,”* then *“‘pumped’ the price…by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase…thereby artificially increasing its price.”*  

*Paragraph 14* continues by stating that after achieving an inflated market price they *“‘dumped’ by secretly selling the security for a profit.”* The language emphasizes both *deception* (false statements) and *economic consequence* (induced purchases causing price inflation). It also quantifies gain—$114 million—linking profit directly to conduct described in those two paragraphs.

These paragraphs satisfy pleading requirements insofar as they identify who acted (*defendants*), what illicit behavior they engaged in (*false statements intended to induce purchases*), how victims were harmed (*losses incurred through overpriced purchases*), and what benefit prosecutors claim they obtained (*profits from subsequent sales*).

---

## District Court’s Dismissal Rationale – Alleged Failure to Allege Deprivation of a Traditional Property Interest  

Defendants moved to dismiss on Rule 12(b)(6) grounds, arguing that an indictment must allege a scheme *to deprive victims of property*. The district judge concluded that while individuals may have been misled about market prospects, what was taken away was “potentially valuable economic information” rather than an established property right protected by federal fraud statutes. In other words, according to the judge’s reading, defendants allegedly sought merely *to influence* followers’ decisions without seeking direct appropriation of money or tangible assets belonging to those followers—a claim he likened—by reference—to jurisprudence emphasizing “traditional property interests.”

The dismissal therefore rested on an interpretation—that an alleged deprivation limited itself to intangible information—and consequently held that *the indictment did not state an offense*. This view treated “information” as outside protected property despite plaintiffs’ contention that deception caused followers ultimately to part with money—a classic element recognized under § 1348’s “money or property” language when someone is induced into a contract requiring payment.

---

## Timeliness and Scope of the Government’s Appeal  

Following entry of judgment dismissing all counts except those already admitted via Knight’s plea, **the United States filed a notice of appeal** within thirty days—well within the statutory period set out by 28 U.S.C. § 2107(a). The appeal was limited specifically to the district court’s dismissal order; no issues concerning sentencing or collateral consequences were raised at this stage because those matters had not yet arisen for any defendant besides Knight.

On appeal, controversy centered on whether **the district court correctly applied federal fraud jurisprudence** when assessing sufficiency under Rule 12(b)(6). Consistent with Fifth Circuit precedent (e.g., *United States v.* Rafoi), appellate review was **de novo**—the appellate panel examined every allegation in the superseding indictment as true and asked whether those allegations constitute all elements required by §§ 1348 and 1349 when read together with governing case law concerning “scheme,” “intent,” and “property.” Accordingly, no evidentiary record beyond pleading material existed; rather, questions hinged entirely upon statutory construction and precedent concerning what constitutes protected property under federal fraud statutes.

Understanding this procedural posture prepares students for subsequent chapters where we examine how the Fifth Circuit evaluated those statutory elements against both prior Supreme Court holdings (e.g., *Ciminelli*, *Kousisis*) and circuit precedent concerning sufficient pleading under fraud statutes.

Subsequent chapters will unpack how this factual matrix interacts with legal doctrine to determine whether an indictment properly alleges a “scheme” plus “intent” within meaningfully protected property interests under federal securities fraud law.
