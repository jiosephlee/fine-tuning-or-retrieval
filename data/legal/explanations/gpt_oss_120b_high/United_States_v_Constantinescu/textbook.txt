Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Chapter 1 – Factual Synopsis and Procedural Posture  

This introductory chapter isolates the “who,” “what,” and “how” of *United States v. Constantinescu* before turning to the procedural steps that carried the case from a grand‑jury indictment through a district‑court dismissal and onto the Fifth Circuit on appeal. Understanding these facts and the procedural landscape is essential for any student who wishes to follow the Court’s legal analysis in later sections.

---

## The Alleged “Pump‑and‑Dump” Scheme Executed via Social‑Media Platforms  

The government’s case rests on a classic “pump‑and‑dump” fraud adapted to today’s digital environment. According to the superseding indictment, each defendant purchased shares of one or more securities and then used their considerable followings on platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, and Twitter to disseminate false or misleading statements about those securities. The postings alleged favorable trading positions, projected steep price appreciation, or otherwise mischaracterized the value of the securities.  

By presenting these deceptive endorsements as trustworthy advice from experienced traders, the defendants allegedly induced their followers—ordinary retail investors—to buy the same securities. The influx of follower purchases created artificial demand and elevated market prices (“the pump”). Once the price was sufficiently inflated, the defendants allegedly sold their own holdings at a profit (“the dump”), leaving their followers holding securities whose market value subsequently collapsed when truthful information entered the market or when trading volume normalized.  

The indictment frames these conduct elements as a coordinated scheme: (1) acquisition of securities by the defendants, (2) dissemination of material falsehoods via social media, (3) inducement of followers to purchase those securities, and (4) subsequent off‑market sale by the defendants at an artificial price peak.

---

## Defendants’ Roles, Follower Bases, and Purported $114 Million Profit  

Eight individuals are named in the indictment:

1. **Edward Constantinescu**  
2. **Perry “PJ” Matlock**  
3. **John Rybarczyk**  
4. **Gary Deel**  
5. **Stefan Hrvatin**  
6. **Tom Cooperman**  
7. **Mitchell Hennessey**  
8. **Daniel Knight**

Each is described as having “large social‑media followings across various platforms” and as presenting themselves publicly as successful stock traders who routinely share real‑time trade updates with their audiences. The government alleges that together they orchestrated a concerted effort that yielded **$114 million** in net profits from the described pump‑and‑dump operations—profits derived largely from the off‑market sales executed after prices were artificially heightened by followers’ purchases.

During the early stage of litigation, **Daniel Knight** entered a guilty plea to one or more counts related to the scheme; his plea removed him from subsequent motions challenging the sufficiency of the indictment. The remaining seven defendants filed a joint motion seeking dismissal on the ground that the complaint failed to allege a cognizable property loss under federal fraud statutes.

---

## Statutory Counts Charged – Conspiracy Under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and Securities Fraud Under §§ 1348 & 2  

The government charged two distinct federal offenses:

* **Conspiracy to Commit Securities Fraud**, 18 U.S.C. § 1349.  
  This statute makes it unlawful for two or more persons “to conspire” either (a) *to commit* any offense defined in § 1341–§ 1350 (including securities fraud), or (b) *to aid* any person in committing such an offense. The essential elements are an agreement among conspirators plus an overt act in furtherance of the illegal objective.

* **Securities Fraud**, principally under **18 U.S.C. § 1348** (“fraudulent acts … in connection with any security”) together with **§ 2** which provides criminal penalties for violations of § 1348/§ 1349 themselves (“any person who knowingly violates… shall be fined…or imprisoned…”). Section 1348 mirrors language found in common wire‑fraud provisions and requires proof that a defendant engaged in a “scheme…to defraud…any person…in connection with…any security,” accompanied by intent “to obtain…money or property” through false pretenses.

Together these statutes target both collective planning (the conspiracy count) and individual conduct (the substantive fraud count) stemming from misleading communications about securities.

---

## Key Provisions of the Superseding Indictment (¶¶ 13–14)  

Paragraphs 13–14 form the factual core upon which both counts rest:

*Paragraph 13* alleges that each defendant *“purchased a security,”* then *“‘pumped’ the price…by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase…thereby artificially increasing its price.”*  

*Paragraph 14* continues by stating that after achieving an inflated market price they *“‘dumped’ by secretly selling the security for a profit.”* The language emphasizes both *deception* (false statements) and *economic consequence* (induced purchases causing price inflation). It also quantifies gain—$114 million—linking profit directly to conduct described in those two paragraphs.

These paragraphs satisfy pleading requirements insofar as they identify who acted (*defendants*), what illicit behavior they engaged in (*false statements intended to induce purchases*), how victims were harmed (*losses incurred through overpriced purchases*), and what benefit prosecutors claim they obtained (*profits from subsequent sales*).

---

## District Court’s Dismissal Rationale – Alleged Failure to Allege Deprivation of a Traditional Property Interest  

Defendants moved to dismiss on Rule 12(b)(6) grounds, arguing that an indictment must allege a scheme *to deprive victims of property*. The district judge concluded that while individuals may have been misled about market prospects, what was taken away was “potentially valuable economic information” rather than an established property right protected by federal fraud statutes. In other words, according to the judge’s reading, defendants allegedly sought merely *to influence* followers’ decisions without seeking direct appropriation of money or tangible assets belonging to those followers—a claim he likened—by reference—to jurisprudence emphasizing “traditional property interests.”

The dismissal therefore rested on an interpretation—that an alleged deprivation limited itself to intangible information—and consequently held that *the indictment did not state an offense*. This view treated “information” as outside protected property despite plaintiffs’ contention that deception caused followers ultimately to part with money—a classic element recognized under § 1348’s “money or property” language when someone is induced into a contract requiring payment.

---

## Timeliness and Scope of the Government’s Appeal  

Following entry of judgment dismissing all counts except those already admitted via Knight’s plea, **the United States filed a notice of appeal** within thirty days—well within the statutory period set out by 28 U.S.C. § 2107(a). The appeal was limited specifically to the district court’s dismissal order; no issues concerning sentencing or collateral consequences were raised at this stage because those matters had not yet arisen for any defendant besides Knight.

On appeal, controversy centered on whether **the district court correctly applied federal fraud jurisprudence** when assessing sufficiency under Rule 12(b)(6). Consistent with Fifth Circuit precedent (e.g., *United States v.* Rafoi), appellate review was **de novo**—the appellate panel examined every allegation in the superseding indictment as true and asked whether those allegations constitute all elements required by §§ 1348 and 1349 when read together with governing case law concerning “scheme,” “intent,” and “property.” Accordingly, no evidentiary record beyond pleading material existed; rather, questions hinged entirely upon statutory construction and precedent concerning what constitutes protected property under federal fraud statutes.

Understanding this procedural posture prepares students for subsequent chapters where we examine how the Fifth Circuit evaluated those statutory elements against both prior Supreme Court holdings (e.g., *Ciminelli*, *Kousisis*) and circuit precedent concerning sufficient pleading under fraud statutes.

--- 

This chapter has set out who is accused, what conduct they are alleged to have undertaken through digital platforms, how Congress criminalizes such conduct, what portions of their grand‑jury charging document articulate those crimes, why a trial judge initially found those allegations insufficient, and how jurisdiction landed before an appellate panel poised for de novo review. Subsequent chapters will unpack how this factual matrix interacts with legal doctrine to determine whether an indictment properly alleges a “scheme” plus “intent” within meaningfully protected property interests under federal securities fraud law.

# Chapter 2 – Governing Law: Securities Fraud Statutes and Fraud Theories  

In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit was asked to decide whether a superseding indictment alleging a “pump‑and‑dump” scheme on social‑media satisfied the federal fraud statutes.  The court’s analysis turns on the statutory text of 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the way that provision imports concepts from the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes, and two competing doctrinal theories of fraud—*fraudulent‑inducement* and *right‑to‑control*.  This chapter unpacks each element so that you can see why the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment was sufficient.

## Statutory Basis for Criminal Securities Fraud  

Section 1348 makes it a crime “to devise…a scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with…any security” and also criminalizes “any scheme…to obtain…any money or property…in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.”  The statute therefore contains two analytical prongs:

1. **A “scheme to defraud.”**  The government must allege a planned course of conduct that is designed to cheat another party.  
2. **An “intent to defraud.”**  The defendant must have a purposeful desire to deceive and to cause some detriment—typically the loss of money or property—to the victim.

Both elements are required for every count under § 1348; a conspiracy charge under § 1349 simply adds an agreement among co‑defendants to commit an offense defined in § 1348.

## Elements of a Violation Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348  

The Fifth Circuit has distilled the statutory elements into a concise formula:

| Element | What the indictment must allege |
|---------|--------------------------------|
| **Scheme to defraud** | A “material misrepresentation” intended to deceive others so as to obtain something of value (money or property). *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018). |
| **Intent to defraud** | An intent **(1)** to deceive and **(2)** to cause some harm that stems from that deception—namely, the victim’s surrender of money or property.*United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d at 712. |

The *Constantinescu* indictment satisfies each prong by alleging that defendants posted false statements about a security’s value (“pump”) thereby inducing followers “to purchase the security,” after which they “secretly sold” (“dump”) for profit—an overt act designed to obtain followers’ money.

The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that an indictment must “enumerate each prima facie element,” fairly inform defendants of the charges, and provide a double‑jeopardy shield. *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551 (5th Cir. 1996); *United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo*, 236 F.3d at 236 (5th Cir. 2000).

## Borrowing from Mail and Wire Fraud  

Section 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” which are codified at §§ 1341–1343, because Congress used essentially identical language—*scheme*, *false pretenses*, *obtain money or property*. The Fifth Circuit therefore looks first to mail‑/wire‑fraud case law when construing § 1348’s elements (*United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th at 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023)).  

Consequently, doctrines such as “material misrepresentation” and “intent to defraud” carry over unchanged, allowing courts to apply well‑developed principles from mail/ wire fraud directly to securities fraud charges.

## Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory  

The Supreme Court’s *Kousisis v. United States* articulated what lower courts now call the **fraudulent‑inducement theory**:

> “[A] defendant ‘uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property’ … regardless of whether the fraudster seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” *Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct.at 1388–1392 (2025).

Key characteristics:

1. **Material Misstatement** – The false statement must be significant enough that a reasonable person would rely on it.
2. **Induced Transaction** – The victim must be persuaded, by reason of that misstatement, to enter into a transaction involving payment or transfer of property.
3. **Objective of Obtaining Money/Property** – The defendant’s aim is securing something of value; actual loss suffered by the victim is *not* an element.
4. **No Requirement That Defendant Provide Equal Value** – The plaintiff may receive securities at market price; what matters is that their money changed hands because of deceit (*Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct.at 1391).

Applying this framework, the *Constantinescu* indictment alleges exactly such conduct: defendants allegedly posted false information (“material misstatement”) so followers would buy securities (“induced transaction”), thereby handing over their money (“objective”).

## Right‑to‑Control Theory and Its Limitations  

By contrast, the **right‑to‑control theory**, rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*, treats a victim’s “right to control his assets” as a traditional property interest protected by federal fraud statutes:

> “…the right … is not a traditional property interest… therefore cannot serve as the basis for a fraud conviction.” *Ciminelli*, 141 U.S.at 316 (2023).

The Supreme Court reasoned that while victims may suffer a lost opportunity or disadvantage when deprived of future control over assets, this does not fall within Congress’s definition of protected property under §§ 1341–1343 (and thus § 1348). Consequently, merely depriving someone of valuable economic information—or its potential future utility—does not satisfy either element of § 1348.

In *Constantinescu* there is no reliance on right‑to‑control reasoning; instead, prosecutors allege victims were induced **to part with money**, which squarely fits fraudulent‑inducement analysis rather than an abstract claim about lost informational rights.

## Supreme Court Guidance: *Ciminelli*  

*Ciminelli* clarified that:

- A claim based solely on deprivation of “potentially valuable economic information” **fails** because such information is not recognized as “property” under federal fraud statutes.
- The decision focuses on what Congress protected—**tangible money or property**, not intangible informational rights (*Ciminelli*, 141 U.S.at 316).

Thus, any indictment predicated only on an alleged right‑to‑control will be vulnerable if it does not connect deception with an actual transfer of money/property.

## Supreme Court Guidance: *Kousisis*  

*Kousisis* inverted *Ciminelli*’s limitation by confirming that:

- A fraud conviction is proper when the defendant **obtains** money or property through deception—even if he does not intend—or succeed—in harming the victim’s net wealth.
- Victims may receive consideration equal in value (e.g., goods or services) yet still be protected because their money changed hands due to false pretenses (*Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct.at 1392).
- The focus returns to whether there was an acquisition of **money or property**, rather than whether damage was inflicted.

These holdings give courts latitude in accepting indictments where prosecutors allege fraudulent orchestration of purchase transactions—exactly how *Constantinescu* frames its scheme.

## Integrating Fraud Theories with Property‐Interest Analysis  

Traditional fraud analysis uses a three‐step test:

1. **Is there a protected property interest?**  
   - Under *Ciminelli*: information alone does not qualify.
   - Under *Kousisis*: any tangible transfer of money/property qualifies regardless of any concurrent benefit conferred on victims.
2. **Did the defendant make a material misrepresentation?**  
   - Borrowed from mail/ wire fraud jurisprudence.
3. **Did the defendant intend both deception and acquisition?**  
   - Intent includes desire simply to obtain victims’ assets; no proof required that plaintiff suffers net loss (*Kousisis*).

In practice, when an indictment alleges “defendants posted false statements … so that investors were induced to purchase securities,” step one is satisfied because investors transferred cash—a protected form of property—to acquire securities based on deception—meeting steps two and three under fraudulent‐inducement theory.

Thus, while right‐to‐control arguments are dead ends post-*Ciminelli*, fraudulent‐inducement remains robust for pump–and–dump schemes where false statements are used explicitly as bait for monetary investment—a precise fit for *Constantinescu*.

---

### Takeaway for Readers  

Understanding why *Constantinescu* survived dismissal hinges on recognizing two doctrinal pillars:

- **Statutory elements borrowed from mail/ wire fraud:** focus on scheme + intent + acquisition.
- **Supreme Court–crafted theories:** fraudulent‐inducement permits liability whenever deceit yields victims’ money/property; right‐to‐control does not create liability because intangible information isn’t “property.”

When you read later sections of this opinion, keep these lenses in mind—they explain why the Fifth Circuit affirmed that the superseding indictment properly alleged both required elements under § 1348 despite defendants’ arguments about lack of traditional property harm.’’

# Chapter 3 – Standard for Indictment Sufficiency in the Fifth Circuit  

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, 2025 WL … (5th Cir. Oct. 2 2025), the Fifth Circuit reaffirmed the court’s long‑standing method for evaluating whether an indictment meets the statutory requirements of a criminal charge. The opinion offers a clear illustration of the “de novo” approach adopted by this circuit and restates the three core obligations an indictment must satisfy: (1) it must allege every prima‑facie element of the charged offense, (2) it must give the defendant fair notice of the accusations, and (3) it must protect the defendant against double‑jeopardy exposure. This chapter dissects each component, cites the controlling appellate authority, and draws practical lessons for prosecutors drafting fraud indictments—particularly those involving securities fraud under §§ 1348‑1349.

---  

## De novo Review Principle – *United States v. Rafoi* (2023)  

The Fifth Circuit reviews indictment sufficiency **de novo**, meaning that the appellate court does not give deference to the district court’s discretionary judgment but instead determines whether, assuming all factual allegations are true, the complaint states an offense **as a matter of law**.  *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023) (“the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated”).  

In *Constantinescu* the panel applied this rule directly: it examined each allegation on its face—e.g., that defendants “posted false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” (Indictment ¶ 13)—and held that those allegations sufficiently pledge a scheme and intent to defraud under § 1348. The de novo standard shields defendants from reliance on a lower court’s potentially erroneous factual gloss, while simultaneously ensuring that prosecutors meet their pleading burden at inception.

---  

## Enumerating Each Prima Facie Element  

A sufficient indictment must **enumerate each material element** of the charged crime.  This requirement derives from Rule 7(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure and was articulated by this circuit in *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996).  

In a securities‑fraud case, § 1348 demands at least two elements:

1. A **scheme to defraud**—a plan or concerted effort designed to deceive another party;  
2. An **intent to defraud**—the purpose of obtaining money or property through that deception.  

*Constantinescu* demonstrates how those elements are incorporated into an indictment:

* The “scheme” is pled by describing defendants’ coordinated purchase of securities followed by false promotional posts (“pump”) intending to raise price;  
* The “intent” is pled through allegations that defendants sold (“dumped”) their holdings at an inflated price for profit (“defendants profited $114 million”).  

By explicitly referencing each required element in separate paragraphs, an indictment satisfies the “enumeration” prong identified in *Guzman‑Ocampo*, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000), which emphasized that omission of any essential element renders an indictment insufficient.

---  

## Fair Notice Requirement for Defendants  

Beyond technical completeness, an indictment must **fairly inform** the accused of what conduct is alleged so that he can mount a meaningful defense—a principle often termed “fair notice.”  *United States v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011) reiterated that courts “must give defendants notice of what acts are charged.”   

In *Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit found that despite dismissing some peripheral details (e.g., specific platforms used), the complaint gave defendants adequate warning they were accused of conducting false statements on social media designed to induce purchases—a concrete description enabling preparation of defenses such as lack of scienter or reliance on public information.

For practical drafting, prosecutors should:

* Identify **who** performed each act (e.g., “Defendant X posted…,” “Defendant Y purchased…”);  
* Describe **what** was said or omitted (e.g., “false and misleading information about price”);  
* State **how** those acts furthered a fraudulent scheme (e.g., “inducing others to purchase securities”).

When these particulars are present, defendants receive proper notice even if certain factual nuances are left undisclosed pending discovery.

---  

## Double‑Jeopardy Safeguard Against Subsequent Prosecution  

A third statutory purpose of an indictment is to forestall **double jeopardy** problems by ensuring that once accused under a particular set of facts, a defendant cannot later be tried again on essentially the same charge without new evidence or a different statutory theory (*United States v.* Gaytan). The Fifth Circuit has emphasized this protective role as part of its three‑prong test for sufficiency (*Gaytan*, supra).

In *Constantinescu* the court noted that dismissal would have left defendants vulnerable—if later re‑indicted on a theory merely reframed as “right‑to‑control” rather than “fraudulent inducement,” it could raise double‑jeopardy issues because both theories arise from identical conduct alleged in ¶ 13–14 of the original indictment. By upholding an indictment that already captures both theories within its language—misrepresentation leading victims to part with money—the appellate court preserves prosecutorial flexibility while respecting constitutional limits on repeated prosecutions.

---  

## Key Precedents Clarifying Sufficiency Standards  

| Case | Holding on Indictment Sufficiency | Relevance |
|------|-----------------------------------|-----------|
| *United States v.* Rafoi (2023) | De novo review; allegations taken as true | Sets procedural baseline |
| *United States v.* Fontenot (2011) | Fair notice essential; focus on clarity | Guides level of detail required |
| *United States v.* Gaytan (1996) | Indictment must enumerate elements, give fair notice, protect against double jeopardy | Articulates three statutory duties |
| *United States v.* Guzman‑Ocampo (2000) | Omission of any material element = insufficiency | Reinforces enumeration requirement |
| *United States v.* Crow (1999) & *U.S.* v.* Crow | Emphasize taking alleged facts as true in sufficiency analysis | Underpins de novo approach |

Together these cases form a cohesive framework applied in *Constantinescu*: take facts as alleged → check each crime element appears → verify defendant understands what conduct is criminalized → confirm no risk of re‑prosecution on same facts.

---  

## Practical Implications for Prosecutors Drafting Fraud Indictments  

The Fifth Circuit’s articulation—particularly as applied in *Constantinescu*—offers concrete guidance for charging decisions:

1️⃣ **Identify All Statutory Elements Explicitly**  
   - For securities fraud under § 1348 reference both “scheme” and “intent.”  
   - Align factual allegations with each element: e.g., misrepresentations = scheme; profit motive = intent.

2️⃣ **Choose an Appropriate Fraud Theory**  
   - Avoid reliance on discredited theories such as “right‑to‑control” (rejected in *Ciminelli*).  
   - Use accepted frameworks like **fraudulent‑inducement** where victims hand over money/property after being deceived (*Kousisis*).

3️⃣ **Provide Specific Conductal Detail**  
   - Mention platforms used (“Twitter,” “TikTok”), type of statements (“price will rise”), timing (“post made shortly after purchase”), and resulting action (“followers bought shares”).  
   - This satisfies fair notice while also strengthening proof against double jeopardy defenses later.

4️⃣ **Tie Plaintiff Harm Directly to Money or Property**  
   - Even if victims receive market‑value securities after purchase—a point raised by defense—the indictment should state that victims were induced to surrender cash or property based on falsehoods; this meets fraud’s “obtain money/property” requirement (*Kousisis*, supra).

5️⃣ **Anticipate Potential Evidentiary Gaps**  
   - Include alternative factual allegations where possible—for example, alleging both direct purchase inducement and secondary advantages like commission arrangements—to preempt attacks alleging insufficiency on one theory alone.

6️⃣ **Maintain Consistency Across Counts**  
   - If charging multiple counts (conspiracy plus substantive fraud), ensure each count individually satisfies enumeration, fair notice,and double‑jeopardy safeguards; redundancy is permissible if it reinforces statutory coverage.

By embedding these practices into their charging documents, prosecutors can minimize motions to dismiss on sufficiency grounds—a strategic advantage underscored by *Constantinescu*, where dismissal would have eliminated a $114 million fraud case before trial.

---  

### Summary  

The Fifth Circuit’s de novo scrutiny demands meticulous alignment between statutory elements and pleaded facts. As clarified in *United States v.* Constantinescu*, an indictment must enumerate every prima facie element (§ 1348’s scheme and intent), give defendants fair notice by describing who did what and how it advanced fraudulent conduct, and safeguard against double jeopardy by capturing all relevant conduct within one charging instrument. Controlling precedents—including *Rafoi*, *Fontenot*, *Gaytan*, and *Guzman‑Ocampo*—formulate this threefold test and provide prosecutors with clear checkpoints when preparing fraud indictments in this circuit. Understanding these requirements not only avoids procedural setbacks but also ensures that federal fraud statutes are invoked consistently with Supreme Court jurisprudence on what constitutes “property” under fraud offenses.

# Chapter 4 – The Fifth Circuit’s Reasoning in **Constantinescu**

In **United States v. Constantinescu**, No. 24‑20143 (5th Cir. Oct. 2 2025), the Fifth Circuit reviewed the district court’s dismissal of a securities‑fraud superseding indictment on the ground that it allegedly failed to allege a protected property interest for the victims. The appellate court conducted a **de novo** sufficiency analysis, held that the indictment sufficiently charged both a *scheme* and an *intent* to defraud, and consequently reversed and remanded.  
This chapter walks you through the court’s reasoning element by element, showing how it distinguished the case from *Ciminelli*, embraced the Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* “fraudulent‑inducement” framework, and relied on controlling precedent to reject the district court’s property‑interest argument.



---  

## “Scheme to defraud” analysis – material misrepresentations about securities on social media  

The fraud statutes at issue—18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 (securities fraud) and § 1349 (conspiracy)—borrow their elemental language from the mail‑ and wire‑fraud provisions. The Fifth Circuit reiterated this borrowing by pointing to **United States v. Greenlaw**, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.​6 (5th Cir. 2023), which treats “scheme” and “intent” under § 1348 much as they are understood under §§ 1341–1343.  

A *scheme* requires **(1)** a *material* misrepresentation or omission, **(2)** an *intent* that the false statement deceive another party, and **(3)** an objective designed to obtain something of value—money or property—from that party.^1  The circuit adopted **United States v. Evans**, 892 F.3d at 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018), for this definition:  

> “A ‘scheme’ … requires a material misrepresentation **intended** to deceive others **in order**to obtain something of value … from [the] deceived.”  

The indictment alleged that each defendant “**pumped**” a security by posting false or misleading statements on social‑media platforms—assertions about their own buying activity, exaggerated expectations of price appreciation, or fabricated market data.^2 Because these statements were **material**—they pertained directly to investors’ decisions whether or not to buy—the allegation satisfied element (1).  

The alleged conduct also satisfied element (2).¶ 13 of the superseding indictment states that defendants posted these false statements "*so that other investors were induced*." The phrase “so that” supplies an explicit purpose clause evidencing an intent **to deceive** followers into believing there was genuine demand for—or imminent price increase in—the securities.^3  

Finally, element (3) was met because the indictment further alleged that defendants subsequently sold (“dumped”) their holdings after inflating prices.^4 By orchestrating purchases followed by deceptive promotion designed expressly to induce subsequent purchases by others, defendants allegedly sought **to obtain money** from those followers—a classic target of § 1348’s prohibition against schemes “to obtain … any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale … [of] any security.”^5  

Collectively then, each conspirator’s conduct—as alleged—constituted participation in an overarching plan meeting all three prongs of what Evans describes as a lawful *scheme* under § 1348.



---  

## “Induced purchase” as deprivation of victims’ money/property  

The next issue was whether inducing followers ­to buy securities counted as depriving victims of protected property.  The Supreme Court has split fraud jurisprudence into two theories: 

1️⃣ The *right‑to‑control* theory (rejected in **Ciminelli**, 145 S.Ct.at 1388–90), which treats a victim’s control over future assets as property;  

2️⃣ The *fraudulent‑inducement* theory (endorsed in **Kousisis**, 145 S.Ct.at 1388–99), which focuses on an actual transfer of **money or other consideration** resulting from reliance on a material misstatement.^6  

The Fifth Circuit stressed that *Constantinescu* implicates only the second theory because every follower who purchased a security paid cash (or equivalent) directly out of pocket—a transfer unmistakably fitting Congress’s term “money or property.”^7  

Under *Kousisis*, "[a] defendant violates[​s] § 1348 when he … uses a material misstatement **to trick** a victim into entering into ***a contract*** that requires handing over her money or property—even if he provides something allegedly equal in value."^8 Because buying shares creates a contractual relationship obligating payment for stock, any deception designed expressly *to make investors hand over cash* satisfies this principle regardless of what they receive later.^9  

Thus, even though followers ultimately owned market‑value shares after their purchases—a fact observed by both parties—the act of transmitting cash triggered by false statements fulfills § 1348’s deprivation requirement.



---  

## “Intent to defraud” analysis – profit motive evidence in ¶ 13‑14 versus requirement for victim harm  

Having established a *scheme*, courts then ask whether defendants possessed an **intent** “to deceive…and cause some harm resulting from the deceit.”^10 In practice this breaks down into two inquiries: 

*Did defendants intend their victims’ reliance?* and  
*Did defendants intend either genuine loss or their own gain?*   

The district court reasoned incorrectly that because plaintiffs never claimed investors suffered net pecuniary loss—and merely suffered loss of informational value—there was no requisite intent.^11 The Fifth Circuit rejected this premise based squarely on **Kousisis**, where the Court held it unnecessary for prosecutors “to prove … [that] …the defendant sought **to hurt** the victim’s bottom line.”^12 Instead, an intention **to obtain** another person’s money—in whatever form—is enough.^13  

In *Constantinescu*, ¶ 13–14 specifically alleges defendants “…pumped … by posting false … information ... so that other investors were induced … [and] ***‘dumped’*** ... . . . **Defendants profited $114 million**.”^14 By tying deception directly To profitability—“the object … was **to obtain** money”—the indictment supplies concrete circumstantial evidence satisfying both prongs:

1️⃣ The language "*so that other investors were induced*" confirms purposeful reliance.*  
2️⃣ The explicit statement regarding $114 million profits demonstrates an overt motive **to receive money**, thereby satisfying Congress’s requirement that fraud be aimed at acquiring something valuable.^15  

The Supreme Court clarified that actual victim loss is immaterial if defendants intended—and succeeded—in acquiring cash.^16 Accordingly the appellate panel concluded there was no deficiency concerning intent.



---  

## Rejection of district court’s reliance on a property‑interest argument  

The district judge applied *Ciminelli*’s right‑to‑control doctrine creatively but inaccurately—a move quickly rebuffed by the appellate panel.  
In *Ciminelli*, plaintiffs attempted conviction solely upon alleging that defendants deprived victims *of valuable economic information* needed for future investment decisions.^17 That theory fails because Congress has long limited federal fraud protections to **traditional property interests** (“the right… is not…a traditional property interest”).^18  

Fifth Circuit judges observed three fundamental errors in extending *Ciminelli* here:

1️⃣ **No reliance on informational deprivation** – The indictment does more than allege informational theft; it alleges factually false statements prompting victims transferring cash.^19  
2️⃣ **Statutory text addresses money/property**, not knowledge alone – A phrase such as “…obtain … any money or property…” cannot be read narrowly enough here without annihilating virtually every securities Fraud case predicated upon inducement.^20  
3️⃣ **Supreme Court already differentiated theories** – In *Kousisis*, Justice Sotomayor labeled fraudulent‐inducement “not a repackaging”⁽²¹⁾ ⁽⎯⎯⎯⎯⎯ see above⁾ . This shows Congress intended two distinct pathways precisely because informational claims differ meaningfully from monetary claims.\ 

Hence, contrary to its deference petitioners argued—that foreclosure eyes typical markets—it could not salvage dismissal merely because victims lost informational advantage rather than physical assets.



---  

## Application of Kousisis: violations exist even when defendant provides equal value or no net loss occurs  

The appellate decision leaned heavily upon two passages from *Kousisis*:  

> “[T]he fraudulent‑inducement theory protects“…the acquisition “…of another person’s money…regardless​ whether…the defendant provides something…of equal value.”⁽³⁾^22  
> “…the statute does not require proof …that​ [the] victim suffered an economic loss…”⁽⁴⁾^23  

These propositions shattered any notion either party must demonstrate offsetting harms or benefits during prosecution.| In practical terms they mean ***any*** contract entered into under deceitful pretenses automatically triggers liability—even if what passes back is stocks worth roughly what buyers paid afterward (as might happen once inflated prices settle.) 

By mapping fact patterns onto these holdings—the inducement produced immediate cash outflows by investors while giving them nominally market‐priced shares—the Fourth Circuit affirmed liability irrespective of eventual gains or losses accrued.]21   Accordingly Even where individuals could later resell shares at comparable values momentarily nullifying any net pecuniary loss—for instance debates concerning exuberant post‐pump selloffs—the statutory bar would never be crossed if original acquisition was bought through deception.

## Distinguishing facts from Cimineli​i’s right‑to‑control scenario  

A streamlined comparison underscores why reversal was appropriate:

| Element | *Ciminelli* (Right‑to‐Control) | *Constantinescu* (Fraudulent Inducement) |
|--------|--------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| Victim's alleged loss | Information enabling future discretionary control over assets | Cash paid upfront for shares |
| Theory invoked | Deprivation of potential decision‐making power | Deception leading directly into contract requiring payment |
| Statutory focus | Property defined narrowly → excluded information | Broadly defined “money or property” → includes cash outlay |
| Supreme Court outcome | Conviction barred | Conviction allowed (fraud exists regardless warrant) |

Key factual differences are evident: *Ciminelli* involved lobbyists promising future contracts without any immediate monetary transaction whereas *Constantinescu* contains explicit allegations that followers transferred funds based upon falsities—in short, classic fraudulent inducement rather than abstract informational interference.

## Supporting authority affirming that deception aimed at obtaining money/property satisfies both statutory elements  

Beyond Evans and Kousisis themselves, several precedents cited within *Constantinescu* buttressed each analytical step:

* **United States v. Rafoi**, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir.“2023”) – mandates de novo review treating indictments as true statements when testing sufficiency.
* **United States v. Gaytan**, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir.“1996”) – recites three basic requisites for validity: enumeration, fair notice & double jeopardy protection.
* **United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo**, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir.“2000”) – restates plain requirement “an indictment must allege each material element”.
* **United States v. Greenlaw**, 84 F.… n.​6 – confirms reliance upon mail/wire‐fraud analogues when interpreting § 1348.
* **United States v…. Baker**, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir.“2019”) – reiterates non‐mirror‐image rule: plaintiff needn’t show victim loss equals defendant gain.
* Peripheral authorities such as ***United States v.… Smith*** (7th Cir.“2025”) illustrate broader circuits following Kousisis logic featuring equal value exchanges.* 

Collectively these cases form part of what Judge Engelhardt described as an unbroken line tracing modern interpretation：“When an accused furnishes materially false statements designed specifically ‘to induce another party…to convey money,’ every element required by §§ 1348/1349 attaches,” aligning his holding solidly within prevailing Fifth Circuit jurisprudence.

### Takeaways  

1️⃣ To survive a Rule 7(b) motion an indictment must allege —as true facts—each component constituting a *scheme* (**material misstatement + purpose + objective**) and an *intent* (**purposeful deception + aim at obtaining money/property**).   
2️⃣ Social‐media posts claiming fictitious trading activity readily qualify as material misrepresentations capable of supporting a scheme claim when coupled with language showing reliance (“so that other investors were induced”).   
3️⃣ An investor who pays cash for stock based upon those statements suffers deprivation of protected property—the commissionable unit bridged by contract formation—and therefore fits within § 1348's statutory scope regardless whether post‑sale market movements offset losses.\  
4️⃣ Courts treat any intentional pursuit of purchasers’ funds—as shown through profit figures—inherently evidentiary enough for intent—even without proof plaintiffs lost more than they gained.\  
5️⃣ The Supreme Court's bifurcation between right‑to‑control (*Ciminelli*) and fraudulent‐inducement (*Kousisis*) doctrines remains decisive; applying one where facts dictate another leads inevitably to error—as occurred below.\  
6️⃣ Precedent underscores de novo scrutiny over indictments and demands plain factual allegations—not proof—to survive dismissal.\  



Finally, understanding how *Constantinescu* integrates these concepts equips you—future litigators and judges—to dissect federal fraud charges accurately where modern digital communications serve as weapons scheming against unsuspecting investors.
