Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# From *Ciminelli* to *Kousisis*: Redefining Fraud’s Property Requirement in Securities Cases  

## Why United States v. Constantinescu matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of a district‑court dismissal in *United States v. Constantinescu* puts a decisive stamp on how federal fraud statutes are applied to modern “pump‑and‑dump” schemes run through social‑media platforms.  The opinion shows a circuit embracing the Supreme Court’s recent *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* decisions, clarifying that a fraud conviction no longer hinges on whether a victim’s “right‑to‑control” some future economic decision is at stake.  Instead, the court affirms that an indictment need only allege a **fraudulent‑inducement** scheme—i.e., a deception that causes a victim to part with money or other property—regardless of whether the victim ultimately suffers a financial loss.  In doing so, the Fifth Circuit signals a doctrinal shift that may broaden prosecutorial reach against influencers who weaponize misinformation in securities markets.

## The Supreme Court’s two‑track framework  

*United States v. Ciminelli*, 145 S.Ct. 1382 (2025), emphatically rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory of fraud.  The Court held that an alleged scheme to deprive a victim of valuable economic information—information that would enable the victim to make better discretionary decisions—does **not** satisfy the “property” element of federal fraud statutes because such information is not a traditional property interest.  By contrast, *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S.Ct. 1388 (2025), upheld convictions based on a **fraudulent‑inducement** theory: where a defendant lies in order to convince a victim to enter into a contract that requires surrendering money or other property, even if the victim receives something of comparable market value or suffers no net pecuniary loss.

These two cases establish parallel but distinct pathways for proving fraud under §§ 1348 and 1349 (the securities equivalents of wire and mail fraud).  The “right‑to‑control” route is closed; the “fraudulent‑inducement” route remains open and now enjoys explicit Supreme Court endorsement.

## How Constantinescu reconciles the two precedents  

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion reads the *Ciminelli* decision as setting firm boundaries around what counts as “property” while treating *Kousisis* as illuminating the permissible scope of fraudulent inducement.  The district court had dismissed the pump‑and‑dump indictment on the theory that it failed to allege deprivation of any traditional property right because defendants allegedly only stole their followers’ access to accurate market information.  The appellate court rejected that reading on two fronts:

1. **The indictment properly alleges deception that causes victims to exchange money for securities.**  Paragraph 13 of the superseding indictment claims defendants posted false statements expressly “to induce … other investors … to purchase the security,” thereby prompting followers to part with cash—a classic conveyance of money or property under § 1348.

2. **Intent to defraud is satisfied by an intent to obtain money or property, not by an intent to cause loss.**  Echoing *Kousisis*, Judge Engelhardt noted that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that defendants sought to make victims poorer; it suffices that they intended to acquire victims’ money or other property via deception.

By mapping each element of §§ 1348/1349 onto these factual allegations, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment meets every pleading requirement under *Rafoi* and *Gaytan*.  In so doing, it effectively adopts *Kousisis*’s broader view of “money or property” while respecting *Ciminelli*’s restriction against using pure informational deprivation as a standalone basis for fraud charges.

## The doctrinal shift away from a narrow property notion  

Historically, many courts treated “property” under federal fraud statutes as encompassing only tangible assets or legally recognized interests such as title, contractual rights, or intangible but proprietary rights (e.g., trade secrets).  The “right‑to‑control” argument sought to stretch this doctrine by treating future decision‑making authority as quasi‑property—a stretch rebuffed by *Ciminelli*.  

*Constantinescu* illustrates how appellate courts are now **recalibrating** that doctrine toward an outcome‑oriented approach:

- **Focus on what flows from the deception** – If the lie prompts a transaction where victims hand over cash (or other tradable assets), the statute is satisfied regardless of whether victims receive fair‐value consideration later.
- **Deference to factual allegations** – Under de novo review, courts will accept at face value any allegation that defendants used false statements "to induce" purchases, without demanding independent proof of actual loss at pleading stage.
- **Alignment with mail/wire fraud jurisprudence** – By borrowing language from its progenitor statutes, scholars can treat securities fraud under §§ 1348/1349 as functionally identical for purposes of “scheme” and “intent” analysis.

This orientation shifts the doctrinal emphasis from “what does the victim lose?” toward “what does the defendant gain?” – a subtle but important pivot endorsed by both *Kousisis* and now by Fifth Circuit precedent.

## Downstream consequences for prosecutors and defendants  

### A more aggressive prosecutorial toolkit  

Influencer‐driven securities scams have proliferated with platforms like TikTok, Instagram Reels, and Discord Discord communities where real‐time stock tips circulate widely.  The *Constantinescu* ruling gives U.S. Attorneys concrete footing to bring indictments alleging only:
1) false promotional statements about a security,
2) an intent **to induce purchases**, and
3) an objective **to obtain money or property** through those purchases.

Grammatically simple pleadings can survive motions to dismiss so long as they allege these core components without having proved actual investor loss at this early stage—a low bar for government lawyers accustomed to grand juries peppered with social media screenshots.

### Potential challenges under due‑process concerns  

The broadened reading raises inevitable constitutional questions: Does permitting any monetary exchange induced by deception encompass overly vague conduct? Critics may argue that labeling every influencer’s exaggerated optimism about a stock as "material" could chill speech protected by § 230 or First Amendment doctrines unless prosecutors can demonstrate falsity and reliance clearly traceable back to their statements.

Supreme Court precedent still requires “materiality,” but materiality can be satisfied where an ordinary investor would find the misstatement significant in deciding whether—and how much—to invest. Courts will likely see an uptick in pre‑trial disputes over what social‐media content meets this threshold—especially given algorithms' role in amplifying reach versus intentional targeting.

### Uniformity across circuits?  

While Fifth Circuit now aligns directly with *Kousisis*, other circuits remain split on applying its reasoning beyond classic investment adviser contexts (e.g., Ninth Circuit cases involving crypto token promotions).  If other appellate panels adopt similar reasoning, we could see national consensus narrowing gaps between dissenters who still cling loosely to traditional property concepts versus those who argue forfeiture-of-information claims are viable under federal fraud statutes—a doctrinal divide largely healed by *Ciminelli*.  

Alternatively, some panels may preserve higher pleading standards for cases lacking clear cash transactions—for example, when influencers simply recommend holding existing positions rather than urging new purchases—potentially creating venue shopping pressures for prosecutors seeking favorable forums.

### Impact on sentencing guidelines & civil remedies  

Because §§ 1348/1349 punish both deception and acquisition of money/property rather than net loss per se, sentencing calculators anchored in “loss amount” may become less predictive for pump‑and‑dump cases where victims are left holding overvalued shares later worth less than purchase price. DOJ guidelines already allow enhancement based on number of victims or use of sophisticated means; legislators may propose further revisions clarifying how artificially inflated securities should factor into loss calculations when actual dollar loss materializes after market correction rather than at time of transaction.

## A broader perspective  

The evolution from *Ciminelli* through *Kousisis* into Fifth Circuit precedent reflects an ongoing pragmatic turn: modern financial markets are increasingly mediated by information streams whose credibility can be weaponized at scale.  By focusing on **deception leading to relinquishment of something valuable**, courts keep pace with new forms of abuse while preserving due process safeguards against overly expansive "property" classifications rooted solely in informational rights.

As investors grow more accustomed—and arguably more vulnerable—to flash advice from charismatic online personalities, we can anticipate further litigation testing these limits: will courts deem statements about expected earnings “material”? Will ‘social proof’ metrics themselves become evidence of fraudulent inducement? Will corporate insiders coordinating coordinated posting campaigns trigger enhanced conspiracy statutes? 

*Constantinescu* says yes—to some extent—but also draws bright lines forbidding prosecutions based solely on abstract harms like denial of better market insight. It invites both prosecutors and scholars alike to hone their focus on tangible economic exchanges sparked by falsehoods—a move likely beneficial for investors seeking redress while respecting constitutional protections for speech and information flow.

## Conclusion  

The Fifth Circuit has effectively stitched together Supreme Court dicta into a coherent rule: **A federal securities fraud charge survives if plaintiffs allege—that via material misrepresentations—the defendants induced victims into giving up money or other property**, irrespective of whether victims suffered an ultimate pecuniary loss or merely forfeited control over future decisions. By doing so, it pushes forward beyond *Ciminelli*’s narrow view without straying from its core principle—that only owned interests count as “property”—while fully embracing *Kousisis*’s expansive fraudulent‑inducement doctrine.

For regulators combating influencer-fueled pump-and-dumps, this decision is welcome news: it clears procedural hurdles and sharpens focus on deceptive tactics rather than abstract informational harm. For scholars warning against unchecked expansion of federal fraud power, it offers an illustration ripe for continued debate—and perhaps future Supreme Court refinement—as our financial ecosystem keeps evolving faster than traditional legal categories can keep up.



# Pump‑and‑Dump in the Age of Instagram and TikTok: Prosecuting ‘Finfluencers’ Under Federal Securities Law  

## Why This Case Matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of a district‑court dismissal in *United States v. Constantinescu* (2025) is the first appellate decision that squarely addresses whether traditional securities‑fraud statutes can be used against social‑media personalities who hype stocks for profit. In a world where a single TikTok video can move tens of millions of dollars of equity, prosecutors need clear guidance on what “scheme to defraud” looks like when the “victims” are followers scrolling through an endless feed rather than sophisticated institutional investors. By mapping the Supreme Court’s recent doctrinal split between the *right‑to‑control* theory (rejected in *Ciminelli*) and the *fraudulent‑inducement* theory (affirmed in *Kousisis*), the Fifth Circuit has effectively told federal prosecutors that influencing a follower to spend money on a security under false pretenses is enough to satisfy both elements of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349. The decision therefore opens the door for more aggressive enforcement against “finfluencers” and raises fresh questions about how far existing statutes stretch into the algorithmic landscape of modern markets.

## The Facts  

The government indicted eight defendants—among them Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, and Daniel Knight—for a coordinated “pump‑and‑dump” operation conducted primarily through Instagram reels, TikTok clips, and Twitter threads. According to the superseding indictment, each defendant presented himself as an expert trader with large followings ranging from hundreds of thousands to several million users. They would buy shares of low‑volume stocks, post repeatedly that they held sizeable positions (“I’m long $X million worth”) and that massive upside was imminent, then encourage followers to buy using persuasive language (“buy now before it rockets”). After a surge in price generated by these posts—amplified further by platform algorithms—the defendants covertly sold their holdings for profit, allegedly netting $114 million overall. One defendant pled guilty; the remaining seven moved to dismiss on grounds that the indictment failed to allege that they intended to deprive anyone of a “traditional” property interest.

## The Legal Landscape Before Constantinescu  

Federal securities‑fraud provisions (§ 1348) are modeled after mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes: a prosecution must prove (1) a scheme or artifice to defraud, (2) intent to defraud, and (3) some obtaining of money or other property by means of false pretenses. Historically courts have treated “property” as something tangible—a cash payment, a security certificate—or at least something that can be measured in pecuniary terms. In *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023), the Supreme Court rejected what it called the “right‑to‑control” theory, holding that fraud based solely on depriving a victim of valuable economic information does not satisfy § 1348 because such information is not itself property. Conversely, *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) upheld convictions under what it termed the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory: a defendant can be liable where false statements cause another party to part with money or property—even if the defendant does not intend any direct loss to be suffered by the victim (“the victim may receive market‑value securities”).

Before *Constantinescu*, district courts were split on whether an indictment alleging only deception—without explicit injury—was sufficient when victims were passive followers rather than identifiable investors who signed contracts or placed direct orders through brokers.

## The Fifth Circuit’s Reasoning  

Judge Engelhardt wrote for a unanimous panel that the indictment satisfied both statutory elements because it pleaded **(1)** a scheme “to pump … [by] posting false … information … so that other investors were induced to purchase” and **(2)** an intent “to obtain … money … by means of false pretenses.” The court relied heavily on *Kousisis*, emphasizing that the fraudulent‑inducement theory protects money or property irrespective of whether an actual loss occurs beyond what is exchanged at market value. By alleging that defendants sought profit from followers’ purchases rather than merely withholding information, the indictment avoided *Ciminelli*’s prohibited right‑to‑control model.

The court also dismissed defendants’ argument that an act of merely providing “valuable economic information” could not constitute fraud because no traditional property interest was taken away. Instead it pointed out that each follower’s purchase involved spending cash—a protected property interest under § 1348—and that deception about price expectations satisfies “material misrepresentation.” Consequently, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal as legally insufficient and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.

## What’s New? The Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory Goes Digital  

*Constantinescu* extends *Kousisis* into an arena where words travel not through FedEx envelopes or telephone lines but through algorithmically curated feeds reaching millions in seconds. By confirming that an influencer who lies about his own holdings can be prosecuted under § 1348 when he induces others to spend money on a security, the Fifth Circuit delivers two pivotal signals:

1. **Materiality Is Not Limited by Traditional Investor Status** – Followers who are simply “likes” or “views” count as victims if they part with cash after relying on false statements.
2. **The Scheme Can Be Distributed Over Multiple Platforms** – Because § 1348 covers any scheme “in connection with…any security,” its scope naturally embraces cross‑platform campaigns even if each individual post falls below traditional thresholds for “securities solicitation.”

The decision therefore clarifies that regulators need not wait for investors to file complaints before moving forward; prosecutors may rely on aggregated social‑media data showing coordinated false statements designed to move price.

## Enforcement Challenges in an Algorithmic Marketplace  

While *Constantinescu* clears doctrinal hurdles, practical enforcement remains uphill:

- **Identifying Coordinated Conduct** – Influencers often operate through networks of secondary accounts (“sockpuppets”) designed to amplify messages via likes and comments rather than direct reposts. Proving conspiracy may require sophisticated forensic analysis of metadata, timing patterns, and platform recommendation engines.
  
- **Algorithmic Amplification as Aiding Conduct** – Social platforms themselves push deceptive posts higher when engagement spikes—a de facto “co-conspirator.” Courts have yet to decide whether platform algorithms can be considered part of a scheme under § 1348 or whether they merely provide a conduit akin to wire communications.
  
- **Data Access & Preservation** – Unlike traditional wire communications subject to subpoena under Title II regulations, social‑media data resides behind proprietary APIs subject to privacy policies and national security shields when servers sit overseas.
  
- **Scale vs Individual Intent** – Prosecutors must demonstrate actual intent by each influencer—not merely negligence—that they knowingly used false statements for profit—a fact‐finding task complicated by cryptic language (“I think this stock could go up”) designed as plausible deniability.
  
These challenges suggest a growing role for specialized units within DOJ’s Criminal Division (e.g., Computer Crime & Intellectual Property Section) collaborating closely with SEC investigators familiar with market manipulations via digital media.

## Jurisdictional Hurdles and Global Influencer Economies  

Finite geographic boundaries no longer contain influencer reach. A Texas resident may post on Instagram while followers access his content from Europe or Asia where securities regulation differs dramatically from U.S.~law. While § 1348 has broad extraterritorial reach insofar as conduct substantially affects U.S.~markets (*United States v.* Cox), courts have been cautious extending criminal jurisdiction absent clear nexus—e.g., involvement of U.S.~residents or U.S.-based exchanges.

In *Constantinescu*, all defendants were U.S.~persons operating primarily from within Fifth Circuit jurisdiction—but future cases may involve non‑U.S.~ citizens who solicit American investors through English-language content hosted abroad. Resolving such issues may depend less on statutory language than on cooperation agreements with foreign regulators and platform-based geofencing mechanisms designed by companies themselves.

## Policy Options: FTC Guidance vs Legislative Amendments  

Given these enforcement complexities, policymakers have two primary levers:

1️⃣ **Strengthen FTC Disclosure Rules** – The FTC already requires clear disclosures when an online endorsement constitutes advertising (§ 5(a)(11)). However—as *Kousisis* demonstrates—fraud is not limited to undisclosed material connections; deceptive statements about one’s own trading position are actionable even absent any commercial relationship with followers. Updated FTC guidance could treat finfluencer posts about personal trades as “investment advice” subject either (a) mandatory registration as investment advisers under the Investment Advisers Act or (b) mandatory preclearance disclosures stating ownership interests and trade execution dates—similar requirements imposed on broker‐dealers under FINRA Rule 2210.
   
2️⃣ **Legislative Amendments Targeting Digital Promotion** – Congress could amend §§ 80b–181(c)(1)(B)(iii) (the anti–pump-and-dump provision), explicitly including “any electronic communication…including but not limited to social media posts,” thereby clarifying coverage without relying on broader mail/wire analogies prone to interpretive uncertainty.
   Additionally, defining “public offering solicitation via mass digital platforms” as subject matter requiring registration could deter casual pumpers while preserving legitimate financial commentary.
   
Both approaches would address gaps left open by reliance solely upon criminal statutes which require proof beyond discretionary judgment while also tackling civil liability pathways (“Securities Act Section 10(b)” claims). Nonetheless policymakers must balance consumer protection against stifling legitimate discussion—a line already blurred by prominent commentators such as Jim Cramer or finance podcasts whose opinions influence markets without formal licensure yet operate within established tolerance zones given their fame versus ordinary finfluencers lacking resources for full compliance regimes.

## Conclusion  

*United States v.* Constantinescu marks an important step in aligning century‑old securities fraud provisions with twenty‑first‑century communication technologies. By anchoring prosecutorial authority solidly within the fraudulent‑inducement theory—clarified through recent Supreme Court precedent—the Fifth Circuit sends an unmistakable message: influencers who weaponize misinformation about their own trading activity can be charged just as readily as traditional stock promoters who issue press releases.

That doctrinal clarity does not erase formidable practical obstacles—from parsing algorithmically magnified posts into conspiratorial conduct to navigating cross‑border reach—but it furnishes law enforcement agencies with a clear legal scaffold upon which ingenuity in investigation can build. What remains uncertain is how quickly regulators will adapt their civil guidance frameworks—and whether Congress will embed these digital realities directly into statutory text—to keep pace with an ever more fluid market where every like may be another step toward illicit enrichment.

In short, while we have yet again witnessed technology outpacing lawmaking speedily enough during any given scandal cycle—remembering Wall Street’s meme-driven surges over meme coins just months prior—the Fifth Circuit’s decision ensures our—not your-later—lawstill has teeth when it comes time for punishing those who turn followers into unwitting cash registers for their own gain.



# Pleading Fraud: De Novo Review versus Rule 8(b) – Lessons from *United States v. Constantinescu*

## Why *Constantinescu* Matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* is already being cited in forums ranging from securities‑law conferences to DOJ training seminars. At its core, the opinion clarifies how a federal indictment must satisfy the “scheme‑to‑defraud” and “intent‑to‑defraud” elements of the securities‑fraud statutes when the alleged conduct consists mainly of false social‑media hype rather than overt theft of cash or securities.  

For criminal prosecutors, the ruling offers a concrete roadmap for surviving early dismissal motions that hinge on whether an indictment “states an offense.” For defendants—and their counsel—it underscores that merely arguing victims were left with market‑value securities (or that they were deprived only of “information”) will not usually defeat a fraud charge once the government has alleged fraudulent inducement of a purchase. In short, *Constantinescu* reaffirms that the federal fraud statutes protect money or property obtained from victims, even when victims receive something “of equal value” in return.

## The Indictment‑Sufficiency Standard Under Rule 8(b)  

Rule 8(b) commands federal criminal pleadings to contain “a short and plain statement … sufficient to give notice … of the nature ... charges.” Courts apply this rule **de novo**—they read the indictment as true and ask whether each statutory element is alleged on its face (see *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th at 993 (5th Cir. 2023)). The Fifth Circuit reiterated that approach in *Constantinescu*: it examined whether the superseding indictment alleged (1) a material misrepresentation; (2) a scheme to use that misrepresentation to obtain money or property; and (3) an intent embodying both deception and an objective to obtain something of value.

The district court had dismissed on a novel theory—that because the alleged victims received securities at market price they suffered no “property loss,” the indictment failed to allege a protected property interest under §1348. The appellate court rejected that premise outright, noting that Rule 8(b)’s purpose is not to ensure proof of actual loss at the pleading stage but merely to ensure that each element—including deprivation of money or property—is **alleged**.  

In practice, this means prosecutors need not couch their allegations in terms of calculated victim losses; they must simply assert that defendants induced victims to part with money (or other property) by means of false statements, and that they intended to obtain that money (or property). The *Constantinescu* opinion shows how precisely those allegations can be phrased without running afoul of Rule 8(b).

## Distinguishing “Fraudulent‑Inducement” From the Rejected Right‑to‑Control Theory  

The Supreme Court’s recent decisions provide the doctrinal backdrop for the Fifth Circuit’s analysis:

* **Ciminelli v. United States** (2025) held that fraud statutes protect traditional property interests—not merely “the right to valuable economic information.” The Court struck down convictions based on a “right‑to‑control” theory where defendants were accused solely of withholding information that would have helped victims make better decisions.
  
* **Kousisis v. United States** (2025) clarified that a “fraudulent‑inducement” theory—where a defendant uses a material misstatement to persuade a victim into a transaction involving money or property—does **not** require proof that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.

*Constantinescu* applies these precedents squarely. The indictment does not allege any “right‑to‑control” claim; instead it says defendants posted false statements designed expressly to **induce** followers to buy shares, thereby transferring cash from followers to defendants’ accounts before they dumped their positions at inflated prices. By anchoring its analysis in *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit confirmed that once an inducement leads victims to surrender money or property—even if they later receive something else in return—the statutory injury requirement is met.

## Intent To Defraud Is Satisfied By Profit Motive  

Defendants’ motion also argued that an intent‐to‐defraud requires an intention **to harm** victims rather than merely an intention **to profit**. The Supreme Court has consistently rejected this narrower reading. In *Kousisis*, Justice Sotomayor emphasized that fraud occurs when a defendant seeks “to obtain” another’s money or property *regardless* of any desire to injure the victim’s bottom line.

The Fifth Circuit echoed this language: it noted that the indictment alleges defendants bought securities first, then posted misleading claims so followers would buy them too—ultimately allowing defendants “to profit.” That profit motive illustrates an intent **to obtain** money and therefore satisfies both prongs of §1348’s intent requirement as articulated in *Evans* (5th Cir. 2018). Importantly, actual economic loss was unnecessary for sufficiency; prosecutors need only demonstrate an objective—on their face—to acquire something of value from another party via deception.

## How Criminal Pleading Differs From Civil Fraud Pleadings  

In criminal cases, Rule 8(b)’s low threshold contrasts sharply with civilian litigation’s heightened pleading standards under Rule 9(b), which demands more specific factual detail when alleging fraud (“the circumstances constituting fraud … shall be stated with particularity”). Civil plaintiffs must describe who made which false statements, what specific information was conveyed, how it was material, and what precise loss resulted—all *before* discovery begins.

The *Constantinescu* opinion underscores why such granular specificity is unnecessary in criminal indictments:

1. **Focus on statutory elements**, not loss quantification  
   An indictment must simply allege deception intended **and** resulting in acquisition of money/property—not measure each victim’s damage.
2. **Narrative sufficiency over evidentiary detail**  
   A short paragraph describing false posts intended to trigger purchases adequately signals notice because it enumerates every essential element.
3. **De novo legal review protects against overly granular demands**  
   Since appellate courts evaluate sufficiency without reference to evidence outside the complaint, any plausibly alleged scheme will survive unless it omits one required element entirely.

That does not mean prosecutors can write vague indictments (“defendant committed fraud”). They still need enough factual granularity—e.g., who posted what statements about which security at roughly what time—to give defendants fair notice and satisfy Rule 8(b)’s notice requirement (*Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551). But they do not have to trace exact dollar losses or show how many followers bought shares at inflated prices—a level more reminiscent of civil pleadings than criminal ones.

## Practical Takeaways for Prosecutors  

Guidance distilled from *Constantinescu*:

| What Drafting Requires | Why It Matters |
|------------------------|----------------|
| **Identify each defendant’s deceptive conduct** – e.g., specific social‑media posts containing false price predictions or fabricated personal holdings | Demonstrates material misrepresentation & satisfies element one |
| **Tie those statements directly to solicitations that cause followers to transfer money or other property** – phrase as “induced purchasers,” “prompted buyers,” etc.| Establishes fraudulent‑inducement theory under §1348 |
| **State clearly you seek acquisition of victim money/property**, not merely control over their decision‐making | Avoids right‑to‑control pitfall highlighted in *Ciminelli* |
| **Assert profit motive or expected gain**, even if you do not allege actual victim loss | Satisfies intent‐to‐defraud per *Kousisis* |
| **Include any substantial allegation about volume/scale** – e.g., total profit $114 million | Gives defendants fair notice while keeping banality low enough for rule compliance |

By threading these elements into each count, prosecutors give themselves maximal protection against Rule 8(b) attacks while staying within constitutional limits—no need for detailed forensic accounting absent discovery.

## Doctrinal Ripple Effects  

1. **Broader Reach Into Influencer Markets**  
   With influencers increasingly touting stocks, cryptocurrency tokens, NFTs or even real estate projects on platforms like TikTok or X, courts now have clear authority—via *Constantinescu*—to treat exaggerated hype as actionable fraudulent inducement so long as there is an underlying transfer worth $ value.
   
2. **Potential Convergence With Civil Enforcement**  
   While civil plaintiffs still face Rule 9(b)’s specificity hurdle, regulatory agencies may lean more heavily on criminal indictments as evidentiary scaffolding when pursuing private actions later under §10(b). A robust criminal charge can accelerate settlement negotiations even absent proven monetary loss.
   
3. **Limits Remain On “Information‑Only” Schemes**  
   Defendants wrapping schemes around pure misinformation without an accompanying solicitation will still fall prey under *Ciminelli*. Prosecutors should thus avoid framing every deceptive statement as fraudulent inducement if no monetary transaction follows.
   
4. **Future Jurisprudence May Refine ‘Money or Property’ Scope**  
   Some commentators fear an expansion toward non‑tangible assets like data vouchers or loyalty points could stretch ‘property’ too far—but current precedent anchors protection firmly on transferrable economic value (*Kousisis*, *Evans*).

## Bottom Line  

*United States v. Constantinescu* exemplifies how federal courts apply—and arguably reaffirm—the de novo review mandated by Rule 8(b). By leaning heavily on Supreme Court guidance from *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit demonstrated that an indictment need only allege deception aimed at obtaining money or property; proving actual loss is unnecessary at this stage. 

For practitioners drafting securities-fraud indictments—or any fraud charge where inducement drives victim transfers—the lesson is straightforward: focus your narrative plumbing on who said what, who was induced to part with cash or another asset, and why your client expected financial gain from those transfers. When you do so concisely yet fully—to satisfy both statutory notice requirements and the de novo scrutiny embodied in Rule 8(b)—your complaint is far more likely than today’s revived district-court opinions deemingly flippant claims—that it will survive early dismissal attacks whenever you bring it before a federal grand jury again.

# Conspiracy Across Platforms: The Reach of § 1349 in Multi‑Defendant Influencer Schemes  

*By [Your Name], Law & Technology Analyst*  

---

## Why *United States v. Constantinescu* Matters  

The Federal Trade Commission has long warned that “pump‑and‑dump” scams are migrating from gray‑market brokers onto Instagram stories, TikTok videos, Discord chatrooms, and livestreams.  What was once the purview of professional financiers is now orchestrated by a constellation of online personalities who command millions of followers across disparate platforms.  The Fifth Circuit’s decision reversing the district court’s dismissal of the *Constantinescu* indictment therefore marks the first appellate endorsement that a federal fraud conspiracy charge can survive a facial challenge when the alleged scheme hinges on coordinated misinformation spread through social media.  In short, prosecutors now have clear authority to treat a networked group of influencers who collectively hype a penny stock as a single conspiratorial enterprise under 18 U.S.C. § 1349.  

Beyond its immediate procedural impact—allowing the government to pursue three separate securities‑fraud counts plus a conspiracy count against each defendant—the opinion revitalizes doctrinal discussions about how the “scheme to defraud” element interacts with modern digital communications.  It also foregrounds two emerging themes that will shape future multi‑defendant fraud prosecutions: (1) whether an implied “joint venture” or “enterprise” suffices to infer an agreement among loosely connected actors, and (2) how prosecutors may marshal digital footprints—tweets, Discord logs, scheduled YouTube uploads—to satisfy the overt‑act requirement of a § 1349 conspiracy charge.  

---

## A Quick Recap of *Constantinescu*  

The government charged eight influencers—including Edward Constantinescu and Perry “PJ” Matlock—with conspiring to run a classic pump‑and‑dump operation: they bought a thinly traded security, posted false or misleading statements about its prospects across Twitter, Instagram Reels, YouTube live streams, and private Discord channels, thereby enticing followers to buy the stock at inflated prices before dumping their own holdings for profit.  The superseding indictment alleged $114 million in aggregate gains and invoked both §§ 1348 (securities fraud) and 1349 (conspiracy to commit securities fraud).  

The district court dismissed because it believed the indictment failed to allege a deprivation of “traditional property.”  On appeal the Fifth Circuit held otherwise.  Relying heavily on Supreme Court precedents—*Ciminelli* (2023) which rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory when only economic information is at stake, and *Kousisis* (2025) which affirmed the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory—the panel concluded that an allegation that defendants induced followers to part with money satisfies both the “scheme” and “intent” elements of § 1348.  By extension, those allegations buttress each conspirator’s liability under § 1349 because they establish a common unlawful objective backed by overt acts (the misleading posts).  

---

## Conspiracy Under § 1349: What the Court Required  

A § 1349 charge requires two core ingredients:

1. **An agreement** among two or more persons to commit an underlying offense—in this case securities fraud under § 1348.
2. **At least one overt act** taken in furtherance of that agreement after it was formed.

The *Constantinescu* panel did not need to grapple with any novel definition of “agreement.”  Instead it focused on whether the indictment plausibly alleged *any* scheme worthy of prosecution—a lower bar than proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt at trial but higher than merely asserting parallel conduct.  Because each defendant was alleged to have posted false statements with the goal of coaxing followers into buying shares—a move expressly designed to generate cash—for their own benefit, those allegations jointly satisfy the statutory definition of an unlawful objective shared by all actors involved.  

Crucially, the court emphasized that “[t]he fraudulent‑inducement theory … protects money and property,” distinguishing it from *Ciminelli*’s rejected right‑to‑control approach.^1 By framing each influencer’s deceptive post as an overt act directed at obtaining victims’ money or property—not merely their informational advantage—the opinion demonstrates that even loosely coordinated activity can meet Congress’s conspiratorial intent requirement when tied together by a common illicit motive.*  

---

## Coordinated Deception Across Platforms  

### Evidence of Agreement

In traditional RICO or racketeering cases prosecutors often rely on meetings recorded in surveillance footage or documented phone calls.  Social‑media conspiracies lack such face‑to‑face rendezvous but generate abundant electronic artifacts:

* **Coordinated posting schedules** documented through shared Google Calendars or third‑party scheduling tools.
* **Cross‑platform tagging** where one influencer mentions another’s handle (“@PJ’s pick”) within a tweet or Stream.
* **Private Discord servers** used by participants to discuss timing (“post tomorrow at noon”) and script language (“use ‘breakout potential’ phrase”).

These data points form what scholars now describe as a “digital handshake.”  While none individually proves an explicit meeting-of-the-minds, taken together they create an inferential chain linking each defendant’s conduct directly back to a unified plan—a standard compatible with Ninth Circuit precedent holding that *“a conspiracy may be inferred from conduct demonstrating concerted action toward an unlawful objective.”*^[2] The Fifth Circuit’s decision does not explicitly address such inferential techniques but its affirmation that mere allegations of coordinated false statements satisfy § 1349 suggests it will accept this modern evidentiary framework when duly pled.  

### Overt Acts

The admission in paragraph 13—that defendants “posted false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase …”—is treated by the court as an overt act satisfying clause two of ¬§ 1349.^[3] In practice this means every public message containing material misrepresentation counts toward fulfilling the overt‐act requirement *so long as it is executed after an alleged agreement has been reached*. Consequently prosecutors need only show that at least one influencer posted after discussing tactics within their private group chat—a low threshold once digital logs become admissible evidence.*

---

## Emerging Doctrines: Joint Venture Liability Meets Digital Networks  

While *Constantinescu* does not expressly adopt any new “joint venture” rule, its analysis aligns neatly with growing scholarship treating multi‑defendant online scams as de facto enterprises rather than loosely affiliated individuals.^[4] In antitrust law courts have long recognized that participants who execute complementary acts toward an illegal end may be held liable even absent explicit contract formation (*United States v. Towne*, 1992). A similar line is being pursued in securities law circles where analysts posit a **“enterprise conspiracy”** model: any group whose members knowingly advance a single fraudulent strategy becomes liable for all others’ acts performed within that enterprise.^[5]

Applying this model would permit prosecutors—even where many influencers never directly communicated—to attach liability based on shared branding cues (“#CryptoBoom”) or mutual reliance upon one another’s follower bases.  Such an approach would effectively expand what constitutes “agreement” beyond formal deliberations into patterns recognizable through algorithmic analysis or network‐graph mapping.*  

Nevertheless caution is warranted.   The Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure still requires proof “beyond reasonable doubt” at trial that each defendant possessed *mens rea*––the conscious desire to join the illicit plan.^[6] Courts may resist conflating parallel conduct with an actual conspiracy if defendants can plausibly argue independent decisionmaking influenced only by market hype rather than an orchestrated plot.* Thus while joint‐venture language offers a powerful prosecutorial lens post‑*Constantinescu*, its adoption will hinge upon how lower courts balance inferential networking evidence against First Amendment protections concerning expressive speech.*

---

## Digital Footprints as Proof—and Their Limits  

A practical question raised by *Constantinescu* concerns admissibility and authentication of electronic data spanning multiple services subject to different terms-of-service rules:

1. **Metadata** embedded in tweets or YouTube uploads—including timestamps, IP addresses, account identifiers—can attest directly to who posted what when.
2. **Server logs** from Discord servers often preserve chat histories even after messages are deleted.
3. **Third‑party archival services** such as Wayback Machine snapshots provide contemporaneous captures useful when accounts are later removed for policy violations.

Courts have increasingly treated such data as reliable unless challenged by relevance or integrity issues.^[7] However defense teams will likely invoke spoliation arguments (“the government accessed these logs without warrant”) or raise authenticating hurdles concerning deepfakes or compromised accounts—a frontier legally untested at scale.* 

Lawmakers might respond by clarifying preservation duties for platforms hosting financial advice or obligating them either civilly—or even criminally—to retain communications flagged by algorithms designed for market manipulation detection.* 

---

## Downstream Effects: Prosecutorial Strategy & Defendants’ Leverage  

The Fifth Circuit ruling effectively clears the path for multi‑defendant indictments targeting entire online ecosystems instead of isolated actors†^ [8]   This could produce several notable shifts:

* **Mass conspiracies** involving dozens—or even hundreds—of microinfluencers become feasible because each additional defendant simply needs evidence tying them into the same promotional timeline.
* **Plea bargaining leverage** intensifies; defendants facing concurrent conspiracy counts tied together by shared electronic evidence may be more inclined toward cooperation deals.
* **Potential chilling effect** on legitimate financial content creation—that is unless clear lines are drawn distinguishing permissible opinion from actionable deception under fraudulent‑inducement theory.
* **Judicial workload increase**, given compounded trials where juries must parse large volumes of digital postings into coherent narratives about collective intent.

From a defense standpoint, counsel may look toward thematic arguments rooted in free speech (the First Amendment protects opinions about market prospects) while demanding strict proof that each alleged post was *material* under SEC standards—a high bar historically met only where investors would reasonably rely upon statements.^[9] Additionally attorneys might seek dismissal based on lack of distinct victimhood if many followers purchased shares voluntarily despite warnings—a nuance reminiscent of *Kousisis*, wherein actual pecuniary loss was deemed unnecessary but demonstrable reliance remains crucial.* 

---

## A Forward Look  

The *Constantinescu* panel did not resolve every doctrinal tension surrounding distributed fraud conspiracies—but its deference to Congress’s broad language in §§ 1348/1349 makes clear why subsequent prosecutorial efforts will increasingly frame influencer networks as single adversarial enterprises rather than coincidental offenders acting alone. As data analytics continue sharpening investigators’ ability to map interaction webs between TikTok creators, Discord moderators, and Twitter bots, courts will inevitably confront new questions about attribution, intent proof standards, and constitutional limits governing speech about speculative investments.*

Legislators concerned about market integrity might consider codifying specific definitions—for example designating large-scale promotional campaigns involving paid promotion across multiple accounts as presumptively conspiratorial unless adequately disclosed—to provide clearer guidance both for market participants and criminal adjudicators.* Until then,* **the key takeaway** *for practitioners is simple*: when influencers synchronize deceptive hype across any combination of social media services—be it Twitter threads timed with YouTube livestreams rehearsed over Discord—their collective conduct now squarely falls within ·§ 1349‘s reach*.   Prosecutors have received judicial validation that sprawling digital schemata are sufficient grounds for charging conspiracy; defense teams must adapt accordingly by focusing early on disproving any shared illicit purpose rather than merely contesting individual misstatements.*

---  
  
> **Bottom line:** *United States v. Constantinescu* signals a watershed moment where federal fraud law catches up with influencer culture. By recognizing coordinated online posts as overt acts feeding a single fraudulent inducement scheme, the Fifth Circuit unlocks robust conspiracy tools capable of holding entire digital marketing crews accountable—a development poised both to deter future pump-and-dump schemes and ignite fresh debates over free expression online.*
