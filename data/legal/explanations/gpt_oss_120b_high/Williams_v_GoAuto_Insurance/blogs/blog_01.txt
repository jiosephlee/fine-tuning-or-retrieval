Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Signature Required? Unpacking the “Certification” Mandate in Louisiana’s Insurance‑Premium‑Finance Cancellation Statute  

## Why This Case Matters  

When an insured finances an auto‑policy premium through a third‑party lender, disputes over who gets to cancel the policy—and when—can have life‑or‑death consequences for coverage. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, 24‑30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025), the Fifth Circuit resolved a narrow but crucial question: does Louisiana’s cancellation statute require a handwritten or otherwise individualized signature on the financing company’s “certification” that all statutory prerequisites have been met before an insurer can terminate coverage? The panel’s answer—no—does more than settle a class‑action footnote; it signals that electronic, automated notices can satisfy a statute originally drafted in an era of paper mail and physical signatures. As premium‑finance arrangements proliferate nationwide, courts across the country will watch this decision closely for guidance on how “certifying” language is applied in an increasingly digital world.

## The Statutory Framework  

Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) compels a premium finance company that wishes to end an insured’s policy to send the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four items: (i) that a valid power of attorney exists, (ii) that the financing agreement is in default and uncured, (iii) that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured specifying its mailing date, and (iv) that copies were sent to all parties having an interest under the agreement.  

The statute then instructs insurers that “[u]pon receipt of such notice…the insurer shall consider that cancellation…requested…and may proceed to cancel.”  §§ 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)–(ii).  

Two procedural safeguards flow from these requirements: first, a fixed point in time by which the policy ends (the “effective date” tied to receipt), and second, an affordance for the insured to cure any default within ten days after receiving notice. Louisiana appellate precedent has repeatedly emphasized “strict adherence” to these steps; errors allegedly render a cancellation ineffective (*Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d at 689; *Eaglin v. Champion Ins.*, 558 So. 2d at 287).

## The Fifth Circuit’s Reasoning  

Judge Southwick’s opinion first wrestles with whether “certifying” implicitly demands an individual’s signature on the cancellation request sent from APAC (American Premium Assistance Co.) to GoAuto Insurance. The statute itself contains no explicit mention of signatures, nor does it define “certify.” Relying on ordinary meaning—“to attest or affirm”—the court holds that a certification can be rendered without any handwritten endorsement so long as it truthfully attests to each statutory element.  

The panel notes that Louisiana courts have previously described certification as simply “a statement certifying compliance” (*KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood*, 115 So. 3d at 37). No appellate decision has ever read “certify” as mandating an actual signature block or identifying employee name, even where case law highlighted missing signatures as potentially problematic (*Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So. 3d at 579). In *Benitez* the Fifth Circuit en banc observed that while an unsigned notice was deficient on multiple grounds, it did not hold that lack of a signature alone invalidated cancellation—leaving room for judicial discretion.

Turning next to “receipt,” plaintiffs argued that because GoAuto never physically handled APAC–signed papers before its automated system terminated coverage, true receipt under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) was lacking. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument by turning to Black’s Law Dictionary definitions of “receive” and “receipt,” which encompass taking possession or obtaining something through electronic transmission—as would occur when GoAuto’s web service processes an incoming e‑mail request in real time. Accordingly, once GoAuto’s computer system accepted APAC’s e‑mail—including the attached certification—the statutory condition was satisfied and instantaneous termination was permissible.

Finally, having determined both certification and receipt requirements were met electronically, Judge Southwick declined to certify the question for review by the Louisiana Supreme Court—a step permissible under *Jefferson v. Lead Indus.*—but denied because there was no compelling reason per established precedent (*Raich* standard).

## How This Fits With Prior Louisiana Authority  

Louisiana appellate decisions have consistently treated failure to follow any statutory element as fatal (*Britten v. Reavis*, 503 So. 2d at 1154). Yet none have addressed whether *the form* of certification matters when technology supplants pen‑and‑paper practices. By refusing to read signing requirements into “certify,” the Fifth Circuit aligns with prior decisions emphasizing substantive compliance over formalistic minutiae—a textualist stance reinforced by an absence of contrary legislative history.

The *Benitez* discussion remains instructive: there too we see a court flagging missing signatures but ultimately allowing other compliance factors—such as proper mailing dates—to prevail when evaluating cancellation effectiveness under unrelated statutes governing notices (specifically motor carrier requirements). In *Williams*, however, those peripheral concerns disappear entirely because § 9:3550(G) expressly places all certification duties onto “the insurance premium finance company,” not onto any particular employee.

This reading also dovetails with *Delatte*’s admonition against adding procedural steps not mandated by statute (“strict adherence…is not an invitation for courts…to add”). By refusing to impose a signature requirement absent textual command, Judge Southwick respects legislative silence and avoids converting an already precise procedural scheme into one susceptible to endless proof‑of‑authorship disputes.

## Practical Implications for Finance Companies and Insurers  

For premium-finance firms operating in Louisiana—and potentially elsewhere where similar statutes exist—the ruling offers clear guidance:

1. **Electronic Certifications Are Sufficient** – A timestamped e‑mail containing a concise attestation checklist fulfills both certification and receipt mandates provided it is sent from a recognized corporate account or system authority.
2. **Signature Stamps Remain Optional** – While APAC still stamps printed copies later in its workflow (perhaps for internal audit purposes), those post‑cancellation additions are legally superfluous.
3. **Logging Becomes Paramount** – Because courts now accept digital transactions as fulfilling statutory formalities, parties must retain demonstrable logs showing when emails were dispatched and accepted by insurer web services.
4. **Risk Management Shifts** – Insurers should verify that their web service listeners correctly authenticate incoming requests (e.g., via mutually authenticated TLS certificates) because any technical glitch could generate unintended cancellations without obvious human oversight.
5. **Consumer Notification** – While insurers can now rely on automated cancellations after ten days post–notice issuance, they must still ensure insureds receive clear communication about their right—and actual ability—to cure default prior to auto-termination.

As insurance regulators across states examine modernizing legacy statutes—often drafted before broadband ubiquity—*Williams* provides persuasive authority for adopting technologically neutral language (“notice…sent,” “receipt”) rather than persisting with antiquated specifications (“by private carrier”).

## Potential Doctrinal Ripples  

The decision sets several doctrinal precedents worth watching:

- **Statutory Interpretation of Procedural Safeguards** – Courts may increasingly read procedural statutes through a lens focused on functional compliance versus ritualistic formality when technology renders traditional assumptions obsolete.
- **Fifth Circuit Uniformity** – While *Williams* directly interprets Louisiana law under Erie principles (as federal law applies federal substantive standards but defers civil procedure), its reasoning will likely be adopted by other federal district courts sitting in Louisiana absent divergent state supreme court guidance.
- **Electronic Signatures Under State Law** – Although Louisiana has embraced e‑signatures through its Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA), this opinion shows broader willingness to accept digital attestations even where statutes remain silent.
- **Potential Legislative Response** – If consumer advocates argue that removing any signature requirement diminishes accountability—a concern magnified when cancellations happen algorithmically—the legislature may amend § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) expressly mandating either an electronic signature or another authentication method.
- **Cross‑State Influence** – Several states employ analogous financing-cancellation regimes (e.g., Texas Statutes § 5411E1). Practitioners will cite *Williams* when arguing against required handwritten certifications where electronic processes dominate market practice.

Overall, while *Williams* resolves only whether signatures are mandatory under one clause, its broader implication is fairly straightforward: technology does not erode statutory purpose so long as it preserves timing certainty and affordances for cure periods prescribed by lawmakers.

## Looking Ahead  

The core tension illuminated by *Williams*—balancing consumer protection against administrative efficiency—will shape future litigation over insurance cancellations in premium finance contexts nationwide:

- Legislators may respond by clarifying “certify” language or adding explicit provisions regarding electronic authentication.
- Courts may confront parallel questions regarding proof-of-delivery via email versus real‑time system calls.
- Insurance regulators could issue industry guidelines requiring minimum audit trails or revocation windows longer than those set merely by statute if concerns arise about erroneous automated terminations.

For now, insurers and finance firms operating in Louisiana can safely design their systems around timestamped e‑mail notifications without worrying about attaching physical signatures—a practical win for operational efficiency matched equally by heightened responsibility toward maintaining reliable digital records.

In short, *Williams v.* GoAuto tells us that} **the word “certify” means what it says**, not what a bygone era imagined it would mean—a lesson that resonates far beyond New Orleans’ exchange corridors into every jurisdiction wrestling with legacy statutes meeting today’s cloud‑based commerce.
