Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Chapter 1 – Facts, Parties, and Procedural Posture  

In every appellate opinion the first step is to understand “who” is suing “whom,” what “the dispute” actually concerns, and how the case arrived at the appellate bench. Williams v. GoAuto Insurance provides a textbook illustration of a multi‑party insurance dispute that began in state court, was removed to federal court, resolved on a narrow question of statutory compliance, and then proceeded on appeal. The following sections walk you through each element so you can read the Fifth Circuit’s opinion with confidence.  

---  

## Plaintiffs: Identity and Interests  

The plaintiffs are three individuals who were at various times insured by GoAuto Insurance Company (“GoAuto”).  

* **Kimberly Williams** – an original plaintiff who purchased automobile liability coverage through GoAuto. She financed the premium payments through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”).  
* **Felita Wright** – also an original policyholder who hired APAC for premium financing. Wright was not named in the initial filing; she became a plaintiff when the complaint was amended to add additional similarly situated insureds.  
* **Nicholas Jenkins** – while not an insured of GoAuto at the time of filing, he is listed as a co‑plaintiff because his automobile accident gave rise to the underlying coverage dispute. Jenkins’ policy was likewise financed through APAC.  

All three alleged that GoAuto’s handling of their policies’ cancellations violated Louisiana law governing insurance‑premium finance arrangements and that the alleged violations amounted to bad‑faith conduct by the insurer.  

---  

## Defendants and the Premium‑Finance Structure  

Two corporate defendants stand behind the litigation:  

* **GoAuto Insurance Company** – the insurer issuing automobile policies and ultimately responsible for cancelling those policies when instructed by its financing partner.  
* **American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”)** – a premium‑finance company that pays insurers’ full premiums up front in exchange for monthly installment payments from policyholders.  

The financing agreement contains two critical provisions:  

1. **Power of Attorney** – APAC receives a limited power of attorney from each insured authorizing it “to act on behalf of” the insured with respect to policy cancellation if premiums are not paid as required. This power enables APAC—not GoAuto—to initiate cancellation proceedings directly with GoAuto once a default occurs.  
2. **Monthly Installment Payments** – Instead of paying the lump‑sum premium directly to GoAuto, each insured makes monthly payments to APAC. If any payment is missed or remains uncured after a statutory ten‑day cure period, APAC may invoke its power of attorney and request cancellation of the policy under Louisiana law (§ 9:3550(G)(3)).  

Understanding this structure is essential because the plaintiffs’ claims turn on whether APAC’s notice‑of‑cancellation requests satisfied every statutory requirement before GoAuto acted to terminate coverage.  

---  

## The Accident Involving a Third Insured  

The factual backdrop includes an automobile collision between a third driver—also insured by GoAuto but not a party to this litigation—and plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. The third driver’s involvement is relevant only insofar as it created an actual claim under Jenkins’s policy at issue when his coverage was allegedly canceled improperly. In other words, the accident illustrates why timely notice of cancellation mattered: had Jenkins’s policy remained effective, his claim against his own insurer would have been processed in accordance with standard tort principles; instead he alleges he was left uninsured because GoAuto terminated his coverage without complying with statutory notice requirements.  

---  

## The State‑Court Class Action  

In Louisiana state court the original plaintiffs—Williams and Jenkins—filed a **class action** asserting two principal theories:

1. **Improper Cancellation** – That APAC’s notices failed either to contain required certifications or to be delivered in accordance with La.​ Rev.​ Stat.​ § 9:3550(G)(3), thereby rendering any subsequent cancellation ineffective under state law.
2. **Bad‑Faith Conduct** – That GoAuto knowingly relied upon defective notices (or failed to act reasonably after receiving them), thereby breaching its duty of good faith and fair dealing owed to its insureds.

The complaint sought monetary damages for loss of coverage plus ancillary relief such as injunctive relief against future improper cancellations. When Wright’s interests were later aligned with those of Williams and Jenkins, she was added through an amendment—a routine procedural step in many class actions where additional similarly situated insureds become known after filing.  

---  

## Removal to Federal Court  

Defendants invoked **removal jurisdiction** shortly after pleading their defenses. Because every plaintiff resided outside Louisiana (or because all claims exceeded $75 000), diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C.§ 1332 existed; alternatively, defendants argued that certain allegations raised potential federal questions regarding preemption or due process (the precise basis is unimportant here). Under 28 U.S.C.§ 1441 defendants properly filed a notice of removal in the United States District Court for the **Middle District of Louisiana**, thereby transferring jurisdiction from the state trial court to federal court while preserving all substantive claims. Removal is allowed only before any non‑dismissal pleading is served on a plaintiff—a timing requirement satisfied here—so the transfer proceeded unchallenged.  

---  

## Summary‑Judgment Motions Focused on Cancellation Procedure  

Once before the district court both sides moved for summary judgment—but **only** on whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana § 9:3550(G)(3). A motion for summary judgment asks the court to decide a claim as a matter of law when there is no genuine dispute over material facts (Fed.R.Civ.P.§ 56).  

* **Plaintiffs’ Position:** They contended that APAP’s notices lacked an individualized signature, were not physically “received” by GoAuto before cancellation occurred, and therefore failed statutorily.
* **Defendant’s Position:** They argued that electronic transmission satisfied both “certifying” and “receipt” requirements because Louisiana statutes do not expressly mandate handwritten signatures or physical possession; furthermore they presented internal records showing APAC’s automated email triggered GoAuto’s web service which then effected cancellation automatically.

Because both sides agreed on all factual details concerning how notices were generated and transmitted—the disagreement rested purely on statutory interpretation—the district court concluded there was no genuine issue requiring trial and granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto.  

---  

## District Court Ruling and Dismissal With Prejudice  

Following its grant of summary judgment, the district court entered an order **dismissing all claims against GoAuto with prejudice**—meaning what has been decided cannot be refiled in any forum (“with prejudice” bars any further litigation on those same grounds). The dismissal effectively terminated Williams’, Wright’s, and Jenkins’ state‑law causes of action concerning bad faith and improper cancellation against GoAuto; claims against APAC remained untouched because they were never addressed by this particular motion or order. The decision thus served as a final determination—subject only to appellate review—on whether GoAuto had complied with § 9:3550(G)(3) when it cancelled each financed policy.  

---  

## Appeal Before the Fifth Circuit  

Dissatisfied with that outcome, each plaintiff filed a notice of appeal within thirty days pursuant to FRCP Rule 4(a)(1). The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit assigned **Docket No.… 24‑30646** and scheduled oral argument for October 3 2025 (the date listed on Judge Leslie H.~Southwick’s opinion). The appeal presented three points:

1️⃣ Whether Louisiana statutes require a handwritten signature on APAC’s certification statements;  
2️⃣ Whether “receipt” necessitates physical possession by GoAuto before cancellation may occur;  
3️⃣ Whether any procedural defect warrants reversal of the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

Because only *GoAuto*’s statutory compliance was contested—summary‑judgment rulings regarding other potential claims were moot—the Fifth Circuit framed its review around those two interpretive questions while also addressing plaintiffs’ request that the issues be certified directly to the Louisiana Supreme Court (which ultimately was denied). 

Understanding this procedural pathway—from private insurance contracts through state‑court class litigation, removal into federal jurisdiction, dispositive summary judgment on statutory compliance, dismissal with prejudice, and finally appeal—is crucial before diving into the appellate analysis that follows in subsequent chapters. It shows how courts prune factual disputes down to legal questions before higher courts decide whether lower courts applied state law correctly—a pattern you will encounter repeatedly in appellate practice.*

# Chapter 2 – Louisiana Statutory Scheme Governing Cancellation of Financed Policies  

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* the Fifth Circuit was called upon to decide whether an insurer’s computerized cancellation process satisfied Louisiana’s detailed statutory scheme governing the termination of insurance policies that are paid for through a premium‑finance arrangement.  This chapter isolates the statutory text at issue, dissects each element that must be “certified,” explains the legislative purposes behind the strict‑compliance mandate, and surveys the key Louisiana appellate decisions that shape how courts interpret these requirements.  Understanding this framework equips you to evaluate any dispute over whether a financed policy was properly cancelled under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3).  

---

## The Statutory Text – §§ 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b)

Louisiana codifies the cancellation process for insurance‑premium finance companies in one paragraph:

> **(a)** *A premium‑finance company may request cancellation of an insurance policy upon an uncured default **by sending** to the insurer, **by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier**, or **via electronic mail** … a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a **statement certifying**:*  
>   *(i)* *the premium‑finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as provided in Paragraph (1);*  
>   *(ii)* *the premium‑finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured;*  
>   *(iii)* *upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as provided in Paragraph (2), specifying the date of sending by the premium‑finance company to the insured;*  
>   *(iv)* *copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the premium‑finance agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur thereunder,* **specifying** the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, holders of a security interest, or other third parties.*  

> **(b)** *Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium‑finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation has been requested … and may proceed to cancel such contract.*

The language makes clear that *the act* that triggers a permissible cancellation is **the insurer’s receipt**—not merely its knowledge—of a properly certified request from the finance company.  The statute lists three modes by which “sending” may occur (mail, private carrier, electronic mail), signaling that Louisiana intended modern communications technology to satisfy its procedural demands.

---

## The Power‑of‑Attorney Embedded in Premium‑Finance Agreements

A financing arrangement between an insured and a premium‑finance company typically contains two interlocking provisions:

1️⃣ **Authority to Pay Premiums** – The finance company pays the full policy premium directly to the insurer on behalf of the insured in exchange for monthly instalment payments from the insured.  

2️⃣ **Power Of Attorney (POA)** – The agreement expressly grants the finance company a limited POA authorizing it “to act on behalf of” or “to perform any act which is necessary” regarding cancellation when the insured defaults on its instalment obligations.

Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i) requires that this POA be *valid* under Louisiana law—meaning it must be executed in compliance with state contract formalities, be specific enough to confer authority over policy termination, and not be withdrawn before default occurs.  Courts have treated these POAs as distinct from general agent authority because they arise directly from a financing contract rather than from an agency relationship created by an insurance policy itself (*Williams*, supra).

---

## The Four Certification Requirements

To satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), a premium‑finance company must accompany its cancellation request with a **statement certifying** that each numbered element is true.  Below we unpack what each certification demands under Louisiana law and how *Williams* illustrates their practical application.

### (i) Valid Power Of Attorney

The finance company must confirm that its POA meets statutory standards at the time it seeks cancellation.  Because certification is a statement rather than proof per se, courts look for whether there is any record—often an attached copy of the financing agreement—showing an unambiguous grant of authority over cancellations (*Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So.\ 2d 686, 689 (La.App.—1993)).  In *Williams* APAC’s request included language stating “[t]he Premium Finance Agreement contains a valid power of attorney,” fulfilling this element without attaching a signed POA copy; no appellate decision has required such attachment so long as there is no genuine doubt concerning validity.

### (ii) Uncured Default

The finance company must certify two facts:

* That a default under “the premium finance agreement” has occurred; and  
* That no timely cure has been made after notice was provided under paragraph (2) (“Ten‑Day Notice”).

Louisiana courts treat “uncured” strictly—if any payment is tendered within ten days after notice was dispatched, cancellation cannot proceed (*Eaglin v. Champion Ins.Co.*, 558 So.\ 2d 284, 287 (La.App.—1990)).  The certification therefore functions as a shorthand declaration that all procedural prerequisites leading up to default have been satisfied.

### (iii) Ten‑Day Notice Sent To Insured

This requirement reflects one half of Louisiana’s articulated legislative purpose: fixing an exact date/time at which coverage ends while granting insurers and finance companies an unambiguous cure period for delinquent payors (*Britten v. Reavis*, 503 So.\ 2d 1149, 1154 (La.App.—1987)).  

The statute does not prescribe who must sign or physically deliver this ten‑day notice so long as evidence exists that it was dispatched on a specific date—typically shown by mailing receipts or electronic logs (“sent … specifying the date”). In *Williams*, APAC’s automated system mailed—or more accurately emailed—the Ten‑Day Notice on day zero; APAC affirmed that “copies … are enclosed” within its certification, satisfying this element despite no handwritten signature appearing on each notice document.

### (iv) Copies Sent To Interested Third Parties

The final certification demands confirmation that anyone with a legal interest in loss—such as security‑interest holders or governmental agencies noted in the financing agreement—received copies of the ten‑day notice.   Courts view this requirement less formally than earlier steps; proof often consists simply of listing names and addresses within the certification (“Copies … were sent … specifying names and addresses”), which Louisiana precedent accepts provided no contradiction appears (*Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*, 546 So.\ 2d 898, 901–02 (La.App.—1989)).  

In *Williams*, APAC certified this element without furnishing separate proof because its internal records substantiated that notices were electronically transmitted simultaneously to all parties identified in each financing contract.

---

## Receipt By The Insurer—Triggering Authority To Cancel

Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b) makes clear that *receipt* by the insurer—not merely knowledge—is required before it may act on the finance company’s request:

> “Upon receipt … the insurer shall consider that cancellation has been requested … and may proceed to cancel such contract.”

### What Constitutes “Receipt”?

Louisiana jurisprudence offers no categorical definition for “receipt” within this context.  In *Williams* the Fifth Circuit turned first to Black’s Law Dictionary—the modern authority most courts invoke when statutory terms lack explicit definitions—and accepted its ordinary meaning: taking physical possession or obtaining something offered or sent (§ “receipt”; § “receive”).  

Relying on practical reality rather than literalist reading, *Williams* held that receipt occurs when **the insurer actually obtains custody or control** overthe requested information—even if through electronic means:

* An email arriving in GoAuto’s computer inbox gave GoAuto immediate access;
* A web service call executed by GoAuto’s system acted upon APAC’s request;
* Subsequent human handling—a stamp placed on printed documents—was merely post‑cancellation bookkeeping rather than prerequisite possession.

Thus “receipt” embraces electronic transmission so long as it furnishes insurer personnel or systems with immediate capacity to act on it—a reading consistent with modern commercial practice yet faithful to statutory language allowing delivery “by electronic mail.”

### Private Carrier Analogy

Before 2017 GoAuto stored printed requests in a filing room owned by *GoAuto Management Services*, prompting plaintiffs’ argument that physical possession never accrued because an APAC employee never handed documents directly over to GoAuto staff.   District Court Judge Jolly answered using an analogy drawn from *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*, L.L.C.*, 452 F.App’x 453 (5th Cir. 2011), where railroads were deemed “private carriers” under a different federal statute despite their unique status among transportation entities.*[1]*  

Here, Judge Jolly treated APAC’s internal courier—a stamp‐affixing employee—as tantamount to “a private carrier” capable under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) to convey documents without requiring direct handoff by GoAuto personnel—a creative yet permissible stretch because none­theless there existed no contradictory Louisiana authority defining “private carrier” vis‑à‑vis premium‑finance cancellations.*[2]*  

Because *Williams* ultimately held even an electronic transmission satisfied both delivery and receipt elements under § (3), it did not need further analysis regarding who physically possessed paper copies after they entered GoAuto’s shared cabinet—a point illustrating how modern technology diminishes reliance on archaic carrier doctrines while still respecting statutory language permitting nontraditional means (“mail…or private carrier…or via electronic mail”).

---

## Legislative Objectives Behind Strict Adherence

Louisiana legislators crafted §§ 9:3550(G)(3) explicitly—to eliminate uncertainty surrounding when coverage ends and protect consumers against sudden loss of insurance protection without warning.*[3]*  

Two overlapping goals emerge from case law:

1️⃣ **Fixing Exact Cancellation Date/Time** – By demanding contemporaneous certifications—particularly verification that notice was sent on a specified date—the statute creates an objective timestamp evidencing exactly when risk terminated (*Britten*, supra).  

2️⃣ **Providing A Minimum Cure Period** – The ten‑day window coupled with mandatory notice ensures insureds receive adequate opportunity to remedy defaults before their contracts dissolve (*Delatte*, supra).

Courts consistently characterize any deviation from these steps—such as missing certifications or failure to send proper notices—to render cancellations ineffective (*Eaglin*, supra). This “strict adherence’’ rule does not merely encourage compliance; it operates as substantive substantive safeguard against arbitrary termination—a principle repeatedly affirmed across multiple appeal decisions cited below.

---

## Judicial Interpretation – The Role Of Precedent

Louisiana appellate courts have addressed these requirements repeatedly since late‑1980s decisions cemented two doctrinal pillars:

| Decision | Holding On Strict Compliance | Relevance To § 9:3550 |
|---|---|---|
| **Delatte v. Lemotte**, 633 So.\ 2d 686 (La.App.—1993) | “Louisian​a courts require **strict adherence** when a finance company exercises its POA.” | Establishes overarching rule requiring every statutory step be performed precisely—not simply substantially.| 
| **Eaglin v. Champion Ins.Co**, 558 So.\ 2d 284 (La.App.—1990) | Any defect results in an ineffective cancellation.| Reinforces consequences when any element—e.g., missing copy—to third parties—is omitted.| 
| **Britten v. Reavis**, 503 So.\ 2d 1149 (La.App.—1987) | Identifies dual function—to fix exact time/date & provide cure period.| Provides legislative rationale underpinning strictness.| 
| **KMJ Servs., Inc.v.Hood**, 115 So.\ 3d 34 (La.App.—2013) | Interprets “certifying compliance” as requiring only statements affirming each condition.| Confirms variation from traditional signed affidavits.| 
| **Hodges v.Colonial Lloyd’s Ins**, 546 So.\ 2d 898 (La.App.—1989) | Allows cancellation once insurer receives notice & statement.| Expands understanding that receipt can be satisfied electronically.| 
| **Benitez v.Elsayed**, 285 So.\ 3d 572 (La.App.—2019) | Notes absence of individual signatures may raise concern but does not automatically disqualify cancellations when certifications are present.| Provides dicta acknowledging flexibility unless glaring defect exists.| 

Together these cases create what scholars call a “strict‐compliance doctrine”: every statutory box must be checked exactly as written—a doctrine applied verbatim by Fifth Circuit judges assessing GoAuto’s computerized procedures (*Williams*).  

The lack of any definitive Louisiana Supreme Court decision interpreting one phrase (“receipt”) does not waive compliance obligations—it simply gives lower courts leeway so long as they remain faithful to plain language & underlying purpose (§§ above). Accordingly both district court and Fifth Circuit concluded there was no demonstrable defect warranting reversal.*

---

## Private Carrier Considerations Within § 9:3550

Although § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) lists three permissible delivery methods—including “private carrier”—the term has rarely been dissected outside corporate mailing contexts.
 
*Private carrier* generally denotes any person or entity not part of regular public postal services yet who undertakes transportation services for compensation (*United States v.~Johnston*, etc.). Under Louisian​a common law derived from maritime contexts (§§ ‘carrier’ definitions), courts often examine whether an entity exercises independent control over dispatched goods.^[4]  

In insurance‑premium financing disputes there is scant precedent mapping this concept onto internal corporate logistics such as document handoffs between affiliated entities—in contrast with classic cargo transportation scenarios.^[5] 

Thus when district court judge Jolly labeled APAC’s internal courier action similar enough to delivery by private carrier—a characterization unsupported by direct precedent—it reflected pragmatic problem solving rather than doctrinal fidelity.^[6] By focusing instead on electronic transmission—a method expressly enumerated by legislature—the Fifth Circuit neutralized ambiguity surrounding who physically handled paper copies post-cancellation.^[7]  

For practitioners this illustrates two takeaways:

1️⃣ When litigating cancellations today you can rely heavily upon electronic conveyance satisfying both “sending” and “receipt,” minimizing reliance upon ambiguous notions such as private carriers;
2️⃣ Nonetheless you should still contemplate whether subsequent physical handling could raise evidentiary disputes about when actual possession occurred—especially if your opponent argues automatic processes cannot constitute receipt absent human verification.^[8]

---

## Summary Checklist For Valid Cancellation Under La.R.S.§ 9:3550(G)

After reviewing statutes and jurisprudence you can test any proposed cancellation against this concise rubric:

1️⃣ Verify financing contract includes an expressly drafted POA granting authority over policy termination—no ambiguities regarding scope or revocation.^[9]  
2️⃣ Certify—in writing—that:
   - POA is valid;
   - Default exists & remains uncured after ten days;
   - A ten‑day written notice was dispatched onDate X;
   - Copies were transmitted/mailed/email’d simultaneously​to every interested party identified in Financing Agreement.^[10]  
3️⃣ Ensure delivery mode complies with one listed method:
   - Traditional mail/deposit,
   - Private carrier—or,
   - Electronic mail/electronic transmission capable set up for automatic inbox capture.
4️⃣ Document ***receipt*** by insurer:
   - Provide email receipts/timestamps showing arrival into insurer’s server,
   - Show automated system log confirming ingestion,
   - Or furnish signed/dated acknowledgment if manual handling required.
5️⃣ After verifying receipt you may proceed with actual termination—including automatic system actions—but retain documentary evidence linking step #4 directly before policy status changes.^[11]

If any tick-mark fails—even theoretically—the cancellation will likely be deemed ineffective under strict compliance doctrine articulated first among others in *Delatte*. Accordingly practitioners should design institutional workflows guaranteeing automated proofs at each juncture rather than relying upon later human confirmations—a lesson directly drawn from GoAuto/​APAC practices scrutinized in *Williams* .

# Chapter 3 – The Parties’ Cancellation Process Under Scrutiny  

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit was asked to decide whether GoAuto’s method of cancelling insurance policies that had been financed by American Premium Assistance Company (“APAC”) satisfied a narrowly‑drawn Louisiana statute.  The appellate discussion hinges on a detailed factual record that describes each step—from the moment an insured misses a premium payment to the final filing of paper documents in a shared cabinet.  This chapter walks you through those steps, explains the statutory language at issue, highlights the plaintiffs’ specific complaints, and shows how the court analyzed each claim.  By following this narrative you will see how courts apply statutory text to modern commercial practices and why precise procedural compliance matters in insurance‑premium finance contexts.

## The Statutory Blueprint for Cancellation  

Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) commands a premium‑finance company to *certify* four items when it seeks cancellation of an insured’s policy:  

1. The financing agreement contains a valid power of attorney authorizing the finance company to act on behalf of the insurer.  
2. The financing agreement is in default and the default remains uncured.  
3. Upon default, a “Ten‑Day Notice of Cancellation” was sent to the insured, with the date of that mailing specified.  
4. Copies of that notice were sent to every party identified in the financing agreement as having an interest in any loss (e.g., lienholders or governmental agencies).  

The statute further requires that the insurer receive “such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company” before it may proceed with cancellation ( § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) ).  The Fifth Circuit emphasized that “strict adherence” means courts must enforce these textual requirements without adding extra procedural steps.

## APAC’s Ten‑Day Default Notice  

When an insured fails to make a scheduled installment, APAC first issues a ten‑day default notice.  Under the record this notice can be sent either by traditional mail or by electronic mail (“e‑mail”) directly to the insured’s address on file.  The notice informs the insured that their policy will be cancelled if payment is not received by midnight on the tenth day following issuance.  The content is straightforward: it repeats the amount owed, reiterates that nonpayment triggers cancellation under the financing agreement’s power‑of‑attorney clause, and advises the insured of their right to cure before cancellation becomes effective.

The ten‑day period serves two statutory purposes identified by earlier Louisiana cases:  

* It pins down an exact date when cancellation may occur, satisfying § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iii).  
* It provides a minimum cure window for defaulted insureds, fulfilling policy‑holder due‑process concerns underlying the “strict adherence” doctrine.

## The Certification Statement Attached to Each Cancellation Request  

Once the ten‑day deadline expires without payment, APAC generates a formal *cancellation request* for GoAuto.  Each request includes a concise certification statement that enumerates precisely the four statutory affirmations:

> “APAC certifies that:  
> (1) The Premium Finance Agreement contains a valid power of attorney;  
> (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured;  
> (3) Upon default, a Ten‑Day Notice of Cancellation was sent to the insured (copies enclosed);  
> (4) Copies of said Ten‑Day Notice were sent to all persons shown by the Premium Finance Agreement to have an interest in any loss.”

The certification does not contain an individual signature; instead APAC relies on its corporate letterhead and a printed statement asserting that “affidavit proof of mailing is available upon request.”  The record shows that both electronic copies of notices and hard‑copy packets are produced for GoAuto’s review.

## Electronic Delivery Mechanism  

The crux of modernity in this case lies in how APAC’s electronic system communicates with GoAuto:

1. **E‑mail Transmission** – At precisely midnight on day ten (or shortly thereafter), APAC’s computer automatically dispatches an e‑mail containing both the cancellation request and attached copies of all required notices.  This e‑mail is addressed to a designated GoAuto mailbox monitored by an internal web service.  

2. **API Call / Web Service Activation** – Simultaneously, APAC’s system invokes a web service endpoint maintained by GoAuto (“the GoAuto web service”).  In effect, APAC makes an API call that says: *“Please cancel policy X; attached is our certification.”*  

3. **Automatic System Response** – GoAuto’s web service listens for incoming calls at all times.  Upon receipt of APAC’s request, it triggers GoAuto’s own policy‑management application which marks policy X as cancelled at that moment—no human review is required before cancellation occurs.

From an evidentiary standpoint, these steps create an electronic “receipt” at precisely when GoAuto’s server registers inbound data from APAC.

## Defining “Receipt” Under Louisiana Law  

Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) says that once an insurer *receives* notice from a premium finance company it “may proceed” with cancellation.  Plaintiffs argued that “receipt” implies physical possession—a paper document placed in GoAuto’s hands—because traditional case law uses terms like “private carrier” for physically conveyed items.

The Fifth Circuit turned to Black’s Law Dictionary for guidance:  

* **Receipt** – “the act of receiving something…especially by taking physical possession.”  
* **Receive** – “to take (something offered, given, sent…); to come into possession.”

Crucially, Black also defines *receive* broadly enough to encompass electronic transmission when one party gains control over digitized data within its own system—a modern interpretation aligned with how commerce operates today.  By accepting these dictionary definitions and noting no contrary Louisiana Supreme Court construction exists for this statute, the appellate court concluded that GoAuto’s server intake qualifies as “receipt.”  Thus fulfillment of § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) does not require manual handling or stamping prior to cancellation.

## Physical Document Workflow After Electronic Cancellation  

Even though policy termination occurs automatically through code execution, both parties still generate paper artifacts:

* **Early‑Morning Printed Documents** – After GoAuto’s system cancels policy X at ~02:00 a.m., an APAC employee prints out hard copies—including segregation sheets holding copies of tenant notices—and affixes a corporate *signature stamp* onto each document rather than signing by hand.
  
* **Post‑2017 Handling Shift** – Prior to 2017 these printed packets were delivered directly into a filing cabinet owned by “GoAuto Management Services,” which was technically separate from GoAuto itself but shared space with APAC staff. Starting in 2017—according to testimony—the practice changed: after printing and stamping by APAC staff,
   - A GoAuto employee physically receives each packet.
   - That employee stamps “received” on each packet.
   - The stacked packets are placed in a common filing cabinet shared between GoAuto and APAC.

These procedures preserve documentary evidence for any future dispute but are *post‑cancellation* actions—they occur after GoAuto's computer has already effected termination based on electronic receipt from APAC’s web service call.

## Plaintiffs’ Specific Criticisms  

### No Individual Signature Before Cancellation  

Plaintiffs contended that because no named employee signed APAC’s certification before cancellation took place, there was no valid “certifying” statement under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Their argument rested on two premises:

1. **Textual Requirement Theory** – The word “certifying” should be read as imposing signatory accountability akin to an affidavit.
2. **Case Authority** – They relied on *Benitez v. Elsayed*, where a district court noted that noticing lacked both identifiable employee signatures and explicit certifications; yet *Benitez* never held conclusively that absence of signature alone defeats statutory compliance.

### No Physical Possession Means No Receipt  

Plaintiffs also argued that because after 2017 a GoAuto employee merely stamped “received”—and prior location ownership complicated holder status—the insurer never took physical possession (“receipt”) before cancelling policies; therefore § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) was violated. They leaned heavily on circuit precedent treating “receipt” as tangible possession—e.g., **Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience**, where private carrier status implied moving physical goods—not data packets transmitted over networks.

## Court’s Step‑By‑Step Analysis  

### Did “Certification” Require an Individual Signature?  

The Fifth Circuit examined three textual sources:

1. **Statutory Language** – Section § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) speaks only about *certifying* certain facts; it never mentions signatures or personal attestation.
2. **Legislative History & Prior Cases** – No prior Louisiana decision has linked “certify” with mandatory signatures in this context; opinions such as *KMJ Servs., Inc.* simply reiterate that *a statement certifying compliance* suffices.
3. **Functional Interpretation** – In modern business environments corporate letters often rely on corporate seals or printed signatures without individual names where authorization resides with the entity itself rather than any single employee.

Finding no textual basis for imposing an individual signature requirement—and noting contiguous precedent does not support such extension—the court concluded there was no statutory violation simply because APAC used its corporate stamp rather than a handwritten signature before cancellation occurred.

### Does Electronic Intake Satisfy “Receipt”?  

To resolve this issue we follow three analytical steps mirroring what judges do when confronting ambiguous statutory terms:

1. **Dictionary Definitions** – As above cited Black proves ‘receipt’ does not mandatorily involve manual handling.
2. **Statutory Context** – Even though § 9:3550(G)(3) mentions sending copies via conventional mail or private carrier *or* e‑mail—a clear legislative nod toward digital transmission—the provision about insurers receiving notice remains silent on formality.
3. **Purpose Analysis** – The purpose behind ‘receipt’ is twofold: (a) provide insurers time to verify authenticity; (b) set an objective line preventing premature cancellations.^[Britten v . Reavis] By accepting reliable digital transmission into its own system—where records are instantly available for verification—the insurer fulfills these goals just as effectively as physically handling paper mail would have done when the statute was drafted.

Accordingly, once GoAuto’s web service recorded inbound data from APAC at approximately midnight/dawn hours—the same moment its automated module executed policy termination—the statutory condition was satisfied regardless of later manual stamping actions or cabinet location changes.

### Summary Judgment Disposition  

Because each procedural element—ten‐day notice issuance (either mailed or e‑mailed), certification without personal signature but containing all mandatory affirmations, digital receipt via validated web service call—met every textual requirement under § 9:3550(G), no genuine issue existed concerning statutory compliance. Hence summary judgment for GoAuto was proper under federal procedural standards—the district court correctly granted it with prejudice and its decision stands affirmed on appeal.

## Key Takeaways for Reading Similar Opinions  

1. **Read Statutes Literally First** – Focus on what words say rather than presuming hidden obligations like signatures unless expressly required.
2. **Match Modern Practices With Older Texts** – Courts often reconcile technological realities with statutes written before digital communications became commonplace through dictionary definitions and purpose-oriented readings.
3. **Separate Timing From Post‑Fact Documentation** – A statute may require completion *before* certain action (e.g., insurer receipt before cancel); any later paperwork does not affect compliance if earlier steps were sufficient.
4. **Use Existing Jurisprudence Cautiously** – When precedent discusses analogous facts but stops short of binding rulemaking (as in *Benitez*), courts may treat those facts as persuasive rather than controlling.
5. **Focus On Functional Equivalence** – What matters most is whether parties have accomplished what legislature intended—in this case ensuring insurers are fully informed before terminating coverage—not whether they followed historic modes (handwritten signatures or physical envelopes).

By dissecting each procedural step against its statutory anchor—as we have done here—you can reliably determine whether alleged procedural defects truly undermine legal compliance or simply reflect evolving business practices compatible with long‐standing legal requirements.^[Delatte v . Lemotte].

# Chapter 4 – Fifth Circuit’s Interpretation of Key Statutory Terms  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance**, No. 24‑30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025), offers a clear illustration of how an appellate court applies ordinary rules of statutory construction to resolve disputed procedural requirements under Louisiana insurance‑premium‑finance law. The opinion concentrates on three contested terms—*certification* versus *signature*, *receipt* in an electronic environment, and the relevance of a *private‑carrier* analysis—and then shows how these constructions fit within the summary‑judgment framework and the Court’s discretion on certification. This chapter dissects each issue step by step, showing you how the judges read the text, considered precedent, and applied those readings to the facts before them.

---

## Certification vs. Signature  

### The textual inquiry  

Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a) of the Louisiana Revised Statutes commands a premium‑finance company “to send … a copy of the notice of cancellation together with **a statement certifying**” four specific factual predicates (the existence of a valid power of attorney, the presence of an uncured default, that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to all interested parties). The statute contains **no** explicit requirement that this “statement” be signed by an individual employee or officer.  

The Fifth Circuit approached the term “certifying” through ordinary‐language analysis rather than by importing extratextual formalities. In plain English, “to certify” means “to attest” or “to declare something true.” The Court noted that Louisiana precedent treats “certifying” as a **contentual** requirement—what is declared—not a **formal** one demanding a handwritten signature (see **Delatte v. Lemotte**, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La.App 1993)). In other words, once a document plainly asserts that each statutory predicate is satisfied, it fulfills the certification function even if no individual signature appears beneath it.

### Precedent on “statement certifying compliance”

Two Louisiana appellate opinions provide guidance on what counts as a sufficient “statement.” In **KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood**, the court explained that the statute requires “a statement certifying compliance with” the four predicates—but stopped short of demanding any particular form or signatory (12‑757 (La.App 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37). Likewise, **Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., Inc** held that cancellation is proper upon “receipt … of such notice … and statement from the premium finance company,” again focusing on substantive content over formal execution (546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La.App 1 6/20/89)). 

Both cases underscore that Louisiana courts have historically interpreted “statement” as a factual attestation rather than a signed instrument. The Williams opinion leverages these authorities to argue that an unsigned document can nevertheless satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) so long as it expressly declares each required fact.

### Benitez v. Elsayed and the question of signatures  

The Fifth Circuit also examined **Benitez v. Elsayed**, where a lower Louisiana appellate panel noted that a cancellation notice was unsigned and did not identify an employee who prepared it (19‑122 (La.App 5/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579). Importantly, Benitez did **not** hold that lack of a signature rendered the cancellation ineffective; instead, it merely observed the deficiency without deeming it fatal lacking other defects.

Williams’ judges adopted Benitez’s cautionary tone but declined to treat missing signatures as fatal because every other element—power‑of‑attorney validation, default status, timely notice—was present and expressly certified in APAC’s electronic communication. The Court reasoned that imposing a signature requirement where none is textually mandated would amount to judicial amendment of statute—a prohibited act under basic principles of statutory construction (see **Rex v Hickey**, not cited but inherent).

### The Fifth Circuit’s holding on certification  

Consequently, the Fifth Circuit concluded that APAC’s electronically generated letterhead document—though unsigned—fulfilled the statutory certification requirement because it contained an unequivocal declaration affirming each mandated predicate. The absence of an individual signature did not create a jurisdictional defect; therefore this portion of plaintiffs’ argument was dismissed as meritless.

## Receipt in the Digital Age  

### Defining “receipt”  

Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) instructs insurers that “[u]pon receipt of such notice…the insurer shall consider that cancellation…requested.” The term “receipt,” although plain English implies physical possession, is legally defined in Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed.) as “[t]he act of receiving something…especially by taking physical possession,” with a companion definition for “receive” as “[t]o take…from some outside source” (Black’s Law Dictionary (2024)). 

The Fifth Circuit accepted these dictionary meanings while recognizing their flexibility when applied to contemporary commercial practice—a principle well entrenched in modern statutory interpretation (“the ordinary meaning rule does not freeze language at its historical point but allows its meaning to evolve with technology”). The Court therefore asked whether electronic transmission could constitute “taking possession or custody” within ordinary usage when modern parties routinely rely upon automated systems for critical business functions.



### Application to APAC‑GoAuto communications  

APAC’s process operates as follows: after ten days without payment APAC’s computer automatically dispatches an e‑mail containing both the ten‑day cancellation notice sent to the insured and a separate request for cancellation addressed to GoAuto (“the insurer”). That e‑mail is routed directly into GoAuto’s web service—a software endpoint programmed to listen for such requests—and triggers GoAuto’s own computer system to cancel the policy instantly.

The Fifth Circuit held that this electronic transmission meets both components embedded in Black’s definitions:

1️⃣ **“Take…from some outside source.”**  
The e‑mail originates from APAC’s server and is transferred across public networks into GoAuto’s server environment—an unmistakable act of taking information from an outside source into possession.

2️⃣ **Physical possession or custody analogues**  
Although no human hand touches paper copies at that moment, possession in contemporary commerce includes control over data stored electronically; modern courts have consistently equated digital access with constructive possession for purposes such as service-of-process rules and record-keeping obligations (“possession may be constructive when one has exclusive control over information”).

Because GoAuto’s automated system physically stores—and immediately acts upon—the incoming data file before any human intervention occurs later in the morning (when an employee affixes a stamp for internal filing), true receipt—within whatever sense Congress intended—is already complete at the moment of digital capture.



### Rejection of a purely physical conception  

Plaintiffs argued that because no person physically handled the printed request until after cancellation had occurred, there was no statutory “receipt.” The Fifth Circuit rejected this reading by emphasizing two points:

* **Statutory purpose:** The legislature sought certainty about when cancellation becomes effective—not about whether clerks touch paper filings first.
* **Technological context:** § 9:3550(G) was enacted after widespread adoption of electronic communication tools ("a modern statute set in current times"), which anticipates non‑paper methods for transmitting notices between business entities operating sophisticated computer systems.



Thus, under ordinary meaning augmented by contemporary commercial practice—the approach championed by many courts when interpreting procedural statutes—the e‑mail entry into GoAuto's system satisfies “receipt,” clearing this hurdle for GoAuto.

## Private‑Carrier Analysis  

### What is a private carrier?  

A private carrier is generally any entity licensed or authorized by law to transport property on behalf of others but which does not serve the public at large like common carriers do (*Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*, 452 F.App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011)). Under Louisiana insurance‑finance law § 9:3550(G), delivery may occur via (“depositing…with a private carrier”)—language designed originally for paper mail handled by couriers other than United States Postal Service.



### District court’s reliance on Huntley  

At trial level, the district court determined that APAC employees who physically placed printed cancellation documents into a shared filing cabinet counted as using a private carrier—a conclusion derived from **Huntley**, where even non‑postal entities serving conveyance functions can qualify if they meet functional criteria set forth by statutes concerning carriers.



### Why the Fifth Circuit found further analysis unnecessary  

Once the appellate panel adopted its broader definition of receipt—including electronic delivery—it concluded there was no longer any substantive question about whether APAC had employed an appropriate carrier under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). If receipt can be achieved through e‑mail or an API call (as held above), then either:

* The ‘private carrier’ route is satisfied automatically because digital transmission can be viewed as ‘depositing…with a private carrier’ (the Internet itself acting as an authorized conduit); or   
* The term becomes superfluous because liability hinges solely upon timely & proper receipt—not upon labeling who performed delivery.

Accordingly, rather than entangle itself in another lexical debate about whether tagging paperwork with an employee stamp converts APAC into—or makes them act through—a private carrier—a determination already rendered moot by acceptance of electronic receipt sufficed for deciding compliance.



### Functional compliance versus formal labeling  

By focusing on whether GoAuto actually received what § 9 requires—in any technologically appropriate fashion—the Fifth Circuit adhered to functionalist jurisprudence common in modern statutory interpretation [cf.* State Farm Mut­ual Auto Ins., Inc.*]. The ruling demonstrates how courts often bypass exhaustive analysis on secondary terminology when primary procedural requirements are met through functional equivalents.

## Standard Applied at Summary Judgment  

Summary judgment under Federal Rule 56 requires moving parties to show there is **no genuine dispute** as to any material fact and that they are entitled to judgment as a matter of law.* In insurance‐premium‐finance disputes relying on state statutes like § 9:3550(G), this procedural gate hinges entirely upon whether each statutory element can be mutually interpreted without factual conflict.



In Williams:

1️⃣ **Power-of-attorney validity** – Remained undisputed; APAP provided documentation presumed valid under contract law principles.
2️⃣ **Default existence & uncured status** – Payment records showed premiums were unpaid past ten days.
3️⃣ **Ten‑day notice sent** – Both parties’ logs reflected email dispatch confirming issuance.
4️⃣ **Notice sent to interested parties** – APAC included copies per agreement (“interest list”) which appeared within their email attachment list.
5️⃣ **Receipt** – As explained above — e‑mail entered GoAuto’s system before policy termination operationally began.
6️⃣ **Cancellation request** – Automated web service call confirmed GoAuto acted upon receipt consistent with § 9:3550(G)(3)(b).

Because every element could be read plainly from documentary evidence without contravening any express provision or requiring inference beyond what parties submitted, no genuine issue remained for trial tribunal consideration—fulfilling Rule 56's claim precondition.’​ At this point you have precisely what summary judgment demands: undisputed reasoned conclusions grounded solely in statutory text.^



Thus affirmed —the district court correctly granted summary judgment.

## Denial of Certification  

### Certification doctrine basics  

Under 28 U.S.C.§1254(2), state supreme courts may answer certified questions from federal courts when those questions are "necessary" for adjudicating pending federal cases AND when they present "substantial" federal interests lacking adequate state jurisprudence.“ The Supreme Court retains discretion—using tight standards articulated most famously in **Jefferson v.? Lead Industries Ass’n**, wherein it warned lower federal courts they must be chary with certifications (“because availability ... is such... we will not risk its continued availability”).¹



### Application to Williams  

Plaintiffs urged discretionary certification because they contended key interpretive phrases (*certifying*, *receipt*) lacked definitive guidance from Louisiana Supreme Court precedent—a classical catalyst for Supreme Court review according to Jefferson.^



Nevertheless:

* The appellate panel found ample adequacy within existing Louisiana appellate decisions—KMJ Servs., Hodges etc.—that squarely addressed similar language.
* Even if those decisions left minor gaps concerning digital communications or signatures they were reasonably resolved through plain-language analysis combined with everyday commercial understanding.
* Granting certification would unnecessarily elevate routine contractual‐interpretation questions—for which ordinary appellate review suffices—to Supreme Court consideration without demonstrating any compelling necessity beyond internal disagreement between litigants.


Accordingly,the Fifth Circuit invoked Jefferson more than once before concluding there was no compelling reason—no unsettled supreme-court question nor exigent federal interest—to seek guidance from Louisiana's highest court.^​ Thus certification was denied.

### Takeaways  

The Williams opinion offers three enduring lessons for law students grappling with statutory construction:

1️⃣ A phrase like *certifying* does not automatically import ancillary formalities such as signatures unless Congress expressly imposes them.
2️⃣ Modern commerce extends ordinary meanings like *receipt* beyond tactile handling; courts will treat electronic capture—as long as it conveys control over information—as constructive possession.
3️⃣ Once primary substantive requirements are met through functionally equivalent mechanisms (electronic notice), secondary label analyses (e.g., whether delivery involved a private carrier) become unnecessary.
4️⃣ Summary‐judgment reviews hinge on whether reasonable minds can disagree about cleanly phrased statutory elements—if not, courts will resolve cases without trial.
5️⃣ Federal courts must exercise caution before certifying state-law questions; only truly unresolved issues affecting federal adjudication merit such referral.



By mastering how these principles operate within Williams v.​GoAuto Insurance you’ll be better prepared both to read future opinions critically and to craft persuasive arguments when confronting ambiguous statutory language in your own practice.
