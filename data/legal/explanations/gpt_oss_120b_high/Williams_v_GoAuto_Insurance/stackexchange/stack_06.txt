Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### What evidentiary burden applies at summary judgment review when parties dispute compliance with a statutory insurance-cancellation procedure?
Question:
Both sides argued over whether GoAuto complied with La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G). The district court granted summary judgment finding compliance based on its own reading of 'receipt' and 'certifying.' I'm unsure how courts allocate proof responsibilities at summary‑judgment stage for strict‑statutory‑compliance disputes—must the moving party prove every element beyond doubt, or can they rely on reasonable doubt raised by the nonmoving party?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
At summary‑judgment review a moving party must **produce admissible evidence that proves every element of the statutory requirement** and thereby demonstrate *that no genuine issue of material fact remains*. The non‑moving party does **not** have to prove “beyond a reasonable doubt” (that is a criminal standard); it simply needs to **show any specific factual dispute** or a plausible alternative construction of the statute that would keep the issue alive. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in **Williams v. GoAuto Ins., No. 24‑30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025)** applies exactly this rule, even though the case concerned “strict‑statutory‑compliance” allegations.

---

### 1.  The procedural rule

* **Rule 56(a), Fed. R.Civ.P.** places the initial **burden of production** on the movant (“the party moving for summary judgment”).  
* **Celotex Corp. v. Catrett**, 477 U.S. 803 (1986) – after satisfying the production burden, the movant must also persuade the court that there is *no genuine issue of material fact* (“the movant must show that there is no... dispute as to any material fact”).  
* **Anderson v. Liberty Lobby**, 477 U.S. 242 (1986) – if any reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non‑movant, summary judgment is inappropriate.  

In practice:

| Stage | Party | What it must do |
|-------|-------|-----------------|
| **Move** | Plaintiff/defendant filing the motion | Provide affidavits, declarations, documents, or other admissible evidence establishing each element of the statutory rule (e.g., receipt of notice, certification). |
| **Shift** | Opponent | Offer evidence that creates *a genuine dispute* about any element—this can be a single affidavit disputing receipt date or an argument that “certifying’’ requires a signature if supported by authority. |
| **Court** | Judge | Resolve any pure questions of law de novo (e.g., meaning of “receipt”), but if there is a factual conflict it will deny summary judgment.|

The standard is *not* “proof beyond all doubt”; it is “sufficient to eliminate any real question as to material fact.”

---

### 2.  How *Williams v. GoAuto* illustrates the rule

1️⃣ **The movant (GoAuto) met its production burden**  
   - The district court received GoAuto’s email logs showing automatic receipt by its computer system and copies of APAC’s “certification” statements on company letterhead.  
   - Those documents were treated as admissible evidence establishing each statutory prerequisite under La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) – namely that a valid power‑of‑attorney existed, default was uncured, notice was sent to the insured and interested third parties, and APAC certified those facts.

2️⃣ **The non‑moving parties could not create a genuine dispute**  
   - Williams and Wright offered only an alternate reading (“receipt” requires physical possession and a signature on the certification).  
   - The Fifth Circuit noted there is **no controlling Louisiana precedent requiring a signature** and accepted Black’s Law Dictionary’s ordinary meaning of “receipt” as taking possession—whether physical or electronic—so their interpretation did not generate a *material* factual disagreement.
   - Because they presented no concrete evidence showing GoAuto actually lacked possession or certification at the moment of cancellation, their argument remained speculative.

3️⃣ **Resulting burden analysis**  
   - The court affirmed because *GoAuto’s evidentiary record satisfied its initial burden*, and *the plaintiffs failed to meet their shifted burden* by producing specific facts contradicting those records.
   - The opinion explicitly says:  

     > “We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice… Once the e‑mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system, that computer system cancels the policy… We affirm… because GoAuto’s procedures comply with the statute.”  

   This language epitomizes how a summary‑judgment court treats strict statutory compliance: it looks at whether the movant’s record shows compliance; absent concrete counter‑evidence, speculation about “reasonable doubt” does not suffice.

---

### 3.  Practical take‑aways for your situation

| Issue | What you need at summary judgment |
|-------|-------------------------------------|
| **Statutory element (e.g., receipt)** | A copy of the electronic notice together with system logs proving *when* GoAuto’s computer accepted it; optionally an affidavit describing the automated process as in *Williams*. |
| **Certification requirement** | The actual written statement from APAP (or its electronic equivalent) asserting each required fact; no separate signature needed unless state authority says otherwise (which Louisiana does not). |
| **Opposing party’s strategy** | Produce an affidavit or declaration showing *actual* lack of possession at cancellation time, or cite controlling authority requiring a personal signature if such authority exists; merely arguing “the statute sounds like it needs a hand‑signed document” will not create a genuine factual dispute in this circuit. |

If you are preparing a motion:

1️⃣ **Attach all relevant business records** (emails, system logs, stamped PDFs).  
2️⃣ **Swear under oath** that those records reflect what actually occurred—this satisfies Rule 56’s “admissible evidence” requirement without needing live testimony at this stage.  
3️⃣ Anticipate opponent’s possible attack on terminology (“receipt,” “certifying”) by including dictionary definitions or industry practice descriptions in your memorandum—as *Williams* did—to preempt claims that these terms are ambiguous enough to generate doubt.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

- **Moving party = proof through admissible evidence**, not “beyond all doubt.”  
- **Non‑moving party = any specific factual contradiction or credible alternative legal construction**, not merely general speculation or policy arguments.  
- In *Williams v. GoAuto*, the Fifth Circuit applied exactly this framework despite dealing with strict statutory compliance; because GoAuto supplied concrete documentation and plaintiffs offered none that materially disputed those facts, summary judgment was proper.

Thus, when you face a similar factual‐statutory clash at summary judgment, focus on producing concrete records for each statutory step and be prepared to counter any opponent’s attempt to raise only theoretical doubts about those records.
