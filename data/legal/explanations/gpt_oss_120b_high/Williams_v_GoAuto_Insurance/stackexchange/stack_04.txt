Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### What level of 'strict adherence' does Louisiana require when canceling financed insurance policies—does any minor defect make the cancellation ineffective?
Question:
"Delatte v Lemotte" and "Eaglin v Champion" are cited saying any defect renders a cancellation ineffective, yet Williams v GoAuto allowed GoAuto's automated process despite lacking a pre‑cancellation signature stamp, suggesting some flexibility. How strictly must insurers follow every procedural element listed in La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)? Does failure to meet even one step automatically invalidate cancellation, or can courts deem minor omissions harmless?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Louisiana requires insurers (and premium‑finance companies acting under a power of attorney) to satisfy *every* element that the statute itself enumerates in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).  A failure to comply with one of those enumerated requirements makes the cancellation ineffective.  However, “strict adherence’’ does **not** expand the statute by adding extra steps that the legislature never mentioned—such as a handwritten signature on the cancellation request.  Courts may treat omissions of non‑required formalities as harmless so long as the statutory conditions are met, as *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* makes clear.

---

### The relevant statutory framework

§ 9:3550(G) tells a premium‑finance company that, before it may ask an insurer to cancel a financed policy, it must send *to the insurer* **(a)** a copy of its notice of cancellation **and** **(b)** a statement *certifying* that:

1. The finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney;  
2. The agreement is in default and the default has not been cured;  
3. A ten‑day notice was sent to the insured; and  
4. Copies of that notice were sent to every party with an interest in any loss (government agencies, lienholders, etc.).

The insurer may then “consider that cancellation … requested” once it has **receipt** of that notice and statement (§ 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)).

---

### What “strict adherence’’ means

Older appellate decisions—*Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d at 689 and *Eaglin v. Champion*, 558 So. 2d at 287—indeed said that *any* defect in the procedure renders the cancellation ineffective.  Those cases were interpreting exactly those four certification items and were warning insurers not to skip any **statutory** step.

*Williams* refines that warning:

> “‘Strict adherence’ … is **not** an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.”  
> —*Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, 5th Cir., No. 24‑30646, slip op. at ___ (Oct. 3 2025).

The Fifth Circuit therefore holds that “defect” refers only to a missing *statutory* element, not to an extra formality the legislature never required.

---

### The signature question

The statute never mentions a signature; it merely requires a “statement certifying” compliance with (i)–(iv).  The *Williams* court examined GoAuto’s process, which relied on an automated e‑mail from APAC and a computer‑triggered cancellation before any employee placed a stamp on the printed request.  The court concluded:

* The word **certifying** does not create a signature requirement;
* An electronic transmission satisfies both the “statement” and the “receipt” requirements;
* Because no Louisiana case has ever held that an unsigned statement defeats a cancellation, there is no basis to read such a requirement into § 9:3550(G).

Thus, while *Benitez v. Elsayed* noted the absence of an employee’s name or signature on a notice, it did **not** hold that omission rendered the notice invalid.  *Williams* follows that line and treats the missing pre‑cancellation signature as legally irrelevant.

---

### When does a defect invalidate cancellation?

1️⃣ **If one of the four certification items is absent** (e.g., no power‑of‑attorney, no default certification, no proof that the ten‑day notice was sent to the insured, or failure to send copies to interested parties), then under both *Delatte/Eaglin* and *Williams* the cancellation is ineffective.

2️⃣ **If something else required by common‐sense practice—but not by § 9:3550(G)—is omitted**, courts will look at whether the omission defeats any statutory purpose (fixed date/time of cancellation & curing period).  As *Williams* says, they may deem such omissions “harmless” because they do not alter those purposes.

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis shows how modern technology fits within “receipt”: an e‑mail or web‑service call counts as receipt if it places the information into the insurer’s control so that it can act on it.  The decision expressly rejected a formalist reading requiring physical possession by an employee before cancellation can occur.

---

### Practical take‑aways for insurers

| Requirement | Must be met? | What happens if omitted? |
|-------------|--------------|--------------------------|
| Valid power‑of‑attorney | **Yes** (statutory element) | Cancellation invalid (*Delatte/Eaglin*) |
| Default uncured | **Yes** | Cancellation invalid |
| Ten‑day notice actually sent to insured | **Yes** | Cancellation invalid |
| Notice sent to all interested parties listed in finance agreement | **Yes** | Cancellation invalid |
| Signature on certification statement / identification of employee | **No** (not required by statute) | No effect on validity (*Williams*) |
| Physical delivery of paper copy before computer cancels | **No**, electronic receipt suffices | No effect (*Williams*) |

In short, insurers must **exactly** follow each step laid out in § 9:3550(G); any omission of those steps kills the cancellation.  But minor procedural lapses—like leaving off a handwritten signature or using an automated workflow—do **not** automatically void a cancellation so long as all statutory criteria are satisfied.

---

### How courts treat “minor defects”

*Williams* demonstrates that when faced with new technology or routine business practices not contemplated in older case law, appellate courts will interpret “receipt,” “certify,” and even “private carrier” flexibly rather than imposing outdated formalities.  The doctrine remains one of **strict compliance with what Congress/legislature actually demanded**, coupled with **leniency for non‑essential formalities** absent clear legislative intent otherwise.

Therefore, when evaluating whether a financed policy’s cancellation was effective under Louisiana law, ask:

1. Does each statutory certification appear?  
2. Did the insurer obtain receipt (in whatever form—paper or electronic) before acting?  

If yes → valid cancellation despite any ancillary irregularity.
If no → legally ineffective cancellation per *Delatte*, *Eaglin*, and reaffirmed in *Williams*.
