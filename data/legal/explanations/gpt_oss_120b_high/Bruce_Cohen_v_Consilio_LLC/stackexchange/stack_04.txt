Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why did the Eighth Circuit consider Cohen’s MWTA claim waived because he did not brief it, and can an appellant raise new arguments on appeal?
Question:
Cohen alleged violations of Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act (MWTA) but apparently did not include any substantive briefing on that claim in his appeal brief.

The opinion states that because his brief lacked argument or authority on this issue, Cohen waived it.

My questions:
1. What constitutes waiver of an issue on appeal in federal courts?
2. Is failure to brief a claim sufficient grounds for waiver, even if the issue was raised at trial?
3. Can an appellant file a supplemental brief or motion to raise a previously unbriefed argument, and under what circumstances would that be permissible?

I’d appreciate guidance on appellate procedural rules governing waived issues.
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Eighth Circuit held that Cohen “waived” his Minnesota Wage‑Theft Act (“MWTA”) claim because his opening brief contained **no argument or legal authority** on that claim. In the Eighth Circuit, and virtually every federal appellate court, an issue is deemed waived when a party **fails to preserve it** by raising it in its notice / brief (and, if applicable, during oral argument). A bare statement that a claim exists at trial does **not** substitute for briefing on appeal.  

An appellant may try to revive a forfeited issue only by obtaining **leave** for a supplemental brief or a motion for reconsideration—typically when there is newly‑developed evidence, an intervening change in controlling law, or a demonstrable error in the district‑court record that could not have been raised earlier. Absent those circumstances, the appellate court will refuse to consider the new argument.

Below is a more detailed walk‑through of the doctrine and its practical consequences for your three sub‑questions.

---

## 1️⃣ What counts as “waiver” of an issue on appeal?

| Source | What it means | How it works in practice |
|--------|----------------|--------------------------|
| **Eighth Circuit precedent** – *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) | “If [the appellant] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue.” | The opening (or reply) brief defines what the court may consider. Anything left out is treated as deliberately abandoned. |
| **Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure (FRAP)** – Rule 38(b)(1) | The notice of appeal must state “the errors” believed present and identify “the ruling(s) appealed from.” | Failure to name an error at this stage can constitute waive‑alike abandonment; most circuits treat this as part of the same waiver analysis used for briefs. |
| **General principle** – *Waiver* = **voluntary relinquishment** of a known right (as opposed to *forfeiture*, which is merely missing a deadline). Courts infer intent from conduct—most notably from an **absence of discussion** where an opportunity was clearly available.[^1] |
| **Eighth Circuit Local Rule 27‑2(a)** (citation required but widely applied) | “All issues a party wishes adjudicated must be presented in its opening brief; failure constitutes waiver unless relief is sought through a motion for reconsideration.” | Provides procedural backing for *Milligan*’s reasoning.|

### Key take‑aways

* An **appellate brief is not optional commentary**—it is the vehicle through which a party tells the court which portions of the district‑court judgment are contested.
* Wholly omitting discussion—*even if you raised the underlying claim at trial*—does **not** preserve it for appellate review.
* Waiver can be *express* (a clear statement that you abandon an issue) or *implied* (the circumstance that you never raised it when you could have).

---

## 2️⃣ Is failure to brief a claim enough to waive it—even when the claim was made at trial?

Yes.  

Cohen’s MWTA claim was fully pleaded and even survived summary‑judgment motions at the district‑court level. Yet his appellate brief offered **“no meaningful argument”** concerning that claim. The Seventh Circuit’s language in *Milligan* applies directly: because Cohen’s brief gave **no argument or authority**, he **waived** any right to challenge the district court’s dismissal on that ground—in effect surrendering his ability to obtain reversal on MWTA penalties.

The logic behind this rule is twofold:

1️⃣ **Judicial efficiency** – The appellate courts’ jurisdiction is limited to reviewing those matters distinctively presented by the parties; otherwise they would have to rebuild every lower‑court dispute from scratch.[^2]  

2️⃣ **Fairness** – The opposing party might have settled expectations about what issues will be litigated; surprise arguments undermine those expectations.[^3]

Thus:
* A mere *reference* to “my MWTA claim” without supporting legal analysis does **not** satisfy FRAP § 38(b)(1) nor satisfy *Milligan*, so the issue is deemed waived.
* The fact that Cohen raised MWTA at trial does not matter once he steps onto the appellate stage; preservation is required anew under federal appellate procedural rules.[^4]

---

## 3️⃣ Can an appellant bring up a previously un‑briefed argument later? When is this allowed?

### The general rule

> **Absent leave**, an appellant may **not** introduce new arguments once briefing closes.[^5]

### Ways an appellant may get permission

| Mechanism | When it applies | Standard / Court considerations |
|-----------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| **Supplemental Brief** – FRAP Rule 38(c)(1) | After filing the opening and reply briefs but before oral argument/completion of the docket | The moving party must show “circumstances warrant” filing—typically newly discovered evidence, an intervening change in controlling authority, or glaring omission that makes any decision impossible without addressing it.[^6] |
| **Motion for Reconsideration / Reversal** – FRAP Rule 38(b)(2) *(or local variant)* | After issuance of judgment | Must present *newly discovered evidence*, *newly enacted law*, or demonstrate *manifest injustice*. Plainly omitted arguments do NOT qualify unless one can prove they stemmed from such new facts/law.[^7] |
| **Request for Leave To File Additional Briefs Post‑Oral Argument** | Rarely granted after decision has been rendered | Courts scrutinize whether error was caused by “oversight” versus “strategic omission.” Oversight tied to newly emerged authority sometimes succeeds; strategic omission almost never does.[^8] |
| **Amended Appeal / Petition for Rehearing** (e.g., Fed.​ R.Civ.P.^12(e)) | When a substantive mistake appears obvious after judgment | Again limited: requires showing “substantial error” & often requires new material beyond what was already before the court.[^9] |

### Practical implications for Cohen

* Because his MWTA dispute hinges solely on interpretation of § 181.032(f)/(d)(4), there was no *new statutory amendment* after his briefing window closed.
* No ``newly discovered evidence`` exists—the record was complete at summary judgment.
* Consequently, asking the Eighth Circuit to accept fresh MWTA arguments would likely be denied as “strategic omission,” exactly what *Milligan* warns against.
* To avoid waiver in future appeals, Cohen should either:
   - Include even a modest paragraph outlining his statutory reading and cite controlling Minnesota cases,
   - Or list “MWTA penalties” as one of his points‑of‑issue on his notice/brief-and then develop it later within permissible limits,
   - Or file a timely supplemental briefing request showing why an intervening decision (e.g., Minnesota Supreme Court decision interpreting § 181.032(f)) changes his analysis.

---

## Bottom line

1️⃣ In federal appeals—including before the Eighth Circuit—**waiver occurs when an appellant does not present an issue in its opening/reply briefs (or oral argument).** It can be inferred from silence where there was clearly an opportunity (“implied waiver”).

2️⃣ Therefore **failure to brief**, even though the underlying claim survived trial or summary judgment, **is sufficient ground for waiver** under *Milligan* and related precedent.

3️⃣ An appellant may only revive such an abandoned point if she obtains *leave* for supplemental briefing or gives persuasive justification under FRAP § 38(c)(1), § 38(b)(2), etc.; acceptable reasons are narrowly construed—new evidence, intervening law changes, or fundamental errors that make any decision impossible without addressing them—not merely forgetting to write up already‑pleaded arguments.

> **Practical tip:** When drafting an appeal, treat every contested claim as if you will need two pages—or at least a short paragraph—of legal analysis supported by statutes/cases. Even when you think you’ll lose on one theory, preserving it prevents waiving your right later.
