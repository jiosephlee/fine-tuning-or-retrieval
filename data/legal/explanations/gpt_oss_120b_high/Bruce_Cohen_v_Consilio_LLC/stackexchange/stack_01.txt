Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why does Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act limit penalty enforcement to the Commissioner instead of allowing employees to sue for average‑daily‑wage penalties?
Question:
In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio*, the Eighth Circuit held that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Cohen argued that plain language of § 181.101 authorizes employees themselves to recover those penalties, but the court disagreed and said the statute reserves that power for the Commissioner.

I’m trying to understand how courts interpret this provision:

1. What does § 181.101(a) actually say about who can impose and collect penalties?
2. How do Minnesota courts treat statutory language that appears “plain and unambiguous” when it conflicts with legislative intent shown in other statutes?
3. Are there any precedents where employees were allowed direct penalty actions under similar wage statutes?

Any clarification on statutory construction here would help me see why the court reached its conclusion.
Answer:
**Short answer**

Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) gives **only the State Labor Commissioner** the authority to assess and collect the daily‑average‑wage (“penalty”) provision in Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). The statute expressly limits that power to the Commissioner, while allowing an employee merely “to prosecute a claim for wages.” Minnesota courts therefore read § 181.101(a)’s plain language as a deliberate legislative choice, especially because other wage statutes (e.g., §§ 181.13 and 181.14) *do* give employees a direct right to penalties—showing that the legislature knew how to grant such rights when it wanted them.

Below is a breakdown of the three issues you raised.

---

### 1️⃣ What § 181.101(a) actually says about who can impose and collect penalties

> “If payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, **the commissioner may charge and collect** the wages earned … **and a penalty** … **Any money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee** concerned.”  
> *Minn.* Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101(a).

Two other sentences in the same subsection are equally important:

* “This section does not prevent an employee from **prosecuting a claim for wages** …”  
* “This section provides a substantive right for employees … as well as the right … to be paid at certain times.”

Taken together, the text:

* **authorizes** an employee only to sue for unpaid wages (or commissions),
* **delegates** any additional penalty‑assessment function exclusively to the Commissioner,
* **requires** any money recovered by the Commissioner (including the penalty) to be turned over to the aggrieved employee.

The Eighth Circuit therefore correctly concluded that an employee cannot sue for average‑daily‑wage penalties under § 181.101(a); only the Commissioner may do so.

---

### 2️⃣ How Minnesota courts treat “plain and unambiguous” language that seems at odds with other statutes

Minnesota follows a strong **plain‑meaning rule**: when statutory language is clear, courts presume it reflects legislative intent.

* **Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.,** 869 N.W.2d at 33 – “When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent….”
* **Burkstrand v. Burkstrand**, 632 N.W.2d at 210 – quoted in *Allan* for the same proposition.

However, Minnesota courts also look at **the legislature’s overall scheme** to determine whether “plain” language was meant to be limited or expanded:

* **Lagasse v. Horton**, 982 N.W.2d at 199 n.2 – notes that “the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish an objective if it wishes,” so an omission is evidence of purposeful restraint.
* The opinion points out that elsewhere in the MPWA—**§ 181.13(a)** (unpaid wages after discharge) and **§ 181.14 subd.​2** (unpaid wages after resignation)—the statute *does* give employees a direct right to charge and collect a penalty after they make a demand (see footnote citing those sections). Because those provisions exist, the legislature’s decision *not* to duplicate them in § 181.101(a) demonstrates an intentional limitation.

Thus, when plain language conflicts with what another statute suggests legislators could have done, Minnesota courts treat the conflict not as ambiguity but as evidence that Congress deliberately *excluded* that remedy from this particular section.

---

### 3️⃣ Precedents allowing employees direct penalty actions under similar wage statutes

The MPWA contains several sections where **employees themselves can obtain penalties**, providing binding precedent that such rights are possible—but only where Congress expressly wrote them:

| Statute | Language granting employee‑direct penalties | Notable case interpreting it |
|---------|--------------------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **§ 181.13(a)** | “…may recover unpaid wages … **and may charge and collect a penalty** …” | *Hull v. ConvergeOne*, 570 F.Supp.3d at 695 – held enforcement of penetration‑penalties is limited to Commissioner *unless* statute expressly gives employees that power—the opposite of § 181.101(a). |
| **§ 181 14, subd.​2** | “…may recover unpaid wages … **and may charge and collect a penalty** …” | Same authority as above; confirms that when Congress supplies “may charge and collect,” employees can sue directly for penalties.|

Other cases cited in *Cohen* reinforce this pattern:

* **Milner v. Farmers Insur­ance Exchange**, 748 N.W.2d at 617 – acknowledges that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee.
* **In re Hubbard**, 778 N.W.D.​ at 323 – used in *Lagasse* to argue that legislative omission signals intentional limitation rather than oversight.

No Minnesota case has ever interpreted § 181.101(a) as giving employees direct standing for average‑daily‑wage penalties; all authorities cited—most prominently *Allan*, *Lagasse*, *Hull*, and *Milner*—support the opposite reading adopted by this Court.

---

### Bottom line

- **§ 181.101(a)** grants **only** the Commissioner authority both to assess and collect MPWA daily‑average‑wage penalties.
- The Minnesota Supreme Court’s established approach treats clear statutory language as controlling, but it will look at parallel statutes (*§ 181 · 13*, *§ 181·14*) as evidence that Congress deliberately withheld that power here.
- When legislators want employees able to sue for penalties they do so explicitly; absent such language—as in § 181.101(a)—employees have no private right of action for those penalties.

Consequently, Cohen’s argument that “plain language” lets him sue for penalties fails because “plain language” tells us precisely what Congress wrote: ***the commissioner alone may impose and collect them***—and Minnesota case law confirms that interpretation without creating any broader private right for employees under this provision.]
