Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Only the Commissioner Can Penalize? Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act Interpreted  

## Why This Decision Matters  

When Consilio announced it would stop paying overtime premiums to its Minnesota document reviewers, its lawyers probably thought they were merely adjusting an internal pay structure—a pragmatic business decision with no legal peril attached. Instead, it landed before an appellate panel that ruled workers cannot sue their employer directly for the “average‑daily‑wage’’ penalties authorized by Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). That holding matters far beyond one legal‑document review firm: it decides whether a worker who has been short‑changed can tap into statutory punitive damages himself—or must rely entirely on the state labor commissioner to chase those dollars from an unwilling boss. If penal sanctions meant to deter wage theft must flow through a government agency, employees shoulder additional procedural burdens while employers retain tighter control over how quickly—or even whether—a deterrent bite materializes.



## Background  

Bruce Cohen began working for Consilio in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney reviewing legal documents—a non‑exempt position under both federal and state overtime rules. In July 2019 Consilio mailed all Minnesota attorneys an internal memo saying it would institute a new “premium overtime’’ policy beginning August 2019 that eliminated any overtime premium altogether; every hour worked would be compensated at base salary alone.  

Cohen sued in August 2020 on his own behalf and on behalf of a putative class alleging Violations of three distinct regimes:

1️⃣ The **Minnesota Payment of Wages Act** (“MPWA”), §§ 181.101 & 181.171;
2️⃣ The state‑level **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act** (“MFLSA”), §§ 177.25 & 177.27;
3️⃣ The **Minnesota Wage Theft Act** (“MWTA”), §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4).

Consilio subsequently paid roughly $256 000 covering Cohen’s overtime back pay and liquidated damages pursuant to his MFLSA claim; Cohen personally received $3 225 in overtime pay plus $462 in liquidated damages and stipulated he had been paid everything he was owed under those provisions. What remained disputed were three categories of statutory penalties:

* An average–daily‑wage award under MPWA;
* $42 000 in civil penalties authorized by MFLSA;
* $42 000 in civil penalties authorized by MWTA.

Both parties agreed it would be most efficient for the court first to decide whether these penalty claims survived summary judgment before any further discovery or pleading work began.^1  

The district court granted Consilio summary judgment across all three theories, reasoning Cohen had been made whole financially and therefore possessed no viable injury left standing.^2  
Cohen appealed.



## The Court’s Reading Of §§ 181.101(a) & 171  

### Textual Landscape  

Section 181​.101(a) gives the State Labor Commissioner exclusive authority once an employee serves a written demand: after ten days without payment the commissioner may “charge and collect” both outstanding wages **and** an “average daily wage” penalty payable **to the employee concerned.”  

Section 171(1) creates a separate civil cause of action permitting “[a] person …[to] bring…redress” when an employer violates any provision listed elsewhere in Chapter 5—including § 181​.106—and states such employers shall be liable for “compensatory damages *and other appropriate relief.*”

The question is whether “other appropriate relief” embraces the commissioner's statutory penalty scheme—or whether § 101 expressly reserves that sanction exclusively for agency enforcement.



### Majority Reasoning  

Judge Kelly held that Congress deliberately confined average–daily­‑wage punishments within § 101’s procedural boxballoon:

1️⃣ **Specific Language Limits Scope** – Because § 101 ties both calculation (“average daily wage”) and disbursement (“must be paid … to the employee”) directly to a post‑demand collection process overseen by the commissioner, extending liability beyond that framework would contradict clear congressional design.\*

2️⃣ **Legislative Silence Signals Intent** – When lawmakers wanted workers themselves empowered with penal remedies they drafted provisions like **§ 13(a)** (“an employee … may charge and collect a penalty”) or **§ 14(2)** which grant *direct* rights without involving administrative machinery.^3 By contrast, § 101 contains no parallel phrase granting employees independent access—its silence therefore reads as purposeful delegations rather than accidental omission.\*

3️⃣ **Precedent Echoes The Same Principle** – Prior opinions—including *Hull v.* ConvergeOne*, which held similarly that only commissioners could enforce certain wage‐penalty provisions, and *Allan v.* R.D.~Offutt Co.—which underscored strict adherence when statutory language is unambiguous—provided supporting scaffolding.\*

Because nothing in either statute opened a private avenue onto those averages-within-daily-wage awards, Cohen had no genuine dispute over his inability to sue Consilio directly for them; therefore summary judgment stood.



### Critical Takeaway  

Kelly embraced formalist textualism backed by judicial restraint: absent unmistakable wording giving individuals independent enforcement power, courts will preserve congressional choices even if those choices make deterrence harder or litigants more dependent upon government agencies.




## How This Construction Differs From Other Minnesotan Penal Statutes  

Minnesota does provide workers direct access to civil penalties—but only where Congress explicitly writes it so:

| Statute | Language Granting Direct Penalty Rights |
|---------|------------------------------------------|
| **§ 13(a)** | “…may charge and collect *a* penalty.” |
| **§ 14(2)** | “…may charge and collect *a* penalty.” |

Both statutes give employees immediate cause–of–action authority lacking any mention of commission‐driven demands or waiting periods.^4 By juxtaposing these sections against § 101(k)’s commission‐centric scheme, Kelly inferred legislative intent rather than mere drafting accident.


Thus MCWPA operates under two distinct paradigms within one act:
* A **commissioner‑only channel** (§ 101) carved out specifically for average–daily‑wage assessments;
* A **private‐right channel** (§ 13/14) where workers themselves pick up their pen­alties directly.


It is precisely this bifurcation—which appears purposeful rather than inadvertent—that guided Kelly’s conclusion.




## Prior Case Law Mapping



* ***Allan v.* Offutt Co.*, 869 N.W.​2d 31 (Minn.​2015)** – When statutory wording is plain legislators’ intent must be honored; courts do not read expansive remedies into ambiguous texts.\  
* ***Hull v.* ConvergeOne*, Inc., 570 F.Supp.3d 681 (D.Minn.​2021)** – Confirmed agencies hold exclusive ownership over certain wage‐penalty collections described inside chapter‐specific provisions.\  
* ***Lagasse v.* Horton*, 982 N.W.​2d 189 (Minn.​2022)** – Stressed that “[t]he legislature knows how” when it assigns particular remedial tools somewhere else .\  
These precedents form part of what Judge Kelly relied upon while refusing expansion beyond what statutory architecture provided.

# Federal Standing Meets State Wage Penalties — The MFLSA Conundrum  

## Why This Case Matters  

When a company changes its overtime policy and decides not to pay premium rates, employees often look to state wage‑law statutes for remedies beyond back pay—namely civil penalties meant to deter unlawful practices. Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC forces courts to confront a thorny question: **Can an aggrieved employee sue for a statutory penalty that, by statutory design, is payable not to the employee but to a state labor commissioner?**  The Eighth Circuit’s decision is a bellwether for how federal courts will treat standing in state wage‑penalty schemes, especially when the underlying conduct may have ceased by the time the case reaches appellate review.  Practitioners across the country will watch how this reasoning shapes class‑action strategy, employer compliance programs, and the broader architecture of wage‑law enforcement.

## Factual and Procedural Snapshot  

Consilio—an attorney document‑review firm with Minnesota operations—issued an internal email in July 2019 announcing that its “premium overtime” program would be eliminated in August 2019.  From that point forward hourly licensed attorney reviewers would be paid at their base rate regardless of hours worked.  Cohen, a Minnesota‑licensed attorney who began work in 2018, sued in August 2020 alleging violations of three Minnesota statutes:  

1. The **Minnesota Payment of Wages Act** (MPWA);  
2. The **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act** (MFLSA); and  
3. The **Minnesota Wage Theft Act** (MWTA).  

The suit sought back‑pay overtime, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.  After settlement of the back‑pay and liquidated‑damage components—Cohen received $3,225 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages—the parties stipulated that no further wages were owed.  They also agreed that “the most expedient” path was for the district court to decide Consilio’s summary‑judgment motion on all claims before any further discovery.

The district court granted summary judgment on every claim.  Cohen appealed.  The Eighth Circuit affirmed on the MPWA and MWTA claims but **vacated** the district court’s grant of summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remanded for further consideration of jurisdiction/standing issues.

## The Court’s Reasoning on Private Penalties Under the MPWA  

The first question was whether Cohen could recover “average daily wage” penalties under MPWA §§ 181.101 & 181.171.  Section 181.101(a) makes clear that **only** the Minnesota Labor Commissioner may serve a demand for payment and, if unpaid within ten days, impose a penalty that must be turned over to the employee **via** the commissioner’s collection process.  The court emphasized prior Minnesotan precedent (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.) that plain‑language statutes control: because § 181.101 expressly limits penalty enforcement to the commissioner, a private employee lacks a direct cause of action for those penalties.

The panel noted that when Minnesota wished to grant a private right to penalties it did so explicitly in other statutes—for example § 181.13(a) authorizes an employee who files a demand after discharge to recover both wages *and* a penalty directly from the employer.  The omission of such language in § 181.101 therefore signals legislative intent—not oversight—to confine penalty collection to the commissioner’s office.  Consequently, Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim was properly dismissed.

## The Heart of the Matter: MFLSA Penalties and Federal Standing  

Cohen’s remaining claim hinged on his entitlement under **Section 6(c)** of the **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act** (the “MFLSA”) to civil penalties “payable to the commissioner.”  Unlike ordinary overtime wages or liquidated damages—which Cohen had already received—the statutory penalties would flow directly into a state fund administered by Minnesota’s Labor Commissioner.

### A Standalone Private Right?  

The Eighth Circuit found itself confronting *standing* doctrine rather than whether Minnesota “authorizes” such penalties—a question already settled by statute.  Because the statute designates **the commissioner** as both plaintiff and beneficiary of any civil penalty, an employee must demonstrate a personal injury sufficient to confer standing under Article III of the Constitution.

Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000) offers two prongs for standing in civil‑penalty actions: (1) an injury “in fact” traceable to conduct alleged by plaintiff; and (2) a redressability component whereby granting relief would give plaintiff a concrete benefit beyond general public interest vindication.  Applying this framework:

* **Injury:** Cohen had already been compensated for all back wages and liquidated damages owed under MFLSA § 6(b).  The only alleged injury left was an abstract institutional injury—the notion that Consilio continued illegal conduct without paying its mandated penalty.
* **Redressability:** Even if a penalty were imposed it would flow into state coffers—not into Cohen’s pocket—so there is no direct monetary benefit to him personally.

Because those elements were lacking—or at best uncertain—the panel concluded there is **no clear basis** for treating Cohen as an “injured party” capable of sustaining a private civil‑penalty action against Consilio.

### Mootness Concerns  

Even assuming standing were established, Cohen still faced a *mootness* hurdle.  Under *Friends of the Earth*, claims may become moot when unlawful conduct has ceased such that no future injury is foreseeable (“the alleged wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur”).  Here, Consilio had already paid back-wages and liquidated damages; whether its “no premium overtime” policy remained in effect was disputed but could be resolved through discovery or additional pleading.

Eighth Circuit jurisprudence stresses that courts should not retain jurisdiction over cases where parties plainly lack any continuing interest (*Sisney v.* Kaemingk). The panel therefore vacated summary judgment on the MFLSA claim—not because it disagreed with its merits—but because it could not adjudicate liability without first determining whether Cohen satisfies standing requirements or whether his claim is moot absent ongoing violation.

Consequently, on remand the district court must decide:

1. Whether Cohen can meet *Friends of the Earth* standing criteria for an employee seeking statutory penalties payable solely to a state official.
2. Whether any residual violation remains capable of recurrence—i.e., does Consilio still enforce its base‑rate‑only overtime policy?

Only after those threshold questions are resolved can substantive liability be addressed.

## Comparative Perspective: Penalty Schemes in Other States  

Minnesota is not unique in routing wage‑penalties through a state agency rather than directly to employees:

* **California** – Civil Code § 2034 provides workers’ compensation awards plus *civil penalties* enforced by labor regulators; however California authorizes private workers’ claims for “civil money penalties” if they prove willful violations.
* **New York** – Labor Law §§ 191–196 allow private employees an action for accrued wages *and* civil fines imposed by either department officials or via private litigation where “the Department shall receive” any fine collected.
* **Illinois** – The One Day Rest Act imposes civil penalties payable directly into an administrative fund but also gives employees limited private rights of action (*Steinberg v.* City of Chicago).

In each jurisdiction courts have grappled with essentially identical standing problems when employees attempt to capture agency‑directed fines as partisans rather than merely as indirect beneficiaries (“the payer”).   A common thread emerges: when statutes explicitly vest enforcement discretion—and receipt of funds—in a governmental official or agency, courts require plaintiffs either (a) explicit textual authorization granting them private rights or (b) an independent injury beyond abstract public interest—often rare indeed.

The Eighth Circuit’s approach dovetails with these precedents by treating *private inability* as fatal unless Congress or state legislatures expressly carve out an exception.

## Practical Takeaways for Plaintiffs  

1️⃣ **Confirm Whether Penalties Are Private Rights:** Before filing class actions targeting statutory fines, scrutinize legislative history and statutory text for language granting employees direct entitlement.\  
2️⃣ **Preserve Procedural Prerequisites:** Many statutes require first filing a demand with—​or obtaining notice from—the labor commissioner before an employee may bring her own suit (e.g., Tennessee's Wage Payment Act).   Attempting litigation without satisfying those prerequisites almost invariably results in dismissal on standing grounds.\  
3️⃣ **Seek Injunctive Relief Simultaneously:** Even if penalty recovery is barred, injunctive relief can forcibly change employer policy—eliminating future exposure—and maintain ongoing injury sufficient for standing.\  
4️⃣ **Structure Claims Around Redressability:** Demonstrating personal financial loss distinct from any government benefit strengthens standing \(e.g., seeking accrual interest on unpaid wages rather than simply civil fines\).\  
5️⃣ **Plan For Settlement Timing:** If settlement covers back wages but leaves policy unchanged, plaintiffs should negotiate contractual clauses compelling policy reversal—a concrete remedy preserving sustained injury necessary for future enforcement.\

## Potential Downstream Effects  

The Eighth Circuit’s vacatur injects uncertainty into how wage‑penalty provisions will be litigated moving forward:

* **Class‑Action Viability:** Plaintiffs’ ability to aggregate “penalty” claims across large employee groups may be curtailed unless they can demonstrate personal injury beyond generalized deterrence.\  
* **Employer Compliance Strategies:** Employers may feel emboldened by knowing even if they pay back wages promptly they might avoid exposure to large statutory fines—provided they cease offending policies before litigation ages into enforceable standing.\  
* **Legislative Reactions:** Observers anticipate Minnesota lawmakers revisiting § 6(c) of MFLSA—or analogous statutes elsewhere—to clarify whether employees possess an independent right-of-action or remain dependent upon commissioner initiation.\  
* **Federal Courts’ Role:** By foregrounding standing rather than substantive liability, this decision underscores federal courts’ willingness to prune wage cases at early procedural stages when Congress has allocated enforcement duties primarily at state level.\

All told, *Cohen v.* signals that plaintiffs cannot rely solely on “penalty‐of‐the‑statute” language as armor against employer defenses; they must articulate personalized stakes that survive constitutional scrutiny.

## Looking Ahead  

The remand will force district judges within the circuit—and perhaps beyond—to issue concrete guidance on two fronts: 

1) How strictly should courts interpret statutory designations of "commissioner" as precluding all private action?  
2) At what point does cessation of offending conduct render an employee’s remaining claims moot?

If lower courts adopt a narrow view akin to what Judge Kelly outlined here—requiring either explicit private rights or demonstrable ongoing injury—we may see fewer successful attempts at recovering agency‑directed civil penalties from private actors nationwide.

Conversely, should appellate precedent emerge permitting broader standing where employees prove likely repeat violations—a more permissive approach—it could reinvigorate wage‑law advocacy across jurisdictions where similar penalty schemes exist.

Either way, lawyers representing hourly professionals should now audit client policies not only through paycheck calculations but also through procedural lenses: do we have legitimate avenues to summon agencies *and* capture whatever remedial bounty lies behind them? As *Cohen* reminds us, dissecting statutory anatomy matters as much as tallying overtime dollars—and perhaps more so when constitutional doctrine intersects with state labor law minutiae.\

# Stipulating Away Your Claims? The Perils of “All Wages Paid” Agreements in Wage Litigation  

*By [Your Name]*  

---

## Why **Cohen v. Consilio** matters  

When a dispute over overtime collapses into a tidy “all wages paid” stipulation, it can feel like everybody’s winner: employees get their back‑pay, employers avoid protracted discovery, and the docket clears quickly. Yet the October 2025 decision from the Eighth Circuit shows how such clauses can also create hidden traps—particularly when statutory penalties and injunctive relief remain on the table.  

Bruce Cohen’s case is a cautionary tale for anyone drafting—or signing—settlement‑type stipulations in wage‑class actions. By agreeing that he had been fully compensated for overtime wages and liquidated damages, Cohen removed any factual dispute about those elements and handed the district court a clean path to summary judgment on his core wage claims. At the same time, his agreement said nothing about civil penalties under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), its Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), or its Wage Theft Act (MWTA), nor about an injunction rescinding Consilio’s “premium overtime” policy. Those unresolved strands forced the appellate court into a more nuanced analysis—vacating part of the district court’s ruling on grounds of jurisdiction and standing.

The decision underscores three practical lessons: (1) stipulations that obligate an employer to pay “all wages” may unintentionally waive penalty claims; (2) employees cannot rely on an unstated reservation of injunctive relief once they’ve reshaped their complaint around a payment agreement; and (3) courts will scrutinize whether a plaintiff retains a live controversy when it seeks civil penalties—even when those penalties flow to a state agency rather than directly to the employee.

---

## The court’s reasoning on summary judgment and stipulations  

The Eighth Circuit began its review where every appellate analysis starts: Did any genuine issue of material fact survive? Under *Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle*, summary judgment is proper when the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law after viewing evidence in the light most favorable to the non‑moving party.  

Because Cohen and Consilio jointly stipulated—by express contract—that “Cohen has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages,” there was no factual dispute over those elements. The district court therefore correctly entered summary judgment on Cohen’s wage‑and‑liquidated‑damages claims under both Minnesota law and the MFLSA.  

The controversy persisted only where the parties’ written agreement was silent: civil penalties under §§ 181.101 (MPWA), § 177.25 (MFLSA), and §§ 181.032/181.171 (MWTA); and an injunction compelling Consilio to rescind its overtime policy. The appellate panel held that Minnesota’s MPWA expressly limits penalty enforcement to the Commissioner—a point reinforced by *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.* The language “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner]” left no room for an employee‑directed claim, so the district court’s grant of summary judgment stood.

In contrast, the MFLSA portion raised novel standing questions. While statutes allow civil penalties payable **to** the Commissioner, *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* permits citizen plaintiffs to pursue such sanctions when they serve a remedial purpose—deterring ongoing violations—and when plaintiffs demonstrate an injury traceable to those violations. The Eighth Circuit concluded it could not determine jurisdiction over Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim without first deciding whether Cohen retained standing after Consilio purportedly ceased its offending practice—a factual determination better suited for trial. Consequently, it vacated that portion of the judgment and remanded for further proceedings.

The MWTA claim was dismissed outright because Cohen offered no legal argument beyond his broad stipulation that he had been fully paid—a classic case where “no meaningful argument” equals waiver, per *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*.

Finally, regarding injunctive relief, Cohen’s shifting pleading strategy left his request unripe for appellate review. He moved from seeking “specific forms” of injunction at filing time to asking merely that Consilio withdraw its policy at briefing—a procedural gap insufficient for appellate review under *InfoDeli* and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a).

---

## Penalties versus wages: A statutory tug‑of‑war  

Cohen’s settlement illustrates why employers push hard for blanket “all wages paid” clauses while plaintiffs often overlook penalty language embedded in state and federal labor statutes.

### Minnesota’s MPWA  
Section 181.101(a) mandates that before any penalty can be imposed, **the Commissioner** must first serve a demand on behalf of an employee and collect unpaid wages within ten days. Only then can she assess a daily‑average‑wage penalty—and any monies collected are remitted directly **to** the employee **through** the Commissioner’s office. Because Congress deliberately kept enforcement authority with an agency rather than individual workers, an employee who signs away all wage claims also relinquishes any independent avenue toward those statutory damages unless she explicitly reserves them.

### Federal MFLSA  
Similarly, § 216(b) authorizes civil monetary penalties payable **to** a designated federal agency (the Department of Labor). Although *Friends of the Earth* permits citizen suits seeking such sanctions when aimed at deterrence, standing remains fact‑dependent: plaintiffs must demonstrate either ongoing violation or imminent recidivism. In Cohen’s case, both parties argued that payment satisfied his wage liability but disagreed whether Consilio’s premium‑overtime policy still existed—a factual question now poised for trial after remand.

### Wage Theft Act  
Minnesota’s MWTA mirrors MPWA in granting enforcement discretion primarily—but not exclusively—to state officials rather than employees themselves. Again, absent language preserving penalty rights beyond “payment of all wages,” an “all wages” stipulation effectively extinguishes future penal recovery avenues.

The practical upshot is simple: **If you agree you’ve been fully compensated without expressly retaining penalty rights, you may have waived them**—even though those remedies flow through an agency rather than into your pocket directly.

---

## Injunctive relief gets left behind  

In wage cases where employers implement policies designed expressly to avoid premium pay—for example superseding statutes requiring overtime premiums—the ultimate objective is often broader than back pay alone: it is about stopping unlawful conduct permanently.

Cohen attempted two distinct injunctive strategies across his pleadings:

1️⃣ **Specific injunctions** tied directly to his class action at complaint filing (e.g., prohibiting any future “premium overtime” structures).  

2️⃣ **General rescission** later asked only in his summary‑judgment brief (“withdraw its 2019 policy”).

Because procedural rules demand new substantive claims be raised via amended complaints—not amended briefing—Courts treat such post‑settlement shifts as untimely or waived unless there is clear notice paired with opportunity for response.*InfoDeli* remains firm on this point because it preserves fairness in litigation and prevents surprise stakes after parties have already executed settlements predicated upon finality.

Consequently, even though Cohen secured back pay via settlement agreement, he abandoned his chance at an injunction simply by failing to preserve it explicitly before settling—or at least before filing an amendment reflecting his change in strategy prior to briefing.*Fed.R.Civ.P.*§ 15(a). On remand we may see another attempt by Cohen (or other putative class members) to raise injunctive claims separately—a move most practitioners would preemptively address within any comprehensive settlement framework.

## Drafting stipulations that protect both sides  

### For Employers  

1️⃣ **Write “full settlement” language wide enough**—but precise enough—to capture not just back pay but **any civil penalties**, **interest**, **liquidated damages**, **statutory damages**, **injunctions**, and **attorney fees** imposed under every relevant statute (MPWA § 181.xxx; MFLSA § 216(b); MWTA § 181.xxx). Use explicit language such as “the Parties hereby release each other from any current or future liability—including civil monetary penalties—arising from violations of ….” 

2️⃣ **Flag agency‐only remedies** early in negotiations (“We understand certain penalties are payable only to state/federal agencies; this settlement includes those amounts as calculated based on current statutes”). Fully acknowledging these nuances reduces later disputes over whether an employee inadvertently waived them by signing an ‘all wages’ clause.

3️⃣ **Include survivorship provisions** tying cessation obligations together with monetary payments (“Employer shall maintain compliance with applicable wage laws post‑settlement”), thereby preserving grounds for injunctive relief should recidivism arise despite payment compliance.

4️⃣ **Seek explicit acknowledgment** from plaintiff counsel that they understand banded remedies flow through agencies rather than directly into their client’s bank account—as part of informed consent documentation—but still elect not to pursue them personally.



### For Plaintiff Counsel  

1️⃣ **Separate payment from penalty waivers** wherever possible—draft distinct releases addressing back pay (“Employee acknowledges receipt”) while leaving language open (“Employee reserves right...”) concerning statutory civil penalties unless expressly waived with consideration (“In exchange for $X in back pay […], Employee hereby waives any claim …”).

2️⃣ **Preserve injunctive rights early** —in addition—or even separate—from monetary settlements (*see* *Veliz v…*, where courts honored joint requests for injunctions alongside payment). Even if you accept back pay now, filing an amendment before final pre‑trial orders guarantees future injunctive relief remains viable should evidence emerge indicating continued noncompliance after settlement execution.

3️⃣ **Clarify standing implications** upfront if you intend your client or class members should drive agencies’ enforcement actions (e.g., petitioning DOL). Highlight potential hurdles (Direct vs indirect injury) so clients understand risks if they rely solely on private suit avenues.



4️⃣ **Conduct statutory mapping** prior to closing deals—the set‐off process touches sometimes obscure provisions (e.g., MPWA § 181.​13 vs § 181.​101) dictating who may impose/receive penalities—and indicate any divergent treatment between states so client expectations match reality.



By integrating these carveouts into settlement drafts experts provide both sides clarity about what has truly been relinquished—and what remains alive as potential leverage against future violations.

*Third,* given divergent circuit precedent on standing for private penal actions (*e.g.*, *Friends v.* Laidlaw versus tighter approaches in Ninth Circuit cases), there is fertile ground for Supreme Court review once multiple circuits split over whether employees retain concrete injury sufficient merely to buttress civil‐penalty deterrence goals after they're paid everything else.*

In practice:

- Employers will likely intensify pressure during early negotiations (“sign our ‘full release’ now or we’ll keep pursuing unique pen­alties potentially worth much more”). 

- Plaintiff counsel will become more meticulous documenting every remedy being disclaimed—not just overt back‑pay sums—to protect client interests against later agency redress expectations or class‐wide objections.


If Minnesota joins other states making civil‐penalty enforcement exclusively agency driven—an approach reflected neatly in §§ 181.\*—the cautionary tale expands nationally across labor jurisdictions sharing similar hybrid structures (*California Labor Code §§ 203–205*, New York Labor Law §§ 191–193). 

As wage‑class litigation continues its resurgence post–COVID era—with companies reaping efficiencies from gig workforces and document‐review crowdsourcing—the interplay between private settlements and public enforcement mechanisms will shape lawyer negotiations in ways far beyond simple paycheck calculations.


---

## Bottom line  

“**All wages paid**” clauses are powerful weapons—but they’re double edged when statutory criminal or civil penalties remain dangling off statute books yet off-limits under narrow language linking those sanctions solely upending agency processes.* In *Cohen v.* Consilio *(2025)* we saw how one well‑written stipulation closed off wage claims while leaving penalty disputes alive enough for appellate intervention—and forced both sides onto R​E​MAND land concerning jurisdictional footing.


Whether you’re representing companies eager to seal off exposure quickly or workers hoping their class action does more than just fill bank accounts once each check clears,
the takeaway is unequivocal:

> *Never sign—or draft—a settlement without explicitly spelling out which remedies survive.*  

If your goal is synergy between cash recovery *and* lasting compliance guarantees against future illegal overtime practices,
that synergy begins long before either party inks their names.
Instead it begins during negotiation table talk about which statutory knives you’re willing—or unwilling—to hand over.
Only then do you avoid being stuck later wondering whether you’ve unintentionally amputated your own right—or your class members’ right—to hold violators accountable beyond past due payroll.



# Missed Injunctions: Why Cohen’s Request to Void an Overtime Policy Fell Flat  

## Why This Case Matters  

Employers’ overtime policies are a frequent flashpoint in wage‑and‑hour litigation, especially when a class‑action alleges unlawful denial of premium pay. Plaintiffs often couple back‑pay claims with an injunction that forces the employer to abandon the offending policy—after all, monetary damages don’t prevent future violations. Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (8th Cir. Oct. 6 2025) shows how even a strong substantive claim can be derailed when the procedural scaffolding for an equitable remedy is absent. The appellate panel’s treatment of Cohen’s injunctive request underscores a timeless lesson: **injunctions demand strict adherence to the pleading rules**, and courts will not entertain “new” relief introduced only in briefing.  

## Procedural Background  

Cohen sued Consilio in federal court alleging that an internal email announcing a “premium overtime policy” actually eliminated overtime premiums for Minnesota‑based licensed attorney reviewers. After Consilio paid Cohen’s back‑pay under Minnesota’s Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) and the parties stipulated that all wages and liquidated damages were satisfied, Cohen continued to press three statutory penalties (MPWA, MFLSA, MWTA) and an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind the overtime policy altogether.

The district court granted summary judgment on all wage‑and‑penalty claims and dismissed the injunction claim without detailed analysis. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the wage judgments but vacated the MFLSA penalty ruling on jurisdictional grounds and **affirmed the dismissal of the injunction** because “the issue is not properly before us.” The panel leaned heavily on Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a) and precedent such as *InfoDeli, LLC* (136 F.4th 801 (8th Cir. 2025)), which warns that new claims cannot be raised through amending briefs alone.  

## The Injunction Issue  

Cohen’s original complaint did enumerate “certain specific forms of injunctive relief” (e.g., prospectively prohibiting any down‑grading of overtime rates). By the time he reached summary‑judgment briefing he had **reframed** his ask as a sweeping order “to rescind its illegal overtime policy.” That shift introduced a **new cause of action** rather than simply adjusting an existing one—a distinction that matters sharply under Rule 15(a).

Rule 15(a) states that “the parties may amend their pleadings only by filing a pleading…with any required certification…unless stipulated otherwise or ordered by the court.” It is meant to preserve fairness by giving defendants adequate notice of what they are being asked to defend against and to give plaintiffs time to develop factual support for their equitable claim. When Cohen tried to rely on his brief alone to pivot from narrowly defined statutory compliance measures to a broad equitable decree, he bypassed both notice requirements and the formal amendment mechanism.

The Eighth Circuit rightfully treated this as an untimely change: **the district court had no jurisdiction over a claim it never saw framed in a pleading or motion**. A plaintiff cannot hide an injunction behind a wage‑and‑hour claim and surprise the defendant at summary judgment with an entirely new demand for blanket policy rescission.

## Rule 15(a) in Action  

The rule operates on two levels:

1️⃣ **Notice** – Defendants must know exactly what they are being sued over so they can marshal evidence and craft defenses. An abrupt expansion from “specific remedial orders” to “full policy nullification” violates this principle.

2️⃣ **Judicial Efficiency** – Courts allocate resources based on what lies within the record at each stage. A claim introduced at briefing would force the judge either to reconstruct evidentiary foundations out of thin air or risk deciding without a proper factual basis—both undesirable outcomes.

Consistent case law backs this view. In *Kuehl v. Federal Bureau of Prisons* (758 F.3d 395 (8th Cir.—2014)) the court held that “a party may not insert novel relief after discovering during discovery that its initial request is insufficient.” Similarly, *Egelhoff v. Wexner Medical Center* (442 U.S._147 (1979)) warned that equitable remedies require “clear articulation” at inception because courts will not infer them later.

Cohen could have avoided this fate by amending his complaint within **Rule 15(b)(1)**—the one‑week window after service of an answer—or by filing a separate motion for preliminary injunction under **Rule 56** after discovery revealed whether Consilio still adhered to its outlawed policy. Either route would have placed his request squarely within procedural bounds and forced Consilio onto notice early enough to contest it meaningfully.

## Practical Guidance for Plaintiffs  

1️⃣ **Spell Out Injunctive Relief Early** – When an equitable remedy forms part of your strategy—especially in wage‑and‑hour cases—draft your complaint with concrete language (“the defendant shall immediately cease enforcement of its August 2019 premium‑overtime policy” rather than vague “appropriate equitable relief”).

2️⃣ **Pair Injunction Claims with Factual Foundations** – Injunctions require proof of irreparable harm and likelihood of success on merits *at least* at the preliminary stage (*e.g.*, *eBay Inc.* v.~MercExchange). Gather evidence during discovery about ongoing violations before courting summary judgment; otherwise you risk appearing speculative when you finally ask for it.

3️⃣ **Use Amendments Strategically** – If you discover midway through discovery that your original relief request is too narrow, file an amendment promptly under Rule 15(b)(1) or seek leave from the court under Rule 15(a). Courts generally favor amendments unless they would prejudice opposing counsel or unduly disrupt schedule—a stark contrast to silently shifting theory at briefing.

4️⃣ **Beware Stipulations That Curtail Future Claims** – Cohen’s parties entered into a stipulation stating it would be “most expedient…to hear …summary judgment…before any further discovery or litigation.” While well‑intentioned, such language can be read as waiving later grounds for additional relief unless expressly preserved in writing or through subsequent motions.

5️⃣ **Separate Injunctive Motions When Summary Judgment Is Anticipated** – Even if you expect payroll back‑pay issues to resolve via summary judgment, file a *motion for preliminary injunction* concurrently so both issues proceed on their own tracks rather than being collapsed into one ambiguous motion record.

## Doctrinal Implications  

The eighth circuit’s decision reinforces an emerging line among federal courts treating **injunctions as distinct procedural tracks**, immune from being grafted onto wage‑and‑hour claims after discovery closes. The rationale dovetails with Supreme Court reasoning in *Friends of the Earth v.~Laidlaw*—the Court emphasized standing and concrete injury—but adds another layer: without proper pleading, there is no concrete injury presented at all.

By refusing to consider Cohen’s last‑minute broad injunction request, the panel signals that **equitable remedies will not be rescued by judicial creativity when parties sidestep Rule 15** . That hangs especially heavy over class actions where plaintiffs routinely rely on injunctions to change corporate practices permanently—the procedural hurdle becomes part of strategic case planning rather than an afterthought.

Future litigators should see this as both warning and roadmap: embed injury narratives early; anticipate evidentiary development; leverage Rules 15(b) and 56 proactively; and avoid treating consent orders or stipulations as “shortcuts” around ordinary pleading duties.

## Bottom Line  

Cohen’s substantive grievance—that Consilio unlawfully stripped overtime premium pay—did survive Walsh-style wage recoveries because those were plainly laid out at trial entry and settled through payment agreements. His quest for broad injunctive relief failed precisely because he tried to grow his remedy midstream without giving either party notice through proper pleadings or motions—a misstep squarely punished under Fed.R.Civ.P. 15(a).

Plaintiffs who truly need an order compelling employers to abandon illegal policies should front‑load those demands into their complaints or amendment filings long before they reach summary judgment harboring wage calculations alone. In doing so they preserve both their right to redress past harms and their ability to halt future misconduct—an outcome far more satisfying than watching an outrightly procedural objection eliminate what could have been a powerful equity win.
