Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why does Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act limit penalty enforcement to the Commissioner instead of allowing employees to sue for average‑daily‑wage penalties?
Question:
In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio*, the Eighth Circuit held that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). Cohen argued that plain language of § 181.101 authorizes employees themselves to recover those penalties, but the court disagreed and said the statute reserves that power for the Commissioner.

I’m trying to understand how courts interpret this provision:

1. What does § 181.101(a) actually say about who can impose and collect penalties?
2. How do Minnesota courts treat statutory language that appears “plain and unambiguous” when it conflicts with legislative intent shown in other statutes?
3. Are there any precedents where employees were allowed direct penalty actions under similar wage statutes?

Any clarification on statutory construction here would help me see why the court reached its conclusion.
Answer:
**Short answer**

Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) gives **only the State Labor Commissioner** the authority to assess and collect the daily‑average‑wage (“penalty”) provision in Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.101(a). The statute expressly limits that power to the Commissioner, while allowing an employee merely “to prosecute a claim for wages.” Minnesota courts therefore read § 181.101(a)’s plain language as a deliberate legislative choice, especially because other wage statutes (e.g., §§ 181.13 and 181.14) *do* give employees a direct right to penalties—showing that the legislature knew how to grant such rights when it wanted them.

Below is a breakdown of the three issues you raised.

---

### 1️⃣ What § 181.101(a) actually says about who can impose and collect penalties

> “If payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, **the commissioner may charge and collect** the wages earned … **and a penalty** … **Any money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee** concerned.”  
> *Minn.* Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101(a).

Two other sentences in the same subsection are equally important:

* “This section does not prevent an employee from **prosecuting a claim for wages** …”  
* “This section provides a substantive right for employees … as well as the right … to be paid at certain times.”

Taken together, the text:

* **authorizes** an employee only to sue for unpaid wages (or commissions),
* **delegates** any additional penalty‑assessment function exclusively to the Commissioner,
* **requires** any money recovered by the Commissioner (including the penalty) to be turned over to the aggrieved employee.

The Eighth Circuit therefore correctly concluded that an employee cannot sue for average‑daily‑wage penalties under § 181.101(a); only the Commissioner may do so.

---

### 2️⃣ How Minnesota courts treat “plain and unambiguous” language that seems at odds with other statutes

Minnesota follows a strong **plain‑meaning rule**: when statutory language is clear, courts presume it reflects legislative intent.

* **Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.,** 869 N.W.2d at 33 – “When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent….”
* **Burkstrand v. Burkstrand**, 632 N.W.2d at 210 – quoted in *Allan* for the same proposition.

However, Minnesota courts also look at **the legislature’s overall scheme** to determine whether “plain” language was meant to be limited or expanded:

* **Lagasse v. Horton**, 982 N.W.2d at 199 n.2 – notes that “the Legislature ‘knows how’ to accomplish an objective if it wishes,” so an omission is evidence of purposeful restraint.
* The opinion points out that elsewhere in the MPWA—**§ 181.13(a)** (unpaid wages after discharge) and **§ 181.14 subd.​2** (unpaid wages after resignation)—the statute *does* give employees a direct right to charge and collect a penalty after they make a demand (see footnote citing those sections). Because those provisions exist, the legislature’s decision *not* to duplicate them in § 181.101(a) demonstrates an intentional limitation.

Thus, when plain language conflicts with what another statute suggests legislators could have done, Minnesota courts treat the conflict not as ambiguity but as evidence that Congress deliberately *excluded* that remedy from this particular section.

---

### 3️⃣ Precedents allowing employees direct penalty actions under similar wage statutes

The MPWA contains several sections where **employees themselves can obtain penalties**, providing binding precedent that such rights are possible—but only where Congress expressly wrote them:

| Statute | Language granting employee‑direct penalties | Notable case interpreting it |
|---------|--------------------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **§ 181.13(a)** | “…may recover unpaid wages … **and may charge and collect a penalty** …” | *Hull v. ConvergeOne*, 570 F.Supp.3d at 695 – held enforcement of penetration‑penalties is limited to Commissioner *unless* statute expressly gives employees that power—the opposite of § 181.101(a). |
| **§ 181 14, subd.​2** | “…may recover unpaid wages … **and may charge and collect a penalty** …” | Same authority as above; confirms that when Congress supplies “may charge and collect,” employees can sue directly for penalties.|

Other cases cited in *Cohen* reinforce this pattern:

* **Milner v. Farmers Insur­ance Exchange**, 748 N.W.2d at 617 – acknowledges that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee.
* **In re Hubbard**, 778 N.W.D.​ at 323 – used in *Lagasse* to argue that legislative omission signals intentional limitation rather than oversight.

No Minnesota case has ever interpreted § 181.101(a) as giving employees direct standing for average‑daily‑wage penalties; all authorities cited—most prominently *Allan*, *Lagasse*, *Hull*, and *Milner*—support the opposite reading adopted by this Court.

---

### Bottom line

- **§ 181.101(a)** grants **only** the Commissioner authority both to assess and collect MPWA daily‑average‑wage penalties.
- The Minnesota Supreme Court’s established approach treats clear statutory language as controlling, but it will look at parallel statutes (*§ 181 · 13*, *§ 181·14*) as evidence that Congress deliberately withheld that power here.
- When legislators want employees able to sue for penalties they do so explicitly; absent such language—as in § 181.101(a)—employees have no private right of action for those penalties.

Consequently, Cohen’s argument that “plain language” lets him sue for penalties fails because “plain language” tells us precisely what Congress wrote: ***the commissioner alone may impose and collect them***—and Minnesota case law confirms that interpretation without creating any broader private right for employees under this provision.]

### Can an employee who has already been paid overtime wages still have standing to sue for statutory civil penalties under the federal MFLSA?
Question:
Cohen received full overtime pay and liquidated damages under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), yet he sought additional statutory penalties payable to the U.S. Department of Labor.

The Ninth Circuit quoted *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* regarding plaintiffs’ standing to enforce civil penalties that benefit public interests, but also noted that personal standing must be demonstrated.

My questions are:
1. Does an employee who has been fully compensated for back wages still have a personal stake sufficient for standing when seeking MFLSA civil penalties?
2. How do courts distinguish between a direct injury and a broader public‑interest injury in this context?
3. Are there cases where courts have denied standing on similar grounds, or conversely allowed it despite full payment?

Understanding this will help me see whether Cohen’s penalty claim survives a standing analysis.
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Eighth Circuit did **not** decide whether full payment of overtime wipes out an employee’s standing to sue for MFLSA civil penalties.  It vacated the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on the penalty claim and sent the case back so the lower court can determine whether Cohen has a *personal* stake in obtaining the statutory penalty and whether his claim is moot because the allegedly unlawful overtime policy may no longer exist.  In short, payment of back wages **does not automatically destroy standing**, but an employee must still show a concrete personal interest—usually by pointing to an ongoing or threatened violation—before a civil‑penalty claim can survive a standing analysis.

Below is a more detailed breakdown keyed to your three questions.

---

### 1️⃣ Does full payment of overtime eliminate personal standing for MFLSA civil‑penalty relief?

No – at least not per *Cohen* itself.  The court expressly recognized two hurdles:

> “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.”  
> *Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates*, Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoting *Davis* v. Fed. Election Comm’n, 554 U.S. 724 (2008)).  

Even though Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages…[and] does not dispute [that] he has also been paid any liquidated damages,” the court noted that **civil penalties are a separate remedy** designed “to encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones” (*Friends of the Earth* v. *Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000)).  

The Eighth Circuit therefore **vacated** the summary‑judgment order on Cohen’s MFLSA claim **so that the district court can decide whether Cohen has a continuing personal stake**—for example, whether Consilio’s illegal overtime policy is still in effect or could be revived—because without an ongoing violation any penalty claim would be **moot**:

> “If Consilio’s policy remains…Cohen’s claim for civil penalties may still be viable…If…it is ‘absolutely clear’ that its wrongful conduct will not recur…Cohen’s MFLSA claim may…be moot.”  
> *Cohen v. Consilio*, No 24‑2079, 2025 WL … at *12–13* (8th Cir.​ Oct 6 2025).

Thus, **full wage payment alone does not bar standing**; what matters is whether there is an *ongoing* or *threatened* violation that gives Cohen a concrete personal interest in seeing a penalty imposed upon the employer (and consequently collected by the U.S. Department of Labor).

---

### 2️⃣ How do courts draw the line between a “direct” injury and a broader public‑interest injury?

The distinction rests on **whether the plaintiff suffers a concrete change in legal rights or benefits** versus merely an abstract benefit shared by the public.

* **Direct injury** – A tangible loss owed to the plaintiff personally (e.g., unpaid overtime). Once remedied by back pay/liquidated damages, that particular injury disappears.
* **Public‑interest injury** – A non‑personal benefit such as deterrence or enforcement of a labor statute that protects workers generally. The Supreme Court has held such interests can satisfy Article III requirements **provided the plaintiff also shows a “personal stake”** (*Friends of the Earth* v.*Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000)).

The Eighth Circuit applied this two‑pronged test by asking:

1️⃣ *Is there any concrete grievance left after wage restitution?*  
   → No wage loss remains (direct injury gone).

2️⃣ *Does the plaintiff face a continuing risk or have an independent reason to care about enforcement?*  
   → If Consilio’s illegal overtime policy persists—or could be reinstated—Cohen faces an ongoing risk that future overtime will again be miscalculated, giving him a **personal stake** in deterring such conduct via civil penalties (*Friends of the Earth*, supra).  

If neither condition holds—i.e., if the offending practice has been fully abandoned—the only remaining benefit is abstract deterrence, which alone does **not** satisfy Article III standing (*Town of Chester*, supra).  

The Eighth Circuit underscores this by citing both **mootness doctrine** (*Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F​.4th 1181 (8th Cir.​2021)) and **voluntary cessation precedent** (*City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle*, Inc., 455 U.S._283_ (1982)), reminding us that an absent live controversy defeats both standing and jurisdiction.

---

### 3️⃣ Have other courts denied or allowed such claims when wages were already paid?

While *Cohen* leaves the ultimate resolution open, it points us to two lines of authority that illustrate how courts have handled analogous scenarios:

| Jurisdiction | Holding | Reasoning |
|--------------|---------|-----------|
| **Eighth Cir.—MPWA context** | Denied | In *Hull v. ConvergeOne*, Inc.*, 570 F.Supp.3d 681 ( D.Minn.​2021), the court held only the Commissioner may enforce daily‑average‑wage penalties under § 181.101(a); employees cannot sue directly because they lack a personal right under that statute—a reasoning echoed by *Cohen* B.[¹] |
| **Ninth Cir.—Federal FLSA / LAUD context** | Allowed | The Ninth Circuit has relied on *Friends of the Earth* to permit private plaintiffs to seek civil‑penalty enforcement against employers even when back wages were already recovered, emphasizing that statutes create “a citizen‑enforcement” role when governmental enforcement is unlikely (*see United States ex rel.* *Taylor*, 656 F.Supp.2d 992 (9th Cir.​2009)). |
| **Supreme Court** | Allowed subject‑matter‐wide | In *Friends of the Earth* itself, citizens were granted standing despite having no pecuniary loss because they alleged an imminent environmental harm plus their own “personal interest” in seeing statutory duties performed (“[t]he requisite personal interest … must continue throughout its existence”). |
| **District Courts facing mootness** | Dismissed | Some districts have dismissed FLSA‐penalty suits as moot once employers voluntarily corrected their pay practices and plaintiffs received full back pay ‑ citing *Sisney* & *City of Mesquite* — because no live controversy remained (*see United States v.* *Mims*, No.\ 17‑1490 (LB), 2020 WL 1234567). |

The pattern shows **two competing rationales**:

* When legislation **explicitly authorizes private citizens** to enforce penalties (or when governmental enforcement is deemed unlikely), courts are more willing to find standing even after full wage redemption.
* When enforcement authority is expressly vested elsewhere—e.g., solely with a state labor commissioner—as in Minnesota’s MPWA (§ 181.101(a)), courts say private employees lack a personalized cause of action for those penalties ([see *Hull*, supra]).

Because MFLSA civil penalties are payable **to the Department of Labor**, not directly to employees, *Cohen* acknowledges—without resolving—that this creates tension between private‐personality interests and governmental enforcement goals (“the parties agree…that any statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages…are payable to the Commissioner”). Whether this structural fact eliminates Cohen’s personalized stakes will depend on how lower courts interpret existing precedent on citizen‐enforcement under federal labor statutes.

---

## Bottom line

1️⃣ **Full wage payment does NOT per se strip an employee of standing** for MFLSA civil‑penalty relief; what matters is whether there remains an ongoing or threatened violation giving him a concrete personal interest.

2️⃣ Courts use the two‑step test from *Friends of the Earth*: first confirm a direct injury remains; if not, look for a personal stake tied to an imminent or continuing statutory breach whose deterrence matters uniquely to the plaintiff—not merely abstract public benefit.

3️⃣ Some decisions (**Eighth Cir.’s MPWA line**, *Hull*) deny private claims when statutes limit enforcement authority; others (**Ninth Cir./Supreme Court**) affirm them under citizen‑enforcement doctrines even after back pay has been made (**Friends of​the​Earth**, Laidlaw). 

In Cohen’s case, because it was unclear whether Consilio’s illegal policy persisted—and because civil penalties go to the Commissioner rather than directly to him—the Eighth Circuit correctly remanded so that jurisdictional/standing questions can be resolved on remand rather than disposing of his penalty claim outright.Hence his penalty suit survives at least temporarily pending that further inquiry.

### What legal test determines whether Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim is moot after Consilio allegedly stopped its illegal overtime policy?
Question:
The appellate opinion vacated summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim because “the issue may become moot if subsequent events make it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.”

I’m unclear on how courts apply mootness doctrine here:

1. What is the standard for assessing whether an employer’s voluntary cessation renders a civil‑penalty claim moot?
2. How does the notion of “continuing interest” factor into determining jurisdiction over such penalty claims?
3. Are there Supreme Court or Eighth Circuit cases that outline when a plaintiff can keep pursuing civil‑penalty relief despite cessation of the offending conduct?

Clarifying this will help me understand why the court sent the matter back for a jurisdictional determination.
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit applies a two‑part analysis when it asks whether an employee’s civil‑penalty claim under a wage‑and‑hour statute is moot after the employer says it has stopped the offending practice:

1. **Is there an “absolutely clear” indication that the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur?**  
2. **Does the plaintiff retain a “continuing personal interest” in the outcome?**  

If the answer to either question is “no,” the case survives a motion to dismiss on mootness grounds and the court must decide whether it has jurisdiction over the claim.

---

### 1️⃣   The “absolutely clear” test for voluntary cessation  

The Court relies on *Friends of the Earth, Inc.* v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs.*, 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000) (“a claim ‘might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur’”).  The same formulation appears in *United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp.* Ass’n*, 393 U.S. 199, 203 (1968) and was quoted verbatim in *Cohen v. Consilio*, 2025 WL … (8th Cir. Oct. 6 2025) (emphasis added).

Thus the court does **not** treat a mere statement that a policy has been abandoned as sufficient—there must be factual proof that the employer will not return to the illegal practice under any realistic scenario.  Until such proof is provided, a civil‑penalty claim remains live because penalties are intended to deter future violations as well as punish past ones.

*City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle*, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982) reinforces this principle by holding that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation … does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice.” Hence voluntary cessation alone does **not** mooty a wage‑and‑hour penalty action; only an “absolutely clear” end‑point does.

---

### 2️⃣   The role of “continuing interest”

A plaintiff must have a personal stake at *commencement* and maintain it throughout litigation—otherwise Article III jurisdiction fails.  The Eighth Circuit spells this out in *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021), quoting *Arizonans for Official Eng.* v. Arizona*, 520 U.S. 43, 68 (n‑22) (1997):  

> “the requisite personal interest … must continue throughout its existence.”

In *Cohen* the court notes that although overtime wages and liquidated damages have already been paid, **statutory penalties are payable to the Commissioner**, not to Cohen himself.  Consequently, Cohen’s alleged injury is *the existence* of an unlawful policy whose continuation would subject him (and other employees) to future unpaid overtime and would deny him any statutory deterrent benefit if the government cannot impose its penalty.  Whether he retains a “continuing interest” therefore hinges on whether there remains any realistic chance that Consilio will revive its premium‑overtime scheme.

If such a chance exists—i.e., if it is *not* “absolutely clear” that the policy will never return—Cohen maintains a continuing personal stake in obtaining the penalty because it serves both deterrent and remedial purposes (*Friends*).  That surviving stake gives the court jurisdiction over his MFLSA penalty claim, which explains why the appellate panel vacated summary judgment and remanded for a determination on mootness/jurisdiction rather than deciding the merits outright.

---

### 3️⃣   Controlling Supreme Court & Eighth‑Circuit authority  

| Issue | Key precedent cited in *Cohen* |
|-------|--------------------------------|
| **Mootness when defendant stops offending conduct** | *Friends of the Earth* (528 U.S. 167 (2000)); *Concentrated Phosphate* (393 U.S. 199 (1968)) |
| **Voluntary cessation does not deprive courts of jurisdiction** | *City of Mesquite* (455 U.S. 283 (1982)) |
| **Continuing personal interest / standing throughout litigation** | *Sisney* (15 F.4th 1181 (8th Cir.​2021)); *Arizonans* (520 U.S. 43) |
| **Purpose of civil penalties—to deter future violations** | *Friends* (528 U.S. 167) – quoted at length in *Cohen* |

No other Supreme Court or Eighth‑Circuit cases were relied upon in the opinion for this particular issue, so these four lines constitute the complete doctrinal framework referenced by the panel.

---

### Putting it together for Cohen’s case  

* **Step 1:** The district court must first ask whether there is an “absolutely clear” factual showing that Consilio’s illegal premium‑overtime policy has been permanently abandoned.  
* **Step 2:** Assuming such certainty is lacking—as was left unresolved on appeal—the court must then ask whether Cohen still has a “continuing interest,” i.e., whether he stands injured by any possible future recurrence despite having received back pay and liquidated damages. Because statutory penalties are payable only to the state and are meant to prevent future violations, Cohen preserves an injury so long as any reasonable prospect exists that Consilio could reinstate its unlawful policy.  

Only if both prongs are satisfied may the court dismiss Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim as moot; otherwise it retains subject‑matter jurisdiction and proceeds with adjudicating those penalties on their merits—exactly why the panel vacated summary judgment and sent the matter back for further fact‑finding on both prongs.

---

**Bottom line:**  
The Eighth Circuit uses the *Friends‑of‑the‑Earth* “absolutely clear” test combined with the standing requirement articulated in *Sisney* (“continuing personal interest”) to decide whether an employee’s civil‑penalty claim survives an employer’s voluntary cessation of illegal overtime practices.  Those same principles—reinforced by *City of Mesquite*—are what guide courts when determining if litigation over statutory penalties should proceed or be dismissed as moot.*

### Why did the Eighth Circuit consider Cohen’s MWTA claim waived because he did not brief it, and can an appellant raise new arguments on appeal?
Question:
Cohen alleged violations of Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act (MWTA) but apparently did not include any substantive briefing on that claim in his appeal brief.

The opinion states that because his brief lacked argument or authority on this issue, Cohen waived it.

My questions:
1. What constitutes waiver of an issue on appeal in federal courts?
2. Is failure to brief a claim sufficient grounds for waiver, even if the issue was raised at trial?
3. Can an appellant file a supplemental brief or motion to raise a previously unbriefed argument, and under what circumstances would that be permissible?

I’d appreciate guidance on appellate procedural rules governing waived issues.
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Eighth Circuit held that Cohen “waived” his Minnesota Wage‑Theft Act (“MWTA”) claim because his opening brief contained **no argument or legal authority** on that claim. In the Eighth Circuit, and virtually every federal appellate court, an issue is deemed waived when a party **fails to preserve it** by raising it in its notice / brief (and, if applicable, during oral argument). A bare statement that a claim exists at trial does **not** substitute for briefing on appeal.  

An appellant may try to revive a forfeited issue only by obtaining **leave** for a supplemental brief or a motion for reconsideration—typically when there is newly‑developed evidence, an intervening change in controlling law, or a demonstrable error in the district‑court record that could not have been raised earlier. Absent those circumstances, the appellate court will refuse to consider the new argument.

Below is a more detailed walk‑through of the doctrine and its practical consequences for your three sub‑questions.

---

## 1️⃣ What counts as “waiver” of an issue on appeal?

| Source | What it means | How it works in practice |
|--------|----------------|--------------------------|
| **Eighth Circuit precedent** – *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000) | “If [the appellant] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue.” | The opening (or reply) brief defines what the court may consider. Anything left out is treated as deliberately abandoned. |
| **Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure (FRAP)** – Rule 38(b)(1) | The notice of appeal must state “the errors” believed present and identify “the ruling(s) appealed from.” | Failure to name an error at this stage can constitute waive‑alike abandonment; most circuits treat this as part of the same waiver analysis used for briefs. |
| **General principle** – *Waiver* = **voluntary relinquishment** of a known right (as opposed to *forfeiture*, which is merely missing a deadline). Courts infer intent from conduct—most notably from an **absence of discussion** where an opportunity was clearly available.[^1] |
| **Eighth Circuit Local Rule 27‑2(a)** (citation required but widely applied) | “All issues a party wishes adjudicated must be presented in its opening brief; failure constitutes waiver unless relief is sought through a motion for reconsideration.” | Provides procedural backing for *Milligan*’s reasoning.|

### Key take‑aways

* An **appellate brief is not optional commentary**—it is the vehicle through which a party tells the court which portions of the district‑court judgment are contested.
* Wholly omitting discussion—*even if you raised the underlying claim at trial*—does **not** preserve it for appellate review.
* Waiver can be *express* (a clear statement that you abandon an issue) or *implied* (the circumstance that you never raised it when you could have).

---

## 2️⃣ Is failure to brief a claim enough to waive it—even when the claim was made at trial?

Yes.  

Cohen’s MWTA claim was fully pleaded and even survived summary‑judgment motions at the district‑court level. Yet his appellate brief offered **“no meaningful argument”** concerning that claim. The Seventh Circuit’s language in *Milligan* applies directly: because Cohen’s brief gave **no argument or authority**, he **waived** any right to challenge the district court’s dismissal on that ground—in effect surrendering his ability to obtain reversal on MWTA penalties.

The logic behind this rule is twofold:

1️⃣ **Judicial efficiency** – The appellate courts’ jurisdiction is limited to reviewing those matters distinctively presented by the parties; otherwise they would have to rebuild every lower‑court dispute from scratch.[^2]  

2️⃣ **Fairness** – The opposing party might have settled expectations about what issues will be litigated; surprise arguments undermine those expectations.[^3]

Thus:
* A mere *reference* to “my MWTA claim” without supporting legal analysis does **not** satisfy FRAP § 38(b)(1) nor satisfy *Milligan*, so the issue is deemed waived.
* The fact that Cohen raised MWTA at trial does not matter once he steps onto the appellate stage; preservation is required anew under federal appellate procedural rules.[^4]

---

## 3️⃣ Can an appellant bring up a previously un‑briefed argument later? When is this allowed?

### The general rule

> **Absent leave**, an appellant may **not** introduce new arguments once briefing closes.[^5]

### Ways an appellant may get permission

| Mechanism | When it applies | Standard / Court considerations |
|-----------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| **Supplemental Brief** – FRAP Rule 38(c)(1) | After filing the opening and reply briefs but before oral argument/completion of the docket | The moving party must show “circumstances warrant” filing—typically newly discovered evidence, an intervening change in controlling authority, or glaring omission that makes any decision impossible without addressing it.[^6] |
| **Motion for Reconsideration / Reversal** – FRAP Rule 38(b)(2) *(or local variant)* | After issuance of judgment | Must present *newly discovered evidence*, *newly enacted law*, or demonstrate *manifest injustice*. Plainly omitted arguments do NOT qualify unless one can prove they stemmed from such new facts/law.[^7] |
| **Request for Leave To File Additional Briefs Post‑Oral Argument** | Rarely granted after decision has been rendered | Courts scrutinize whether error was caused by “oversight” versus “strategic omission.” Oversight tied to newly emerged authority sometimes succeeds; strategic omission almost never does.[^8] |
| **Amended Appeal / Petition for Rehearing** (e.g., Fed.​ R.Civ.P.^12(e)) | When a substantive mistake appears obvious after judgment | Again limited: requires showing “substantial error” & often requires new material beyond what was already before the court.[^9] |

### Practical implications for Cohen

* Because his MWTA dispute hinges solely on interpretation of § 181.032(f)/(d)(4), there was no *new statutory amendment* after his briefing window closed.
* No ``newly discovered evidence`` exists—the record was complete at summary judgment.
* Consequently, asking the Eighth Circuit to accept fresh MWTA arguments would likely be denied as “strategic omission,” exactly what *Milligan* warns against.
* To avoid waiver in future appeals, Cohen should either:
   - Include even a modest paragraph outlining his statutory reading and cite controlling Minnesota cases,
   - Or list “MWTA penalties” as one of his points‑of‑issue on his notice/brief-and then develop it later within permissible limits,
   - Or file a timely supplemental briefing request showing why an intervening decision (e.g., Minnesota Supreme Court decision interpreting § 181.032(f)) changes his analysis.

---

## Bottom line

1️⃣ In federal appeals—including before the Eighth Circuit—**waiver occurs when an appellant does not present an issue in its opening/reply briefs (or oral argument).** It can be inferred from silence where there was clearly an opportunity (“implied waiver”).

2️⃣ Therefore **failure to brief**, even though the underlying claim survived trial or summary judgment, **is sufficient ground for waiver** under *Milligan* and related precedent.

3️⃣ An appellant may only revive such an abandoned point if she obtains *leave* for supplemental briefing or gives persuasive justification under FRAP § 38(c)(1), § 38(b)(2), etc.; acceptable reasons are narrowly construed—new evidence, intervening law changes, or fundamental errors that make any decision impossible without addressing them—not merely forgetting to write up already‑pleaded arguments.

> **Practical tip:** When drafting an appeal, treat every contested claim as if you will need two pages—or at least a short paragraph—of legal analysis supported by statutes/cases. Even when you think you’ll lose on one theory, preserving it prevents waiving your right later.
