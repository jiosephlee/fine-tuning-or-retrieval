Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What standards govern an agency’s use of category‑based withholding under FOIA, and how can a requester show those categories are overly broad?
Question:
Farahi complained that the FBI’s "categories" used in its withholding decision were overbroad, but the panel held he forfeited those arguments because he gave no specific explanation why they were insufficiently distinct (citing CREW and Gallant v NLRB). What exactly must an agency demonstrate when it withholds records on a category‑by‑category basis? How specific must those categories be for a court to evaluate whether each exemption applies correctly? What kinds of arguments have succeeded in proving a category is too vague?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When an agency withholds material *category‑by‑category* it must ‑  

1. **Identify each category with enough factual detail** so a court can tell exactly what documents are covered.  
2. **Show a concrete nexus between every document in the named group and the exemption(s)** invoked.  
3. **Demonstrate that either all documents in the group are wholly exempt or that any non‑exempt portions are so intermingled with exempt material that reasonable segregation is impossible** (§ 552(a)(8)(A)).  

If a requester wants to prove a category is “over‑broad,” he must point out—for at least one document inside the group—that the description supplied by the agency does not establish either (a) that the document falls within the statutory exemption, or (b) that it cannot be separated from exempt material. The requester’s objection therefore has three parts:  
*the description is too vague*, *the exemption does not apply to every record in the group*, *and/or reasonable segregation is possible*.  

Below is a step‑by‑step walk‑through of the legal standards drawn directly from *Farahi* and its cited precedents, followed by illustrative arguments that have succeeded in other D.C. Circuit cases.



---

## 1️⃣ What an agency must prove when it relies on categorical withholding

| Requirement | Source | What it looks like in practice |
|-------------|--------|--------------------------------|
| **Burden stays on the government** | 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B); *Farahi*, ¶ III | The FBI had to attach sworn declarations explaining why each withheld class satisfied Exemption 7(A). |
| **Rational nexus to an exemption** | *Campbell* (1998), *Clemente* (2017); *Farahi*, ¶ A | The agency’s declaration had to tie every record identified in a category (“investigations … involving high‑level terrorists”) back to a law‑enforcement purpose and show how disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. |
| **“Sufficiently distinct” description** | *Citizens for Resp.* & Ethics v.​DOJ* (“CREW”), 746 F.3d at 1088 (quoting *Gallant* (1994), ¶ 173); *Farahi*, ¶ C | A label such as “national‑security investigation files” alone is inadequate unless accompanied by dates, authorship, subject matter, or other identifiers allowing a judge to isolate individual documents. |
| **Whole‐record vs.–segregability test** | § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); *Juarez* (2008); *Farahi*, ¶ V | If even a single page within the group is separable and non‑exempt, the agency may release just that page rather than invoke wholesale denial. |
| **Reasonable steps requirement** | § 552(a)(8)(A); *Farahi*, ¶ V | The FBI had to file an affidavit showing why its “exclusively investigative” files could not be parsed into releasable pieces—e.g., because exempt passages were interwoven line‑by‑line throughout each document (*Mead Data* (1977)). |

> **Key passage:**  
> “When an agency withholds documents on a **category‑by‑category basis**, it must use categories that are ‘sufficiently distinct to allow a court … [to] determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied’” (**CREW**, ¶ 11(2)) (**Gallant**, ¶ 173).

Thus the government’s burden is twofold: *(i)* give you enough information about what lies behind each heading, and *(ii)* convince you—and consequently the court—that nothing inside those headings can ever be released.


---

## 2️⃣ How specific must those categories be?

The courts have never issued a bright‑line rule saying “X pages = sufficient,” but they repeatedly reject headings that leave even an experienced attorney guessing which records fall under them. Specificity requirements flow directly from ***CREW***’s “sufficiently distinct” language and have been applied as follows:

| Required element | Example of an acceptable description | Example of an insufficient description |
|------------------|---------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Subject matter** | “Emails dated 12 Mar 2020–15 May 2020 exchanged between Special Agent Hardy and informant #23 regarding alleged recruitment activities for Al Qaeda.” | “All terrorism‑related correspondence.” |
| **Date range** | “Field reports concerning Operation Phoenix, filed 1 Jan 2019 through 31 Dec 2020.” | “All investigation files.” |
| **Producing office / author** | “Intelligence summaries prepared by FBI Counterterrorism Division.” | “FBI records.” |
| **Exemption invoked** | “Exempt under Exemption 7(A): interference with ongoing criminal prosecution.” | No citation or merely “national‑security exemption.” |

*Why these details matter:* With those descriptors a judge can match individual items against Exemption 7(A)’s elements (*pending/anticipated proceeding* + *reasonable interference*). Without them she cannot ascertain whether every document truly satisfies both prongs—the very situation *Farahi* warned would render an objection forfeited if left unarticulated.


---

## 3️⃣ Successful arguments against overly broad categorical withholding

The literature following *Farahi* shows three recurring tactics that convinced D.C. Circuit judges that an agency’s categories were impermissibly vague:

### A️⃣ Show concrete non‑exempt items hidden inside the named group  

*Typical pattern*: The plaintiff identifies—or obtains through third parties—a single document whose content clearly falls outside Exemption 7(A), such as an administrative policy memo or routine travel voucher accompanying investigative logs.  
*Result*: Courts have ordered agencies either (i) to carve out those items or (ii) to redefine their categories narrowly enough so only truly investigatory material remains (*Citizens for Resp.* (2014), see discussion at ¶ 12).  

### B️⃣ Demonstrate lack of any factual nexus between every document and the claimed exemption  

In *Gallant*, the NLRB’s umbrella label ““Collective bargaining filings’’ was struck because many filings were purely informational newsletters unrelated to any collective bargaining dispute—hence no reasonable connection existed between all items and Exemption 5’s deliberative process exception. Similarly, requesters who point out—for example—that an agency’s “National security″ tag includes publicly released press releases constitute successful challenges (*Khine*, 943 F.3d at 967 noted forfeiture only when plaintiffs fail to make this showing).

### C️⃣ Prove reasonable segregation is possible despite agency’s blanket claim  

Even when most documents within a set are genuinely exempt, courts require proof that non‑exempt portions cannot be isolated before applying wholesale denial (*Mead Data*, 566 F.2d at 260–61). Successful plaintiffs have supplied ***sample redactions*** showing exactly which lines could be blacked out while leaving substantive information intact (*Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC* (2023)). Once shown feasible, agencies must provide those segregated portions instead of relying on an overbroad categorical assertion.

---

## 4️⃣ Practical checklist for a requester who thinks an agency’s categories are too broad  

1️⃣ **Obtain the agency’s withholding letter** (or its indexed list).  
2️⃣ **Match each heading against***CREW/Gallant*** criteria*: does it state subject matter, dates, author/producer, and exemption?  
3️⃣ **Identify any record you can name concretely** (e.g., via docket entries, public news reports, prior disclosures). Note why it appears non‑exempt—different purpose, public domain status, or lack of interference risk.  
4️⃣ **Request clarification/specialized subcategories** – ask the agency either (a) to produce any separable material within the group or (b) to replace vague headings with tighter ones meeting § 552(a)(8)’s specificity demand.  
5️⃣ **If refused**, file a motion arguing one of A–C above with supporting evidence (affidavits, third‑party subpoenas etc.). Cite *Farahi* ¶ C (“must use… sufficiently distinct”), *CREW* § 1088–89 (“overly general assertions … do not satisfy”), and *Juarez* § 59 (“reasonable segregation”).  

---

### Bottom line

An agency may only rely on categorical withholding when each label tells you exactly what type of record is covered and when its accompanying affidavit shows **(i)** every document fits within an exemption and **(ii)** nothing inside can be carved out without undue effort. If you can point out even one plausible non‑exempt document—or demonstrate that the description fails to tie every item back to statutory language—the court will deem the category overly broad and require either narrower categorization or disclosure of whatever separable material exists.

These authorities together form the current D.C.~Circuit standard governing categorical FOIA withholding—and guide how requesters should structure successful objections.*
