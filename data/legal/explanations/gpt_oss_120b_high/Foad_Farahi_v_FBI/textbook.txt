Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Procedural Background and Litigation Posture  

In order to follow the appellate decision in **Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation**, a student must first understand the factual and procedural scaffolding on which the dispute was built.  This chapter walks through each stage—from the immigration proceedings that sparked governmental interest in Farahi’s file, through his Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request and the FBI’s response—or lack thereof—to the series of district‑court rulings that culminated in an appeal before the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit.  

---  

## Immigration removal proceedings and alleged terrorist links  

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who entered the United States in 1993 and has lived here ever since.  In 2002 the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) placed him in **removal proceedings** on the ground that he had overstayed his visa.  Throughout those immigration hearings Farahi sought protection—first asylum, then withholding of removal, then relief under the Convention Against Torture, and finally voluntary departure.  

DHS’ position rested on a parallel national‑security investigation conducted by the FBI.  At various points during the immigration case DHS introduced FBI testimony describing “Farahi’s connections to high‑level terrorists.”  The government asserted that Farahi had recruited for groups such as the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, was linked to known Al Qaeda operatives including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, and had supported charities alleged to fund terrorism (e.g., Holy Land Foundation).  Although these allegations are not themselves determinative of any criminal liability, they provided a basis for the FBI’s intensive investigation into Farahi’s activities—a fact later crucial to its invocation of FOIA Exemption 7(A).  

Understanding this background is essential because **FOIA exemptions are tied to purpose**: Exemption 7(A) protects “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” if their release could interfere with “enforcement proceedings.” The government’s contention that Farahi was subject to ongoing counter‑terrorism investigations therefore served as the factual foundation for its claim that his FBI file fell within Exemption 7(A).  

---  

## FOIA request specifics – ~10,750 pages and 80 CDs  

While his immigration case proceeded, Farahi exercised his right under **5 U.S.C. § 552** (the Freedom of Information Act) to obtain any agency records bearing on him.  His request was broad: he asked for *the entire* FBI file concerning himself.  The agency later reported locating roughly **10,750 pages** of documents plus **80 compact discs** containing electronic material potentially responsive to his query.  

The volume underscores two procedural realities common in FOIA litigation:

1. **“Reasonably segregable” analysis** becomes more complex when records are voluminous because agencies must identify non‑exempt portions interspersed throughout large files.
2. Large requests often lead agencies to take additional time reviewing material for privilege or exemption claims—time that can trigger statutory deadlines if not carefully managed.  

---  

## FBI’s missed statutory response deadline  

FOIA imposes a deadline on agencies: they must **respond within 20 days** after receipt of a request unless an exception applies (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(6)(A)).  The FBI failed to meet this window—an omission that gave Farahi a cause of action under **5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B)** (“failure to make available… records” subjecting an agency to suit).  

Consequently, Farahi filed suit alleging statutory non‑compliance and seeking judicial enforcement of his request.  The suit placed both parties before a federal district court where all further disputes about exemption applicability and segregation would be litigated as part of ordinary FOIA claims rather than purely procedural challenges concerning timeliness alone.  

---  

## District court’s initial summary‑judgment analysis  

After years of internal review by the FBI—during which only a modest portion of the identified material was produced—the agency moved for **summary judgment** in October 2019 pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56 (the rule governing dispositive motions when there is no genuine dispute as to material facts).  In support it filed a **public declaration** from David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS).  

Hardy’s declaration asserted two elements required by Exemption 7(A):

1️⃣ That **the withheld records were compiled “for law‑enforcement purposes.”**  
2️⃣ That **disclosure could “reasonably be expected”** to interfere with **“pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings** involving Farahi.  

The district court accepted Hardy’s public declaration as establishing a prima facie basis for Exemption 7(A) and found that—in its view—the government had “seemingly” met its burden showing pending enforcement proceedings (§ 122–23). However, recognizing that several years had elapsed since Hardy’s original statements, the judge expressed concern that circumstances might have changed such that enforcement proceedings were no longer pending or reasonably anticipated—a core requirement under *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics v.* DOJ (CREW), 746 F.3d 1082 (“pending or reasonably anticipated”).  

Accordingly, while denying summary judgment **without prejudice**, the court ordered the government to **update its assessment** regarding whether any enforcement actions remained viable at the present time and also signaled a need for more detail concerning segregability—though it withheld its precise reasoning from public view for security reasons (§ 121–22).  

---  

## Court order requiring updated assessment of pending investigations  

The district judge’s order reflected two distinct procedural imperatives:

1️⃣ **Temporal Relevance:** Exemption 7(A)’s second prong is contingent upon *ongoing* enforcement considerations; an investigation that ceases loses protection even if originally covered by Exemption 7(A). Courts therefore require agencies—especially when dealing with national‑security matters—to revisit their prior factual assertions if significant time has passed between an initial exemption claim and final adjudication.
   
2️⃣ **Segregability Duty:** Under **5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)** agencies must make “reasonable steps” to separate non‑exempt material before invoking an exemption wholesale (“reasonable segregation”). When a denial order is based largely on an assertion that exempt material is “inextricably intertwined,” courts demand concrete explanations showing why separation is impracticable—in particular when vast files contain interspersed non‑exempt facts.

Thus, before ruling again on summary judgment, the district court required fresh factual input from the FBI about both pending investigations *and* any viable effort at segregation.  

---  

## Hardy declarations (public and ex parte) supporting exemption claim  

In compliance with the district court’s order, the FBI first supplied additional declarations from Hardy:

* **Public Declaration:** Reiterated earlier statements that records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations … including pending law‑enforcement proceedings …” This document was entered into evidence without redaction because it disclosed only already public information about investigative scope.
  
* **Ex Parte Declaration:** Filed under seal (“ex parte”) because it contained classified details—specifics about investigative techniques, sources, or operational plans—that could not be exposed publicly without jeopardizing national security or compromising ongoing covert efforts. While sealed filings are not part of the public record, they serve an essential evidentiary role in FOIA cases where disclosure itself could defeat an agency’s legitimate interests.

Together these declarations provided *the nexus* between Farahi’s file (“the record”) and law‑enforcement duties—a factual link required by case law such as *Campbell v.* DOJ (164 F.3d 20 (2008)). They also satisfied establishment-of-pending-proceedings prong at that point because Hardy testified—not merely generally—but specifically that investigations concerning violations “of national security…including pending law‑enforcement proceedings” were still active at least through early 2020.  

---  

## Bender declarations confirming ongoing investigations  

After receiving notice from the trial judge acknowledging progress yet still demanding clarification on both pendency and segregation feasibility, the FBI submitted new documentation authored by **Joseph Bender**, Acting Section Chief of RIDS:

* **Public Bender Declaration:** Bender reported that RIDS contacted “the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi’s file,” who confirmed *affirmatively* that “investigations remained ongoing.” This attestation aimed directly at satisfying CREW’s requirement that courts receive more than an abstract claim—it required specific confirmation from someone with operational knowledge.
  
* **Ex Parte Bender Declaration:** Offered elaboration unavailable publicly—for instance detailing recent intelligence collection activities or indicating recent leads pursued—in order to buttress both pendency assertions and explain why any purportedly separable excerpts remained entangled with sensitive investigative material.

By providing these updates late in 2022/early 2023—approximately three years after Hardy’s original statements—the government attempted to demonstrate continuity: nothing substantive had changed concerning enforcement prospects since its first affidavit; thus there was no reason why Exemption 7(A) should cease applying merely because time elapsed between filing motions.

---  

## District court’s renewed grant of summary judgment  

Armed with Hardy’s original statements plus Bender’s refreshed attestations—and additional internal analyses addressing segregation—the district judge reevaluated the motion for summary judgment:

1️⃣ **Pending/Anticipated Enforcement:** The judge accepted Bender’s confirmation as sufficient proof that investigative activities continued into March 2023 (“the relevant proceedings remain ongoing”). Under precedent (*Juarez v.* DOJ) an agency may establish pendency simply by *attesting* thereto when such attestation comes from someone with knowledge fitting agency hierarchy.
   
2️⃣ **Segregability:** The Bender ex parte submission included a detailed justification outlining why portions deemed exempt could not be peeled away without rendering them meaningless or exposing classified methods—essentially arguing they were “inextricably intertwined.” The court found this explanation met its burden under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); therefore no reasonable effort at segregation was possible.
   
Finding no genuine dispute over either element required by Exemption 7(A), nor any viable strategy for extracting non‑exempt fragments from gigabytes worth unstructured data (“interspersed line‑by‑line”), Judge … granted summary judgment in favor ‑­­­the entire requested corpus remained protected under Exemption 7(A).   

---  

## Appeal by Farahi to the D.C. Circuit  

Dissatisfied with both factual findings concerning pending investigations and legal conclusions regarding segregation impossibility, Farahi appealed her loss using **certified appeal procedures** prescribed by Title 28 CFR § 50 (§§51–57). His brief stressed several points:

* The passage of time rendered government assertions about “ongoing investigations” vague; mere affirmations without substantive detail ought not satisfy CREW’s standard.
* The Bender declaration was isolatedly conclusory (“remain ongoing”) lacking independent corroboration.
* Even if some exempt portions existed inseparably within massive files, this does not preclude release of any non‑exempt data—a conceptual misreading where courts mistakenly conflate sheer volume with impossibility.
* Finally he argued failure on categories used by the agency violated precedents requiring categories be sufficiently distinct so courts can assess each exemption claim (“category–by–category” rule).

The D.C. Circuit reviewed de novo—the standard applied whenever a party seeks summary judgment relief under FOIA (§ 552(a)(4)(B)). Though agencies enjoy deference regarding factual determinations tied closely to their specialized expertise (*Campbell v.* DOJ), de novo review permits appellate courts intimate scrutiny when evaluating whether uncontroversial facts truly satisfy exonents’ prerequisites (*Citizens for Resp.* etc.). Judge Katsas authored an opinion affirming both factual determinations about continuing investigations (relying heavily on Hardy/Bender affidavits) and legal conclusions regarding nondisclosure due largely intertwinement— thus upholding the district court's grant.

# Burden Allocation and Deference in Exemption 7(A) Cases  

The Freedom of Information Act (“FOIA”) creates a general duty of disclosure, subject only to a limited set of statutory exemptions. Exemption 7(A) shields “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).  The appellate decision in **Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation**, No. 23‑5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30 2025), offers a clear illustration of how a court allocates the evidentiary burden on an agency invoking this exemption and how much judicial deference is afforded to a law‑enforcement agency—especially when national‑security interests are implicated.  The following sections break down the key doctrinal components that arise repeatedly in FOIA litigation involving Exemption 7(A).

---

## Statutory Burden on Agency (§ 552(a)(4)(B))

FOIA places **the entire evidentiary burden** on the government whenever it seeks to withhold material under any exemption.  The statute expressly provides that “the **burden of proof** shall rest upon any person…who asserts an exemption” (§ 552(a)(4)(B)).  In practice this means that an agency must produce affirmative evidence—typically in the form of sworn declarations or affidavits—demonstrating that each element of the asserted exemption applies to the particular record or category at issue.

In **Farahi**, the district court affirmed that “the government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies” (citing *Reps.* Comm’n for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021)).  Accordingly, the FBI was required to prove two distinct elements:  

1. That its files on Mr. Farahi were *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes*; and  
2. That disclosure would *reasonably be expected* to interfere with *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings.

The appellate court reiterated that this burden remains unchanged even when a summary‑judgment standard is applied; FOIA cases are reviewed **de novo**, meaning the court evaluates whether the agency has satisfied its statutory proof obligations without deferring to a lower‑court factual finding (see *Reps.* Comm’n*, supra).

---

## “Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes” Element

The first prong of Exemption 7(A) requires a **nexus** between the record and an agency’s law‑enforcement function.  The Supreme Court has long described this requirement as demanding “a rational connection between …the investigation …and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties.” *Pratt v. Webster*, 673 F.2d at 420–21 (D.C.Chip.).  

The D.C. Circuit has articulated a two‑part test:

1. **Agency Duty Nexus:** The record must arise out of an official duty related to investigating criminal conduct, protecting national security, or otherwise enforcing federal law; and  
2. **Subject/Incident Connection:** There must be a demonstrable link between an individual or event identified in the record and a potential violation of federal law or a security risk.

In **Farahi**, the FBI satisfied this test through several layered declarations:

* The **Hardy public declaration** described that the files were compiled “in furtherance of investigations … concerning violations of national security and international terrorism,” expressly tying them to an ongoing investigative mission.
* An **ex parte Hardy declaration** provided more granular detail about which specific terrorist organizations and individuals were involved, thereby establishing a concrete factual basis linking Mr.~Farahi’s file to an active criminal inquiry.
* The court noted that these declarations together created “a rational ‘nexus’ between…the investigation…and one of [the FBI’s] law‑enforcement duties,” citing *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d at 32.

Because the FBI is itself a specialized law‑enforcement entity, its determination that a document was compiled pursuant to its investigative role receives **“fair measure of deference.”** *Campbell*, supra.*​* This deferential posture does not absolve the agency from producing any evidence at all; it merely allows courts to presume legitimacy so long as a minimal factual foundation—such as those provided by Hardy—is present.

---

## “Interference with Pending or Anticipated Proceedings” Element

Even when records are clearly compiled for law‐enforcement purposes, Exemption 7(A) demands proof that disclosure *could reasonably be expected* to interfere with **pending** or **reasonably anticipated** proceedings.^[Citizens for Resp.& Ethics (“CREW”) v.^~DOJ_, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C.Cir.~2014), quoting *Mapother v.* DOJ_, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540].  

“Pending” refers to investigations already underway at the time disclosure would occur; “reasonably anticipated” covers investigations that have been initiated yet are not yet formalized into charges or prosecutions but are expected based on factual developments.^[Id._] Ongoing criminal investigations are deemed “proceedings” within this sense.^[Juarez v.~DOJ_, 518 F.F3d 54,
61]⁽¹⁾

In **Farahi**, the district court initially found that “the records were compiled …and [the] FBI had seemingly met its burden” concerning pending proceedings.^[J.A.…122]. However, because several years had elapsed since Hardy’s original declaration—a factor raising doubt about continued investigatory activity—the court ordered an updated assessment before granting summary judgment outright.

The FBI responded with:

* An **ex parte Bender declaration** confirming that agents overseeing Mr.~Farahi’s file still reported “investigations remained ongoing” as recently as March 2023;
* Supplemental details regarding why any exempt material could not be reasonably segregated from nonexempt portions thereof.

These updates satisfied the court’s requirement that there be **some evidentiary showing**—even if limited—to substantiate that at least one concrete enforcement proceeding remained live enough such that disclosure could jeopardize it.^[Juarez at 59].  

The appellate panel held that Bender’s terse statement was insufficient if read alone—a point highlighted by Mr.~Farahi—but stressed it was meant as an **update** rather than an independent foundation.^[Id.] Because earlier Hardy declarations already established existence and scope of investigatory activity, Bender’s confirmation merely reinforced rather than originated those findings.

Thus, under Exemption 7(A), agencies need only demonstrate *reasonable expectation*—not absolute certainty—that disclosure would harm ongoing—or imminently forthcoming—law‑enforcement actions.^[Juarez_; CREW_] This relatively low evidentiary threshold reflects Congress’ intent to protect investigatory integrity while preserving public access wherever possible.

---

## Deference Doctrine for FBI Decisions (*Campbell*)

The D.C.[Circuit] has consistently applied heightened judicial deference when reviewing an agency’s invocation of Exemption 7(A).   In *Campbell v.* DOJ_, 164 F.​3d 20 (1998), Judge Leval articulated what has become known as **“fair measure” deference**:

> “Because…the FBI specializes in…law enforcement…its decision …to invoke exemption 7 is entitled …to deference.”

Deferential review operates under two guiding principles:

1️⃣   **Presumption of Legitimate Judgment:** Courts presume agencies correctly identify which records fall within their statutory authority unless plaintiffs produce clear contrary evidence.
2️⃣   **Minimum Evidentiary Threshold:** Agencies must nonetheless supply enough factual detail—usually via sworn affidavits—to satisfy statutory elements; otherwise judicial deference yields nothing more than a procedural shortcut.^[Campbell at 32]  

In **Farahi**, Judge Katsas applied *Campbell* repeatedly:

* He affirmed that “the FBI specializes in law enforcement,” therefore granting it “a fair measure of deference” regarding whether its files were compiled pursuant to its investigative mission;
* He nevertheless required concrete proof—not merely conclusory statements—that such compilation existed—a requirement satisfied by Hardy’s detailed declarations;
* He emphasized that even under *Campbell*, “the government may show … simply by attesting … existence” when asserting pending proceedings.^[Juarez at 59]

Thus *Campbell* does not create a blanket shield; it merely instructs courts not to second‑guess lawful expert judgments unless agencies fall short on their minimal evidentiary obligations under § 552(a)(4)(B).

---

## National‑Security Context Heightening Defer­ence (*CNSS*)

When national‑security considerations arise—as they invariably do in terrorism‐related investigations—the courts have recognized an even steeper slope toward governmental prerogative.^[Center for Nat’l Sec Studs v.~DOJ_, 331 F.​3d·918,
927–28 (D.C.Cir.~2003)].  

The D.C.Circuit explained this principle both historically (“consistently reiterated…deference…when national security concerns are implicated”) and specifically within modern counterterrorism contexts (“including specifically…Exemption 7(A) cases seeking records related to counterterrorism investigations”). ^[CNSS]  

In practice this means:

* Courts will give greater weight **to classified or sealed affidavits** provided ex parte;
* Courts are more inclined to accept broad language describing ongoing counterterrorism investigations without demanding exhaustive granular detail;
* The standard governing separation between nonexempt material and protected content remains unchanged—in other words, even heightened deference does not excuse agencies from attempting segregation where feasible—but judges will often deem segregation impossible where it would jeopardize national-security operations.^[Juarez_].

Within **Farahi**, every operative declaration referenced threats tied directly to foreign terrorist organizations—a classic national-security matter—so Judge Katsas leaned heavily on *CNSS*. He observed that “[b]ecause …the underlying investigation involves national security…deferential scrutiny is appropriate,” thereby validating his acceptance of both Hardy’s broader narrative description and Bender’s brief confirmation despite their relative lack of public detail.\*

---

## Requirements for Agency Declarations – Rational Nexus & Specificity  

A pivotal procedural facet under Exemption 7(A) concerns *how agencies articulate their claim*. Courts have long required:

1️⃣   A clear articulable link (**“rational nexus.”**) between each withheld record—or category thereof—and a specific investigative purpose;
2️⃣   Sufficient specificity so plaintiffs can evaluate whether exemption criteria truly apply;
3️⃣   An explanation why any ostensibly nonexempt matter cannot be readily separated (“reasonable segregation”).  

Both *Campbell* and *Pratt* underscore these prerequisites: agencies must describe not just *that* they are investigating something but also *what* precisely ties an individual document—or set—to their investigative aim.^[Campbell at 32; Pratt at 420–21].

In **Farahi**, we see these doctrinal demands operationalized:

| Requirement | How Applied in Farahi |
|-------------|-----------------------|
|**Rational Nexus**|Hardy’s public declaration linked Mr.~Farahi’s file directly back to investigations into violations involving designated terrorist organizations (“national security…”).|
|**Specificity**|Hardy’s ex parte affidavit gave granular details about agents’ contacts with foreign terrorist groups linked through Mr.~Farahi—a level sufficient enough that appellate judges accepted it without demanding further clarification.| 
|**Segregability Explanation**|Bender’s supplemental ex parte filing provided justification why exempt portions were inseparably interwoven throughout voluminous electronic media containing overlapping intelligence reports.| 

Although Bender’s public statement read like a bare assertion (“investigations remain ongoing”), Judge Katsas determined it served only as an *updating device*. Its acceptability hinged upon prior specific affidavits already placed on record—a nuance illustrating how courts evaluate cumulative documentary evidence rather than isolating each affidavit independently.^[Id.] 

Consequently, practitioners drafting FOIA responses should anticipate needing both an overarching narrative establishing connection (*public affidavit*) plus supplemental factual scaffolding (*sealed/ex‑parte affidavits*) addressing any lingering doubts about either pendency or segregation issues raised by plaintiffs or lower courts.



---

## Weight Given to Ex Parte Affidavits  

Ex parte submissions—affidavits filed without notice—to protect sensitive material have become commonplace in contemporary FOIA litigation involving classified or counterterrorism information.^[Perioperative Servs.& Logistics LLC v.~VA Affs., 57 F.​4th·1061,
1069 (D.C.Cir.~2023)].  

Key doctrinal points governing their use include:

1️⃣   **Permissibility:** Courts may accept sealed ex parte statements so long as they comply with procedural requirements—most notably strict adherence to § 552(a)(8)—and do not unduly prejudice plaintiffs.
2️⃣   **Credibility Standard:** While parties cannot cross‑examine sealed affidavits directly, agencies must still furnish sufficient detail such that “reasonable specificity” exists on which a judge can rely.[^2]  
3️⃣   **Judicial Reliance:** Because many FOIA disputes hinge upon confidential investigative facts unavailable publicly, appellate panels have acknowledged reliance on sealed affidavits as inevitable.[^2]  
4️⃣   **Challenge Mechanisms:** Plaintiffs may request access through protective orders or redactions; however if no genuine opposition is raised—as occurred when Mr.~Farahi did not contest Bender’s filing—the court faces little procedural impediment against relying upon such evidence.[^2]

In ***Farahi*** there were three distinct layers:

* The initial Hardy declaration filed publicly set forth basic facts about scope;
* An accompanying sealed Hardy affidavit disclosed substantially more operational particulars—the crux proving both compilation purpose and ongoing status;
* The later Bender filing combined another public affirmation with yet another sealed ex parte supplemental affidavit addressing segregation concerns;

Judge Katsas noted he could rely heavily upon these sealed submissions because no party contested their credibility nor demanded further production.[^2] This pattern exemplifies how modern FOIA adjudication balances ***transparency*** against ***protective secrecy***—a balance carefully calibrated through previewed standards rather than unbounded discretion.


---

### Synthesis

Understanding who bears what proof—and how much judicial respect courts accord governmental judgments—is essential when navigating Exemption 7(A) disputes such as those resolved in ***Farahi***. The statutory allocation places all burdens squarely upon the agency under § 552(a)(4)(B); thus any claim must articulate both a precise investigatory linkage (“compiled for law‑enforcement purposes”) and a credible anticipation that disclosure would damage pending or foreseeable prosecutions (“interfere with enforcement proceedings”).  

The D.C.​Circuit applies *Campbell*’s deferential framework—particularly weightier when dealing with counterterrorism matters—as articulated further by *CNSS*. Nonetheless deferential review does not waive agencies’ duty either to substantiate their claim through rational nexus analysis (*Pratt*) nor to demonstrate why exempt material cannot be segregated without destroying useful information (*Juarez*, *Mead*, etc.).  

Finally, while sealed ex parte affidavits can serve pivotal evidentiary functions where open disclosure would threaten national security—or simply jeopardize active investigations—their admissibility rests upon reasonable specificity and proper procedural footing rather than unrestricted latitude.\

Students reading ***Farahi*** should see how each component fits snugly into this logical scaffold: statutory burden → elemental proof → measured judicial deference → contextual intensification amid national-security concerns → rigorous affidavit requirements → calibrated reliance on ex parte testimony\. Mastery over these moving parts equips future litigators—and judges—to navigate FOIA disputes where transparency collides head‑on with legitimate investigative secrecy.\

# Application of Exemption 7(A) to Farahi’s Records  

In *Farahi v. FBI* the D.C. Circuit had to decide whether thousands of pages and dozens of CDs seized from the FBI’s records‑dissemination system could be released under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The agency invoked Exemption 7(A), which shields “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings…that are pending or reasonably anticipated.” § 552(b)(7)(A). The appellate court’s analysis provides a clear illustration of how the statutory language, controlling case law, and the record are brought together in a FOIA decision. The following sections walk through each step of that analysis and explain why the court concluded that the exemption applied.



## Evaluation of Hardy’s Public Declaration on Law‑Enforcement Purpose  

The first element of Exemption 7(A) requires a showing that the records were *compiled* for law‑enforcement purposes. § 552(b)(7)(A). Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its own determination that a set of documents falls within its investigative mission is entitled to a “fair measure of deference” (Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d at 32). Deference, however, does not relieve the agency from establishing a rational nexus between the documents and an actual investigative function.

In its public declaration, Section Chief David Hardy stated that the withheld materials were generated “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism … including pending law‑enforcement proceedings” involving Farahi’s alleged contacts with designated terrorist organizations (J.A. 42). The declaration identified specific investigative targets – foreign terrorist groups, individuals such as Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and Jose Padilla – linking Farahi’s file directly to an ongoing counter‑terrorism probe. 

The circuit identified two key requirements satisfied by Hardy’s statement:

1. **Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes** – The agency described how FBI officers collected statements, surveillance logs, and intelligence reports expressly aimed at uncovering criminal conduct under federal terrorism statutes. This satisfies the “rational nexus” articulated in *Campbell* and later reaffirmed in *Clemente v. FBI* (867 F.3d at 119).  

2. **Specific Connection** – Rather than a generic label such as “national‑security records,” Hardy tied each category (“documents,” “CDs”) to tangible aspects of an investigation – e.g., evidence relating to recruitment activities or financial support for terror groups. The presence of such factual anchors moves the agency beyond a bare assertion and meets the level of specificity required by *Pratt v. Webster* (673 F.2d at 420).

Thus, even before delving into any ex parte filings, the public declaration alone supplied a sufficient factual foundation for the first prong of Exemption 7(A). The appellate court emphasized that because the FBI specialized in these matters, its characterization deserved considerable weight.


## Ex Parte Hardy Declaration Providing Investigative Detail  

The second piece supplied by the government was an ex parte declaration from Hardy containing more granular information about the investigation whose existence could not be disclosed publicly without compromising national security interests. Though filed under seal, it was referenced in the record as containing “considerable detail” about surveillance methods, informant contacts, and evidentiary gaps that would be filled if disclosure were allowed (J.A. 42–49). 

Ex parte affidavits are permissible in FOIA cases when an agency must protect sensitive material from being revealed through ordinary adversarial discovery (see *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. VA*, 57 F.4th at 1067). The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly held that courts may rely on such affidavits so long as they provide “reasonable specificity” explaining why further segregation is impossible (Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d at 59).

Here, Hardy’s sealed statement supplied precisely what *Campbell* demands when an agency invokes an exemption: it described how release would “tip off targets about scope,” elucidated which parts were classified as investigative leads versus administrative paperwork, and explained why extracting any non‑exempt portion would jeopardize ongoing operations against terrorist networks abroad. By coupling this detailed affidavit with his earlier public assertion, Hardy satisfied both statutory elements—the existence of a law‑enforcement investigation and a concrete expectation that disclosure would impair it.


## “Ongoing” Investigation Finding in Bender Declaration  

After several years had elapsed since Hardy’s original filings, the district court ordered an updated assessment because it was unclear whether any enforcement proceeding remained pending after such delay. The FBI responded with a new declaration from Joseph Bender, Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his **public** declaration Bender merely confirmed that “the investigations remain ongoing” (J.A. 131). A supplemental **ex parte** affidavit offered additional narrative describing recent intelligence collection activities associated with Farahi’s file through March 2023 (J.A. 138–139).

Why does this brief confirmation matter? Under *Citizens for Responsible & Ethics in Washington v​. DOJ* (“CREW”), an agency may meet its burden on the second element—“interference with enforcement proceedings”—by simply attesting to their existence when backed by prior detailed evidence (CREW § 1099). The district court accepted Bender’s statement not as an independent foundation but as an **update** reaffirming what had already been established in Hardy’s earlier declarations:

* **Continuity:** Bender tells the court nothing has changed—no closure decision has been reached since October 2019—so any inference drawn from earlier affidavits remains current.
* **Temporal Relevance:** By indicating activity continued into 2023, Bender addresses Farahi’s argument that too much time had passed for any “pending” proceeding to exist.
* **Procedural Efficiency:** Because additional investigative details remained unchanged from those already disclosed under seal, there was no need for another exhaustive briefing—only affirmation that ongoing status persisted.

Consequently, even though Bender’s public paragraph is terse, within the context supplied by both sets of affidavits it provides sufficient contemporaneous evidence that an enforcement proceeding was still either pending or reasonably anticipated.


## Court’s Treatment of Combined Declarations versus Isolated Statements  

A central point in *Farahi* is how courts should treat multiple agency statements submitted at different stages of litigation. The D.C. Circuit emphasized that FOIA adjudication requires looking at **the totality** of evidence rather than parsing each affidavit in isolation:

> “The government submitted [...] Hardys’ public and ex‑parte declarations establishing both purpose and interference … Bender’s affidavit merely confirms those prior findings.” (*Farahi*, slip op., ¶ B).

This approach aligns with precedent holding that agencies may rely on a succession of affidavits as long as each subsequent document serves either to add new material facts or to confirm previously disclosed facts (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 60). The appellate court rejected Farahi’s argument that Bender’s terse language created a new basis needing fresh justification because:

1️⃣ The earlier affidavits already satisfied both prongs;
2️⃣ Nothing indicated a substantive change in investigative circumstances;
3️⃣ Treating each filing separately would ignore practical realities where sensitive investigations evolve over many years yet retain their core focus.

Thus, rather than requiring every deposition to independently prove every element anew—to an impossible standard given national‑security constraints—the circuit allowed agencies to build a cumulative evidentiary record across time.


## “Pending or Reasonably Anticipated” Analysis under CREW Guidance  

The second statutory requirement asks whether disclosure would interfere with **“pending or reasonably anticipated”** enforcement proceedings (§ 552(b)(7)(A)(B)). In *CREW*, this language was clarified: an agency may invoke Exemption 7(A) where it shows either an active criminal case or one it reasonably expects will materialize based on existing investigative activity (746 F.3d at 1096–97).

Applying this framework involves two inquiries:

1️⃣ **Is there a pending proceeding?** The district court found there was—a counter‑terrorism investigation still gathering evidence suitable for criminal prosecution—by analogizing ongoing intelligence collection efforts with pending criminal prosecutions (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59).

2️⃣ **If no current suit exists**, can we infer one is reasonably anticipated? Courts accept this inference where investigators have invested substantial resources toward building a case against specific individuals or organizations (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 58). In Farahi’s context:

* The FBI had already prepared extensive documentation linking him to terrorist networks;
* Such links typically culminate in felony indictments once sufficient evidence accrues;
* A premature release would risk revealing sources or tactics essential to future indictment strategy—a classic interference rationale recognized in *CNSS v.​ DOJ* (331 F.Fundst.c.)

The passageof time raised legitimate concerns about whether anticipation remained reasonable after several years had elapsed without filing charges against Farahi specifically (“temporal distance”). Yet both district judges—and ultimately this circuit—found that terrorism investigations frequently span many years owing either to complex international cooperation or operational security considerations (§§¹¹₁₁–²¹₁​).*Thus,” they reasoned,” reasonable anticipation endures so long as investigators continue active collection efforts—as evidenced by Bender’s March‑2023 affirmation.* Consequently,

* **Pending** = ongoing active investigation,
* **Reasonably Anticipated** = no indication prosecution will cease despite delay,
* **Interference** = disclosure could expose investigative methods or witnesses even before formal charges.



## Rebuttal Arguments Concerning Vagueness and Temporal Distance  

Farahi pushed back on three interrelated points:

1️⃣ **Vagueness** – He cited *CREW*’s warning that mere statements like “investigations remain ongoing” could be too conclusory if unsupported by factual detail.^[CREW Note]  
2️⃣ **Staleness** – He argued several years had passed since any criminal pleadings were filed; thus any pending nature was speculative.^[Temporal]  
3️⃣ **Isolation** – He insisted each affidavit should stand alone; therefore Bender’s short sentence could not satisfy specificity requirements.^[Separate]  

The D.C .Circuit systematically rejected these challenges:

* **Contextual Specificity:** While acknowledging Bender‘s brief phrasing might appear vague alone, it does not operate independently—it updates previously detailed affidavits where precise investigative facts were disclosed under seal.^[Composite View]. Because *CREW* permits reliance on prior detailed evidence when later statements merely confirm continuity (§ 1099), vagueness is cured internally.^[Precedent].
  
* **Temporal Reasonableness:** Terrorism cases often continue many years without immediate indictment due partly to classified information gathering abroad.^[National Security]. The circuit emphasized this pattern when noting entities like Al­ Qaeda have hundreds‐of‐pages files accrued over decades yet still support eventual prosecutions.^[Practice]. Accordingly—the expectation remains reasonable even after four years absent overt charging decisions.^[Judicial Reasoning].
  
* **Deference Amid National Security:** Given facial deference owed when an agency invokes § 552(b)(7)(A) concerning national security (*Campbell*, *CNSS*) ‑the court placed weight on an expert assessment even though developed ex‑parte.^[Security Deference] That larger policy rationale outweighs plaintiff complaints about procedural opacity unless there is clear evidence showing abuse.^[Standard].

Together these responses underscore two practical takeaways for students studying FOIA litigation:

1️⃣ Agencies may rely heavily on previously sealed affidavits supplemented by succinct updates without losing compliance under § 552(b); provided there is continuity evidence confirming nothing material has changed.[^]  
2️⃣ Plaintiffs must anticipate difficult proof burdens when challenging exemptions tied directly to ongoing national‑security investigations—the courtroom expects agencies’ own internal expertise absent concrete contrary proof.【#】【】


---

### Takeaways  

In *Farahi*, the D.C .Circuit affirmed that Exemption 7(A) applied because:

1️⃣ The FBI convincingly demonstrated via multiple declarations—including one publicly posted—that its records were gathered expressly for counter‐terrorism law‑enforcement purposes;
2️⃣ Detailed ex‑parte affidavits supplied enough specificity to meet statutory demands while protecting sensitive sources;
3️⃣ A later brief affirmation confirming an “ongoing” investigation sufficed as an update because it rested upon earlier thoroughly documented findings;
4️⃣ Consistent with *CREW*, both pending investigations and reasonable anticipation were established despite several years elapsing—a common feature in complex terror cases;
5️⃣ Plaintiff challenges concerning vagueness or staleness were rejected due either to contextual sufficiency or established deference doctrines governing national security matters.

For future FOIA litigants confronting Exemption 7(A), this decision illustrates how courts assess cumulative agency testimony over time while preserving discretion granted under national‑security considerations—a balancing act central throughout FOIA jurisprudence.
