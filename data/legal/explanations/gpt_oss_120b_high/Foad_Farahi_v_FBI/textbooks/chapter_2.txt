Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Burden Allocation and Deference in Exemption 7(A) Cases  

The Freedom of Information Act (“FOIA”) creates a general duty of disclosure, subject only to a limited set of statutory exemptions. Exemption 7(A) shields “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).  The appellate decision in **Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation**, No. 23‑5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30 2025), offers a clear illustration of how a court allocates the evidentiary burden on an agency invoking this exemption and how much judicial deference is afforded to a law‑enforcement agency—especially when national‑security interests are implicated.  The following sections break down the key doctrinal components that arise repeatedly in FOIA litigation involving Exemption 7(A).

---

## Statutory Burden on Agency (§ 552(a)(4)(B))

FOIA places **the entire evidentiary burden** on the government whenever it seeks to withhold material under any exemption.  The statute expressly provides that “the **burden of proof** shall rest upon any person…who asserts an exemption” (§ 552(a)(4)(B)).  In practice this means that an agency must produce affirmative evidence—typically in the form of sworn declarations or affidavits—demonstrating that each element of the asserted exemption applies to the particular record or category at issue.

In **Farahi**, the district court affirmed that “the government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies” (citing *Reps.* Comm’n for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021)).  Accordingly, the FBI was required to prove two distinct elements:  

1. That its files on Mr. Farahi were *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes*; and  
2. That disclosure would *reasonably be expected* to interfere with *pending or reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings.

The appellate court reiterated that this burden remains unchanged even when a summary‑judgment standard is applied; FOIA cases are reviewed **de novo**, meaning the court evaluates whether the agency has satisfied its statutory proof obligations without deferring to a lower‑court factual finding (see *Reps.* Comm’n*, supra).

---

## “Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes” Element

The first prong of Exemption 7(A) requires a **nexus** between the record and an agency’s law‑enforcement function.  The Supreme Court has long described this requirement as demanding “a rational connection between …the investigation …and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties.” *Pratt v. Webster*, 673 F.2d at 420–21 (D.C.Chip.).  

The D.C. Circuit has articulated a two‑part test:

1. **Agency Duty Nexus:** The record must arise out of an official duty related to investigating criminal conduct, protecting national security, or otherwise enforcing federal law; and  
2. **Subject/Incident Connection:** There must be a demonstrable link between an individual or event identified in the record and a potential violation of federal law or a security risk.

In **Farahi**, the FBI satisfied this test through several layered declarations:

* The **Hardy public declaration** described that the files were compiled “in furtherance of investigations … concerning violations of national security and international terrorism,” expressly tying them to an ongoing investigative mission.
* An **ex parte Hardy declaration** provided more granular detail about which specific terrorist organizations and individuals were involved, thereby establishing a concrete factual basis linking Mr.~Farahi’s file to an active criminal inquiry.
* The court noted that these declarations together created “a rational ‘nexus’ between…the investigation…and one of [the FBI’s] law‑enforcement duties,” citing *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d at 32.

Because the FBI is itself a specialized law‑enforcement entity, its determination that a document was compiled pursuant to its investigative role receives **“fair measure of deference.”** *Campbell*, supra.*​* This deferential posture does not absolve the agency from producing any evidence at all; it merely allows courts to presume legitimacy so long as a minimal factual foundation—such as those provided by Hardy—is present.

---

## “Interference with Pending or Anticipated Proceedings” Element

Even when records are clearly compiled for law‐enforcement purposes, Exemption 7(A) demands proof that disclosure *could reasonably be expected* to interfere with **pending** or **reasonably anticipated** proceedings.^[Citizens for Resp.& Ethics (“CREW”) v.^~DOJ_, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C.Cir.~2014), quoting *Mapother v.* DOJ_, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540].  

“Pending” refers to investigations already underway at the time disclosure would occur; “reasonably anticipated” covers investigations that have been initiated yet are not yet formalized into charges or prosecutions but are expected based on factual developments.^[Id._] Ongoing criminal investigations are deemed “proceedings” within this sense.^[Juarez v.~DOJ_, 518 F.F3d 54,
61]⁽¹⁾

In **Farahi**, the district court initially found that “the records were compiled …and [the] FBI had seemingly met its burden” concerning pending proceedings.^[J.A.…122]. However, because several years had elapsed since Hardy’s original declaration—a factor raising doubt about continued investigatory activity—the court ordered an updated assessment before granting summary judgment outright.

The FBI responded with:

* An **ex parte Bender declaration** confirming that agents overseeing Mr.~Farahi’s file still reported “investigations remained ongoing” as recently as March 2023;
* Supplemental details regarding why any exempt material could not be reasonably segregated from nonexempt portions thereof.

These updates satisfied the court’s requirement that there be **some evidentiary showing**—even if limited—to substantiate that at least one concrete enforcement proceeding remained live enough such that disclosure could jeopardize it.^[Juarez at 59].  

The appellate panel held that Bender’s terse statement was insufficient if read alone—a point highlighted by Mr.~Farahi—but stressed it was meant as an **update** rather than an independent foundation.^[Id.] Because earlier Hardy declarations already established existence and scope of investigatory activity, Bender’s confirmation merely reinforced rather than originated those findings.

Thus, under Exemption 7(A), agencies need only demonstrate *reasonable expectation*—not absolute certainty—that disclosure would harm ongoing—or imminently forthcoming—law‑enforcement actions.^[Juarez_; CREW_] This relatively low evidentiary threshold reflects Congress’ intent to protect investigatory integrity while preserving public access wherever possible.

---

## Deference Doctrine for FBI Decisions (*Campbell*)

The D.C.[Circuit] has consistently applied heightened judicial deference when reviewing an agency’s invocation of Exemption 7(A).   In *Campbell v.* DOJ_, 164 F.​3d 20 (1998), Judge Leval articulated what has become known as **“fair measure” deference**:

> “Because…the FBI specializes in…law enforcement…its decision …to invoke exemption 7 is entitled …to deference.”

Deferential review operates under two guiding principles:

1️⃣   **Presumption of Legitimate Judgment:** Courts presume agencies correctly identify which records fall within their statutory authority unless plaintiffs produce clear contrary evidence.
2️⃣   **Minimum Evidentiary Threshold:** Agencies must nonetheless supply enough factual detail—usually via sworn affidavits—to satisfy statutory elements; otherwise judicial deference yields nothing more than a procedural shortcut.^[Campbell at 32]  

In **Farahi**, Judge Katsas applied *Campbell* repeatedly:

* He affirmed that “the FBI specializes in law enforcement,” therefore granting it “a fair measure of deference” regarding whether its files were compiled pursuant to its investigative mission;
* He nevertheless required concrete proof—not merely conclusory statements—that such compilation existed—a requirement satisfied by Hardy’s detailed declarations;
* He emphasized that even under *Campbell*, “the government may show … simply by attesting … existence” when asserting pending proceedings.^[Juarez at 59]

Thus *Campbell* does not create a blanket shield; it merely instructs courts not to second‑guess lawful expert judgments unless agencies fall short on their minimal evidentiary obligations under § 552(a)(4)(B).

---

## National‑Security Context Heightening Defer­ence (*CNSS*)

When national‑security considerations arise—as they invariably do in terrorism‐related investigations—the courts have recognized an even steeper slope toward governmental prerogative.^[Center for Nat’l Sec Studs v.~DOJ_, 331 F.​3d·918,
927–28 (D.C.Cir.~2003)].  

The D.C.Circuit explained this principle both historically (“consistently reiterated…deference…when national security concerns are implicated”) and specifically within modern counterterrorism contexts (“including specifically…Exemption 7(A) cases seeking records related to counterterrorism investigations”). ^[CNSS]  

In practice this means:

* Courts will give greater weight **to classified or sealed affidavits** provided ex parte;
* Courts are more inclined to accept broad language describing ongoing counterterrorism investigations without demanding exhaustive granular detail;
* The standard governing separation between nonexempt material and protected content remains unchanged—in other words, even heightened deference does not excuse agencies from attempting segregation where feasible—but judges will often deem segregation impossible where it would jeopardize national-security operations.^[Juarez_].

Within **Farahi**, every operative declaration referenced threats tied directly to foreign terrorist organizations—a classic national-security matter—so Judge Katsas leaned heavily on *CNSS*. He observed that “[b]ecause …the underlying investigation involves national security…deferential scrutiny is appropriate,” thereby validating his acceptance of both Hardy’s broader narrative description and Bender’s brief confirmation despite their relative lack of public detail.\*

---

## Requirements for Agency Declarations – Rational Nexus & Specificity  

A pivotal procedural facet under Exemption 7(A) concerns *how agencies articulate their claim*. Courts have long required:

1️⃣   A clear articulable link (**“rational nexus.”**) between each withheld record—or category thereof—and a specific investigative purpose;
2️⃣   Sufficient specificity so plaintiffs can evaluate whether exemption criteria truly apply;
3️⃣   An explanation why any ostensibly nonexempt matter cannot be readily separated (“reasonable segregation”).  

Both *Campbell* and *Pratt* underscore these prerequisites: agencies must describe not just *that* they are investigating something but also *what* precisely ties an individual document—or set—to their investigative aim.^[Campbell at 32; Pratt at 420–21].

In **Farahi**, we see these doctrinal demands operationalized:

| Requirement | How Applied in Farahi |
|-------------|-----------------------|
|**Rational Nexus**|Hardy’s public declaration linked Mr.~Farahi’s file directly back to investigations into violations involving designated terrorist organizations (“national security…”).|
|**Specificity**|Hardy’s ex parte affidavit gave granular details about agents’ contacts with foreign terrorist groups linked through Mr.~Farahi—a level sufficient enough that appellate judges accepted it without demanding further clarification.| 
|**Segregability Explanation**|Bender’s supplemental ex parte filing provided justification why exempt portions were inseparably interwoven throughout voluminous electronic media containing overlapping intelligence reports.| 

Although Bender’s public statement read like a bare assertion (“investigations remain ongoing”), Judge Katsas determined it served only as an *updating device*. Its acceptability hinged upon prior specific affidavits already placed on record—a nuance illustrating how courts evaluate cumulative documentary evidence rather than isolating each affidavit independently.^[Id.] 

Consequently, practitioners drafting FOIA responses should anticipate needing both an overarching narrative establishing connection (*public affidavit*) plus supplemental factual scaffolding (*sealed/ex‑parte affidavits*) addressing any lingering doubts about either pendency or segregation issues raised by plaintiffs or lower courts.



---

## Weight Given to Ex Parte Affidavits  

Ex parte submissions—affidavits filed without notice—to protect sensitive material have become commonplace in contemporary FOIA litigation involving classified or counterterrorism information.^[Perioperative Servs.& Logistics LLC v.~VA Affs., 57 F.​4th·1061,
1069 (D.C.Cir.~2023)].  

Key doctrinal points governing their use include:

1️⃣   **Permissibility:** Courts may accept sealed ex parte statements so long as they comply with procedural requirements—most notably strict adherence to § 552(a)(8)—and do not unduly prejudice plaintiffs.
2️⃣   **Credibility Standard:** While parties cannot cross‑examine sealed affidavits directly, agencies must still furnish sufficient detail such that “reasonable specificity” exists on which a judge can rely.[^2]  
3️⃣   **Judicial Reliance:** Because many FOIA disputes hinge upon confidential investigative facts unavailable publicly, appellate panels have acknowledged reliance on sealed affidavits as inevitable.[^2]  
4️⃣   **Challenge Mechanisms:** Plaintiffs may request access through protective orders or redactions; however if no genuine opposition is raised—as occurred when Mr.~Farahi did not contest Bender’s filing—the court faces little procedural impediment against relying upon such evidence.[^2]

In ***Farahi*** there were three distinct layers:

* The initial Hardy declaration filed publicly set forth basic facts about scope;
* An accompanying sealed Hardy affidavit disclosed substantially more operational particulars—the crux proving both compilation purpose and ongoing status;
* The later Bender filing combined another public affirmation with yet another sealed ex parte supplemental affidavit addressing segregation concerns;

Judge Katsas noted he could rely heavily upon these sealed submissions because no party contested their credibility nor demanded further production.[^2] This pattern exemplifies how modern FOIA adjudication balances ***transparency*** against ***protective secrecy***—a balance carefully calibrated through previewed standards rather than unbounded discretion.


---

### Synthesis

Understanding who bears what proof—and how much judicial respect courts accord governmental judgments—is essential when navigating Exemption 7(A) disputes such as those resolved in ***Farahi***. The statutory allocation places all burdens squarely upon the agency under § 552(a)(4)(B); thus any claim must articulate both a precise investigatory linkage (“compiled for law‑enforcement purposes”) and a credible anticipation that disclosure would damage pending or foreseeable prosecutions (“interfere with enforcement proceedings”).  

The D.C.​Circuit applies *Campbell*’s deferential framework—particularly weightier when dealing with counterterrorism matters—as articulated further by *CNSS*. Nonetheless deferential review does not waive agencies’ duty either to substantiate their claim through rational nexus analysis (*Pratt*) nor to demonstrate why exempt material cannot be segregated without destroying useful information (*Juarez*, *Mead*, etc.).  

Finally, while sealed ex parte affidavits can serve pivotal evidentiary functions where open disclosure would threaten national security—or simply jeopardize active investigations—their admissibility rests upon reasonable specificity and proper procedural footing rather than unrestricted latitude.\

Students reading ***Farahi*** should see how each component fits snugly into this logical scaffold: statutory burden → elemental proof → measured judicial deference → contextual intensification amid national-security concerns → rigorous affidavit requirements → calibrated reliance on ex parte testimony\. Mastery over these moving parts equips future litigators—and judges—to navigate FOIA disputes where transparency collides head‑on with legitimate investigative secrecy.\
