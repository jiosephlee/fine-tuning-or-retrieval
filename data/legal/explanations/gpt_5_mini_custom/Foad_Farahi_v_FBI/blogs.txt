Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Refreshing 7(A): How the D.C. Circuit Handles Stale FOIA Declarations

## Why this case matters
FOIA cases involving long-running law‑enforcement or national‑security investigations pose a recurring, practical problem: evidence that an investigation was “ongoing” at one point can grow stale. In Farahi v. FBI, the D.C. Circuit affirmed the FBI’s use of Exemption 7(A) to withhold a voluminous file, but the opinion is most notable for how the court treated the passing of time. The district court had sensibly ordered the FBI to update its declarations; on appeal the panel accepted a short, confirmatory declaration because it operated as a time‑stamp on earlier, detailed public and ex parte submissions rather than as a standalone justification. That approach reconciles this court’s earlier warning against conclusory “ongoing investigation” assertions (in CREW) with the ordinary deference due to law‑enforcement agencies, particularly in national‑security cases.

## The facts in brief
Foad Farahi sought his FBI file; the FBI located over 10,000 pages and reviewed them, disclosing some material but withholding most under Exemption 7(A). The agency supported withholding with a detailed public declaration and a classified ex parte declaration from an initial RIDS chief (Hardy). Because several years had elapsed, the district court denied the agency’s first summary‑judgment motion without prejudice and ordered an updated assessment. The FBI supplied a public and ex parte renewal, including a terse public declaration from a successor RIDS chief (Bender) stating the investigation “remains ongoing,” with the ex parte material providing further detail. The district court granted summary judgment, concluding that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, and that nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed.

## How the court reasoned
The panel recited familiar law: Exemption 7(A) covers records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes when disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its invocation of Exemption 7 enjoys a measure of deference; that deference is heightened in national‑security contexts.

On the two core elements, the court found the FBI carried its burden. The agency’s public Hardy declaration and the sealed Hardy ex parte submission together established a clear nexus between the records and terrorism and national‑security investigations. As to the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement, the court addressed the staleness problem squarely. It acknowledged CREW’s caution that a merely vague statement that an investigation remains ongoing may not be enough, but explained that the Bender declaration should not be read in isolation. Bender functioned as an update — a relatively terse confirmation that “nothing had changed” since Hardy’s detailed public and ex parte descriptions — rather than a freestanding justification. The district court had already concluded that the FBI “seemingly” met its burden before ordering the update; the Bender declaration thus operated as a timestamp corroborating continuing pendency. Finally, the court accepted the district court’s segregability ruling, endorsing the view that when nonexempt bits are interspersed line‑by‑line with exempt material, disclosure of isolated words or phrases may not be “reasonably segregable.”

## What the decision adds (and what it does not)
Farahi does not alter the core Exemption 7(A) framework. It does, however, provide useful guidance about how courts can—and should—handle the passage of time in protracted FOIA litigation:

- An agency need not relitigate the entire justification for withholding every time a case lingers. A short, contemporaneous declaration confirming that the circumstances described in an earlier, detailed declaration remain true can be adequate, provided the earlier record contained sufficient specificity to show the nature and stakes of the investigation.

- CREW’s admonition against conclusory “ongoing” assertions remains good law. The difference here is context: the D.C. Circuit treated Bender’s terse statement as a supplementation of, not a substitute for, the prior detailed submissions (including an ex parte declaration), and it relied on the totality of the filings.

- Ex parte, sealed submissions continue to have a place in FOIA litigation when public disclosure of the very material at issue would defeat the government’s showing. The court reiterated that reliance on sealed explanations is permissible — even though it handicaps the requester — so long as the district court reviews them and explains its conclusion on the public record to the extent possible.

- Segregability analysis remains pragmatic: courts will accept that tiny, interspersed nonexempt fragments are not “reasonably segregable” if disclosure of the fragments would reveal meaningful information or if segregation is infeasible without undermining the protected content.

## Practical takeaways — for agencies, requesters, and courts
For agencies: if you anticipate protracted FOIA battles, prepare an initial declaration that is specific about the investigation’s subject, scope, and the concrete ways disclosure would interfere. If the case drags on, a lean, contemporaneous update that ties back to the original detailed declaration can preserve Exemption 7(A) without rehashing classified particulars. When using ex parte submissions, explain their purpose and provide the district court with what it needs to assess pendency and segregability; consider offering a public summary that reveals as much as possible without tipping off targets.

For requesters: a passage‑of‑time challenge is a live and often effective tactic, but it works best when the requester can show that the agency’s earlier justifications were thin or conclusory. Press for updated declarations, move to compel fuller explanations (including narrowing the universe of withheld categories), and emphasize any indicia that the investigation has in fact wound down. When the agency relies on ex parte material, press the court to provide as much public reasoning as possible and to test the necessity of secrecy.

For judges: ordering an update is a sensible tool to combat stale claims. District courts should make clear what they expect in such updates — a factual attestation to ongoing investigative steps, an explanation of why disclosure would harm enforcement, and, where segregation is contested, concrete reasons why nonexempt material cannot be culled without revealing sensitive content. When possible, explain reliance on sealed material in a way that preserves the public record’s integrity without exposing secrets.

## Broader doctrinal implications and limits
Farahi underscores the balance the D.C. Circuit has repeatedly struck: courts must police conclusory invocations of exemptions, but they also owe deference to agency expertise in law‑enforcement and national‑security matters. The opinion does not loosen CREW’s standard; it clarifies that a “refresh” affidavit can satisfy the “pending or reasonably anticipated” prong when it functions as a contemporaneous confirmation of an earlier, sufficiently detailed showing. That equilibrium preserves a practical pathway for agencies to defend legitimate secrecy claims in long cases but leaves intact a route for challengers when the original record is conclusory or vague.

A cautionary note: the approach in Farahi depends on there being an initial submission that meaningfully describes the investigation. Where an agency’s original declaration offers only generalized assertions, a short follow‑up will not save the claim. And the continued reliance on ex parte declarations keeps transparency concerns alive; judges and litigants will continue to wrestle with how much public justification is necessary to afford meaningful review without compromising investigations.

## Bottom line
Farahi is a pragmatic decision about timing and proof in FOIA litigation. It confirms that courts will demand freshness from agencies in long-running cases, but it also recognizes that a terse update tied to earlier, substantive declarations can satisfy Exemption 7(A)’s “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement. The ruling should push agencies to draft initial declarations with an eye toward longevity and push requesters to challenge vagueness early and demand timely updates.



# Behind Closed Doors: Ex Parte Declarations, Waiver, and FOIA’s National‑Security Exception in Practice

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is less about a new gloss on Exemption 7(A) than it is about process — who gets to test an agency’s secrecy claims and on what record. The panel affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI after the agency supplemented its public declarations with sealed, ex parte submissions. Significantly, the court endorsed the district court’s reliance on those sealed materials to find both that enforcement proceedings were “pending or reasonably anticipated” and that no meaningful, non‑exempt information was reasonably segregable. Farahi thus underscores the continued tolerance, at least in national‑security and counterterrorism FOIA disputes, for ex parte submissions as the means by which courts evaluate exemptions that would themselves be revealed by public explanation.

That tolerance has real stakes. Ex parte submissions can accomplish two things at once: they enable courts to adjudicate claims without publishing the closely held operational facts that could harm investigations, and they deprive requesters of an adversarial testing ground for the agency’s assertions. Farahi illustrates how courts will reconcile those tensions and highlights practical steps both requesters and agencies should take to protect their interests.

## The short procedural story

Foad Farahi, whose immigration record included allegations tying him to terrorism‑related activity, sought his FBI file under FOIA. The FBI located a massive set of potentially responsive records and, after years of review, withheld most of them under Exemption 7(A). The agency supported its summary‑judgment motions with a public declaration and with sealed, ex parte declarations from FBI officials. When the district court — concerned about the passage of time — asked the FBI to update its assertions about whether investigations remained ongoing, the FBI filed a terse public update plus a fuller ex parte submission. The district court, having reviewed the sealed material, concluded that the investigations remained ongoing and that meaningful segregable material was not reasonably separable; it granted judgment for the FBI. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, noting both the substantive sufficiency of the government’s showing and the procedural posture: Farahi had not opposed the government’s request to make parts of its filings ex parte.

## What the court said (and relied upon)

The panel applied familiar principles. Exemption 7(A) protects records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes when release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (CREW; Juarez). The D.C. Circuit reiterated substantial deference to the FBI’s characterization of its files as law‑enforcement records and to its judgment about interference where national‑security investigations are implicated (Campbell; CNSS). On segregability, the court accepted that densely interspersed, sensitive material may make disclosure of isolated words or phrases meaningless and therefore not “reasonably segregable” (Mead; Perioperative Services).

Two procedural points are critical to the panel’s decision. First, the FBI’s initial public and sealed declarations did the heavy lifting, and the later, brief public confirmation was treated as an update rather than an attempt to establish pendency anew. Second, Farahi did not oppose the district court’s order allowing sealed, ex parte filings; the panel treated that failure as fatal to his contention that the court should not have relied on the sealed material. The panel expressly invoked Perioperative Services for the proposition that ex parte submissions may be “inherent” when public reasoning would itself reveal the secrets at issue.

## Doctrinal footing for ex parte review — and its limits

Farahi sits comfortably on established precedents that permit courts to accept classified or sensitive ex parte submissions in FOIA cases. The doctrinal rationale has two prongs. Substantively, a court must be able to test the agency’s factual assertions (e.g., that an investigation is ongoing and would be harmed by disclosure) but doing so in public might itself disclose the very operational details that the agency seeks to protect. Procedurally, when a requester does not object to ex parte procedures — and when the sealed declarations are sufficiently detailed — courts have treated sealed filings as permissible and sometimes indispensable tools for adjudication.

That said, the decision also reflects limits that the D.C. Circuit has pressed in other contexts. Blanket, conclusory assertions are insufficient; public declarations must establish a rational nexus to law‑enforcement duties, and sealed declarations must give the court reasonable specificity to evaluate harm and segregability (see Campbell, CREW, Juarez). The court’s willingness to rely on sealed materials therefore depends on the quality of the sealed submission and the context of the case. Farahi’s outcome turned in part on the fact that the agency’s sealed declarations were not the only record — there were prior detailed public and ex parte declarations and a factual context from immigration proceedings that corroborated the FBI’s claims.

## The adversarial deficit and accountability concerns

Ex parte review inevitably produces an adversarial deficit. When requesters cannot see or rebut the factual foundations of the agency’s claim, a core function of adversarial litigation — testing credibility, pointing to omissions, offering alternative readings of the evidence — is constrained. Courts and agencies have long recognized this tension and attempted to mitigate it. The D.C. Circuit has required detailed affidavits and has permitted in camera review and other protective mechanisms to preserve the integrity of judicial fact‑finding without public disclosure.

Farahi demonstrates how procedural choices by requesters can compound the deficit. The panel noted that Farahi did not oppose the FBI’s motion to file sealed ex parte declarations; that concession foreclosed a later attack on the practice. In other words, even where courts accept ex parte submissions as sometimes necessary, requesters who acquiesce in that path forego later procedural arguments and limit their appellate options. Moreover, courts should be conscious of the cumulative institutional effect: routine reliance on sealed material without meaningful public explanation risks eroding FOIA’s transparency function.

## Practical strategies for requesters

Requesters facing national‑security FOIA denials should approach ex parte procedures with care. If an agency moves to file sealed declarations, requesters who are genuinely concerned about the sensitivity of specifics can still preserve adversarial testing by timely objecting to purely ex parte procedures and proposing alternatives. A requester should consider asking the court for in camera review of representative samples of the withheld records, for the appointment of a special master or amicus with the requisite security clearance, or for the production of a redacted but informative public declaration that outlines factual bases without operational specifics. Where feasible, requesters should press for a particularized showing that the sealed material is both necessary and sufficiently detailed, and they should preserve the record by formally opposing ex parte treatment if they intend to challenge it on appeal.

Requesters should also press on segregability with specificity. Generic assertions that there “must be” segregable material in a voluminous file are unlikely to prevail. Instead, requesters should identify categories or document types that, in their view, ought to be non‑exempt and ask the court to order the agency to justify, category by category, why those materials are intertwined with exempt content.

## Practical strategies for agencies

Agencies that need to rely on sealed submissions can strengthen both their litigation position and public legitimacy by providing as much non‑sensitive information as possible in the public record. A robust public declaration that sets forth the legal framework, the general nature and nexus of the investigation to law‑enforcement duties, and a categorical explanation of withheld material reduces the sense that ex parte filings are a black box. Agencies should use sealed declarations to supply operational detail that would be damaging if publicized, but those sealed filings ought to be detailed and specific so courts can meaningfully evaluate harm and segregability.

Agencies should also be responsive to district court questions designed to limit stale or conclusory assertions. Farahi’s outcome turned partly on the agency’s willingness to update its representations about the pendency of investigations and to explain why segregability was impracticable. Where possible, agencies should anticipate judicial scrutiny by preparing category‑by‑category rationales and by proposing less intrusive alternatives (sample in camera submissions, redacted summaries, disclosure to cleared counsel or special masters) when full ex parte treatment might be contested.

## Downstream implications

Farahi will likely be cited as a practical exemplar for courts and agencies navigating FOIA disputes that touch national security: sealed, ex parte submissions remain a permissible and sometimes necessary part of the toolkit, and a requester’s failure to contest their use can be dispositive of later procedural challenges. But the decision does not erase the underlying judicial responsibilities. Courts remain obligated to demand specificity, to guard against conclusory tropes, and to require meaningful public justification when secrecy is not strictly indispensible. For litigants and courts alike, Farahi is a reminder that process matters: who asks for secrecy, who agrees to it, and how judges scrutinize the sealed record will often determine whether FOIA’s transparency promise is vindicated or sealed shut.

## Conclusion

Farahi reaffirms the D.C. Circuit’s pragmatic accommodation of secrecy in FOIA litigation involving counterterrorism and national security. It also delivers a clear, if subtle, lesson to requesters: procedural concessions matter. The ruling should prompt thoughtful litigation planning on both sides. Requesters should preserve the record and press for alternatives to unilateral ex parte submissions; agencies should provide as much public detail as safely possible while using sealed filings to supply the specifics courts need. The balance between secrecy and accountability is delicate — Farahi demonstrates how courts will mediate that balance, but it also shows that litigants can shape the outcome long before the judge looks behind the curtain.



# No Meaningful Snippets: Segregability’s Limits When Sensitive Data Is Interspersed

## Why this case matters
Foad Farahi v. FBI is a reminder that sheer volume of records does not guarantee a public payday under FOIA. Even where an agency locates 10,750 pages and dozens of CDs, the D.C. Circuit affirmed that “any reasonably segregable portion” need not be produced if the non‑exempt material is so entwined with exempt matter that release would meaningfully risk law‑enforcement interests. The opinion squarely confronts a recurring tension in FOIA litigation: what counts as “information” that should be disclosed versus isolated words or phrases whose release, divorced from context, would be either useless or harmful. For practitioners — both requesters and agencies — the decision signals how courts will treat categorical withholdings and segregability showings in national‑security and counterterrorism files.

## What the court did
The court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI under Exemption 7(A), concluding (1) the contested records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and (2) disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The panel accepted the FBI’s public and ex parte declarations showing an ongoing, broad counterterrorism investigation and explained why disclosure could tip off targets.

On segregability, the court relied on longstanding D.C. Circuit principles — most prominently Mead Data’s point that FOIA’s focus is “information, … not simply words” — to hold that the FBI adequately demonstrated non‑segregability. The court accepted the district court’s view, informed by an ex parte submission, that non‑exempt snippets were randomly interspersed line‑by‑line through documents and therefore not “reasonably segregable.” It also emphasized that where an agency explains, with “reasonable specificity,” why documents cannot be further segmented, a court may rely on such affidavits — even where some material might be redacted — particularly in the national‑security context.

Importantly, the court rejected the requester’s arguments that the passage of time or the terse nature of a follow‑up declaration undermined the government’s showing. It reasoned that the later, concise declaration was an update to the earlier, detailed Hardy declarations and the sealed material, not a standalone justification. Nor did the panel find abuse in the district court’s reliance on ex parte material, noting that recipients had not opposed using sealed declarations and that secrecy is sometimes inherent to resolving FOIA disputes about sensitive investigations.

## Parsing the reasoning: deference, context, and the limits of line‑by‑line redactions
Three doctrinal themes run through the opinion.

First, deference to law‑enforcement agencies — especially the FBI in counterterrorism matters — remains robust. The court reiterated that the FBI’s invocation of Exemption 7 is “entitled to deference,” a posture that matters both for the threshold law‑enforcement nexus and for the assessment of anticipated interference.

Second, the court re‑emphasized Mead Data’s functional test of segregability. It is not enough that certain words or phrases could be quoted in isolation; the question is whether those snippets convey meaningful, releasable information without harming legitimate government interests. When non‑exempt material is “randomly interspersed” and context‑dependent, the court accepted that meaningful disclosure would require revealing damaging context.

Third, the opinion is permissive about the use of ex parte, sealed affidavits to show why disclosure would harm an investigation and why segregability is not feasible. The court accepted sealed detail about the investigation and relied on it to conclude that no protective, targeted redactions would yield usable information. The logic: explaining why a passage cannot be extracted without harm sometimes cannot be done publicly without causing the very harm that disclosure would cause.

## What this means for Vaughn indices and segregability practice
Farahi pushes back on a common assumption that large document sets must produce at least some public, redacted content. Practically:

- Vaughn submissions and declarations matter more than ever. An agency that can combine a public declaration with a detailed, albeit sealed, ex parte explanation of why snippets would be harmful is likely to prevail on segregability in sensitive investigations. The court explicitly endorsed reliance on government affidavits that “show with reasonable specificity” why documents cannot be further segmented.

- Mere quantity of responsive pages is not a safe harbor for requesters. The D.C. Circuit accepted that a small amount of non‑exempt material, if interspersed line‑by‑line and context‑dependent, is not “reasonably segregable.” Claiming that “some words” must be releasable will not suffice.

- Categorical withholding can pass muster if categories are discrete and the requester challenges them timely and with specificity. The court flagged CREW’s requirement that categories be “sufficiently distinct,” but it also held that Farahi forfeited his challenge by not explaining how the FBI’s categories were overbroad. That’s a warning: plaintiffs must identify specific defects in categories or risk losing the argument.

- Ex parte submissions are likely to remain an accepted — though controversial — tool in national‑security FOIA litigation. The court recognized the procedural cost to requesters but concluded the cost is sometimes unavoidable to avoid exposing sensitive information in the litigation record.

## Practical takeaways for requesters (and what to do instead)
Farahi suggests several tactical responses for FOIA requesters facing broad withholdings in sensitive cases:

- Narrow your request up front. Limit by date range, document type, or topic headings to increase the chances that an agency will locate discrete document types that can be meaningfully redacted.

- Insist on a Vaughn index (or equivalent) that ties claimed exemptions to specific entries. If the agency offers only categorical descriptions, press it to explain why a representative sample of each category cannot yield non‑harmful redactions.

- Seek a sample‑based in camera review. When ex parte submissions will be used, ask the court to review a small number of representative pages in camera so the judge can test the agency’s segregability claims without creating a public record.

- Move to compel targeted disclosures. Instead of demanding production of thousands of pages, litigate for a few carefully chosen documents that, if released redacted, would serve the requester’s purposes and test the agency’s segregability rationale.

- Be precise and timely in challenging categories. If you think the agency’s categories are overbroad, explain why with examples; otherwise, courts may treat the challenge as forfeited.

## Doctrinal and policy implications
The decision sits comfortably within established D.C. Circuit precedent (Mead Data, Juarez, CREW, and recent decisions like Perioperative Services) but amplifies a consequential point: FOIA’s promise of transparency is limited when the information is context‑laden and the government persuasively shows that disclosure would undermine active investigations. That posture favors agencies in national‑security cases and could chill public access to even small, potentially informative disclosures.

At the same time, the opinion leaves open guardrails. The court repeatedly anchored its decision in the existence of detailed, specific (if partly sealed) factual showings. That suggests a path for judges to police overbroad secrecy: require meaningful, document‑level explanations (or in camera sampling) and reject blanket rhetoric that non‑exempt words are useless. The tension remains between procedural fairness — how a requester can meaningfully contest sealed affidavits — and the practical necessity of protecting ongoing investigations. We should expect more FOIA plaintiffs to litigate aggressively for sampling, targeted in camera review, and tighter Vaughn indices. We should also expect agencies to refine their segregability explanations to explain why context makes isolated disclosure harmful and why redaction would not suffice.

## Conclusion
Farahi is a useful reminder: volume alone does not compel release, and FOIA’s “reasonably segregable” requirement is about usable information, not the mechanical extraction of words. For requesters, the ruling underscores the importance of precision, sampling strategies, and early motions to secure meaningful judicial testing of agency claims. For agencies, it affirms that careful, specific segregability declarations — even when part of the record must remain sealed — can carry the day in sensitive law‑enforcement contexts. The decision doesn’t rewrite the law; it simply applies the circuit’s existing segregability doctrine in a fact pattern where context and danger to an ongoing counterterrorism investigation justified withholding even a mountain of pages.



# What Counts as ‘Enforcement Proceedings’ Under 7(A)? Elasticity and Deference in Counterterrorism Files

## Why Farahi matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation (No. 23‑5243, Sept. 30, 2025) is an instructive gloss on a familiar tension in FOIA Exemption 7(A): how much specificity must the government provide to show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings,” and how long can an agency rely on that showing in sprawling national‑security or counterterrorism files? Farahi confirms that investigations—criminal or otherwise—that continue to gather evidence count as “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings for Exemption 7(A) purposes, and that courts will give substantial deference to FBI attestations, especially when the agency supplements public declarations with sealed, ex parte detail and when the public record (for example, parallel immigration adjudications) corroborates the asserted law‑enforcement concern. At the same time, the opinion underscores limits: courts must scrutinize stale or conclusory assertions and can require updated showings before allowing indefinite withholding.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for the FBI. The agency had located more than ten thousand pages responsive to Farahi’s FOIA request and withheld most of them under Exemption 7(A). The FBI supported its position with a public Hardy declaration (and a more detailed sealed, ex parte Hardy declaration) explaining that the records were compiled in counterterrorism investigations and that disclosure could tip off targets—an explanation rooted in classic interference concerns recognized in CNSS and Juarez. After the district court requested updated assurances about pendency, the FBI supplied a short public Bender declaration confirming that investigations “remain ongoing,” and a supplemental sealed submission addressing segregability. The district court credited the combined public and ex parte record and found the withheld material protected and not reasonably segregable; the D.C. Circuit agreed.

Two strands run through the court’s analysis. First, because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency and the records relate to national security, a “fair measure of deference” applies; that deference is especially weighty where ex parte submissions provide nonpublic factual detail. Second, CREW’s admonition that vague assertions of ongoing investigations may be insufficient remains in place, but CREW does not require public, line‑by‑line exposition where the government has supplied credible public attestations supplemented by sealed detail and where broader context corroborates a continuing investigative interest. The court treated Bender’s terse confirmation not as a standalone foundation but as an update layered on the earlier, detailed Hardy declarations and the public immigration record that described alleged ties between Farahi and known terrorists.

On segregability, the court relied on settled law (Mead and its progeny) that allows the government to withhold whole records when nonexempt material is so inextricably intertwined with exempt content that disclosure would reveal sensitive investigative information. The district court’s sealed explanation satisfied that showing, and the appellate court accepted its use of ex parte material for that purpose.

## Interpreting elasticity and restraint

Farahi illustrates the D.C. Circuit’s posture: elasticity in what counts as “enforcement proceedings,” coupled with procedural safeguards to prevent rubber‑stamping. The elasticity comes in three related forms. First, the phrase “pending or reasonably anticipated” admits ongoing criminal investigations that are still in the evidence‑gathering phase, not only formal charging decisions. Second, the court reaffirms that an agency’s attestation of “existence” of an investigation can be sufficient, particularly where the agency is the FBI and the subject matter implicates national security. Third, sealed ex parte submissions may fill the gap between what the public declaration says and what the government reasonably cannot disclose on the public docket.

But this elasticity is not toothless. CREW remains the counterweight: courts should (and here did) demand more than a stale, conclusory claim that an investigation is ongoing if sufficient time has passed or if the public record raises doubt. The district court’s decision to require an updated declaration before ruling on summary judgment — and the appellate court’s willingness to treat the latest short public declaration as an update to a previously detailed record — shows how courts can demand contemporaneous justification without forcing the government to litigate national‑security facts on the public record.

## Risks and downstream effects

The principal risk is predictable: counterterrorism investigations are often sprawling, long‑running, and document‑heavy, and treating an agency attestation plus sealed detail as sufficient risks de facto indefinite withholding of voluminous records. Unchecked, that would erode FOIA’s disclosure imperative. Farahi mitigates, but does not eliminate, that risk: courts can and will ask for updated attestations and can require particularized explanations when the passage of time or the record makes a boilerplate assertion implausible.

A second downstream effect is procedural: ex parte declarations will continue to be a routine tool in national‑security FOIA litigation. Farahi recognizes the practical reality that some information cannot safely enter the public docket, and it leans on precedents that accept sealed affidavits in the segregability and pendency analysis. Practically, then, the contest between declarant detail and FOIA requester access will increasingly be fought in chambers, not on the public record — a consequence that favors agencies but also gives judges a central gatekeeping role.

Third, the opinion highlights the evidentiary power of parallel public proceedings. The court relied on allegations in Farahi’s immigration record and public reporting of associated convictions to corroborate the FBI’s sealed assertions. Requesters can, and likely will, develop public‑record strategies to undercut agency claims by showing that the context does not plausibly support an ongoing investigation. Conversely, agencies will increasingly cite related litigation, indictments, or administrative proceedings as corroborative support.

## Practical pointers: what agencies and requesters should do

For agencies: when asserting Exemption 7(A), link the withheld material to identifiable investigative objectives and explain concretely how disclosure would interfere with those objectives. If you must rely on sealed ex parte submissions, make clear to the court how the sealed information supplements the public declaration and provide a compact chronology: what the initial investigation looked like, what evidence is still being gathered, and why disclosure now would prejudice future enforcement. Update declarations when significant time passes and when courts ask for contemporaneous confirmation.

For requesters: press for specificity and temporal limitation. Do not accept a single, conclusory “investigation ongoing” statement; ask the court to require an updated declaration or in camera review that addresses whether the investigation remains in an evidence‑gathering posture. Challenge overbroad categorical descriptions of records under the CREW/Gallant line by demanding that categories be “sufficiently distinct” and by identifying likely segregable content. Where possible, marshal public evidence (administrative filings, press reports, indictments or convictions of associates) to test or undercut the government’s narrative. And when agencies use ex parte submissions, push the court to explain, at least in camera, why no segregable information can be disclosed so that appellate review is feasible.

## Bottom line

Farahi reinforces the D.C. Circuit’s pragmatic balance: substantial deference to agency attestations in national‑security contexts, but not an unbounded license to lock up records forever. The opinion accepts that an ongoing investigation need not be a formal indictment to trigger Exemption 7(A), and it validates the use of sealed, ex parte detail to substantiate otherwise terse public assertions. At the same time, the decision preserves judicial tools — requiring updated declarations, insisting on a rational nexus to law‑enforcement duties, and demanding meaningful segregability explanations — that can curb indefinite withholding when the government’s claims become stale or implausible. For FOIA litigants on both sides, the practical takeaway is familiar: specificity, contemporaneity, and a credible link between documents and concrete investigative objectives are the currency that will buy (or challenge) Exemption 7(A) protection.
