Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Under FOIA Exemption 7(A), do "enforcement proceedings" include ongoing criminal investigations, or must there be a filed case?
Question:
The panel says "ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings'" and that the government may show such proceedings by attesting to their "existence" (citing CREW and Juarez). I'm confused because the statute says disclosure that could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. Does an open-ended counterterrorism investigation, without charges filed, qualify? How "concrete" must a prospective proceeding be to satisfy 7(A), and what distinguishes a general intelligence/information-gathering operation from a qualifying enforcement proceeding?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Under Exemption 7(A) the courts treat an ongoing criminal investigation as an “enforcement proceeding” even if no charges have yet been filed, so long as the investigation is “pending or reasonably anticipated” and is the kind of investigation that is gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case. The government can meet its burden by attesting to the existence of such an investigation, but a purely vague or stale assertion may not suffice—there must be enough factual context to show a concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding and a risk that disclosure would interfere with it.

What the Farahi opinion says (and how to apply it)
- The statutory test: Exemption 7(A) covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” (Farahi v. FBI, slip op. § I–III (citing 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A) and CREW).)

- Ongoing investigations are proceedings. The court explicitly states that “ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’” for 7(A) purposes (Farahi § IV.B, citing CREW). Juarez is quoted to the same effect: “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.” (Farahi § IV.B (quoting Juarez).)

- How concrete must the prospective proceeding be? The governing standard is that proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” A simple attestation to the investigation’s “existence” can suffice (CREW), but CREW also warns that a “vague” assertion that an investigation is ongoing may not be enough. Farahi applies that balance: the court accepted an initial public declaration attesting the investigation was ongoing plus an ex parte declaration supplying non‑public details, and it treated a later, terse confirming declaration as an update rather than a standalone basis. In short: the government needs to show more than a conclusory, stale claim—enough factual context to permit the court to conclude a concrete, prospective enforcement proceeding exists and that disclosure would interfere with it. (Farahi § IV.B.)

- What distinguishes general intelligence/information‑gathering from a qualifying enforcement proceeding? Farahi reiterates the familiar doctrinal requirement that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes” — courts look for a rational “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties” and a connection between the subject and a possible security risk or violation of federal law (citing Campbell and Pratt). If an operation is purely general intelligence collection with no tie to investigating violations of law or to a possible prosecution, it is less likely to qualify. By contrast, an investigation that is gathering evidence of possible criminal activity (even if charges are not yet filed) and that would be jeopardized by disclosure falls within Exemption 7(A). (Farahi § IV.A & IV.B, citing Campbell, Pratt, Juarez.)

Practical takeaways for FOIA practice or litigation
- An open‑ended counterterrorism investigation can qualify under 7(A) without a filed indictment if the agency shows the investigation is ongoing and is gathering evidence for a possible criminal case. (Farahi § IV.B; Juarez quoted.)

- The government’s safest course is a factual declaration that (a) the investigation is ongoing, (b) it is aimed at potential violations of federal law (nexus to law‑enforcement duties), and (c) disclosure would risk tipping off targets or otherwise frustrate evidence collection. Ex parte declarations may be used to provide the needed specifics without revealing the very information at issue. (Farahi §§ III–IV.)

- Plaintiffs can (and the court in Farahi did) press the government for updated declarations if significant time has passed, and they can argue that conclusory, stale, or overly broad attestations are insufficient under CREW. But where the public declaration plus nonpublic detail together show a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding,” courts in this circuit will sustain Exemption 7(A). (Farahi § IV.B.)

Bottom line
You do not need an already‑filed criminal case for Exemption 7(A) to apply. What matters is that there be a pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceeding—i.e., an investigation actively gathering evidence for potential prosecution—and enough factual showing (often by declaration, public and/or ex parte) to demonstrate that disclosure would meaningfully risk interference. Farahi confirms that posture and applies the CREW/Juarez line: attestations can suffice, but they must be more than vague or stale.

### How does de novo review square with the court’s deference to the FBI’s national-security judgments in FOIA cases?
Question:
The opinion says summary judgment is reviewed de novo and the government bears the burden, but also that the FBI’s invocation of Exemption 7 gets deference, especially where national security is implicated (citing Campbell and CNSS). What exactly is being reviewed de novo versus what gets deference? Is the court deferring to predictive judgments about harm while independently verifying the legal standards are met? How far does that deference go when key details are in ex parte declarations?
Answer:
Short answer
The court is doing two things at once. It reviews the summary-judgment ruling de novo and checks, as a legal matter, whether the FOIA exemption’s statutory elements are met. At the same time it gives the FBI “a fair measure of deference” on the agency’s factual and predictive judgments—especially those implicating national security—so long as the agency supports those judgments with sufficiently specific declarations. In Foad Farahi the court independently decided (de novo) that Exemption 7(A)’s legal requirements were satisfied, but it relied on (and deferred to) the FBI’s factual/predictive showings in the public and ex parte declarations to reach that legal conclusion.

What “de novo” covers
- Procedural posture: As the opinion states, “In FOIA cases, as in all others, we review a grant of summary judgment de novo.” (Op. § III.)
- Legal inquiry: That means the court independently determines whether, as a matter of law, the exemption applies—i.e., whether the withheld records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” (Exemption 7(A)) and whether the agency satisfied the segregability requirement. The government nevertheless bears the burden of proving those elements. (Op. § III.)

What gets deference (and why)
- Agency factual and predictive judgments: The opinion explains that “because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference,” and that such deference is “particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.” (Op. § III.) That is deference to the FBI’s factual characterizations and its predictive judgments about harm (e.g., whether disclosure would tip off targets or interfere with ongoing investigations).
- The caveat: Deference is not a rubber stamp. The court still requires the agency to back its claims with competent declarations that establish a rational nexus to law‑enforcement duties and a factual foundation for the anticipated harm. The court will evaluate whether the agency’s affidavits “show with reasonable specificity” why withheld material fits the exemption and why non‑exempt material is not reasonably segregable. (Op. §§ III–IV; citing Juarez/Mead.)

How that played out in this opinion
- Multiple layers of evidence: The court credited the public Hardy declaration and an ex parte Hardy declaration that supplied “considerable detail,” and treated Bender’s later, terse declaration as an update rather than a standalone justification. The court refused to accept a lone conclusory statement (it warned that a “vague” attestation may not suffice), but it accepted the agency’s combined public and sealed submissions as meeting the government’s burden. (Op. § IV.B.)
- Ex parte declarations: The opinion confirms courts may rely on ex parte/under‑seal declarations when necessary and that such reliance is “inherent” where revealing the agency’s reasoning would itself disclose the sensitive material. The court nonetheless reviewed those sealed submissions to determine adequacy, and it noted that the plaintiff’s failure to oppose the use of ex parte material limited his ability to challenge reliance on it. (Op. § V.)

Putting it together — rule of thumb
- The court independently asks: do the statutory elements of the exemption exist? (de novo)
- For the factual predicates (existence/continued pendency of an investigation, the nature/scope of the risk from disclosure, why segregation is not feasible), the court affords deference to the FBI’s expertise and predictive judgments—but only if those judgments are supported by sufficiently specific, credible declarations (public and/or ex parte). Vague or unsupported assertions will not carry the day.

This opinion: de novo legal review + substantial but qualified deference to agency factual/predictive judgments, with the court policing adequacy and specificity of the agency’s evidence (including sealed declarations) before concluding the exemption applies.

### Is the opinion’s citation to 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B) a typo when discussing Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In Section IV.B, the court quotes the interference-with-proceedings standard but cites § 552(b)(7)(B). My understanding is that (b)(7)(A) is the interference-with-enforcement-proceedings exemption, while (b)(7)(B) concerns fair trial rights. Is this just a scrivener’s error, and does it have any legal consequence for the holding?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — that citation in IV.B is a scrivener’s error, and it has no legal effect on the panel’s holding.

Why: the opinion consistently frames the dispute under Exemption 7(A) and applies the interference-with-enforcement-proceedings standard tied to § 552(b)(7)(A). For example, the Court’s statement of the statute and the Exemption 7(A) standard at the start of Part I cites § 552(b)(7)(A) (“Exemption 7(A) covers records … Id. § 552(b)(7)(A).”), and the framing of the burden in Part III likewise refers to § 552(b)(7)(A) (“…must show … see id. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW …”). The passage in IV.B that says “Exemption 7(A) also requires …” but cites § 552(b)(7)(B) is inconsistent with those prior citations and with the remainder of the analysis in IV.B, which repeatedly invokes the Exemption 7(A) interference standard and the relevant precedent (CREW, Juarez, CNSS).

Legal consequence (or lack of one): the mis‑citation is harmless. The Court’s text, precedent citations, and ultimate disposition all make clear the panel meant Exemption 7(A) and applied the interference-with-enforcement-proceedings standard tied to § 552(b)(7)(A). Because the substantive analysis and holding are grounded in Exemption 7(A) throughout the opinion, the typographical miscitation to (B) does not alter the rule applied or the outcome.

### When do FOIA declarations become too "stale," and what obligates an agency to update them?
Question:
The district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice due to the passage of time and required an updated assessment on whether proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated. Are there established D.C. Circuit benchmarks for when agency declarations become stale in 7(A) cases? What factors (e.g., years elapsed, nature of investigation, prior confirmations) trigger a duty to supplement, and is that duty grounded in FOIA, summary-judgment standards, or the court’s inherent authority?
Answer:
Short answer
There is no fixed D.C. Circuit “staleness clock.” Courts assess staleness contextually. Where a FOIA exemption (here Exemption 7(A)) depends on the existence of ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, a district court may require the agency to refresh its factual showing before granting summary judgment if enough time has passed or circumstances could have changed. That requirement flows from (1) the agency’s burden under FOIA to show the exemption applies at the time of adjudication, and (2) the court’s role in testing that showing at summary judgment — not from a freestanding statutory “update” clause in FOIA. See Farahi v. FBI (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025).

What the Farahi opinion says (and why it matters)
- The district court originally denied the FBI’s motion for summary judgment “without prejudice” because of the “significant passage of time” and ordered an updated assessment about whether proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated. The court later accepted the FBI’s updated declarations and granted summary judgment. Farahi, slip op. § II–III (describing district court action and later Bender declaration).
- The D.C. Circuit approved that process: an agency must prove Exemption 7(A) (that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated”), and if the agency’s proof becomes out-of-date, the district court may ask for an update rather than resolve the motion on stale facts. The court emphasized that an agency may show pendency simply by attesting to the existence of an investigation, but vague or conclusory assertions may not suffice (citing CREW). Farahi, slip op. §§ III–IV(B).

Factors courts look at (drawn from Farahi)
- Passage of time: The district court in Farahi ordered a refresh because of “significant passage of time.” The opinion does not set a numerical benchmark — time is evaluated in context.
- Specificity of earlier declarations: Detailed, non-conclusory initial affidavits/ex parte declarations make staleness less problematic; terse or “conclusory” updates standing alone may be inadequate. Farahi contrasted the detailed (public + ex parte) Hardy declarations with the terser Bender declaration, treating Bender as a confirmatory update rather than a standalone showing. Farahi, slip op. §§ II–IV(B).
- Nature and breadth of the investigation: Broad, ongoing national-security or counterterrorism investigations are inherently more likely to remain “ongoing” over long periods; that makes an agency’s representation that the investigation continues more plausible. Farahi relied on the breadth and sensitivity of the underlying investigation in upholding the update. Farahi, slip op. § IV(B).
- Prior court findings or posture: If a court previously found that the agency “seemingly” met its burden, a narrow confirmatory update may be sufficient; if the prior showing was weak, a fuller update will be needed. Farahi explains that the district court’s earlier “seemingly” conclusion meant Bender’s statement could be read as a permissible update rather than a fresh, standalone proof. Farahi, slip op. § IV(B).
- Whether the agency used ex parte material: Where the agency supplied classified or sensitive ex parte material earlier, a public confirmatory statement may suffice because the court already saw the detailed basis in camera. Farahi upheld reliance on ex parte submissions and explained that courts can rely on them without putting details on the public record. Farahi, slip op. §§ II, V.

What legally obligates an agency to update?
- FOIA itself sets the substantive standard (Exemption 7(A) — proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated”) and requires the agency to segregate and release nonexempt material. Farahi, slip op. § I, V.
- The practical obligation to update declarations before summary judgment arises from the court’s duty, in resolving a summary-judgment motion, to ensure the agency satisfies its FOIA burden at the time of adjudication. In short: FOIA defines what must be shown; summary-judgment practice and the court’s gatekeeping function require the factual showing to be current. Farahi, slip op. §§ III–IV(B).
- The opinion treats the district court’s order for updated declarations as an appropriate exercise of those judicial responsibilities; it does not treat the duty to update as coming from a separate statutory command beyond FOIA’s exemption standard and segregability requirement. Farahi, slip op. §§ II, III, V.

Practical takeaways for litigants (how courts will apply these principles)
- Agencies: update declarations when substantial time has passed since the earlier affidavit, when circumstances may have changed, or when the earlier showing was thin or conclusory. If you relied on classified/ex parte material previously, remind the court that it reviewed those materials and provide a focused public confirmation if appropriate (as the FBI did through Bender).
- Requesters: press for an update when a long interval separates the declaration and the court’s decision, and challenge conclusory or stand‑alone assertions of continued pendency (invoke CREW’s warning about vagueness, as Farahi did).
- Expect no bright-line rule: courts weigh time, specificity, the nature of the investigation, and what the court has already seen in camera.

Bottom line
Farahi confirms that staleness is a contextual, court-driven concern: FOIA supplies the substantive elements the government must prove; summary‑judgment procedure and the court’s obligation to assess that proof at the time of decision create the practical duty to refresh declarations when the record is no longer current. There is no fixed number of years that automatically makes a declaration “stale”; instead judges balance passage of time, the initial sufficiency of the agency’s proof (including any ex parte evidence), and the character of the underlying investigation.

### Can a court properly find no reasonably segregable material based solely on ex parte submissions?
Question:
The district court relied on Bender’s supplemental ex parte declaration to conclude there was no reasonably segregable information, and the D.C. Circuit approved that approach (citing Perioperative Services). What must an agency show, even ex parte, to meet its segregability burden? Is there a minimum level of public explanation (e.g., a Vaughn index or categorical description) required before a court can rely entirely on sealed specifics? How do courts ensure that "meaningful" nonexempt information isn’t overlooked when the adversary can’t test the assertions?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — a court can accept an agency’s claim that nothing “reasonably segregable” exists based on ex parte (sealed, in‑camera) submissions, but only if those submissions (together with whatever public record exists) satisfy the agency’s statutory and judicially imposed burden. Concretely, even ex parte the agency must show with reasonable specificity why non‑exempt material cannot be separated and released; courts may rely on sealed specifics only after reviewing them in camera and after ensuring the public record supplies enough information (or categories) for meaningful judicial review. Farahi illustrates these principles.

What the law requires (and how Farahi applied it)
- Statutory baseline. FOIA requires agencies to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); § 552(b). That duty does not vanish simply because most of a file is exempt.

- The legal standard for agency proof. Courts may rely on government affidavits that “show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir.). Where nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material — e.g., line‑by‑line interspersion — disclosure may not be “reasonable.” Mead Data, 566 F.2d at 260–61; cited and applied in Farahi.

- What “reasonable specificity” means in practice. Farahi shows the practical approach:
  - The FBI supplied both public and sealed declarations. Public material (Hardy) established the law‑enforcement context and the ongoing investigation; the ex parte materials (Hardy and Bender sealed submissions) supplied detailed factual bases the court could not put on the public record. The district court found that Bender’s sealed submission gave a “detailed justification” for non‑segregability, and the D.C. Circuit accepted that, noting a court may rely on sealed affidavits that meet the Juarez standard. Farahi, slip op. §§ III, V; J.A. 139.
  - The court stressed context: the sealed materials were not the only proof — they updated and fleshed out the public record. The court refused to treat a terse public update in isolation because the sealed record provided the necessary detail.

Is a Vaughn index or public categorical description always required?
- No bright‑line rule in Farahi. The opinion does not hold that a Vaughn index is always required before a court may rely on sealed specifics. Rather:
  - Agencies must provide categories that are “sufficiently distinct” when they withhold by category so a court can assess the claimed exemptions (Gallant; CREW cited in Farahi § IV.C). If a requester challenges category definitions, the agency needs to show they’re not overbroad.
  - But if public declarations plus sealed ex parte submissions (reviewed by the court in camera) supply the necessary “reasonably specific” explanation, the court may rely on them without disclosing the sealed detail on the public docket. That is what happened in Farahi: the public declarations established the framework and the sealed declarations supplied the specifics the court needed to conclude nothing meaningful was reasonably segregable.

How courts protect against overlooking meaningful nonexempt information when the adversary can’t test assertions
- In‑camera review and judicial scrutiny. The main safeguard is that the judge — not opposing counsel — reviews the sealed material and must be satisfied that the agency’s explanation meets the Juarez standard. Farahi explicitly recognizes that reliance on ex parte submissions “handicap[s] FOIA plaintiffs,” but accepts that handicap where in‑camera review is the only practical way to adjudicate the dispute without disclosing the very material the agency claims must remain secret (citing Perioperative Servs.). Farahi § V.
- Requirement of reasonable specificity and categorical distinctness. Courts will not accept bare, conclusory assertions. They require affidavits/declarations that explain why segregation would be harmful or impracticable (Juarez, Mead), and they expect categories to be sufficiently particular for meaningful review (Gallant/CREW). If the public record is too skeletal and the sealed material does not supply the needed specifics on in‑camera review, the court should deny the agency’s claim.
- Adversarial posture matters. If a requester objects to ex parte procedures or challenges an agency’s segregability showing, that will pressure courts to require more public detail or to test the sealed showing more rigorously. In Farahi the plaintiff did not oppose the ex parte filings, which the panel noted as limiting his ability to complain about the court’s reliance on them.

Takeaway for practitioners / students
- Agency side: to sustain a no‑segregability finding you need a declaration that (1) explains how exempt and nonexempt material are intermingled or why segregation would meaningfully harm interests protected by the exemption, and (2) supplies sufficient factual detail (even if submitted ex parte) so a judge can, on in‑camera review, conclude the Juarez standard is met.
- Requester side: challenge conclusory public declarations, insist on reasonably distinct categories when the agency withholds by category, and object to ex parte proceedings if you believe the sealed process forecloses meaningful adversarial testing; if you do not object, appellate review will be harder (see Farahi’s forfeiture point).
- Courts: may accept sealed submissions after in‑camera review, but must ensure the agency’s showing is reasonably specific and that categories are not so broad as to preclude meaningful review.

In short: ex parte submissions can suffice, but only if the judge — after reviewing the sealed material and the public record — is satisfied that the agency has explained with reasonable specificity why no meaningful, nonexempt information can be released. Farahi applies Juarez/Mead and Perioperative to reach that result.

### Did the FOIA Improvement Act’s foreseeable-harm requirement add anything beyond Exemption 7(A)’s own harm standard here?
Question:
FOIA’s 2016 amendments require agencies to show reasonably foreseeable harm from disclosure unless disclosure is prohibited by law. Exemption 7(A) already asks whether release "could reasonably be expected to interfere" with enforcement proceedings. Does the D.C. Circuit treat the foreseeable-harm requirement as coextensive with the 7(A) standard, or as an additional independent showing? I didn’t see a separate foreseeable-harm analysis—was it subsumed, or is there circuit precedent that a proper 7(A) showing satisfies the FOIA Improvement Act by itself?
Answer:
Short answer: In Farahi the D.C. Circuit treated the FOIA Improvement Act’s “reasonably foreseeable harm” language as satisfied by the traditional Exemption 7(A) showing — the court did not perform a separate, independent “foreseeable-harm” inquiry. It framed and resolved the case under Exemption 7(A)’s own standards (that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere” with “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings) and accepted the FBI’s attestations as doing the work the statute requires.

Explanation and support from the opinion
- The opinion opens by noting the 2016 FOIA amendments and their “reasonably foreseeable harm” requirement, but the substantive analysis that follows is squarely cast in Exemption 7(A) terms: the government had to show the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated.” (Section III & IV (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A)).)

- In analyzing harm the court relied on the familiar 7(A) framework and precedent (CREW, Juarez): ongoing investigations count as “proceedings,” the government may establish pendency by attestation of their “existence,” and disclosure can harm an investigation by “tipping off the targets.” (See IV.B; citing CREW 746 F.3d at 1096–99 and Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59; opinion cites the “tipping off” justification and accepts the Hardy/Bender declarations.)

- The court never separates the FOIA Improvement Act’s foreseeability phrase into an independent step to be analyzed apart from Exemption 7(A). It credited the FBI’s declarations (public and ex parte) that the investigation remained ongoing and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere, and thus concluded the Exemption 7(A) showing satisfied the statutory requirement. (See IV.B–C and the court’s discussion of the Hardy and Bender declarations.)

Takeaway for your question
- Farahi shows the D.C. Circuit treating a proper Exemption 7(A) showing — concrete, attested ongoing investigations and an explanation of how disclosure would interfere (e.g., by tipping off targets) — as doing the work of the FOIA Improvement Act’s “reasonably foreseeable harm” requirement. In other words, in this opinion the foreseeability requirement was effectively subsumed into the court’s traditional 7(A) analysis rather than applied as a distinct additional hurdle.

### What suffices to show records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes," and may the court rely on allegations from separate immigration proceedings?
Question:
The court invokes Pratt/Campbell’s "rational nexus" test and says broader context—including allegations presented in Farahi’s immigration case (e.g., ties to designated terrorist organizations and individuals)—confirms the law-enforcement purpose. Is it proper for the court to consider those allegations from another proceeding to bolster the nexus, especially when the detailed basis is in ex parte declarations? How specific must the public record be about a subject’s connection to a possible violation to meet Pratt without revealing sensitive details?
Answer:
Short answer
- To show records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” under Exemption 7(A), the agency must establish the Pratt/Campbell “rational nexus” — i.e., show a rational link between the records (or subject) and the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and a connection between the individual/incident and a possible security risk or violation. The FBI’s reasonably specific public declarations can carry that burden, and the court may consider corroborating public materials from other proceedings (e.g., immigration filings) as part of the broader context. Farahi shows how that works: the court relied on the public Hardy declaration, a sealed ex parte Hardy declaration, and public allegations in Farahi’s immigration case to conclude the nexus was satisfied. (See J.A. 42; Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2.)

What the doctrine requires (Pratt / Campbell / CREW)
- Pratt/Campbell requires a “rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,’” and a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d at 32, quoting Pratt, 673 F.2d at 420–21 (quoted in the opinion at § IV.A). The D.C. Circuit gives the FBI “a fair measure of deference,” particularly where national security is implicated. Foad Farahi v. FBI, slip op. § IV.A.

What suffices in practice
- Reasonably specific agency declarations: a public declaration that explains the investigative subject and purpose (the Hardy public decl.) plus more detailed ex parte material to the court sufficed here. The court said the public declaration attested the records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and the sealed declaration supplied “considerable detail.” J.A. 42; § IV.A.
- Corroborating public records from separate proceedings: the court may rely on public allegations in other proceedings as part of the “broader context.” Farahi’s own immigration record (which contained allegations that he had ties to designated terrorist organizations, links to known/convicted terrorists, and support for terrorist‑financing charities) was used by the court to confirm the FBI’s characterization of the file. The opinion explicitly relied on those immigration allegations as corroborative context. Foad Farahi, 2024 WL 483601, at *1–2; slip op. § IV.A and § IV.B.
- Ex parte submissions to the judge: where disclosure of investigative detail would itself reveal sensitive information, the agency may provide sealed ex parte declarations to the judge. The court accepted that practice here and treated the sealed material as supplying the additional necessary detail. Slip op. §§ II–IV.

Limits and cautions
- Vague, conclusory assertions alone are insufficient. The court reiterated CREW’s warning that a “vague” assertion investigations “remain ongoing” may not suffice. The Bender update in this case was terse, but the court found it acceptable only because it supplemented earlier, more detailed declarations (public and sealed) and because the district court had earlier found the agency had “seemingly” met its burden. Slip op. § IV.B (citing CREW, 746 F.3d at 1098–99).
- The public record need not (and often should not) disclose the very details that would compromise an investigation. Pratt/Campbell requires a connection to a possible violation, not a full public airing of investigative tactics or sources. The opinion shows the court will accept a mix: public attestations that identify the investigative subject and the nature of the suspected wrongdoing (here, alleged ties to FTOs and to known terrorists) plus sealed detail for the court where necessary. Slip op. §§ IV.A–B; V.
- Segregability: even when some words/phrases might be nonexempt, the agency can withhold in full if nonexempt material is “interspersed line‑by‑line” so that meaningful, nonharmful disclosure is not “reasonably segregable.” Mead, 566 F.2d at 261 (quoted in slip op. § V).

Practical takeaway for a litigant
- If the agency’s public declarations give a clear statement of the investigative subject and the type of suspected wrongdoing, and the court can see corroborating public facts (e.g., allegations in another proceeding), that will often satisfy the Pratt/Campbell nexus without forcing disclosure of sensitive details. If the public declaration is short or conclusory, the agency needs to supply more detail (publicly or ex parte to the court) to avoid CREW’s “vague” problem. The Farahi opinion illustrates both the permissible use of corroborating public allegations from other proceedings and the court’s willingness to consider sealed ex parte material when necessary to protect ongoing investigations.

### Can Exemption 7(A) justify withholding for decades, and what recourse does a requester have as time passes?
Question:
This investigation seems to date back many years, and the court accepts that it remains ongoing. Is there any temporal limit on 7(A) withholdings, or can they effectively persist indefinitely if an investigation never formally closes? If a requester suspects the "ongoing" status is outdated, is the remedy just to refile a new FOIA request later, or can courts require periodic updates or in camera review to test the continued applicability of 7(A)?
Answer:
Short answer
- There is no fixed chronological cut‑off in Exemption 7(A). The exemption survives so long as the agency shows the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” (Farahi, citing 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A) and CREW.)
- But courts will not simply accept a stale, conclusory “ongoing investigation” claim. If substantial time has passed a court can (and in Farahi did) require the agency to update its factual showing, permit in camera/ex parte submissions, scrutinize segregability, and deny summary judgment until the agency carries its burden.

What the Farahi opinion teaches about time and remedies
1) No per se temporal limit — functional test controls.
- The D.C. Circuit reaffirmed that Exemption 7(A) depends on whether proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” not on an absolute time limit. So withholding can persist for many years if the agency demonstrates a continuing investigation that would be harmed by disclosure (see Farahi §§ II–IV.B; quoting CREW and Juarez).

2) Courts may demand updated proof when time has passed.
- Farahi shows a concrete mechanism: the district court, noting the “significant passage of time,” ordered the FBI to update its declarations about whether proceedings remained pending. The court later credited the updated (public + ex parte) declarations and granted summary judgment. That outcome illustrates that courts will ask for fresh factual attestation rather than rubber‑stamp old declarations (see Farahi § II, J.A. references).

3) Conclusory assertions can fail; context matters.
- The panel reiterated CREW’s point that a vague “investigation remains ongoing” may be insufficient. Farahi held the later terse affidavit (Bender) was acceptable only because it was read in context of earlier, detailed public and ex parte Hardy declarations and the district court’s prior finding that the FBI “seemingly” met its burden (Farahi § IV.B). So a lone, conclusory update risks rejection.

4) Ex parte/in camera submissions and segregability.
- The opinion affirms that courts may accept ex parte, sealed declarations to avoid revealing the very information at issue, and may rely on them in determining both pendency and segregability (Farahi § V). At the same time, agencies must take “reasonable steps” to segregate and disclose nonexempt information; where nonexempt material is interspersed line‑by‑line it may be found not “reasonably segregable” (Farahi § V; Mead discussion).

Practical recourse for a requester (drawn from Farahi)
- If you suspect the “ongoing” status is stale, litigate the denial rather than simply refiling. Ask the court to: (a) order an updated agency declaration addressing whether proceedings remain pending or reasonably anticipated (as the district court did), (b) require the agency to justify any categorical withholdings with sufficiently distinct categories, and (c) conduct or order in camera/ex parte review if necessary to test the agency’s classified or sensitive factual showing. Farahi shows courts will use these tools.
- Emphasize CREW: challenge conclusory or boilerplate language and point out the passage of time as a basis for a court to demand more detail.
- Press segregability: argue the agency failed to take “reasonable steps” to release nonexempt portions; if the agency relies on the interspersion rationale, be prepared to counter with concrete examples or argument showing that meaningful information could be released.

Bottom line
Exemption 7(A) can lawfully justify long‑running withholdings if the agency satisfies the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard. But Farahi confirms courts will not rely on stale or conclusory assertions without updated, particularized attestations (including, where appropriate, sealed/ex parte submissions) and will enforce the segregability requirement. Litigation — seeking updated declarations, in camera review, and testing segregability — is the meaningful way to challenge a withholding that you believe has outlived any legitimate “ongoing” inquiry.
