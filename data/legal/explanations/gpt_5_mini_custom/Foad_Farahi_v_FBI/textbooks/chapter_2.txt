Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Governing FOIA Framework and Standards

This chapter explains the statutory framework and the doctrinal standards that the court applied in Farahi v. FBI, No. 23-5243. It focuses on the burdens the government must carry to invoke FOIA Exemption 7(A), the role and required content of agency declarations (including ex parte/in camera submissions), the segregability obligation, and the appellate standard of review. Each legal point is tied to how the district court and this Court treated the FBI’s submissions in Farahi.

## FOIA baseline: disclosure mandate and government’s burden

FOIA begins with a strong presumption of disclosure: agencies must release requested records unless a statutory exemption applies. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). When a requester sues, the statutory scheme places the evidentiary burden on the government to justify any withholding. The court in Farahi repeatedly framed that allocation: the government had to show that a claimed exemption covered the withheld material, and it did so through sworn agency declarations. The opinion reminds students that courts do not presume exemptions; the agency must carry its burden under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), and summary judgment in FOIA is an adjudication of whether that burden has been met on the administrative record.

## Exemption 7(A): law-enforcement purpose and interference with proceedings

Exemption 7(A) excepts from disclosure records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). That provision has two discrete elements the agency must prove: (1) the records were compiled for a law‑enforcement purpose, and (2) disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. In Farahi the FBI satisfied the first element by showing, through public and sealed declarations, that the file was created in the course of national‑security and counterterrorism investigations. The opinion highlights that when the FBI investigates alleged ties to terrorism — as the agency alleged here — the required “nexus” to a law‑enforcement duty and the connection to a possible security risk are readily established.

## “Pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings requirement

The statutory interference language has been judicially read to require that the enforcement proceedings be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” That standard, taken from CREW v. DOJ and earlier decisions, means the agency must show more than a speculative future interest: there must be a concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding or an ongoing investigation gathering evidence for a possible case. In Farahi the court credited the FBI’s public Hardy declaration and the more detailed ex parte Hardy declaration showing an ongoing counterterrorism investigation. The court treated the later Bender declaration as an update confirming that the previously described investigations remained ongoing rather than as an initial, standalone showing.

## Deference to law enforcement and national‑security considerations

The opinion reiterates the settled principle that courts afford agencies — and the FBI in particular — a measure of deference when they invoke law‑enforcement FOIA exemptions. Cases like Campbell and CNSS emphasize that this deference is especially appropriate where national‑security or counterterrorism interests are implicated. That deference does not relieve the agency of its evidentiary burden, but it means courts will credit agency explanations about law‑enforcement purposes, investigatory scope, and the risk of harm from disclosure, so long as the agency’s submissions are sufficiently specific and plausible. In Farahi, the nature of the allegations in related immigration proceedings (ties to designated organizations and convicted terrorists) provided contextual support for the FBI’s claim of ongoing national‑security investigations.

## Use of affidavits/declarations: sufficiency and specificity

Agencies ordinarily meet their FOIA evidentiary burden through sworn declarations (affidavits) by knowledgeable officials. Those declarations must describe with “reasonable specificity” the nature of the documents, the basis for withholding, and the reasons disclosure would cause harm, so a court can meaningfully assess the exemption claim without disclosing sensitive material. The Farahi litigation illustrates several practical points about this requirement. First, declarations can become stale: the district court denied the FBI’s first summary‑judgment motion without prejudice and ordered updated declarations because of the passage of time. Second, declarations need not be entirely public; the government may submit classified, sensitive, or detailed factual material ex parte or in camera to provide the court the necessary specificity without making the information public. The Court accepted that approach here: the public Hardy declaration established the existence of an investigation, the sealed Hardy declaration supplied detail, and the Bender declaration confirmed the continuing pendency of the proceedings.

## Segregability requirement and the “inextricably intertwined” doctrine

FOIA requires agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt material and to provide any “reasonably segregable portion” of a record after deletion of exempt portions. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); § 552(b). Courts will accept agency affidavits that, with reasonable specificity, explain why nonexempt material cannot be sensibly segregated. The “inextricably intertwined” doctrine allows withholding an entire record where nonexempt portions are so intermingled with exempt information that disclosure of the nonexempt material would itself reveal the exempt content or would be impracticable to separate. In Farahi the district court — after reviewing the FBI’s sealed ex parte submission — concluded the agency had adequately shown that meaningful nonexempt information was not reasonably segregable because relevant nonexempt phrases and words were interspersed throughout and disclosure would undermine the investigation. The opinion cites Mead and Juarez for the proposition that isolated words or phrases are not the FOIA’s focus and that line‑by‑line interspersion can make segregation unreasonable.

## Categorical withholdings and sufficiency of categories

When an agency withholds records on a category‑by‑category basis, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to permit judicial review of the asserted exemptions. See Gallant and CREW. That rule prevents overbroad, catch‑all labels that preclude meaningful assessment of whether particular exemptions fit particular material. In Farahi the Court noted that Farahi argued the FBI’s categories were overbroad but did not explain in what respect; because he failed to develop the point, the Court treated that challenge as forfeited. The lesson is twofold: agencies should define withholding categories clearly, and requesters must specifically contest any overbreadth to preserve their appellate arguments.

## Ex parte, in camera submissions in national‑security FOIA cases

FOIA litigation sometimes requires the court to examine sensitive material that cannot be publicly disclosed without defeating the very interests the government seeks to protect. In such circumstances courts accept ex parte, in camera submissions so the judge can evaluate the agency’s claims without enlarging the public record. The Farahi opinion acknowledges this practice as necessary and permissible in national‑security cases; it also notes a pragmatic consequence: reliance on sealed submissions handicaps the FOIA plaintiff, but that handicap is inherent when the only way to decide the dispute is to examine the withheld material privately. Practically, the opinion stresses that a plaintiff cannot complain about the court’s use of ex parte material when the plaintiff did not object to the agency’s filing of that material under seal.

## Appellate standard of review

Finally, the opinion restates the governing standard of review: courts review grants of summary judgment in FOIA cases de novo. The government retains the burden of proving the exemption. De novo review means the appellate court independently assesses whether the record (including agency declarations and any in camera material) demonstrates compliance with the statutory requirements discussed above. In Farahi the D.C. Circuit accepted the district court’s factual findings as supported by the record — including sealed declarations — and affirmed that those findings satisfied Exemption 7(A) and the segregability obligations.

Conclusion

Together these principles show how FOIA law balances the public’s right to information against legitimate law‑enforcement and national‑security interests. Farahi illustrates the mechanics: the government used public and sealed declarations to establish that records were law‑enforcement files connected to ongoing counterterrorism investigations, justified nondisclosure under Exemption 7(A), explained why nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable, and persuaded both the district court and this Court under de novo review that the statutory requirements were met.
