Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 3: # Applying 7(A) Prong One: Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes

## The rational-nexus test — what the court looks for
Exemption 7(A) applies only to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” That phrase is not self-explanatory: courts ask whether there is a rational “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties” and whether the records have a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Those formulations come from Pratt v. Webster and are repeated in Campbell and later precedent. In practice, then, an agency must do more than label a file “law enforcement”; its declarations must explain why the investigation served a law-enforcement function and how the subject or incident implicated a potential violation or security risk that the agency is charged to investigate. Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, courts give its factual judgments a “fair measure of deference,” but that deference does not eliminate the requirement that the agency articulate a rational link between its duties and the records at issue.

## What the public Hardy declaration said and why it mattered
In Farahi the FBI’s public Hardy declaration fulfilled the first part of that test by stating that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and by tying those investigations to “pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” That language maps closely onto the Pratt/Campbell inquiry: it identifies the agency’s law-enforcement duty (investigating national-security and terrorism violations) and asserts that the records were created to advance that duty. A public declaration of this sort serves two functions: it provides the court (and the public) a baseline showing the agency’s claimed purpose for the records, and it anchors any sealed explanations that the agency relies on later.

## The role of the ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration
The opinion makes clear that the public declaration did not stand alone. The Hardy ex parte declaration, filed under seal, supplied “considerable detail” about the nature and scope of the investigation. Although the appeals court properly refused to disclose the sealed content, it treated the sealed declaration as filling out the public statement: the sealed material showed how the records were used in specific investigative contexts and why they were part of law-enforcement work. Pedagogically, this illustrates a common FOIA pattern: a public affidavit states the general law-enforcement purpose while a sealed affidavit supplies factual specifics that would themselves be sensitive if revealed. The court accepted that combination as sufficient to show a rational nexus without requiring the agency to put operational details on the public record.

## Corroboration from the case record — Farahi’s own concessions
The court did not rely on FBI declarations in isolation. It emphasized contextual corroboration drawn from the record in the parallel immigration litigation: Farahi conceded before the district court that the FBI had investigated him for links to one or more State-Department-designated foreign terrorist organizations and for ties to an individual who had been detained as an enemy combatant and later convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. The immigration-court record described Farahi as having connections to high-level terrorists, alleged recruiting activity, and links to convicted or indicted terrorists (the opinion specifically references claims about connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and to Jose Padilla, and alleged support for charities later tied to terrorism financing). Those concessions and record facts make plausible the FBI’s assertion that its records were compiled to pursue national-security and terrorism-related law-enforcement duties.

## How the court put the parts together — deference plus facts = satisfied prong one
Applying the law to these materials, the court concluded that the FBI carried its burden on the first prong of Exemption 7(A). The methodology matters: the court evaluated the public declaration’s general statement of purpose, the ex parte declaration’s sealed specifics, and the independent corroboration in the litigation record. Because the FBI’s role is inherently law-enforcement, the court afforded its declarations deference, but that deference was anchored to the evidentiary showing described above. The combined record established a rational nexus between the investigation that produced Farahi’s file and the FBI’s law-enforcement duties, and it showed a connection between Farahi (and the incidents described) and possible national-security violations. That is enough, under Pratt and Campbell, to satisfy the “compiled for law enforcement purposes” requirement of Exemption 7(A).
