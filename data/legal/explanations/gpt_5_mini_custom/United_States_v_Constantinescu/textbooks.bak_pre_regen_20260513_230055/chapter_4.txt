Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 4: # The Case Study: United States v. Constantinescu et al.

This chapter uses the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Constantinescu et al. as a vehicle to explore how federal securities-fraud prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 are constructed and tested at the indictment stage. The defendants in Constantinescu were social‑media traders alleged to have run a classic “pump‑and‑dump” scheme: they posted misleading statements about securities to induce their followers to buy, causing prices to rise, then secretly sold their holdings at a profit. The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that the government had alleged only a deprivation of economic information—not a traditional property interest—but the Fifth Circuit reversed, holding that the indictment adequately alleged a fraudulent‑inducement theory and the requisite intent to defraud. The opinion provides a compact lesson on the elements of § 1348 prosecutions, the interplay between the “right‑to‑control” and “fraudulent‑inducement” theories, and the standards for challenging indictments under Rule 12.

## Allegations: false posts about positions and price potential; induced purchases; secret sales

At the factual heart of the indictment were a set of factual allegations tailored to the archetypal pump‑and‑dump. Each defendant maintained large followings on social platforms and publicly portrayed himself as a skilled trader who regularly disclosed his trading activity. The indictment alleges a consistent pattern: defendants would first purchase a target security, then “pump” the price by posting false and misleading information—misstatements about their positions in the security and about the security’s prospects—to induce followers to buy, thereby artificially inflating the market price. Once the price rose, the indictment alleges, defendants would “dump” by secretly selling their holdings for profit.

For purposes of an indictment challenge, those kinds of allegations are significant because they tie the misrepresentations to a concrete economic act by the victims: purchasing securities (transferring money or property). The alleged sequence—acquisition by the schemer, public misrepresentations designed to cause followers to buy, and secret sales by the schemer—maps directly onto the fraudulent‑inducement theory: the misstatement induces a transaction in which the victim parts with money or property. In assessing an indictment’s sufficiency, courts accept such allegations as true and ask whether the indictment alleges the offense’s material elements; thus carefully pleaded chains of conduct like these are often dispositive at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage.

## Charges: § 1349 conspiracy and § 1348 securities fraud, aiding and abetting

The indictment charged the defendants with substantive securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349. The indictment also invoked aiding and abetting liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2 for acts by co‑conspirators. Understanding these statutes—and the overlay of conspiracy and aider/abettor doctrines—is essential.

Section 1348 makes it unlawful to “engage in any fraudulent activity in connection with” securities or to obtain money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with a security. Section 1349 criminalizes conspiracies to commit offenses like § 1348; it requires an agreement and an overt act in furtherance of the conspiratorial plan. Aiding and abetting under § 2 is a theory of derivative liability: a defendant who willfully causes the commission of a crime by another—by intentionally facilitating, encouraging, or assisting—can be convicted even if not the principal actor.

At the indictment stage the government need only allege the elements of these offenses with sufficient factual particularity to inform defendants of the charges and to provide a double jeopardy bar against future prosecutions. For a § 1348 count in a pump‑and‑dump case, the necessary allegations ordinarily include (1) false or misleading statements or omissions (material misrepresentations), (2) in connection with the purchase or sale of a security, (3) made as part of a scheme to defraud and (4) with the requisite scienter—i.e., intent to defraud. Conspiracy counts must allege an agreement and at least one overt act undertaken to effectuate the scheme.

## Procedural posture: Rule 12 dismissal; de novo appellate review

Procedurally, the Constantinescu defendants moved to dismiss the superseding indictment before trial. A pretrial dismissal of an indictment is governed by Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12(b) (challenges to sufficiency of indictment), and an appellate court reviews such dismissals de novo. When assessing an indictment challenge the court “must take the allegations of the indictment as true” and determine whether the allegations, so taken, allege every material element of the charged offense. The Fifth Circuit reiterated the well‑established tripartite function of an indictment: it must (1) set out each prima facie element of the charged offense, (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges, and (3) provide a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. Those standards are drawn from the court’s prior precedents—Gaytan, Guzman‑Ocampo, Fontenot—and shape the analysis at the pleading stage.

It is critical to appreciate that sufficiency review is not a mini‑trial. The court does not weigh evidence, nor does it resolve factual disputes. If the indictment, construed in the light most favorable to the government, alleges the elements, dismissal is inappropriate; conversely, if the indictment fails to allege an essential element, dismissal is proper.

## District court’s rationale: deprivation of information vs. property

The district court granted the defendants’ Rule 12 motion, concluding that the indictment alleged only a deprivation of valuable economic information rather than a deprivation of a property interest protected by the federal fraud statutes. Its analysis invoked Ciminelli v. United States, where the Supreme Court held that the federal wire‑fraud statute could not be used to punish a scheme that sought merely to deprive a public entity of the “right to control” information necessary to make discretionary decisions; the Court emphasized that the property protected by the fraud statutes must be a traditional property interest, and that the “right to valuable economic information” is not such an interest.

Applying that principle, the district court viewed the government’s allegations as a variant on the rejected right‑to‑control theory: defendants had, the court concluded, sought to control the flow of information to their followers rather than to obtain their followers’ money or property. Under that view, the asserted harm was informational—followers were deprived of truthful information they might have used in choosing whether to trade—so the indictment did not state a § 1348 offense. This was the pivotal legal error the Fifth Circuit addressed on appeal.

## Issues on appeal: scheme to defraud and intent to defraud sufficiency

Two legal issues framed the appeal: first, whether the indictment adequately alleged a “scheme to defraud” as required by § 1348, given the limitations imposed by Ciminelli; and second, whether the indictment alleged the requisite “intent to defraud.” Both questions turn on how to distinguish the disallowed “right‑to‑control” theory from permissible fraudulent‑inducement theories and on the meaning of “obtaining” money or property under the statute.

On the first issue, the government advanced what the Supreme Court has characterized as the “fraudulent‑inducement theory”: a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into entering a transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. The key difference from the right‑to‑control theory is that the fraudulent‑inducement theory targets the victim’s transfer of money or property as the object of the fraud, rather than a defendant’s control of information needed for the victim’s discretionary judgments. The Supreme Court’s subsequent decision in Kousisis v. United States reinforced that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is a valid basis for conviction and is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli.

On the second issue—intent—the defendants argued the indictment showed only that they sought to profit, not that they intended to injure followers financially. The question thus became whether § 1348 (as interpreted in light of Kousisis) requires proof that the fraudster intended the victim to suffer a net monetary loss. Kousisis answered that question: the fraud statutes do not require that the defendant have sought to leave the victim worse off on the bottom line. It is sufficient that the defendant intended to obtain the victim’s money or property by deception; whether the victim in fact suffers a net loss, or whether the victim receives market‑value consideration, does not negate the existence of a fraud if the deceit induced the transfer.

## Legal doctrine applied: scheme to defraud; fraudulent‑inducement vs. right‑to‑control; intent

Constantinescu clarifies how the doctrinal contours settled in Ciminelli and Kousisis play out in practice. Three doctrinal points are especially important for students and practitioners.

First, “scheme to defraud” requires an affirmative allegation of deceptive conduct—a material misrepresentation or omission—intended to deceive in order to obtain something of value. The Fifth Circuit relied on its precedent that treatment of § 1348 often borrows concepts from mail/wire fraud jurisprudence. Thus, the indictment must allege both materially deceptive conduct and that the deception aimed to obtain value.

Second, courts distinguish between (a) a right‑to‑control theory that seeks to criminalize the manipulation of information flow (which Ciminelli rejected because the “right to valuable economic information” is not a traditional property interest) and (b) the fraudulent‑inducement theory that makes the transfer of money or property the object of the fraud. If an indictment alleges that deceptive statements induced victims to transfer money or property (for example, by purchasing securities), it invokes the latter theory and therefore falls outside Ciminelli’s proscription. Constantinescu’s indictment, by alleging induced purchases that transferred money/property to effectors of the scheme, was thus a fraudulent‑inducement case.

Third, “intent to defraud” under § 1348 requires an intent to deceive and an intent that some harm result from the deceit. But Kousisis clarified that the “harm” element does not require the defendant to intend the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss; it suffices that the defendant intended to obtain the victim’s money or property through deception. Consequently, a defendant who induces a victim to enter a transaction by false statements—even if the defendant provides something of ostensibly equal value in return—can be guilty if the object or purpose of the deceit was to obtain the victim’s money or property. The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu relied on this reasoning to reject the defendants’ contention that their desire to profit, rather than to injure followers, negated intent to defraud.

Together these points yield a practical rule for pleading: if the government sufficiently alleges that deceptive communications caused targeted investors to part with money or property, and that the deception was designed to obtain that money or property, the indictment will ordinarily survive a motion to dismiss under § 1348 even if the investors later received securities at market value or otherwise were not left economically worse off.

## Holding: indictment states an offense; reversal and remand

Applying the foregoing principles to the factual allegations, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment adequately alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud. The court emphasized that the indictment did not rest on the right‑to‑control theory; instead it pleaded a fraudulent‑inducement theory—defendants induced their followers to purchase securities through materially false and misleading posts and then sold the securities secretly for profit. Those allegations tied the deceptive conduct to the followers’ transfers of money/property and to the defendants’ object of obtaining money. The court further relied on Kousisis to hold that the government need not allege that the defendants intended their followers to suffer a net pecuniary loss. Because the indictment, read as a whole, sufficiently alleged each material element of the charged offenses, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded for further proceedings.

The decision thus provides a textbook example of when a pump‑and‑dump indictment will survive a Rule 12 challenge: there must be allegations that the misrepresentations were material, were intended to deceive, and were employed to induce victims to transfer money or property in a securities transaction.

## Concessions: defendants concede deception; dispute centers on property and intent

Notable in the briefing and opinion is the parties’ narrow focus. The defendants expressly conceded that the indictment alleged deceptive conduct—that is, material misrepresentations were pleaded. The contested questions, therefore, were limited to whether the alleged scheme sought to obtain a protected property interest and whether the defendants possessed the requisite intent to defraud. This narrow framing is helpful for doctrinal analysis because it isolates the legal consequences of Ciminelli and Kousisis: once deception is conceded, the question becomes whether the deception is alleged to have been used to induce transfers of money or property (fraudulent inducement) rather than to mislead decision‑makers by controlling information (right‑to‑control). It also directs attention to the mens rea question (intent to obtain money or property), which the Fifth Circuit resolved in the government’s favor under Kousisis.

For practitioners, the lesson is practical: when defending a § 1348 indictment that alleges deceitful communications, focus your Rule 12 attack on whether the indictment links the deception to an alleged transfer of money or property and whether the indictment pleads intent to obtain that money or property. Conversely, prosecutors should ensure indictments explicitly allege the causal link between deception and transfers and allege that the object of the defendant’s misconduct was to obtain money or property.

## Applying the lessons: drafting, challenging, and trying pump‑and‑dump cases

Several practical implications flow from Constantinescu.

- Drafting indictments: In a pump‑and‑dump context, a successful indictment should include concrete allegations of (1) the defendant’s misrepresentations (with times and general content), (2) the defendant’s own trading activity (purchases before the posts, secret sales after the posts), (3) the contemporaneous reactions of followers (increased purchases or volume), and (4) the defendant’s pecuniary motive or the proceeds realized. These allegations show that deception was used to induce transfers of money or property and that obtaining money was the object of the scheme—two points that defeat a Ciminelli‑style defense.

- Pretrial challenges: When moving to dismiss, defense counsel should stress any insufficiency in tying misstatements to an induced transfer of money or property, and argue (if possible) that the government’s theory is really about manipulating information, not obtaining property. After Kousisis, however, arguing that the defendant did not intend to leave victims worse off will not, by itself, make the indictment deficient; courts now focus on the object of the scheme (obtaining money/property), not on net loss.

- Trial strategy: At trial, provenance of intent remains central. The government will try to show through circumstantial evidence—timing of purchases and sales, patterns of posting, communication among co‑conspirators, and realization of profits—that defendants intended to induce purchases to enable profitable sales. Defense proof that posts were opinions or predictions rather than knowingly false factual statements may be decisive; materiality and scienter remain fact issues.

- Aiding & abetting and conspiracy: Because the government often cannot prove who sold what to whom at the individual level in complex online markets, conspiracy and aiding‑and‑abetting counts allow prosecutors to pursue liability for coordinated conduct. Indictments should thus allege agreement and overt acts for conspiratorial liability and specific acts of assistance or facilitation for § 2 liability.

## Conclusion: doctrinal summary and teaching points

United States v. Constantinescu et al. exemplifies how modern securities‑fraud law accommodates deceptive conduct in digital markets within the constraints set by the Supreme Court. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal teaches several clear rules: an indictment that alleges deceptive communications used to induce victims to transfer money or property states a scheme to defraud under § 1348; Ciminelli does not bar prosecutions premised on fraudulent inducement; and Kousisis makes clear that the government need not prove an intent to cause a victim’s net pecuniary loss—only an intent to obtain money or property by deception.

For law students, the opinion is a useful study in statutory interpretation (what does “property” mean in the fraud statutes?), mens rea (what does intent to defraud require?), the relationship between Supreme Court precedent and circuit practice, and the mechanics of indictment‑stage review. For practitioners, it is a reminder to plead the causal link between deceit and transfers carefully, to plead intent with illustrative facts, and to tailor Rule 12 arguments to distinctions that remain open after Ciminelli and Kousisis.
