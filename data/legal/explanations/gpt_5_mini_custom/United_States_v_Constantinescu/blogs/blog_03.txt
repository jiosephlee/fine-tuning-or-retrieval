Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# What an Indictment Must Say: Pleading Standards for Criminal Securities Fraud in the Fifth Circuit

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is already shaping the contours of how prosecutors must plead—and how courts must review—social‑media “pump‑and‑dump” securities cases. At a moment when influencers and self‑styled traders can move millions of dollars of market value with a single post, the Fifth Circuit’s decision pulls the pleading rules into sharp focus. The court reversed a district court dismissal and made clear that an indictment that alleges the mechanics of a pump‑and‑dump and ties false statements to induced purchases and ill‑gotten profits can survive a facial sufficiency challenge under 18 U.S.C. § 1348.

## The procedural backbone: how courts review indictments

The opinion is a useful primer on pleading law in criminal securities prosecutions. The court reiterated the familiar but important principles: indictment sufficiency is reviewed de novo; courts accept the indictment’s allegations as true when deciding a facial challenge; and an indictment must allege each material element of the charged offense, give the defendant fair notice of the charges, and protect against double jeopardy. The Fifth Circuit relied on its own precedents—Gaytan and Guzman‑Ocampo—and recent Supreme Court decisions interpreting the fraud statutes to frame what § 1348 requires: a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, with the scheme involving a material misrepresentation intended to obtain money or property.

That framework matters because defendants often seek to short‑circuit prosecutions at the indictment stage by arguing that alleged conduct—particularly speech—does not implicate the fraud statutes. The court’s analysis shows the limits of that early gatekeeping: an indictment that alleges deception plus an object of obtaining money/property passes muster even if many evidentiary issues remain for later.

## How the Constantinescu indictment cleared the bar

What carried the day for the government was concrete pleading of the pump‑and‑dump mechanics and the object of the fraud. The superseding indictment alleged that defendants, each with large social‑media followings, purchased securities, posted false and misleading statements to induce followers to buy (thereby “pumping” prices), and then secretly “dumped” shares to realize profits—$114 million in alleged gains. Those allegations track the material elements of § 1348: scheme to defraud (material misrepresentations to induce purchases) and intent to defraud (allegations that defendants sought to obtain money by inducing purchases and then sold their positions).

Importantly, the court addressed the specter of two Supreme Court cases that have recently constrained fraud prosecutions. Ciminelli rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory—you cannot predicate fraud solely on depriving someone of valuable economic information. The Fifth Circuit distinguished that scenario here: the indictment alleged not mere withholding of information but affirmative inducement to part with money/property (the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory). And Kousisis clarified that the prosecution need not plead that the fraudster intended to leave victims worse off economically; it is enough that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the scheme. The Fifth Circuit applied those rulings to hold that pleading inducement of purchases and illicit profits sufficiently alleges both the scheme and intent elements.

## What the court left open

Although the indictment survived, the opinion leaves significant battlegrounds for later proceedings. Materiality and causation—how the alleged false statements actually affected market behavior and whether individual followers purchased (and thus transferred money or property) because of particular posts—were not resolved at the pleading stage. Proof that followers relied on specific misrepresentations, that posts were materially false rather than nonactionable opinion or mere puffery, and that the defendants’ sales were traceably connected to the induced purchases, will all be fought at discovery, at trial, and on motions in limine.

First Amendment defenses also loom. The opinion does not decide when market‑talking posts cross the line into fraud, and it acknowledges the doctrinal difference between actionable misrepresentations and protected expression. That distinction—especially in cases involving recommendations, analysis, or “hot tips” on social platforms—will be a central question at later stages.

Finally, scienter and the specificity of the alleged scheme (who said what, when, to whom, and with what contemporaneous trading) are factual issues that the court expressly left for later proof rather than pleading.

## Practice takeaways

For prosecutors: Constantinescu is a roadmap for what an indictment should say in social‑media securities cases. Plead the elements: identify the statute (§ 1348 and conspiracy § 1349), describe the fraudulent‑inducement theory, and set out who made which statements, on what platform, when, and to how many followers. Allegations tying posts temporally to induced buying and to subsequent secret sales and proceeds make the “object” of obtaining money clear—especially in light of Kousisis. Quantify alleged gains where possible and describe concealment or secrecy to support intent. Avoid reliance on a right‑to‑control theory alone; frame the conduct as inducing purchases (i.e., victims were induced to part with money/property).

For defense counsel: Constantinescu narrows—but does not eliminate—early dismissal routes. Challenges that the indictment relies on a right‑to‑control theory will now be less likely to succeed where the government alleges induced purchases and gains. Defenders should press for a bill of particulars to pin down dates, posts, follower counts, trading records, and the alleged link between posts and specific purchases. Preserve First Amendment objections by developing the record on whether statements were opinion or puffery, and attack scienter and causation through targeted discovery (platform metadata, trading timestamps, and communications showing intent). Where allegations remain generalized or conclusory, renew motions to dismiss and press for specific pleading under Rule 7 and the Fifth Circuit’s precedents.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu clarifies that, at the indictment stage in the Fifth Circuit, prosecutors do not need to plead proof of victim net loss or the granular causal chain from post to purchase to sale. They do, however, need to allege a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: material misstatements intended to and resulting in victims’ parting with money or property and the fraudster’s receipt of ill‑gotten gains. The decision opens the door wider for criminal enforcement of social‑media‑driven market manipulation—but leaves substantial doctrinal and evidentiary fights for the trials ahead, where materiality, causation, scienter, and First Amendment limits will be waged on the merits.
