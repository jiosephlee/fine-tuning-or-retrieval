Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Case Background and Charges

This chapter sets out who the defendants are, the government’s theory of the alleged social‑media “pump and dump,” the specific kinds of misrepresentations the indictment alleges, the scale of the alleged fraud, and the federal criminal counts the government brought. Understanding these factual and charging allegations is essential to following the court’s later analysis of whether the indictment states an offense under the federal securities‑fraud statutes.

## Defendants’ identities and large social‑media followings

The superseding indictment names eight defendants: Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight. The opinion emphasizes that each defendant had a substantial social‑media audience across multiple platforms and regularly posted about trading activity. The indictment portrays them not as private, anonymous traders but as public influencers who cultivated credibility as skilled stock traders and used their online audiences to communicate about securities and trading decisions. That public presence is central to the government’s theory because it is the means by which the alleged scheme would reach (and persuade) investors.

## Alleged pump‑and‑dump mechanics: buy, post false/misleading statements to induce purchases, secretly sell

The government’s core factual theory is a classic pump‑and‑dump adapted to social media. According to the indictment, the defendants would first acquire shares of a targeted security. They would then “pump” its market price by posting false or misleading statements about the security to their social‑media followers, creating increased demand and driving the price upward. Once the price had been inflated by the buying activity their posts generated, the defendants would “dump” their positions by secretly selling the shares for a profit. The alleged scheme thus depends on a sequence of acquisition, deceptive publicity to induce other investors to buy, and secret liquidation to realize gains before the artificially inflated price collapsed.

## Nature of the misrepresentations: trading positions and potential price of securities

The indictment alleges the misrepresentations took the form of false or misleading public posts concerning the defendants’ own trading positions and the prospects or potential price movements of the targeted securities. In other words, the alleged statements misrepresented what the defendants held or how successful a trade would be (or would appear to be), and they conveyed misleading information about the likelihood that the security’s price would rise. The opinion treats these allegations as allegations of material misrepresentations intended to deceive followers into making purchasing decisions they otherwise might not have made.

## Alleged profits from the scheme (approximately $114 million)

The indictment quantifies the alleged proceeds: the defendants are accused of profiting roughly $114 million from the scheme. That figure appears in the opinion as evidence of the scale and commercial nature of the alleged fraud. The size of the alleged profits is relevant both to the government’s theory that the defendants intended to obtain money and to the court’s assessment of whether the indictment alleged a scheme to obtain money or property (as opposed to merely depriving followers of non‑property information).

## Counts charged: conspiracy to commit securities fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1349) and substantive securities fraud (18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2)

The superseding indictment charges the defendants with (1) conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and (2) multiple substantive counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, with liability for aiding and abetting under 18 U.S.C. § 2. Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to defraud in connection with any security and schemes to obtain money or property by false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises in connection with the purchase or sale of a security. Section 1349 makes it a crime to conspire to commit an offense under the securities fraud statute, and § 2 provides for liability for those who intentionally aid, abet, counsel, command, induce, or procure the commission of the charged offenses. The indictment therefore alleges both an agreement among defendants to carry out the scheme (the conspiracy count) and the underlying fraudulent acts—false and misleading statements aimed at inducing followers to purchase securities—constituting substantive securities fraud (and, for some defendants, aiding and abetting).

Procedurally, the opinion notes that Knight pleaded guilty, while the remaining defendants moved to dismiss the indictment. The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it failed to allege deprivation of a traditional property interest, prompting the government’s appeal. The Fifth Circuit’s later analysis turns on whether the indictment’s allegations—viewed as true at this stage—adequately plead a scheme and intent to defraud under the statutes cited.

# Procedural History and Standard of Review

## District court dismissal for failure to state an offense (no deprivation of a traditional property interest)

This case began with a superseding indictment charging eight defendants with a classic “pump and dump” securities‑fraud scheme: purchasing thinly traded securities, using social‑media posts to misrepresent positions and prospects to followers, inflating market prices, and then selling at a profit. The indictment alleged roughly $114 million in proceeds and charged conspiracy and multiple substantive counts under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349, and 2. One defendant, Daniel Knight, pleaded guilty; the remaining defendants moved to dismiss.

The district court granted that motion. Its dismissal rested on a legal conclusion: the indictment, the court believed, alleged only that the defendants deprived their followers of potentially valuable economic information (a “right to control” theory), not that the defendants schemed to obtain the followers’ money or other traditional property. Citing the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli—that federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests and not a mere deprivation of valuable economic information—the district court concluded the indictment failed to state an offense and dismissed the charges.

## Government’s timely appeal

The government filed a timely appeal to the Fifth Circuit challenging the district court’s dismissal. The appeal presented a question of legal sufficiency: whether, taken as the indictment alleges, the defendants’ conduct alleged a cognizable “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” under the securities‑fraud statute, or instead rested improperly on a right‑to‑control theory barred by Ciminelli.

## De novo review of indictment sufficiency

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reviewed the district court’s dismissal de novo. De novo review means the appellate court independently considers whether the indictment, on its face, adequately alleges the elements of the charged offenses without deferring to the district court’s legal conclusions. The governing principle is that questions of law—such as whether an indictment states an offense—are reviewed anew by the court of appeals.

## Taking indictment’s allegations as true at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage

When assessing an attack on indictment sufficiency, courts accept the indictment’s factual allegations as true. That rule prevents a district court from resolving factual disputes or weighing evidence at the pleading stage; the motion to dismiss tests legal sufficiency, not factual proof. But this rule is limited to the four corners of the charging document: the court asks whether the pleaded facts, assumed true, constitute the elements of the crime. If an essential element is not alleged, dismissal is appropriate; if the indictment alleges each material element, factual disputes and evidence are matters for trial.

## Indictment sufficiency requirements: enumerate each element, fair notice, and double‑jeopardy protection

Federal appellate decisions set a straightforward tripartite test for indictment sufficiency. A valid indictment must (1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense, (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges so the defendant can prepare a defense, and (3) provide enough detail to protect the defendant from future prosecutions for the same offense (the double‑jeopardy function). In short, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense.

Applying those principles here meant the Fifth Circuit examined whether the superseding indictment, while purporting to describe a pump‑and‑dump scheme, actually pleaded the essential elements of securities fraud—most importantly a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud”—in a way that charged defendants with obtaining money or property by false pretenses (the traditional property interest the fraud statutes protect). The district court had concluded the indictment fell short on that point by framing the alleged harm as a deprivation of economic information; the Fifth Circuit, reviewing de novo and accepting the indictment’s allegations as true, asked whether the charging document instead alleged a fraudulent‑inducement theory that seeks to obtain victims’ money or property.

# Governing Law: Securities Fraud under § 1348 and Its Mail/Wire Fraud Roots

This chapter explains the legal framework the Fifth Circuit applied in United States v. Constantinescu to evaluate whether the superseding indictment adequately alleged securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The opinion treats § 1348 as rooted in the same core concepts that govern mail and wire fraud prosecutions, and it relies on recent Supreme Court decisions to define what counts as a protected “money or property” interest and what mental state § 1348 requires. Understanding those elements and how modern cases constrain theories of liability is essential to following the court’s reasoning in reversing the district court’s dismissal of the indictment.

## § 1348 elements: scheme to defraud and intent to defraud

Section 1348 makes it a crime to engage in a “scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with” a security and to obtain “by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property” in connection with securities transactions. The Fifth Circuit distilled the statutory requirements into two core elements: (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” When reviewing an indictment, the court treats the indictment’s allegations as true and asks whether those allegations, taken in the light most favorable to the government, plausibly allege each material element of the offense. Thus, for § 1348 charges, the indictment must plead the defendant’s deceptive scheme and the defendant’s culpable intent.

## Borrowing concepts from mail and wire fraud jurisprudence

The Fifth Circuit acknowledged that § 1348 has been infrequently construed in the circuit and therefore borrows analytically from the more developed mail-and-wire fraud jurisprudence. Courts commonly import concepts and tests from mail and wire fraud cases when construing § 1348 because the statutes share core language about schemes and obtaining money or property by false pretenses. That borrowing shapes how to analyze what counts as a “scheme to defraud,” what qualifies as “money or property,” and what “intent to defraud” requires.

Because of that pedigree, two doctrinal strands from mail/wire fraud law are central: (1) the distinction between theories that target deprivation of a traditional property interest and theories that seek to punish deprivation of nontraditional interests (like mere information), and (2) the differentiation between different ways a defendant’s misrepresentations can lead a victim to hand over money or property (the “fraudulent-inducement” theory versus the “right-to-control” or information-deprivation theory).

## Scheme to defraud: material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value

The “scheme to defraud” element focuses on deception that is material and intended to procure something of value. The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu quoted and applied the line of authority that a scheme to defraud requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” In practice, that formulation asks two linked questions: did the defendant make or employ a material misrepresentation or omission, and was that deception intended to induce the victim to part with money or property?

Two distinct theories of how deception can work are relevant here. The “right-to-control” (or information-deprivation) theory treats the victim’s right to make discretionary economic decisions as the property interest allegedly taken; under this theory the defendant’s lie prevents the victim from making informed choices. The Supreme Court in Ciminelli rejected criminal liability based solely on that theory because the “right to valuable economic information” is not a traditional property interest that the federal fraud statutes protect. Thus, an indictment that alleges only that the defendant deprived the victim of information—without alleging that the victim was tricked into giving money or property—fails to state an offense under those precedents.

By contrast, the “fraudulent-inducement” theory alleges that the defendant’s misrepresentation tricked the victim into a transaction that required handing over money or property. The fraudulent-inducement theory squarely targets the deprivation of money or property—the paradigmatic protected interests under the fraud statutes—and is therefore not foreclosed by Ciminelli. In Constantinescu the indictment charged a classic fraudulent-inducement scenario: defendants allegedly posted materially false and misleading claims on social media that induced followers to buy securities, thereby causing followers to part with money or property and enabling the defendants to sell at inflated prices. The Fifth Circuit relied on that distinction to conclude the indictment alleged a scheme to obtain money or property rather than merely withholding information.

## Intent to defraud: intent to deceive and to cause some harm from the deceit

The intent element for § 1348 mirrors the mail-and-wire fraud standard: a defendant must have acted with an intent to deceive and with an intent that some harm result from the deceit. That formulation is a two-part mental-state requirement—(1) culpable knowledge and deception and (2) a purpose that the deception produce a harmful consequence.

What counts as “harm” in this context was clarified by the Supreme Court in Kousisis. The Court rejected the argument that fraud liability requires proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. Instead, Kousisis held that it is sufficient that the defendant intended to “obtain” the victim’s money or property as an object of the scheme. The defendant’s provision of something of value in exchange (for example, goods or services that may match the victim’s outlay in economic value) does not negate liability if the defendant used material misrepresentations to procure the victim’s money or property. Thus, intended or actual net pecuniary loss to the victim is not a necessary ingredient of the intent element so long as obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the scheme.

Applying that rule, the Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu found the indictment’s allegations—posting false claims to induce followers to buy securities so defendants could sell at a profit—sufficient to allege the requisite intent. The indictment specifically alleged that the scheme’s object was to generate profit (the government alleged $114 million in proceeds), which demonstrates the defendants “had money in mind.” Under Kousisis, that objective satisfies the “intent to defraud” requirement even though followers may have received marketable securities rather than a pure net loss.

## Kousisis formulation: deception plus obtaining money or property as an object of the scheme

Kousisis supplies a concise framing that the Fifth Circuit relied on: a fraud conviction lies when the defendant “‘engaged in deception’” and obtaining the victim’s “money or property” was “‘an object’ of his fraud.” This formulation integrates the two core elements—scheme and intent—into a practical test for indictment sufficiency. It highlights two takeaways that are central to Constantinescu’s disposition.

First, the government must plead deception that is material and causally connected to victims’ parting with money or property; allegations that defendants merely suppressed or distorted information without alleging induced monetary transactions risk falling into the Ciminelli prohibition. Second, the government need not show the defendant sought to make the victim poorer overall; it suffices that the defendant sought to obtain money or property through deception. Thus, an indictment alleging a pump-and-dump that purposely induces followers to buy securities (thus transferring money or property into the market and enabling the defendant’s selling at an inflated price) satisfies the Kousisis test.

In Constantinescu the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment did allege both deception and an objective of obtaining money or property: defendants made material misrepresentations on social media designed to induce followers to buy securities, and the scheme’s object was defendants’ financial gain. Because the indictment pleaded those elements, it met § 1348’s requirements and could not be dismissed for failing to allege the protection of a traditional property interest.

Conclusion

For § 1348 prosecutions, courts evaluate whether the charged scheme included material deception intended to cause victims to part with money or property and whether the defendant intended to obtain that money or property as an object of the scheme. The Fifth Circuit treated § 1348 as governed by mail-and-wire fraud precedents, applied Ciminelli to reject information-deprivation theories that do not allege a transfer of money or property, and relied on Kousisis to hold that a defendant need not intend the victim’s net pecuniary loss—only that obtaining the victim’s money or property be an object of the fraud. Those principles explain why the court reversed the dismissal of the pump-and-dump indictment in Constantinescu.

# Issue One: Scheme to Defraud and the Property Interest Requirement

A central question in this appeal is whether the superseding indictment alleges a “scheme to defraud” that targets a traditional property interest—money or property—or whether it instead alleges only the deprivation of valuable economic information (a non‑traditional interest that, under the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli, cannot support a fraud conviction). The Fifth Circuit answers that the indictment here alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme that sought to obtain money (or its equivalent) from followers who were induced to buy securities, and therefore it pleads a deprivation of a protected property interest.

Defendants’ argument: mere deprivation of information
The defendants’ dismissal argument rests on characterizing the indictment as alleging only that they deprived their followers of valuable economic information—i.e., that by concealing their true trading positions and motivations the defendants kept followers from information necessary to make fully informed trading decisions. If the indictment were limited to that kind of “right to control” or information‑control theory, the defendants say, it would run headlong into Ciminelli: the Supreme Court held that federal fraud statutes protect traditional property rights, not a mere right to valuable economic information. Thus, the defendants urge, the indictment fails to allege the deprivation of money or property and must be dismissed.

Ciminelli and the invalidated right‑to‑control theory
Ciminelli is the touchstone for this part of the analysis. In that case, the government prosecuted a defendant under a theory that he had schemed to deprive a state agency of the right to control the disposition of public funds by using a lobbyist to steer contracts. The Supreme Court rejected that “right‑to‑control” theory because it framed the victim’s “property” as the right to valuable economic information and control, which the Court held is not a traditional, protected property interest under the federal fraud statutes. The upshot: an indictment that alleges only that a defendant deprived someone of information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions cannot sustain a fraud conviction under §§ 1341/1343/1348.

The fraudulent‑inducement theory after Kousisis
Kousisis clarifies the distinction between an invalid right‑to‑control charge and a valid fraudulent‑inducement charge. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, a defendant makes a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into a transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. The key is that the misrepresentation induces the transfer of money or property—what is protected is the money or property that the victim parts with, not a mere informational right. Kousisis also rejects the notion that fraud requires the defendant to have intended to leave the victim worse off; it is enough that the defendant sought to obtain the victim’s money or property by deception.

Applying the frameworks to the indictment’s allegations
Read against these precedents, the indictment here does not rest on a right‑to‑control theory. It alleges a pump‑and‑dump scheme in which defendants, having purchased shares, posted false and misleading social‑media statements designed to induce followers to purchase the targeted securities. Those induced purchases are alleged to be the mechanism by which the followers parted with money or transferred property (cash in exchange for shares), after which the defendants sold into the inflated market and profited—$114 million, the indictment claims. This is the paradigmatic fraudulent‑inducement allegation: misrepresentations caused followers to enter into securities purchases that required them to hand over money or property, and obtaining that money/property was the object of the scheme.

Why this is not Ciminelli in disguise
Two features separate the indictment from the Ciminelli‑type theory that the Supreme Court rejected. First, Ciminelli’s problem was that the government attempted to treat a victim’s informational control as the victim’s property; here, the indictment treats the followers’ purchases—their parting with money or transfer of property—as the target of the deception. Second, Kousisis expressly distinguishes fraudulent inducement from a right‑to‑control theory: fraudulent inducement protects the money or property the victim transfers as a result of the deception. Because the indictment alleges that the defendants’ misrepresentations induced purchases (and that defendants profited when they sold), it pleads deprivation of a traditional property interest rather than mere withholding of information.

Conclusion: the indictment adequately alleges a scheme to deprive victims of money/property
For pleading purposes the court must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and determine whether the indictment alleges each material element of the charged offense. Here the indictment alleges material misrepresentations, an intent to induce purchases, and the obtainment of money/property through those induced purchases. Those allegations fit the fraudulent‑inducement theory validated in Kousisis and avoid the non‑actionable right‑to‑control theory struck down in Ciminelli. Accordingly, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme to defraud that targets money or property and thereby survives the defendants’ motion to dismiss on this ground.

# Issue Two: Intent to Defraud and Whether Harm Must Be Alleged

This section examines the Fifth Circuit’s treatment of intent to defraud in United States v. Constantinescu and its conclusion that an indictment need not allege an intent to leave victims economically worse off. The court relied primarily on the Supreme Court’s recent guidance in Kousisis and applied it to the “pump and dump” allegations to conclude that intent to profit by deceptive inducement is enough to plead the intent-to-defraud element of the securities‑fraud statutes.

## Defendants’ argument: intent to profit, not to harm; no victim loss alleged

The defendants pressed a narrow view of the intent-to-defraud element: they acknowledged that the indictment alleges deception (false and misleading social‑media posts) but argued that the government did not allege an intent to harm their followers because the followers received securities in return for their money. In the defendants’ view, the indictment therefore pleads only an intent to profit, not an intent to cause the kind of economic harm that the fraud statutes protect against. The district court accepted that framing and dismissed the indictment for failure to allege that the defendants sought to deprive victims of a traditional property interest.

The Fifth Circuit rejected that approach as inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent. To understand why, you need to see how the courts distinguish between (1) depriving a victim of the right to control information (the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli) and (2) tricking a victim into parting with money or property through a material misrepresentation (the fraudulent‑inducement theory upheld in Kousisis).

## Kousisis: fraud complete upon scheming to obtain victim’s money or property, regardless of net loss

Kousisis is the pivotal precedent the Fifth Circuit relied on. There the Supreme Court explained that under the fraudulent‑inducement theory a defendant violates the federal fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain a victim’s money or property through material misrepresentations—even if the defendant provides something in return and even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. The key point is that the fraud statutes protect the act of inducing a victim to hand over money or property by deception; they do not require proof that the victim was left economically worse off.

The Fifth Circuit reads Kousisis to mean that a defendant’s desire to profit does not negate intent to defraud. A scheme whose object is to induce victims to purchase securities (thereby causing them to part with money or property) fits squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Kousisis rejects any rule that would require the government to show a “mirror‑image” loss where the defendant’s gain must exactly equal the victim’s loss or that a net loss must be proved as an element of the offense.

## Application: alleged posts designed to induce purchases show intent to obtain money via deceit

Applying Kousisis, the court focused on the indictment’s allegations that defendants purchased securities, posted false and misleading information to induce others to buy, and then sold their holdings at inflated prices. Those allegations describe a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: the defendants used material misstatements to trick their followers into entering transactions that required handing over money or property. Under the statute and Kousisis, that objective—obtaining money or property by deceit—is itself the intent to defraud.

The opinion emphasizes that the indictment alleges more than a bare desire to keep information away from followers (the right‑to‑control theory). It specifically alleges that the defendants’ conduct was calculated to cause followers to buy securities, which involved the followers parting with money and thereby created an object of the scheme (the defendants’ profit). Allegations that the defendants “pumped” prices through misrepresentations and then “dumped” their holdings are probative of an intent to obtain money by deception; they adequately plead the intent‑to‑defraud element at the indictment stage.

## Receiving market‑value securities does not negate fraud liability

A principal legal takeaway is that a victim’s receipt of market‑value securities—as opposed to receiving nothing—does not defeat a fraudulent‑inducement charge. Kousisis makes clear that the fraud statute punishes inducing a victim to part with money or property under materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the defendant provides value in return or whether the transaction leaves the victim with a net economic loss. The Fifth Circuit reiterates this point: even if followers ultimately held securities whose market value equaled what they paid, they were still induced by materially false statements to hand over money, and the defendants’ object to obtain money by deceit is sufficient to allege intent to defraud.

The court also cites prior Fifth Circuit authority rejecting a required “mirror‑image” alignment of gain and loss; what matters for the charged offense is that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the scheme—not that the victim suffered an identifiable net pecuniary injury.

## Reinforcement: no mirror‑image loss/gain requirement; obtaining money was an object of the scheme

Summing up, the Fifth Circuit concludes that the indictment sufficiently pleads that the defendants intended to defraud because the charging instrument alleges both deceit and an objective to obtain money or property through that deceit. The “pump and dump” allegations—posting false and misleading information to induce purchases and then selling at the inflated prices—allege a scheme whose object was monetary profit obtained by deception. Under Kousisis and related circuit law, that is enough to satisfy the intent‑to‑defraud element at the pleading stage; the government does not have to allege that the victims were left economically worse off for the indictment to stand.

Practical note for students: intent is a fact question to be proved at trial, but an indictment need only allege facts sufficient to inform the defendant of the charged offense. Here, the indictment’s detailed allegations of false statements, induced purchases, and profitable dumps sufficiently put defendants on notice that obtaining money through deception was the charged object of the scheme.

# Disposition and Significance

## Holding: reversal and remand
The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded the case for further proceedings. The court held that the superseding indictment adequately alleged both a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Key to that conclusion was the indictment’s allegation that defendants posted materially false and misleading statements on social media to induce their followers to purchase securities, and that defendants then sold those securities for profit. Because those allegations describe fraudulent inducement that caused victims to part with money or property, the indictment pleaded a protected property interest and satisfied the elements of securities fraud sufficiently to survive a facial challenge.

## What proceeds on remand
On remand the criminal prosecution resumes in the district court; the dismissal is vacated and the indictment stands. Practically, that means the case will proceed to normal pretrial criminal proceedings (e.g., arraignment if not already done, motions, discovery disputes, potential plea negotiations, and, if necessary, trial). The Fifth Circuit’s opinion resolves the legal sufficiency question at the pleading stage, not the factual merits; the defendants retain all other defenses and may still contest materiality, causation, mens rea, or proof at trial.

## Charging guidance: avoid a right‑to‑control framing; plead fraudulent inducement to obtain money/property
A central lesson from the opinion is the distinction between the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli and the fraudulent‑inducement theory endorsed by Kousisis. Prosecutors should avoid framing allegations as depriving victims merely of “valuable economic information” or the nebulous “right to control” assets; Ciminelli bars convictions based solely on that theory because the right to information is not a traditional property interest. Instead, § 1348 allegations should be structured to show that the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce victims to part with money or property—for example, by alleging that victims were tricked into purchasing securities. Pleadings should clearly identify (1) the misrepresentations, (2) how those statements induced victims to transfer money or property (such as purchasing shares), and (3) the defendant’s objective of obtaining money or property (e.g., proceeds from secret sales).

## Practical import for social‑media pump‑and‑dump cases
The opinion makes clear that traditional securities‑fraud theories extend to schemes that operate through social media. When a social‑media influencer posts false claims about a stock and followers buy based on those claims, the subsequent transfer of money (payment for securities) can be the property interest protected by § 1348. Thus, allegations that influencers “pumped” a stock through false posts and then “dumped” their holdings can, if pleaded with sufficient specificity, state a federal securities‑fraud offense. In practice, prosecutors should focus on tying particular posts to particular purchases and on alleging the influencer’s deceptive intent and personal financial gain; defense counsel may respond by challenging the causal link, materiality, and proof of intent.

## Doctrinal takeaway: intent to cause net loss is not required post‑Kousisis
A doctrinally significant point reiterated in this opinion is that, after Kousisis, the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. The relevant intent is an intent to deceive and an intent that the deception result in the transfer of money or property to the defendant (or as an object of the scheme). Thus, a defendant who obtains money or property by inducing a purchase through materially false statements can be guilty of securities fraud even if the victim receives the promised good or service or is not left with a net pecuniary loss. This undermines arguments that a scheme cannot be fraud because the victims technically received market‑value securities or because the defendant’s aim was profit rather than injuring investors.

## Concluding note
United States v. Constantinescu clarifies how post‑Ciminelli and Kousisis doctrines apply in the social‑media era: prosecutors may charge social‑media driven “pump and dump” schemes under § 1348 by pleading fraudulent inducement to obtain money or property, and courts should reject attempts to recast such allegations as mere deprivation of information. The Fifth Circuit’s decision disposes only of the pleading sufficiency issue; factual and affirmative defenses remain for resolution at trial or on further motion practice.
