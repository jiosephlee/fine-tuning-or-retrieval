Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How did the indictment satisfy § 1348’s “money or property” requirement without invoking the discredited right-to-control theory?
Question:
The panel says Ciminelli bars convictions based solely on depriving victims of valuable economic information, but upholds this indictment because it alleges fraudulent inducement to part with money. I’m struggling to see the property interest here: the followers bought market-traded securities (arguably at then-market prices), and there was no alleged right-to-control theory. Is the idea that the victims’ cash used to buy shares is the protected property, even if they received securities of equal value at the time? What makes this different from simply providing (allegedly false) information that affects someone’s decision-making, which Ciminelli seemed to reject?
Answer:
Short answer: The indictment pleaded a fraudulent‑inducement theory, not the right‑to‑control theory Ciminelli rejected. It alleges defendants used material misrepresentations to trick followers into buying securities — i.e., to “part with their money or property” — and the Court relied on Kousisis to hold that obtaining that money/property is all § 1348 requires, even if the victims received securities of (alleged) market value in return.

Why that matters (doctrinal explanation tied to the opinion)

- Ciminelli stands for a narrow point: a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on depriving someone of “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory) because that right is not a traditional property interest. As the opinion summarizes, “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” so the right‑to‑control theory “cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” (Opinion § III.A, quoting Ciminelli.)

- Kousisis, by contrast, recognizes the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property. The Constantinescu panel quotes Kousisis: the fraudulent‑inducement theory “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory” and “protects money and property.” (Opinion § III.A.)

- Critically, Kousisis also makes clear that § 1348 does not require the victim to be left economically worse off. A fraud is complete when the defendant induces the victim to part with money or property under materially false pretenses, even if the fraudster provides something of equal value in return. The Fifth Circuit relied on that point when rejecting the defendants’ argument that receiving market‑value securities defeats § 1348 liability. (Opinion § III.B, discussing Kousisis and quoting that the statutes reach schemes to “obtain” the victim’s “money or property,” “regardless of whether the fraudster . . . seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”)

Application to these facts (as alleged in the indictment)

- The indictment alleges defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” The Court reads that allegation as alleging that defendants induced followers to “part with their money or property” by buying the stock — i.e., the cash (or other consideration) used to buy the shares is the protected property. (Opinion § III.A.)

- The panel treats this as materially different from Ciminelli because the harm here is not mere informational deprivation; it is an alleged scheme whose object was to obtain money (the alleged $114 million profit is cited as showing they “had money in mind”). Under Kousisis and the authorities the panel cites, that suffices for § 1348.

Bottom line

The indictment survived because it alleges fraudulent inducement to buy (a transfer of money/property), not merely withholding of information. Under the Court’s reading of Kousisis (as applied in Constantinescu), obtaining the buyer’s money/property through lies satisfies § 1348 even if the buyer receives securities of apparent market value in return.

### How does the fraudulent-inducement theory apply when victims purchase on an open market from unknown counterparties?
Question:
Kousisis involved a contract directly induced by misrepresentations, making it easy to see how the defendant “obtained” the victim’s money. Here, purchases occurred on public markets. The opinion says defendants pumped and then dumped, but not every induced buyer necessarily bought from a defendant. Does § 1348 require that the defendant actually obtain the particular victim’s money, or is it enough that some investors were induced to buy and defendants monetized the inflated price by selling into that demand? How does the government satisfy the “obtain money or property” element in a dispersed open-market setting?
Answer:
Short answer
Section 1348 does not require the government to allege (or later prove at the indictment stage) that a defendant literally took cash out of the pockets of each particular buyer on an exchange. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory affirmed in Constantinescu (and explained in Kousisis), it is enough to allege that the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce purchases on the public market, that those induced purchases inflated the price, and that the defendant monetized that inflation by selling into the demand (i.e., obtaining money or property). The Fifth Circuit treated the allegation that defendants “pumped” demand by false posts and then “dumped” into that demand for $114 million as a sufficient allegation that the defendants sought to “obtain” money.

Why — the doctrine and standards (as applied in Constantinescu)
- Fraud under § 1348 requires (1) a scheme to defraud (a material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value) and (2) intent to defraud (an intent to deceive and cause harm). The court borrows concepts from mail/wire fraud cases but applies them to securities fraud. (Constantinescu II–III; quoting Greenlaw and Evans.)
- Ciminelli forbids treating the deprivation of merely valuable economic information (the “right‑to‑control” theory) as a protected property interest. Constantinescu distinguishes that: the indictment here alleges the fraudulent‑inducement theory, not a right‑to‑control theory. Fraudulent inducement targets money/property because it induces a victim to part with money or property (Kousisis ¶¶ discussed in the opinion).
- Kousisis further holds that a fraudster can be liable even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss; the statute protects schemes that obtain the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether the victim later receives something of equal value. Constantinescu relies on that point to reject the defendants’ “we only took their information” argument.

How that controls an open‑market (dispersed buyer) pump‑and‑dump
- The opinion treats a pump‑and‑dump on public markets as a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme when the indictment alleges: (a) defendants made false/misleading representations to induce purchases by followers; (b) those induced purchases artificially inflated price; and (c) defendants sold into that artificially created demand and realized profits. Those allegations, taken as true at the indictment stage, satisfy the “obtain money or property” element. (See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14; court’s discussion at III.A–B.)
- The government need not allege that the defendant’s sale was made to the very investor who was induced. The court cites the principle (and Baker) that the fraud statutes do not impose a “mirror image” requirement that the victim’s loss be the defendant’s gain. It is enough to allege that the scheme caused purchasers to part with money and that the defendant monetized the scheme by selling into the market. (Constantinescu III.B; citing Kousisis and Baker.)
- Practically, then, at the pleading stage the government satisfies “obtain” by alleging a causal scheme: defendants’ misrepresentations induced market purchases, price rose, and defendants sold shares for profit (the opinion notes the indictment’s allegation of $114 million in profits). Those allegations create the requisite nexus between deception and obtaining money under § 1348.

What this means for pleading and proof (limited to what the opinion says)
- For an indictment, Constantinescu shows the government can properly charge pump‑and‑dump § 1348 schemes where it pleads that false statements induced market purchases and that defendants sold into the inflated market to obtain money.
- The opinion does not resolve the evidentiary or causal proof questions at trial (e.g., how to prove the defendant’s postings caused particular price movements or profits), but it holds that the allegations described suffice to state an offense at the pleading stage.

Takeaway for your question
You don’t need an allegation that the defendant literally received the cash from each particular buyer on the exchange. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory as applied in Constantinescu (and Kousisis), it is sufficient to allege that the defendant’s misrepresentations induced purchases in the open market, that those purchases inflated the price, and that the defendant then sold into that inflated market to obtain money or property. The indictment’s allegations that defendants “pumped” demand and “dumped” for $114 million were enough to plead the “obtain” element under § 1348.

### Post-Kousisis, does the Fifth Circuit still require an intent to cause economic harm, or only an intent to obtain money through deception?
Question:
The opinion cites Evans for intent to defraud as intent to deceive and intent to cause harm, but then relies on Kousisis to say no net loss is required and it’s enough that defendants had “money in mind.” How are courts reconciling Evans’s “harm” prong with Kousisis? Is “harm” now satisfied whenever the object is to obtain money by deceit, even if the plan doesn’t contemplate leaving the victim worse off on a net basis?
Answer:
Short answer: After Kousisis the Fifth Circuit does not require proof that the defendant intended to leave victims worse off on net. Evans’s two-part formulation—intent to (1) deceive and (2) cause some harm—remains the governing language, but Constantinescu (applying Kousisis) interprets the “harm” prong to be satisfied when the fraudster’s object is to obtain money or property by deception (i.e., when the defendant “had money in mind”), even if the victim receives something of market value and is not left with a net pecuniary loss.

Explanation and how the opinion reconciles Evans and Kousisis
- Evans articulated the intent-to-defraud element as requiring both an intent to deceive and “to cause some harm to result from the deceit.” Constantinescu recounts that formulation (citing Evans, 892 F.3d at 712) but then turns to the Supreme Court’s decision in Kousisis.
- Kousisis clarified that the fraudulent‑inducement theory reaches schemes in which a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster . . . seeks to leave the victim net pecuniary loss.” As Constantinescu puts it, Kousisis holds that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The Fifth Circuit therefore treats an intent to obtain money/property by deception as satisfying the “harm” element. See Constantinescu III.B (collecting Kousisis language and stating defendants “had money in mind”).
- The opinion distinguishes Ciminelli: Ciminelli forbids convictions based solely on deprivation of valuable economic information (the “right‑to‑control” theory), but it does not invalidate the fraudulent‑inducement theory where the indictment alleges the victim was induced to part with money or property. Constantinescu relies on that distinction to show the indictment here alleges a fraudulent‑inducement scheme (victims were induced to buy securities), not a mere withholding of information.

Practical framing for pleading/proof
- If the alleged scheme induces victims to part with money or property by materially false statements, a court following Constantinescu (and Kousisis) will treat the intent‑to‑defraud element as satisfied even if the defendant also provides something of value or the victim’s net economic position is not worsened.
- By contrast, where the government’s theory rests only on depriving the victim of valuable information or control (no alleged transfer of money/property), Ciminelli still bars a fraud conviction.

Bottom line: Evans’s two‑prong language survives as the standard, but Kousisis (as applied in Constantinescu) narrows what “harm” means: causing the deprivation of money/property (i.e., intending to obtain money by deceit) satisfies the harm requirement even absent an intent to inflict net pecuniary loss.

### Are predictions about a stock’s “potential price” actionable misrepresentations at the indictment stage, or are they non-actionable puffery/opinion?
Question:
The indictment mentions posts misrepresenting trading positions and the potential price of securities. I understand that lying about one’s present trading position is a statement of fact, but statements about future price often look like opinion or puffery. At the Rule 12 stage, does the court simply assume materiality and falsity for both types of statements, or is there a doctrinal line about when optimistic price predictions can support a § 1348 charge?
Answer:
Short answer: At the Rule 12/indictment stage, optimistic price predictions are not categorically non‑actionable. Under the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning in United States v. Constantinescu, allegations that price statements were false or misleading and were used to induce followers to buy securities can support a § 1348 fraudulent‑inducement charge. A court at the pleading stage accepts those allegations as true and asks only whether the indictment alleges the elements of a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud — it does not resolve on the merits whether a particular statement was mere puffery.

Why — the governing doctrinal line in Constantinescu
- Constantinescu frames § 1348 claims by borrowing mail/wire fraud concepts: the government must plead a “scheme to defraud” (material misrepresentation intended to obtain value) and an “intent to defraud.” (Op. § III.)
- The opinion distinguishes two different theories the courts use: the right‑to‑control theory (which seeks to punish deprivation of valuable economic information) and the fraudulent‑inducement theory (which punishes using a material misstatement to trick a victim into handing over money or property). Constantinescu explains that Ciminelli rejected convictions based solely on deprivation of economic information (the right‑to‑control theory), but that does not bar fraudulent‑inducement charges that allege victims were induced to buy securities. (Op. III.A.)
- The Supreme Court in Kousisis (as discussed in Constantinescu) confirms that fraudulent inducement protects money/property and that a defendant can be liable even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. Thus, alleging that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” is sufficient at the indictment stage to plead the object and intent of the fraud. (Op. III.A–B.)

What that means for “predictions” versus puffery
- Pure puffery or subjective opinion—generic boasts about a stock’s “potential” that are not presented as factual assertions and that no reasonable investor would treat as fact—can be non‑actionable. The opinion recognizes that an indictment cannot be sustained by alleging only a deprivation of information (Ciminelli), and courts will not label obvious puffery as a material misrepresentation on the merits.
- But where the indictment alleges (as this one does) that defendants made false or misleading statements about securities (including claims about trading positions and price expectations) with the specific purpose of inducing purchases so the defendants could “pump” and “dump,” those allegations meet § 1348’s elements at the pleading stage. In short: allegations that predictions were knowingly false, presented as factual assertions, or used to induce purchases are actionable at the indictment stage under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. (Op. ¶¶ 13–14; III.A–B.)

Practical takeaways for the Rule 12 context
- At the Rule 12 stage the court accepts the indictment’s factual allegations as true and asks whether they, if proven, would constitute an offense. Constantinescu reversed dismissal because the indictment pleaded a fraudulent inducement to buy securities (a protected money/property deprivation), not merely a withholding of economic information. (Op. II–IV.)
- Whether a particular statement is legal puffery or a material misrepresentation is often a factual/contextual question (specificity, speaker’s expertise, whether the statement was framed as fact, whether it actually induced purchases). That factual inquiry is generally inappropriate to resolve on a motion to dismiss where the indictment adequately pleads the elements.

Bottom line: optimistic price predictions are not automatically shielded as non‑actionable puffery at the indictment stage. If the indictment pleads that those predictions were false/misleading, communicated to induce purchases, and were part of a scheme to obtain money (as Constantinescu finds was alleged), the charge survives a Rule 12 sufficiency challenge.

### What is the scope of § 1348’s “in connection with” requirement and do social-media posts satisfy it without a transactional relationship?
Question:
The statute requires a scheme to defraud “in connection with” the purchase or sale of a security. Here, the alleged misstatements were public social-media posts rather than direct solicitations to identified buyers. How tight must the nexus be? Is it sufficient that the posts were intended to induce trading activity generally, or does the government need to allege a closer link to specific purchases or sales?
Answer:
Short answer
United States v. Constantinescu holds that § 1348’s “in connection with” requirement is satisfied when the charged misrepresentations are alleged to have been made to induce others to buy securities — even when those misrepresentations are public social‑media posts and there is no allegation of a direct transactional relationship between the defendant and specific buyers. What matters is that the scheme fraudulently induced the transfer of money or property (the purchase), not that the government identify particular contracting counterparties.

Why — doctrinal basics from Constantinescu
- § 1348 is analyzed like mail/wire fraud: the crime requires a scheme to defraud (a material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value) and an intent to defraud. Constantinescu, slip op. § III (citing Greenlaw and Evans).
- Constantinescu distinguishes two theories the Supreme Court has discussed:
  - The “right‑to‑control” theory (rejected in Ciminelli) — charging fraud for merely depriving someone of valuable economic information — cannot support conviction because that theory does not protect a traditional property interest. Constantinescu III.A (discussing Ciminelli).
  - The “fraudulent‑inducement” theory — charging fraud where a defendant uses material falsehoods to trick victims into contracts that require handing over money or property — does protect money/property and can support a § 1348 charge. Constantinescu III.A (discussing Kousisis).
- Under Kousisis (as applied in Constantinescu), the government need not show the defendant intended to leave victims worse off; it is sufficient that the defendant sought to “obtain” money or property by inducing purchases. Constantinescu III.B (discussing Kousisis).

What the opinion says about social‑media posts and the “nexus”
- The court upheld the indictment because it alleged defendants bought shares, posted false and misleading information on social media to induce followers to purchase the securities, and then sold for a profit — i.e., a classic “pump and dump.” Indictment ¶¶ 13–14; Constantinescu III.
- The court treated public posts intended to induce trading generally as adequate to plead that the scheme was “in connection with” the purchase of securities: the posts allegedly caused followers to part with money by buying the securities (a fraudulent‑inducement), so the statutory nexus is met even without a direct sales relationship between defendant and each buyer. Constantinescu III.A–B.

Limits the opinion recognizes
- The opinion reiterates Ciminelli’s core limit: allegations that merely the receipt or denial of economic information was deprived do not suffice to charge fraud under the right‑to‑control theory. Constantinescu III.A. So public posts that only (if proved) withheld or distorted information without inducing purchases would risk dismissal.
- The government still must plead the usual elements: material misrepresentation, intent to deceive, and that obtaining money/property was an object of the scheme. Constantinescu § III (citing Evans, Kousisis).

Practical takeaway for pleading
- To satisfy “in connection with” where fraud involves social media, plead (as the government did here):
  1. concrete allegations that defendants posted material falsehoods,
  2. those posts were intended to and were likely to induce recipients to purchase the security, and
  3. the defendant obtained money/property as a result (e.g., sold shares at inflated prices).
- Mere allegations of depriving the public of information, without an allegation that the deception induced purchases (the transfer of money/property), will not meet the statute under Ciminelli.

Bottom line: public social‑media posts can satisfy § 1348’s “in connection with” requirement so long as the indictment alleges a fraudulent‑inducement — i.e., that the posts were made to induce purchases (the transfer of money/property) and that obtaining that money/property was the object of the scheme. Constantinescu reverses dismissal on precisely that theory.

### Why proceed under § 1348 instead of § 10(b)/Rule 10b-5, and what elements differ in the criminal context (e.g., reliance, loss causation)?
Question:
The conduct sounds like classic pump-and-dump manipulation often charged under § 10(b) and Rule 10b-5, but this case proceeds under § 1348. Does § 1348 avoid civil-law concepts like reliance and loss causation that sometimes appear in § 10(b) cases? In a criminal § 1348 prosecution, what must the government prove about materiality, causation, or market impact compared to a Rule 10b-5 theory?
Answer:
Short answer
- The government proceeded under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 because this is a criminal securities‑fraud statute that targets schemes to obtain money or property by deception. As the Fifth Circuit explains in United States v. Constantinescu, § 1348 borrows the mail/wire‑fraud framework and asks whether the indictment plausibly alleges (1) a scheme to defraud (a material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain something of value) and (2) an intent to defraud (an intent to deceive and to obtain money/property or cause harm).  
- That criminal framework does not require the civil Rule 10b‑5 proof problems that often arise—most importantly, it does not rest on the “right‑to‑control” theory condemned in Ciminelli and, under Kousisis as applied in Constantinescu, does not require proof that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. But the government still must allege inducement to part with money or property (here, that followers were induced to buy securities) and that obtaining money/property was an object of the scheme.

What the opinion actually holds (doctrine and proof)
- The Fifth Circuit frames § 1348 in the mail/wire fraud mold: securities fraud under § 1348 requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” A “scheme to defraud” means a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” An “intent to defraud” requires an intent both to deceive and to cause some harm or to obtain money/property. (Constantinescu, § III; quoting Greenlaw and Evans.)
- Constantinescu distinguishes two theories: the rejected “right‑to‑control” theory (depriving someone of valuable economic information alone is not a protected property interest — Ciminelli) and the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory (inducing someone to hand over money or property through a lie). The indictment here alleged fraudulent inducement (followers were induced to purchase securities), so it did not run afoul of Ciminelli. (Constantinescu § III.A.)
- The court also relies on Kousisis to reject the notion that the government must prove a victim was left economically worse off. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The indictment’s allegation that defendants “pumped and dumped” and thus sought money was sufficient to allege intent to defraud. (Constantinescu § III.B; citing Kousisis.)

What the government must (and need not) prove in a criminal § 1348 prosecution, per Constantinescu
- Must prove (allegations the indictment must plausibly make):  
  - Material misrepresentations or deceptive conduct (the “scheme to defraud”); and  
  - Intent to deceive and to obtain money/property (or to cause harm) — i.e., the object of the scheme was to obtain money (here, by selling after pumping). The indictment should allege the deceptive communications induced victims to part with money/property (e.g., that followers bought the stock because of the misrepresentations). (Constantinescu §§ II–III.)  
- Does not require (as an element of the criminal charge, per the opinion):  
  - Proof that the victims were deprived only of “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory) — that cannot by itself support fraud under Ciminelli; and  
  - Proof that the victims suffered net pecuniary loss or that the fraudster intended to leave victims worse off — Kousisis makes clear the government need not show net loss. (Constantinescu §§ III.A–B.)

Where “reliance,” “causation,” and “market impact” fit in practice
- Reliance/inducement: Constantinescu treats the fraudulent‑inducement theory as focused on whether the defendant used a material misstatement to trick victims into a transaction that required handing over money/property. So while the government need not plead the private‑law elements of a Rule 10b‑5 civil claim, it must plausibly allege that victims were induced to act (i.e., that the misrepresentations caused followers to buy). The indictment’s allegation that defendants “induced their followers to purchase securities” supplies that element. (Constantinescu § III.A.)  
- Loss causation/ net loss: The court says the government need not prove the victims were made worse off overall; obtaining money/property for the defendant suffices as the object of the fraud even if the victim received market‑value securities or some offsetting benefit. (Constantinescu § III.B; citing Kousisis and Baker.)  
- Market impact: Allegations that the deception “artificially inflat[ed]” the price and enabled defendants to sell at a profit are offered in the opinion as one plausible way to show how the scheme produced the defendants’ ill‑gotten gains. Proving market impact or price inflation will be one evidentiary path to proving inducement and that the object was to obtain money/property, but the opinion does not adopt an independent extra element requiring market‑wide impact distinct from the scheme/intent inquiry.

Practical takeaway for a pump‑and‑dump prosecution
- Prosecutors use § 1348 when they want to criminally punish schemes to obtain money/property by deception. Under Constantinescu they can proceed on a fraudulent‑inducement theory: allege material misstatements, allege the defendant intended to deceive and obtain money, and allege that victims were induced to purchase (i.e., parted with money or property). They do not have to prove the narrower civil elements of reliance and loss causation in the same way civil plaintiffs must, nor can they rely solely on a right‑to‑control theory based on withholding information. (Constantinescu §§ III.A–B.)

If you want to work through how an indictment should be pleaded in this posture, you can map each required allegation in Constantinescu to the facts the government will need to allege (material false statements; specific acts showing intent to deceive and to obtain money; facts showing followers were induced to buy; and allegations explaining how defendants realized gains).

### What exactly must an indictment allege under Rule 12 to survive a motion to dismiss for failure to state an offense?
Question:
The court emphasizes taking the indictment’s allegations as true and only checking for the material elements. In practical terms, could defendants argue at this stage that the followers got fair value or that posts were not actually misleading, or are those purely trial issues? How specific must the indictment be about the mechanics of the scheme (who bought from whom, timing of trades, which posts were false) to provide fair notice and double-jeopardy protection under Fifth Circuit standards?
Answer:
Short answer
At the Rule 12(b)(6) pleading stage the court asks only whether the indictment, taken as true, alleges each material element of the charged offenses with enough factual particularity to give fair notice and protect against double jeopardy. Defendants cannot normally prevail on a motion to dismiss by inviting the court to resolve factual disputes (e.g., that followers received “fair value” or that the posts weren’t misleading). Those are defenses or factual issues for trial (or for a motion for acquittal), unless the indictment itself fails to allege an essential element.

What the opinion requires (doctrine and standards)
- Standard for pleading sufficiency: the court takes the indictment’s allegations as true and reviews de novo whether the indictment “allege[s] each prima facie element of the charged offense,” and fairly inform[s] the defendant and provide[s] a double-jeopardy defense. (United States v. Rafoi; Fontenot; Gaytan; Guzman‑Ocampo, cited at II of the opinion.)
- Elements for § 1348 securities fraud (as used by the court): (1) a “scheme to defraud” (a material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain something of value), and (2) an “intent to defraud” (intent to deceive and to cause harm to result from the deceit). The court relied on Greenlaw and Evans for that framing. (See III.)
- Interaction with Ciminelli and Kousisis: Ciminelli rejects a “right‑to‑control” theory that treats deprivation of valuable economic information alone as a protected property interest. By contrast, the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory—alleging that the defendant tricked victims into handing over money or property (e.g., inducing purchases)—does allege deprivation of money/property and is a valid basis for fraud charges. Kousisis confirms the government need not allege that the victim suffered net pecuniary loss; it is enough that the defendant sought to obtain money or property by deception. (See III.A–B.)

Applying these points to your practical questions
- Can defendants argue at the pleading stage that followers got fair value or that posts were not actually misleading?
  - Generally no. On a Rule 12 motion the court does not resolve factual disputes or credit arguments that contradict the indictment’s pleaded facts. As the Fifth Circuit explained, the court must accept the indictment’s allegations (here: buying a security, posting false and misleading information to induce purchases, and secretly selling for profit) and ask whether those allegations, if true, allege the material elements of § 1348. Challenges that the victims actually received fair value or that the statements were not misleading are factual defenses for trial or for a post‑trial motion for acquittal—unless the indictment itself contains no allegation of a material misrepresentation or intent to obtain money/property. (See II, III.A–B.)
- How specific must the indictment be about the mechanics (who bought from whom, timing of trades, which posts were false)?
  - The Fifth Circuit requires that an indictment allege “each material element” and give fair notice and double‑jeopardy protection (Gaytan; Guzman‑Ocampo). But it does not require minute transactional detail at the pleading stage. In Constantinescu the court found adequate an indictment that alleged the scheme’s essential mechanics: defendants purchased securities, posted false/misleading information to induce others to buy (thereby inflating price), and then sold for profit (alleging a $114 million profit). Those allegations were enough to allege a fraudulent‑inducement scheme and intent to obtain money/property.
  - Practical rule of thumb from the opinion: the indictment should identify the nature of the scheme, the role of each defendant, the means used to deceive (e.g., posting false/misleading information), and the object (inducing purchases / obtaining money or property), plus sufficient temporal and contextual detail to put the defendant on notice and to prevent reprosecution for the same conduct. It need not list every individual post, every purchaser, or every trade timestamp to survive a 12(b)(6) attack—so long as the core elements and factual context are pleaded rather than mere boilerplate.
  - Conversely, an indictment that is purely conclusory (alleging fraud in the abstract without alleging a material misrepresentation, a deceptive means, or an intent to obtain money/property) risks dismissal. That is the line drawn between inadequate pleading (dismissible) and a sufficient factual indictment (for trial).

Takeaway
At the pleading stage the Fifth Circuit asks whether the indictment, accepted as true, alleges the material elements of a fraudulent‑inducement scheme to obtain money or property. Arguments that victims got fair value or that statements were not misleading are typically defensive/factual and belong at trial (or in an evidence motion), unless the indictment itself fails to allege an essential element (material misrepresentation, intent to obtain money/property). The indictment need not plead every minutia of mechanics, but it must include sufficient factual detail to allege the elements and to give defendants fair notice and double‑jeopardy protection—as the court found the superseding indictment did in Constantinescu.
