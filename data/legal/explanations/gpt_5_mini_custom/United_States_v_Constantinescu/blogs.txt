Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# From Right-to-Control to Fraudulent Inducement: The Fifth Circuit’s Property Analysis in Constantinescu

## Why this case matters
United States v. Constantinescu is the latest appellate nod that criminal law can reach modern, influencer-driven “pump and dump” schemes without collapsing into the right‑to‑control pitfalls the Supreme Court cautioned against in Ciminelli. The Fifth Circuit reversed a district court’s dismissal of securities‑fraud charges under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, holding that an indictment alleging social‑media hyping that induced followers to buy securities adequately pleads a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite intent to obtain money or property. The opinion is important for two reasons. First, it shows how courts can apply the Supreme Court’s recent property‑focused decisions while still permitting criminal liability for deceptive inducements that cause victims to part with money. Second, it signals that § 1348 is likely to remain a go‑to federal vehicle for prosecuting market‑touting influencers whose lies translate into sales and profits.

## The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning, in brief
The appeal turned on two related doctrinal limits: (1) Ciminelli’s rejection of the right‑to‑control theory (criminal liability cannot rest solely on depriving someone of valuable economic information) and (2) the Supreme Court’s validation of the fraudulent‑inducement theory in Kousisis (a defendant violates the fraud statutes where he uses material misstatements to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property, even if the victim does not suffer a net loss). Taking the indictment’s allegations as true, the Fifth Circuit concluded that Constantinescu does not rely on a right‑to‑control theory; it instead alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme. The indictment says defendants knowingly posted false and misleading statements to induce followers to buy securities, thereby inflating the price so defendants could sell at a profit. Those allegations, the court held, sufficiently identify a scheme to obtain money or property and an intent to defraud under § 1348. The fact that investors received securities in return or may not have suffered a net loss does not defeat the indictment, because Kousisis squarely dispensed with any requirement that the victim be left economically worse off.

## Why the court’s framing matters
The opinion walks a careful line that maps onto the Supreme Court’s recent freighted distinctions. Ciminelli closed a tempting shortcut for prosecutors—criminalizing the withholding of information or the diminution of a supposed “right to control” a decider’s choice. Constantinescu, by contrast, characterizes the defendant’s conduct as affirmative misrepresentations that induced purchases. That difference is determinative: fraud statutes protect traditional money‑and‑property interests, and when a false statement procures a transfer of money or property as an object of the scheme, the statutory elements are satisfied.

Two features of the court’s framing deserve attention. First, the court stresses that “obtaining” money or property need not mean leaving the victim strictly worse off. This is critical for market cases: when an influencer’s hype causes purchases, victims may later have marketable securities that are worth something, but the government’s theory is that the defendant’s object was to get purchasers’ money into his hands (or to profit by selling into the artificially inflated market). Second, the court treats the line between protected information and property loss as bright when the alleged scheme produces a concrete transactional consequence—a purchase—rather than merely distorting a decisionmaker’s informational baseline. That is the doctrinal hinge between Ciminelli’s limitation and Kousisis’s allowable theory.

## Doctrinal implications and prosecutorial practice
Constantinescu has three foreseeable doctrinal effects.

First, it reinforces that § 1348 prosecutions can be tailored to modern market manipulation by distinguishing fraudulent inducement from right‑to‑control allegations. Prosecutors need to plead (and later prove) that deceptive statements were aimed at causing purchases (or other transfers) and that obtaining money or property was an object of the scheme. Indictments that allege only that defendants suppressed or distorted information without showing a transfer will still run afoul of Ciminelli.

Second, the decision encourages the Department of Justice to lean on § 1348 in influencer and social‑media hyping cases. The Fifth Circuit explicitly noted that § 1348 “borrows key concepts” from mail and wire fraud, and it applied the same property analysis. Because § 1348 is tailored to securities, it presents a straightforward criminal lever where the inducement causes purchases of securities that finance the fraudster’s gain. Expect more indictments of this kind where the pleading shows a causal link between hype and purchases and alleges the defendant obtained proceeds (even if indirectly).

Third, Constantinescu foregrounds—but does not resolve—several evidentiary and causation challenges that will shape trial litigation. The government must contend with the reality that victims often transact in opaque, multi‑party markets: followers typically buy from other market participants, not the influencer. Under Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit’s reading, that indirectness is not dispositive if the defendant’s object was to obtain money or property via the induced purchases (for example, by selling into the inflated market or otherwise capturing the benefit). But proving that the defendant “had money in mind” and that purchases were caused by the misrepresentations will be contested at trial.

## Open questions and boundaries
Constantinescu leaves important questions open about the contours of “property” and the limits of criminal enforcement in influencer economies.

One unresolved issue is the precise boundary between a fraudulent inducement and mere persuasion or puffery. Social media is rife with opinion, boasting, and trading chatter. Courts will have to police the line between constitutionally protected opinion or non‑actionable puffery and materially false statements of fact that a reasonable investor could rely upon. The Fifth Circuit’s focus on allegations of false and misleading posts is a necessary, but not always sufficient, safeguard; the equipment at trial will be proving falsity and materiality.

Another open issue concerns how to treat “indirect” realizations of gain. If an influencer never sells the hyped security but benefits through correlated positions, affiliate payouts, or other indirect streams, does that qualify as “obtaining” victims’ money under § 1348? Constantinescu suggests courts will look to whether obtaining money or property was an object of the fraud, but the line between causal object and incidental benefit will be litigated.

A related question is the degree of precision required in indictments. The Fifth Circuit emphasized the indictment’s allegations that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Prosecutors should heed that formulation: pleadings must tie the alleged misstatements to the induced transfers and to the defendant’s profit motive. Bare allegations of “pump and dump” without factual predicates tying statements to purchases and to an objective of obtaining money risk dismissal under Ciminelli’s shadow.

Finally, there are broader normative and constitutional concerns. Criminalizing market speech risks chilling commentary on platforms where millions follow financial advice. The court’s approach preserves space for robust prosecution of objectively false, deceitful inducements, but careful jury instructions and proof requirements—materiality, scienter, causation—will be crucial to avoid overbroad application.

## Practical downstream effects
Practitioners should expect immediate tactical shifts. Prosecutors will be more willing to charge social‑media hyping under § 1348 where the factual record shows coordinated deception intended to drive purchases. Defenders will double down on challenges to materiality, causation, and the sufficiency of allegations that the defendant “obtained” victims’ money. Securities regulators and DOJ may coordinate more closely: SEC investigations often produce the records prosecutors need to show inducement and profit. And courts in other circuits will likely be asked to apply the same Ciminelli‑Kousisis framework to influencer contexts, which could produce further refinement or, conceivably, inconsistent lines on the limits of “property.”

## Conclusion
Constantinescu is a useful blueprint for marrying the Supreme Court’s recent restrictions on right‑to‑control theories with an analytically coherent criminal response to fraudulent inducements in modern markets. The Fifth Circuit did not rewrite the property element of fraud statutes; it applied the Supreme Court’s guidance to hold that inducing purchases by materially false statements targets money and property, not merely information. The opinion clears a doctrinal path for criminal prosecutions of pump‑and‑dump influencers, but it also keeps front and center the evidentiary guardrails—materiality, scienter, causation—that will determine how far that path extends.



## Intent Without Loss? Mens Rea Under § 1348 After Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit’s Take

Why this matters: The Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu is an important early test of how lower courts will apply the Supreme Court’s recent fraud decisions—most notably Kousisis v. United States and Ciminelli v. United States—when prosecuting modern, social-media-driven “pump and dump” schemes under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. At issue is a basic doctrinal question with big practical consequences: what must the government prove about a defendant’s mens rea when the alleged scheme enriches the promoter but leaves victims holding marketable securities (or otherwise receiving value) so that they may not show a net loss? The Fifth Circuit says that, consistent with Kousisis, a profit motive can satisfy the required intent to defraud so long as the indictment alleges a fraudulent inducement to part with money or property.

How the panel reasoned: The panel began by situating § 1348 within the broader fraud jurisprudence, borrowing concepts developed under the mail- and wire-fraud statutes. That framing set up the two core elements: (1) a “scheme to defraud” involving a material misrepresentation and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Defendants conceded deception, so the dispute focused on whether the indictment alleged both a scheme to obtain money or property and the requisite intent.

The opinion leans heavily on two Supreme Court benchmarks. First, Ciminelli foreclosed convictions premised solely on a “right-to-control” theory—that is, a theory that treats the victim’s right to valuable information as the protected property interest. The Fifth Circuit was careful to distinguish Constantinescu: the indictment here was not trying to criminalize withholding or manipulating informational control alone; instead, it alleged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme in which influencers made false or misleading statements to induce followers to buy securities (i.e., to transfer money or property).

Second, and crucially, the panel relied on Kousisis for the proposition that the fraud statutes do not require evidence that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. Kousisis accepts the fraudulent-inducement theory: a fraud is complete when a defendant induces a victim to transfer money or property by material misrepresentations, even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return or the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. Applying that principle, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment adequately alleged defendants “had money in mind,” that they used deception to induce purchases, and that obtaining money was an object of the fraud. Those allegations suffice to plead intent to defraud under § 1348.

Putting the decision in context: The panel’s approach is straightforward in one sense: it follows Kousisis’ textual logic and rejects the district court’s dismissal that depended on a hyper-technical reading of “property” that would exempt many modern frauds built on inducement rather than pure information control. But the opinion also highlights a doctrinal seam running through recent fraud law. Prior decisions (and traditional formulations) describe intent to defraud as requiring an intent both to deceive and to cause some harm to result from the deceit. Kousisis narrows what “harm” means for the fraudulent-inducement theory—harm is realized through the induced transfer of money or property, not necessarily through a showing of net loss. The Fifth Circuit embraces that refinement while acknowledging Ciminelli’s limiting principle: information alone isn’t a “protected” property interest absent a showing that deception produced a transfer of money or property.

Why “having money in mind” matters—and what it doesn’t: The phrase “had money in mind,” drawn from Kousisis and echoed here, is doing real doctrinal work. It signals that the prosecution’s theory must show the defendant sought to obtain money or property as an objective of the scheme. That is different from prosecuting someone merely for hyping a stock or making optimistic predictions. To survive the kinds of pretrial challenges before the district court here, an indictment must allege (and later the government must prove) purposeful deception aimed at eliciting purchases or transfers. Secret selling after pumping, coordinated timing, demonstrable profits tied to misleading posts, and instructions that targets act now are the kinds of facts that make the “money in mind” inference plausible.

Defense levers and practical fallout: The Fifth Circuit’s ruling will sharpen defense strategies in several predictable ways. First, defendants will press Ciminelli’s boundary—arguing that alleged misconduct is really only about manipulation of information or opinion, not inducement to transfer money. When conduct looks more like puffing or opinion-based promotion, defendants will urge dismissals or favorable jury instructions insulating speech and predictive statements. Second, defendants will litigate mens rea vigorously: they will press evidentiary challenges to show lack of specific intent to deceive (e.g., that posts were careless, optimistic, or non-actionable opinion) or that defendants lacked intent to obtain money (arguing instead motives like self-promotion, platform growth, or liquidating positions for unrelated reasons). Third, defenses will emphasize materiality—if alleged statements were non-actionable hype rather than materially false representations about a company’s business or trading positions, the fraudulent-inducement theory is weaker.

For prosecutors and courts, the ruling foreshadows practical conflicts at the jury-instruction stage. Judges will need to craft instructions that reflect Kousisis’ principle—explaining that a defendant can be guilty even when a victim receives something of value in return—while also making clear that mere profit-seeking without deception, or mere expression of opinion, does not constitute fraud. Trial courts will face the hard task of explaining where persuasive advocacy or puffery ends and criminal deception begins. That balance will be especially fraught in cases involving social-media influencers whose business model blends commentary, self-branding, and trading.

Doctrinal implications and what may come next: Constantinescu reinforces a post-Kousisis landscape in which prosecutors can pursue criminal liability for inducements that produce transfers of money or property even if victims are not left economically worse off. At the same time, the decision preserves the Ciminelli guardrail: courts must be vigilant to ensure prosecutions allege an actual transfer of money or property induced by deception rather than mere manipulation of information or discretionary choice.

Expect several downstream effects. First, the decision invites more criminal enforcement against social-media pump-and-dump schemes; indeed, indictments that properly plead inducement and “money in mind” will be harder to dismiss. Second, defense-oriented litigation will increasingly target the line-drawing between puffery and materially false statements, and between information-control theories barred by Ciminelli and inducement-based theories permitted by Kousisis. Finally, this area is ripe for further appellate guidance—either clarifying what quantum of proof is needed to infer intent to obtain money from a promoter’s conduct or further refining how courts should instruct juries about “harm” and “value” in fraudulent-inducement cases.

Bottom line for practitioners: For prosecutors, the lesson is to plead and prove inducement—tie false statements to induced transfers and to demonstrable profits. For defense counsel, the path forward is to keep pressing Ciminelli’s limits, attack proofs of deliberate deception, and demand careful jury instructions that distinguish criminal deception from risky speech or harmless market activity. Courts will be the arbiters of that line, and Constantinescu shows how lower courts are beginning to reconcile the modern realities of online trading fraud with the Supreme Court’s recent doctrinal recalibration.



# What an Indictment Must Say: Pleading Standards for Criminal Securities Fraud in the Fifth Circuit

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is already shaping the contours of how prosecutors must plead—and how courts must review—social‑media “pump‑and‑dump” securities cases. At a moment when influencers and self‑styled traders can move millions of dollars of market value with a single post, the Fifth Circuit’s decision pulls the pleading rules into sharp focus. The court reversed a district court dismissal and made clear that an indictment that alleges the mechanics of a pump‑and‑dump and ties false statements to induced purchases and ill‑gotten profits can survive a facial sufficiency challenge under 18 U.S.C. § 1348.

## The procedural backbone: how courts review indictments

The opinion is a useful primer on pleading law in criminal securities prosecutions. The court reiterated the familiar but important principles: indictment sufficiency is reviewed de novo; courts accept the indictment’s allegations as true when deciding a facial challenge; and an indictment must allege each material element of the charged offense, give the defendant fair notice of the charges, and protect against double jeopardy. The Fifth Circuit relied on its own precedents—Gaytan and Guzman‑Ocampo—and recent Supreme Court decisions interpreting the fraud statutes to frame what § 1348 requires: a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, with the scheme involving a material misrepresentation intended to obtain money or property.

That framework matters because defendants often seek to short‑circuit prosecutions at the indictment stage by arguing that alleged conduct—particularly speech—does not implicate the fraud statutes. The court’s analysis shows the limits of that early gatekeeping: an indictment that alleges deception plus an object of obtaining money/property passes muster even if many evidentiary issues remain for later.

## How the Constantinescu indictment cleared the bar

What carried the day for the government was concrete pleading of the pump‑and‑dump mechanics and the object of the fraud. The superseding indictment alleged that defendants, each with large social‑media followings, purchased securities, posted false and misleading statements to induce followers to buy (thereby “pumping” prices), and then secretly “dumped” shares to realize profits—$114 million in alleged gains. Those allegations track the material elements of § 1348: scheme to defraud (material misrepresentations to induce purchases) and intent to defraud (allegations that defendants sought to obtain money by inducing purchases and then sold their positions).

Importantly, the court addressed the specter of two Supreme Court cases that have recently constrained fraud prosecutions. Ciminelli rejected the “right‑to‑control” theory—you cannot predicate fraud solely on depriving someone of valuable economic information. The Fifth Circuit distinguished that scenario here: the indictment alleged not mere withholding of information but affirmative inducement to part with money/property (the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory). And Kousisis clarified that the prosecution need not plead that the fraudster intended to leave victims worse off economically; it is enough that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the scheme. The Fifth Circuit applied those rulings to hold that pleading inducement of purchases and illicit profits sufficiently alleges both the scheme and intent elements.

## What the court left open

Although the indictment survived, the opinion leaves significant battlegrounds for later proceedings. Materiality and causation—how the alleged false statements actually affected market behavior and whether individual followers purchased (and thus transferred money or property) because of particular posts—were not resolved at the pleading stage. Proof that followers relied on specific misrepresentations, that posts were materially false rather than nonactionable opinion or mere puffery, and that the defendants’ sales were traceably connected to the induced purchases, will all be fought at discovery, at trial, and on motions in limine.

First Amendment defenses also loom. The opinion does not decide when market‑talking posts cross the line into fraud, and it acknowledges the doctrinal difference between actionable misrepresentations and protected expression. That distinction—especially in cases involving recommendations, analysis, or “hot tips” on social platforms—will be a central question at later stages.

Finally, scienter and the specificity of the alleged scheme (who said what, when, to whom, and with what contemporaneous trading) are factual issues that the court expressly left for later proof rather than pleading.

## Practice takeaways

For prosecutors: Constantinescu is a roadmap for what an indictment should say in social‑media securities cases. Plead the elements: identify the statute (§ 1348 and conspiracy § 1349), describe the fraudulent‑inducement theory, and set out who made which statements, on what platform, when, and to how many followers. Allegations tying posts temporally to induced buying and to subsequent secret sales and proceeds make the “object” of obtaining money clear—especially in light of Kousisis. Quantify alleged gains where possible and describe concealment or secrecy to support intent. Avoid reliance on a right‑to‑control theory alone; frame the conduct as inducing purchases (i.e., victims were induced to part with money/property).

For defense counsel: Constantinescu narrows—but does not eliminate—early dismissal routes. Challenges that the indictment relies on a right‑to‑control theory will now be less likely to succeed where the government alleges induced purchases and gains. Defenders should press for a bill of particulars to pin down dates, posts, follower counts, trading records, and the alleged link between posts and specific purchases. Preserve First Amendment objections by developing the record on whether statements were opinion or puffery, and attack scienter and causation through targeted discovery (platform metadata, trading timestamps, and communications showing intent). Where allegations remain generalized or conclusory, renew motions to dismiss and press for specific pleading under Rule 7 and the Fifth Circuit’s precedents.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu clarifies that, at the indictment stage in the Fifth Circuit, prosecutors do not need to plead proof of victim net loss or the granular causal chain from post to purchase to sale. They do, however, need to allege a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: material misstatements intended to and resulting in victims’ parting with money or property and the fraudster’s receipt of ill‑gotten gains. The decision opens the door wider for criminal enforcement of social‑media‑driven market manipulation—but leaves substantial doctrinal and evidentiary fights for the trials ahead, where materiality, causation, scienter, and First Amendment limits will be waged on the merits.



# Influencers, Pump-and-Dumps, and the Next Wave of ‘FinTok’ Prosecutions

## Why Constantinescu matters

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal in United States v. Constantinescu is one of the clearest signals yet that federal prosecutors have a viable path to use the securities-fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, against social-media “influencers” who tout stocks and then quietly sell into the induced demand. At issue was nothing exotic: the government’s superseding indictment alleges a classic pump-and-dump in which a group of well-followed posters bought securities, posted false and misleading statements to inflate prices, and then “dumped” their positions for roughly $114 million in alleged proceeds. The district court had dismissed the indictment on the ground that what was alleged was only a deprivation of “valuable economic information,” a theory the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli. The Fifth Circuit reversed, framing the allegations as a permissible fraudulent-inducement theory under Kousisis: defendants are accused of tricking followers into parting with money or property (by buying securities), not merely withholding or controlling information.

This is not a novelty prosecution shoehorned into an ill-fitting statute. Rather, Constantinescu shows how recent Supreme Court decisions have clarified the doctrinal lines prosecutors must clear—and the contours of what is still protected speech.

## What the court held and why its reasoning matters

The panel took doctrinal care in two ways. First, it distinguished the indictment from the “right-to-control” theory invalidated in Ciminelli. In Ciminelli the fraud prosecution depended on the idea that depriving a public actor of valuable economic information about contracting opportunities could constitute the kind of traditional “property” the federal fraud statutes protect; the Court said it could not. Here, by contrast, the Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the indictment alleges active inducement to buy securities—that is, the followers parted with money (or property) as the direct object of the deception. Under the Court’s later guidance in Kousisis, that fraudulent-inducement theory is a proper basis for a § 1348 prosecution because it protects money and property, not a amorphous right to control information.

Second, the panel relied on Kousisis to reject the defendants’ argument that fraud requires an intent to leave the victim economically worse off. Kousisis made clear that obtaining money or property by deceit is the relevant object, regardless of whether the fraudster provides something of equal value or whether the victim’s net position is ultimately harmed. The indictment’s allegation that the influencers posted falsehoods to induce purchases and then sold for profit was therefore enough, at the pleading stage, to allege intent to defraud.

Put succinctly: the Fifth Circuit treated on-platform touting as classic fraudulent inducement when the government alleges concrete misrepresentations about positions and prospects designed to make followers buy—and when the defendant secretly plans to exit.

## Drawing the line from puffery to criminal fraud

A central practical question is where the boundary lies between actionable fraud and non-actionable opinion or “sales puffery.” The opinion leaves room for the usual First Amendment and fraud-law protections. Materiality and deception remain essential: blanket cheerleading—“this stock’s a rocket!”—is qualitatively different from a knowingly false statement about one’s trading position (“I just bought 10,000 shares and I’m holding for months”) or a dishonest claim about imminent corporate developments. The court’s reasoning tracks that conventional divide. The fraudulent-inducement theory requires a material misrepresentation intended to cause the victim to part with money or property; mere enthusiasm or hyperbole without a false factual predicate will not meet that standard.

But Constantinescu places particular weight on lies that affect a follower’s transactional choice—claims about current holdings, purchase history, timing, or plans to sell. An influencer who explicitly promises not to sell before followers accumulate positions, or who claims to be buying when in fact offloading, risks fitting squarely within the fraudulent-inducement frame the court endorsed.

## Historical context: scalping, modern amplification, and enforcement appetite

The decision fits into a longstanding enforcement concern—“scalping” or undisclosed self-dealing in market promotion—that predates social media. Regulators have long been wary of coordinated market promotion where the promoter secretly profits from the very demand they create. What’s new is scale and velocity: platforms, algorithmic feeds, and private channels like Discord and Telegram allow promoters to reach large, receptive audiences quickly and to coordinate narratives in near real time.

Constantinescu gives the Department of Justice a doctrinally grounded lever to pursue these modern scalpers under § 1348 when the allegations allege deceptive inducement to transact. That does not make every misstatement a crime, but it does sharpen prosecutorial focus on demonstrable falsehoods about trades, positions, exit plans, and undisclosed incentives.

## Spillover beyond equities and interaction with other statutes

Although Constantinescu resolves the § 1348 question for securities, its practical reach will depend on whether the marketed asset is legally a “security.” For equities the fit is obvious. For many digital assets—crypto tokens, NFTs, certain ICOs—the securities analysis is fact-intensive (Howey and its progeny), and some tokens will fall outside § 1348. In those cases prosecutors still have options: traditional wire and mail fraud statutes, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 & 1343, share many doctrinal ancestors with § 1348 and can be used when the nexus to a “security” is frail or absent. The Fifth Circuit itself acknowledged the circuit’s tendency to borrow mail/wire fraud concepts when construing § 1348.

That prosecutorial flexibility means promoters of non-equity assets are not in a safe harbor merely because an instrument isn’t a security. Deceptive inducements to hand over money in exchange for any asset may attract wire-fraud prosecutions, and regulatory referrals to the SEC can bring parallel civil enforcement and disgorgement claims if securities laws are implicated.

## Practical takeaways for influencers and community operators

For individuals with sizable followings and for those who operate or moderate investor-oriented communities, the Constantinescu decision crystallizes several practical compliance points. First, avoid specific factual lies about your holdings, transactions, or plans: false statements about purchases, holdings, or intended exit points are the sort of allegations that sustain fraudulent-inducement counts. Second, be transparent about compensation or conflicts of interest. While the opinion does not rest on statutory disclosure regimes, undisclosed paid promotion or secret coordination is exactly the kind of fact that makes a misrepresentation material. Third, documentation and contemporaneous records matter: prosecutors often rely on messaging histories, transaction records, and platform analytics at the investigative stage, so maintaining clear business practices can be protective. Fourth, private, invitation-only channels raise risks: Discord and Telegram groups that facilitate coordinated buying and rapid dissemination magnify the chances that a prosecutor will find an intent-to-defraud inference.

For platforms and counsel, the ruling argues for clearer policies on financial recommendation labeling, paid-promotion disclosure, and retention of messages and payment records that could be relevant to investigations.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu does not criminalize enthusiasm, nor does it turn every influencer’s off-the-cuff market take into a federal offense. But it draws a consequential doctrinal line: knowingly false, material misstatements intended to induce followers to part with money can be prosecuted under § 1348 as fraudulent inducement. In an era of FinTok, pump-and-dump conspiracies can be scaled with unprecedented speed, and the decision hands prosecutors a coherent theory—rooted in Kousisis and careful to avoid Ciminelli’s forbidden “right-to-control” framing—to pursue those who allegedly manufacture demand by deception. For influencers, community operators, and would-be promoters of emerging assets, the prudent course is transparency and restraint; for enforcement watchers, Constantinescu is likely the opening chapter in a new wave of cases targeting online market manipulation.
