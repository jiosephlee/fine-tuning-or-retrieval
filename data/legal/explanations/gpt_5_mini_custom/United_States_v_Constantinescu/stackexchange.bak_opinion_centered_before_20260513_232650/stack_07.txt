Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### Are misstatements about one’s trading position and intent to sell considered "material" under § 1348?
Question:
The opinion notes defendants "concede deception," so it doesn’t dig into materiality. In a pump-and-dump case, are false statements about the speaker’s current holdings, purchases, or plans to sell treated as material as a matter of law because they would influence a reasonable investor? How do courts differentiate between non-actionable puffery (e.g., rosy price targets) and actionable misrepresentations about personal trading activity or intent?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — at least at the pleading/indictment stage, false statements about a speaker’s holdings, purchases, or planned sales are the sort of specific factual representations that courts treat as plausibly “material” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 when the government alleges they were used to induce others to buy or sell. United States v. Constantinescu does not decide materiality (the defendants “concede deception”), but it treats allegations that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information” about their trading positions and then induced followers to purchase as sufficient to allege a § 1348 scheme under the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

Legal standard and how Constantinescu applies
- The opinion frames a § 1348 “scheme to defraud” as requiring a material misrepresentation intended to deceive and obtain something of value (see Constantinescu § III). Because the Court treated § 1348 as borrowing concepts from mail/wire fraud, the relevant inquiry is whether the misrepresentation was used to induce the victim to part with money or property.
- Constantinescu rejects the idea that this is merely a right‑to‑control/info case (Ciminelli): here the indictment alleged defendants “induced their followers . . . to purchase securities”—i.e., to hand over money—so the fraudulent‑inducement theory applies. Under that theory, misrepresentations that cause someone to buy securities are the paradigmatic kind of material misstatement the statute protects against (see Constantinescu § III.A–B, discussing Kousisis).
- The Court therefore treated allegations that defendants misrepresented their trading positions and posted false information about securities as sufficient at the pleading stage to allege a material misrepresentation tied to obtaining money (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14; Constantinescu §§ I, III).

Puffery vs. actionable misstatements about trading activity
- The division courts draw (and Constantinescu implicitly draws) is practical: vague, aspirational, or promotional statements about future price performance (classic “puffery”) are often treated as non‑actionable because they are general opinion or prediction, not likely to be the kind of specific, verifiable fact that induces a purchaser to part with money.
- By contrast, statements about one’s present holdings, recent purchases, or present intent to sell are concrete, factual, and directly probative of whether a follower will buy or hold. In the pump‑and‑dump context those kinds of statements are exactly the kind of misrepresentations alleged to have induced purchases, so courts treat them as plausibly material (Constantinescu’s indictment specifically alleges misrepresentations about “their trading positions” used to induce purchases).
- Put another way (consistent with Constantinescu): specificity, verifiability, and a direct causal link to the alleged induced transaction push a statement toward materiality; vagueness, general optimism, or unverifiable predictions push it towards puffery.

What this means in practice
- At the indictment/pleading stage: allegations that influencers lied about holdings/intent to sell in order to induce purchases are sufficient to plead a § 1348 fraudulent‑inducement scheme (as the Fifth Circuit held in Constantinescu).
- At later stages (summary judgment, trial): materiality becomes a fact question in most cases; only statements that are obviously immaterial or pure puffery as a matter of law will be disposed of before trial. Constantinescu shows the court will not adopt a bright‑line rule excusing misrepresentations about trading activity merely because defendants claim they were trying to withhold “information” rather than steal property.

Bottom line
Constantinescu supports the commonsense rule courts use in pump‑and‑dump prosecutions: specific false statements about one’s holdings, purchases, or intent to sell are the sort of factual misrepresentations that will be treated as material for § 1348 purposes when they are alleged to have induced others to trade. By contrast, rosy, vague price predictions are more likely to be non‑actionable puffery; the dividing line is specificity, verifiability, and whether the statement is alleged to have directly induced the victim to hand over money.
