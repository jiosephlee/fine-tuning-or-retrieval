Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### If victims later profit or there’s no net transfer, how does Baker’s rejection of a mirror-image requirement affect injury?
Question:
The court cites Baker for the proposition that fraud does not require a mirror-image loss equal to the defendant’s gain. In a volatile market where some followers might profit despite the scheme, how does the government prove the requisite injury or deprivation? Is the act of inducing purchases with material falsehoods itself the legally cognizable deprivation, regardless of subsequent price movements and outcomes for particular investors?
Answer:
Short answer: Under the reasoning the Fifth Circuit applies in Constantinescu, Baker’s rejection of a “mirror‑image” requirement means the government need not show that every follower was left economically worse off or that the defendant’s gain exactly matched a particular follower’s loss. The legally cognizable deprivation for the fraudulent‑inducement theory is the victim parting with money or property because of a material misrepresentation — i.e., inducing a purchase — and later market movements do not negate that alleged deprivation for purposes of stating and proving the offense.

Why and how that follows from the opinion
- Doctrine the court uses. Constantinescu frames the case under the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” The opinion contrasts that with Ciminelli’s rejected “right‑to‑control” theory and cites Kousisis for the principle that fraud liability attaches when the defendant schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property “regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” 
- No mirror‑image requirement. The opinion relies on Baker to explain that the fraud statutes do not impose a “mirror image” rule requiring the victim’s loss to equal the defendant’s gain. Thus, even in a volatile market where some followers may later realize gains, Baker (as cited) supports treating the induced transfer of money/property itself as the relevant injury when that transfer was procured by fraud.
- What the government must allege and prove. As the court explains, the government must allege and ultimately prove (1) a scheme to defraud involving material misrepresentations intended to deceive, and (2) an intent to obtain money or property by those misrepresentations. At the indictment stage, alleging that defendants posted false and misleading information “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” is sufficient to allege the requisite deprivation. The subsequent fact that some investors later profited does not defeat the allegation that those investors were induced to part with money or property by fraud.

Practical corollaries (kept to what the opinion supports)
- Market outcomes after the inducement are not dispositive of the statutory injury element: the fraud is complete when the defendant induces the transfer under materially false pretenses. (See Kousisis as quoted in Constantinescu.)
- Evidence of later profits or losses may be relevant to contested factual issues at trial (e.g., causation, who relied, or the amount of loss for sentencing/evidence purposes), but they do not erase the government’s allegation that a deprivation occurred when victims were fraudulently induced to buy.

Bottom line: Under Constantinescu (following Kousisis and Baker), inducing purchases with material falsehoods is the legally cognizable deprivation — the victim’s parting with money/property — even if later market moves mean some followers net out ahead.
