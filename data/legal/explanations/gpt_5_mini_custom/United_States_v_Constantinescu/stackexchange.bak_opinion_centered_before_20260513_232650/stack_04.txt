Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### What level of factual specificity must a § 1348 indictment include at the motion-to-dismiss stage?
Question:
The court applies the standard that indictments need only allege each material element and be taken as true, and it reverses dismissal. For securities fraud based on social-media posts, how specific must the indictment be about which statements were false, which securities were involved, and when the posts occurred to satisfy Rule 7(c) and the notice/double-jeopardy functions? Is identifying the general pump-and-dump scheme and its mechanics enough, or do courts typically require more granularity in fraud indictments?
Answer:
Short answer
At the motion‑to‑dismiss stage under the Fifth Circuit’s view in United States v. Constantinescu, an indictment under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 need not allege every particular false tweet/post, every specific security transaction, or exact timestamps. What is required is a pleading that (1) alleges each material element of the offense (a scheme to defraud and intent to defraud), (2) supplies a factual narrative showing how the fraudulent‑inducement theory operated (false or misleading representations intended to induce purchases of securities), and (3) gives sufficient facts to fairly inform the defendants of the charge and to protect against later prosecutions. In Constantinescu the court held that a general pump‑and‑dump description — social‑media misrepresentations that induced followers to buy securities, followed by secret sales and alleged profits ($114 million) — met that standard and survived dismissal.

Why that is the standard (doctrine and the opinion)
- Governing principle: an indictment must allege each material element of the charged offense and is to be taken as true on a sufficiency review. The court cites United States v. Gaytan and Guzman‑Ocampo for the three functions an indictment must perform: (1) enumerate prima facie elements, (2) fairly inform the defendant, and (3) provide a double‑jeopardy defense. (Opinion § II.)
- Elements applied: § 1348 requires a “scheme to defraud” (material misrepresentation intended to obtain money or property) and an “intent to defraud.” The Fifth Circuit borrowed analysis from mail/wire fraud doctrine and looked to recent Supreme Court decisions (Ciminelli and Kousisis) to define permissible theories. (Opinion § III.)
- Ciminelli/Kousisis limits: the indictment cannot rest solely on a “right‑to‑control” theory (depriving victims of valuable economic information), but it can plead a fraudulent‑inducement theory — i.e., misrepresentations used to induce purchases so the defendant obtains money/property. Constantinescu held the indictment here pleads that theory and thus does not run afoul of Ciminelli. (Opinion § III.A–B.)

What the opinion shows about required factual specificity
- Sufficient factual content in Constantinescu: the superseding indictment described the mechanics of the pump‑and‑dump (purchase, post false/misleading social‑media statements, induce followers to buy, secretly sell), alleged that specific misrepresentations were used (misrepresenting trading positions and price potential), alleged the induced purchases, and alleged the profit amount ($114 million). The court treated those allegations as enough to plead both the scheme and the intent to obtain money/property. (Opinion § I, III.)
- What the court did not require at dismissal: the opinion does not demand that the indictment catalog every allegedly false post, name each security, or list exact dates and times of each posting/transaction before surviving a Rule 12(b)(3) challenge. The court accepted a general, factual narrative that tied misrepresentations to induced purchases and to the defendants’ monetary objective.

Practical takeaway for drafting/attacking indictments
- For a prosecutor: plead the material elements and a coherent factual narrative showing how misrepresentations caused victims to part with money or property (identify the scheme, the means of deception, the class of victims, and the object of the fraud). Alleging the scope (e.g., monies obtained) strengthens the indictment’s sufficiency.
- For a defendant attacking an indictment at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage: challenge whether the indictment actually alleges (a) a fraudulent‑inducement (not merely deprivation of information) and (b) an intent to obtain money/property. If the government’s allegations show that defendants induced purchases and obtained money, Constantinescu says dismissal is improper even without line‑by‑line recitation of each post or transaction.
- Caveat: the opinion addresses sufficiency at the pleading stage. Specificity needs may rise in other contexts (motions for a bill of particulars, trial, or for particularized discovery), but Constantinescu shows courts will accept a well‑pleaded, non‑conclusory scheme narrative rather than exhaustive transactional detail when deciding a motion to dismiss.

Bottom line: identifying a coherent pump‑and‑dump scheme, its mechanics (social‑media misrepresentations that induced followers to buy), and the fact that defendants obtained money (with an alleged amount) satisfied Rule 7(c)/notice/double‑jeopardy functions in Constantinescu; the court did not require pleading each individual false statement, each security, or exact posting dates at the motion‑to‑dismiss stage.
