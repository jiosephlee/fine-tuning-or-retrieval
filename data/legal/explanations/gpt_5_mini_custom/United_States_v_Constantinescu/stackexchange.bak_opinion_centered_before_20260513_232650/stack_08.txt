Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### For the § 1349 conspiracy count, must the government prove an overt act, or only an agreement and intent?
Question:
The indictment charges conspiracy to commit securities fraud under § 1349. In this circuit, does § 1349 require proof of an overt act, or is an agreement plus the requisite intent to commit § 1348 sufficient? How do courts typically prove a conspiratorial agreement in the context of coordinated social-media promotions and trading activity?
Answer:
Short answer: The Fifth Circuit in United States v. Constantinescu did not decide whether § 1349 requires proof of an overt act. The opinion reversed the district court on the sufficiency of the *substantive* securities‑fraud allegations (scheme and intent under § 1348) and says nothing about the overt‑act element of a § 1349 conspiracy count. From this opinion alone you cannot read a holdings‑level answer on the overt‑act question.

Why: Constantinescu’s analysis is expressly about whether the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme to obtain money or property and the requisite intent to defraud (relying on Ciminelli and Kousisis). The court framed the issue as whether the indictment “sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud” (Op. § II–IV) and reversed because the indictment alleges a fraudulent‑inducement theory (buy, “pump” via false social‑media posts, induce followers to buy, then “dump” for profit). The opinion does not analyze the elements of conspiracy under § 1349 (e.g., whether an overt act is required), nor does it alter the court’s discussion of the substantive § 1348 elements. See Op. § I (indictment charged a § 1349 conspiracy and § 1348 substantive counts) and § IV (reversal on sufficiency of scheme/intent).

What the opinion does show about proving an agreement in this factual context: although Constantinescu stops short of discussing conspiracy elements, it illustrates how a government can plead facts that support an inference of a coordinated fraudulent plan. The indictment the court upheld alleges a recurring, common pattern: defendants with large social‑media followings “would purchase a security, ‘pump’ the price by posting false and misleading information . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security, and ‘dump’ by secretly selling the security for a profit,” and it alleges a large aggregate profit ($114 million). Those kinds of allegations — repeated parallel conduct (buy → coordinated posts → sales), identity of misrepresentations, common objective (profiting by inflating price), and a co‑participant’s guilty plea (Knight) — are the same sorts of facts courts rely on to infer a conspiratorial agreement from the opinion’s perspective. See Op. § I–III (describing the alleged modus operandi and profit).

Bottom line for your research/practice: use Constantinescu to support that pleading a fraudulent‑inducement scheme and intent under § 1348 is sufficient to defeat a motion to dismiss on the substantive fraud theory. But do not cite it as deciding whether § 1349 itself requires an overt act — that question was neither briefed nor decided in the opinion. If you need a definitive answer on the overt‑act element in the Fifth Circuit, consult Fifth Circuit precedent treating conspiracy elements or pattern jury instructions (Constantinescu does not resolve that point).
