Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### Where do First Amendment concerns fit when the alleged fraud consists of social-media posts about stocks?
Question:
Defendants’ conduct is framed as deceptive posts about their positions and price targets. Are opinions, predictions, and hype about securities protected speech unless they include verifiable false statements of fact (e.g., lying about one’s holdings or intent to sell)? At the indictment stage, do courts ever dismiss § 1348 charges on First Amendment grounds, or are such defenses typically resolved after a factual record is developed?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Fifth Circuit in United States v. Constantinescu makes the practical point courts care about: speech about stocks (opinions, predictions, hype) can be protected, but it is not protected if the government’s indictment pleads a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme — i.e., material, verifiable misrepresentations used to induce followers to hand over money or property (buy securities). Constantinescu reversed a dismissal where the indictment alleged exactly that (false/misleading posts inducing purchases), because that alleges deprivation of money/property under the fraud statute, not merely deprivation of “valuable economic information.”  
- At the indictment stage, courts will not routinely dismiss § 1348 charges on First Amendment grounds where the indictment adequately alleges a scheme to induce purchases by materially false statements. First Amendment defenses are typically litigated after a factual record is developed unless the indictment plainly fails to allege the statutory elements.

Why — the legal standard and how the opinion applies it
- Elements the court checks at the indictment stage: a scheme to defraud (material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain something of value) and intent to defraud. The Fifth Circuit reviews an indictment de novo and must accept the allegations as true for purposes of sufficiency. (Constantinescu § II–III.)  
- The key doctrinal distinction the court applies is between:
  - the “right‑to‑control” (or deprivation of valuable economic information) theory rejected in Ciminelli — which targets the loss of information and does not protect a traditional property interest; and
  - the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory — where a defendant uses material misstatements to trick a victim into a transaction that causes the victim to part with money or property. The Supreme Court and the Fifth Circuit treat the latter as a proper basis for fraud charges under § 1348 (Constantinescu § III.A (discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis)).  
- Constantinescu holds that because the indictment alleged defendants posted false and misleading information to induce others to buy securities (i.e., to obtain their money/property), it pleaded a fraudulent‑inducement scheme and thus properly alleged the property element the district court had thought missing. The court also relied on Kousisis to explain that the government need not show the fraudster intended to leave victims economically worse off — it is enough that the object was to obtain money/property by deception (Constantinescu § III.B).

What that means for common fact patterns you asked about
- Verifiable false statements of fact that induce purchases (for example, lying about current holdings, falsely claiming one will not sell, or falsely asserting fabricated price movements or material facts) fit squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement theory described in Constantinescu and are not shielded by the First Amendment at the indictment stage. The opinion treats the indictment’s allegations of misrepresentations about positions and price prospects as sufficient (Constantinescu I, III).  
- Pure opinion, puffery, predictions, or enthusiasm that do not contain material, falsifiable misstatements are more likely to be protected speech — but Constantinescu does not set out a First Amendment test for those borderline cases. The controlling inquiry in this circuit at the charging stage is whether the indictment alleges the elements of a fraud (material misrepresentation, intent to deceive, and obtaining money/property). If it does, dismissal on First Amendment grounds is unlikely.  
- Practically, First Amendment arguments tend to be fact‑dependent (did the statement assert a verifiable fact or was it non‑actionable opinion/puffery?) and therefore are often resolved after discovery or on a more developed record rather than at the indictment stage — unless the indictment on its face obviously alleges only protected speech and fails to plead the statutory elements. Constantinescu illustrates that when an indictment alleges a fraudulent‑inducement scheme (posts that induced purchases), the court will not dismiss at the pleading stage.

Bottom line
- If the government’s indictment alleges material, verifiable misrepresentations used to induce purchases (i.e., the fraudulent‑inducement theory), Constantinescu shows courts will sustain the indictment notwithstanding free‑speech labels like “opinion” or “hype.” Challenges that speech is protected under the First Amendment are feasible, but they are usually resolved later after the evidentiary record is developed unless the indictment plainly fails to allege the statutory elements.
