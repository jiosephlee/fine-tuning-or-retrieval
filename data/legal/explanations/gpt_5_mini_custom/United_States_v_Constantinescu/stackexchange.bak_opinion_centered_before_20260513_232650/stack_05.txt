Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### Is reliance or actual loss required for criminal securities fraud under § 1348?
Question:
Civil Rule 10b-5 cases talk about reliance and loss causation. Here, the court (relying on Kousisis) says no actual economic loss is required. For § 1348 specifically, does the government need to prove that any follower actually relied on the misstatements when buying, or is it enough to show a deceptive scheme intended to obtain money or property in connection with securities purchases? How does the "in connection with" element operate in criminal prosecutions compared to civil securities-fraud claims?
Answer:
Short answer
No — under the Fifth Circuit’s reading of § 1348 in United States v. Constantinescu, the government does not have to prove a victim suffered a net pecuniary loss or that the victim proved reliance in the same way a private plaintiff must under Rule 10b‑5. What § 1348 requires (as applied here) is a scheme to defraud and an intent to obtain money or property “in connection with” the purchase or sale of securities — i.e., the government must link the deceptive scheme to securities purchases (the fraudulent‑inducement theory), not rest on a mere deprivation of economic information (the right‑to‑control theory).

Why (doctrine and the opinion)
- Elements the court focuses on: Constantinescu explains § 1348 requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” The “scheme” element requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” United States v. Greenlaw, quoted in Constantinescu (citing Evans). The “intent” element requires an intent to deceive and an intent that some harm result from the deceit. Constantinescu § III.

- Fraudulent‑inducement vs. right‑to‑control: The Supreme Court in Ciminelli rejected convictions based solely on a right‑to‑control theory (depriving someone of valuable economic information is not a traditional property interest). Constantinescu relies on that rule but distinguishes it: the indictment here pleaded the fraudulent‑inducement theory (defendants used false statements to induce followers to buy securities, i.e., to part with money/property). As the panel put it, that theory “protects money and property” and is not foreclosed by Ciminelli. Constantinescu § III.A.

- No requirement of actual economic loss: Constantinescu follows Kousisis (quoted in the opinion) to hold that the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. Kousisis held a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” Constantinescu § III.B (quoting Kousisis). The panel therefore rejected the argument that absence of net loss defeats the indictment.

How “in connection with” operates in criminal prosecutions (as the opinion treats it)
- It requires a nexus between the deceptive scheme and securities transactions — the scheme must target the buying or selling of securities as the means to obtain money or property. In Constantinescu that nexus was alleged by charging that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” Indictment ¶ 13; Constantinescu § III.A–B.

- It is not the same as the civil reliance/loss‑causation requirements. Private §10b‑5 plaintiffs must show reliance and loss causation to recover under the civil rule. The criminal inquiry under § 1348, by contrast, centers on whether the defendant devised a deceptive scheme with intent to obtain money/property in connection with securities transactions (fraudulent inducement), and not on whether the victims proved reliance or ended up net‑worse off. Constantinescu makes this distinction explicitly by applying Kousisis and Ciminelli.

Practical point (what the government must still prove)
- While the government need not show a plaintiff’s traditional civil reliance or net pecuniary loss, it still must allege and ultimately prove the deceptive scheme was intended to and did operate “in connection with” securities purchases — e.g., that the misrepresentations induced purchases (or were intended to do so). Constantinescu accepted an indictment alleging inducement to purchase as sufficient at the pleading stage. The opinion also makes clear that proving inducement (and intent to obtain money/property) is the key criminal proof point, not proof of a net economic injury.

Bottom line
Under Constantinescu (reading Kousisis and Ciminelli), § 1348 criminal prosecutions can be grounded on a fraudulent‑inducement theory: the government must prove a deceptive scheme and intent to obtain money or property in connection with securities purchases, but it need not prove the victims suffered a net pecuniary loss or satisfy the civil rule‑10b‑5 reliance element in the way a private plaintiff would. Evidence that followers in fact purchased after the misstatements will be relevant (and often dispositive) to proving inducement, but proof of actual net loss or the full civil reliance/loss‑causation framework is not an element of the § 1348 offense as the opinion describes it.
