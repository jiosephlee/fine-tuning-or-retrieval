Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 5: # Doctrinal Significance and Litigation Strategies Post-Constantinescu

United States v. Constantinescu, a Fifth Circuit decision issued in October 2025, is now a touchstone for how prosecutors must plead and prove fraud in the era of social‑media “influencers” who tout stocks. The opinion clarifies two interlocking doctrinal points that are critical for charging and defending influencer-driven pump‑and‑dump cases. First, Ciminelli’s limitation—that a conviction cannot rest solely on deprivation of valuable economic information or a “right‑to‑control” theory—remains controlling. Second, consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in Kousisis, a fraudulent‑inducement theory that alleges a defendant used material misrepresentations to induce victims to part with money or property remains a valid and prosecutable theory even if victims receive market‑value securities and do not suffer a net pecuniary loss. Constantinescu therefore instructs prosecutors how to frame an actionable scheme and gives defense counsel the roadmap to identify those boundaries.

This chapter synthesizes Constantinescu and the controlling Supreme Court decisions it relies on, and then translates the law into practical charging, pleading, and trial strategies. It is organized to guide both prosecutors and defenders in cases where social‑media touting, coordinated posting, and rapid trading form the core of the alleged fraud.

## Charging decisions: § 1348 versus mail/wire fraud and Rule 10b‑5; structuring counts

Section 1348 (18 U.S.C. § 1348) is the federal securities fraud statute that expressly criminalizes schemes to “defraud any person in connection with” securities and schemes to “obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of” securities. Constantinescu treats § 1348 as a natural home for influencer pump‑and‑dump prosecutions because it reaches deceptive schemes tied to securities and borrows analytical concepts from mail and wire fraud caselaw. Prosecutors should ordinarily charge § 1348 where the alleged misconduct is squarely in the securities context—posts about buy/sell positions, concrete trading recommendations, coordinated amplification designed to move a stock price, and profits traceable to securities transactions.

There are three pragmatic considerations when selecting statutes and structuring counts:

- Parallel or fallback counts: It remains common to include multiple fraud counts—§ 1348, wire/mail fraud (18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343), and Rule 10b‑5 (in civil SEC enforcement or as the predicate for certain related civil claims). § 1348 is the most targeted criminal vehicle for securities schemes; mail/wire fraud can cover the same conduct insofar as interstate communications or mail services are used, and may serve as a fallback. Where a defendant had no brokered securities relationship or where the allegedly deceptive conduct did not occur “in connection with” a securities transaction, mail/wire fraud counts may capture additional theories, but prosecutors should prefer § 1348 when the deception expressly concerns securities.

- Conspiracy and aiding‑and‑abetting: Constantinescu involved conspiracy (18 U.S.C. § 1349) and substantive § 1348 counts. Conspiracy counts are strategically useful because they allow prosecutors to proceed based on an agreement and overt acts that may be easier to prove than the full mens rea for every defendant on every substantive count. Aiding‑and‑abetting (18 U.S.C. § 2) counts (or charging individuals as principals under a theory of co‑conspirator liability) can extend culpability to coordinators, promoters, and technical facilitators who did not post themselves but who orchestrated the campaign.

- Pleading overlap and Ciminelli discipline: Critical to constant success post‑Ciminelli is that charging papers not rely principally on a right‑to‑control theory—i.e., allegations framed only as depriving investors of “information” or a “right to decide” about their assets—without also alleging that the scheme’s object was the obtaining of money or property. When multiple statutes are charged, ensure that the indictment’s core factual narrative alleges (a) the misrepresentations or omissions, (b) the victims’ purchase or disposition of securities (i.e., the transfer of money or property), (c) the defendants’ profit motive and realized gains traceable to the transactions, and (d) the temporal coordination between communications and trading.

Structuring counts with these elements avoids dismissal under Constantinescu and gives the prosecution legally sustainable predicates for different defendants and roles.

## Drafting indictments after Ciminelli and Constantinescu

The central drafting lesson from Ciminelli and Constantinescu is simple but decisive: avoid framing the alleged harm primarily as deprivation of valuable economic information; instead, allege a fraudulent‑inducement theory that centers on the transfer of money or property as the scheme’s object.

Concrete drafting practices:

- Plead the object of the scheme as obtaining money or property. Explicitly state that the defendants’ posts and messages induced specified classes of followers to purchase particular securities (naming the issuers, symbols, dates, and approximate volumes where possible). Allegations that followers “purchased shares” or “transferred funds” to brokerage accounts are crucial; as Constantinescu held, these allegations convert the scheme into one aimed at money/property rather than mere control of information.

- Describe the misrepresentations in detail. Identify representative posts or statements, including date/time, platform, verbatim wording or paraphrase, and whether the post was presented as a present fact (e.g., “I hold X shares,” “I just bought in at $Y”) versus opinion or puffery. Attach or incorporate by reference screenshots and, where available, metadata showing authorship, timestamps, and reach (e.g., number of followers, likes, retweets).

- Tie trading activity to communications. Draft allegations that show chronological and causal connection: purchases by defendants before or during promotional messages; purchases by followers after posts; defendants’ sales (“dumps”) after price appreciation attributable to the promotional campaign; realized gains. Specificity strengthens the claim that the object of the scheme was to obtain money derived from induced purchase activity.

- Quantify profits and impacts. When possible, allege specific dollar amounts of realized profits and the number of shares sold. These figures are important later for tracing proceeds, establishing loss for sentencing, and rebutting arguments that no one lost money because victims received market‑value securities.

- Avoid right‑to‑control language as the principal theory. Do not draft allegations that treat the alleged fraud as mere suppression of information or control of discretionary decisions. If describing information deprivation to explain how deception operated, simultaneously allege the conversion of that deception into transfers of money/property.

- Use geographic and transactional detail to support venue and wire/mail predicates. Identify interstate communications (messages, DMs, emails), platform servers, IP addresses if known, and transactional routes used to move funds or securities. These details cover mail/wire fraud counts and aid in service of subpoenas and preliminary discovery.

Well‑drafted indictments anchored in the fraudulent‑inducement theory survive the Ciminelli challenge and, as Constantinescu shows, will satisfy the Fifth Circuit’s pleading standards.

## Proving materiality and intent: evidence and techniques

Proving a securities fraud under § 1348 requires showing a scheme to defraud (material misrepresentation) and an intent to defraud. After Constantinescu—guided by Kousisis and prior Fifth Circuit precedent—the prosecutor must prove that defendants used deception to obtain money or property (the object of the scheme) and that they acted with the requisite intent to deceive and obtain that object. The following evidence streams and techniques are central to proof:

- Social‑media posts and metadata. Preserve and authenticate posts. Secure platform records via subpoenas or warrants: post content, timestamps, user IDs, deletion logs, follower counts, promoted posts, and advertisement purchase records. Metadata linking a post to a defendant’s device or account, and records showing a post’s reach or engagement, helps establish materiality (did the communication reach a significant audience?) and causation (did follower purchases occur after exposure?).

- Direct messages and communications. DMs, group chats, emails, and texts among co‑conspirators can show coordination, knowledge of falsity, intentional concealment, and contemporaneous state of mind (e.g., planning to “pump” and then “dump”). Communications with brokers, placement agents, or paid promoters are often probative.

- Trading records and broker statements. Obtain brokerage account histories for defendants and alleged victims when authorized. Trading blotters, order timestamps, and account statements show the temporal relation between posts and purchases/sales, volume changes, and realized gains. Evidence that a defendant bought shares before posting and sold after a spike supports the pump‑and‑dump narrative.

- Traceable proceeds and financial records. Bank transfers, withdrawals, and proceeds transfers demonstrate that defendants obtained money as an object of the scheme. Forensic accounting can trace profit flows through accounts, shell entities, and crypto conversions.

- Market data and expert testimony. Experts can explain market microstructure: how a promotional campaign can inflate prices, liquidity and bid‑ask effects in low‑volume securities, and whether the timing and magnitude of price moves are consistent with intentional manipulation. Experts also explain what a reasonable investor would view as material—helpful for jury understanding.

- Follower purchases and loss analysis. While Kousisis holds that net loss is not required for a fraud conviction, proving that followers purchased after exposure strengthens materiality and causation. If victims did sustain losses, empirical analyses that quantify losses make restitution and sentencing arguments more concrete.

- Forensics and device evidence. Deleted posts, burner accounts, coordinated posting via shared devices or schedulers, and use of anonymizing tools may be revealed by device forensics and platform logs. These often show consciousness of guilt.

Choreographing this evidence into a coherent timeline is often decisive. Demonstrate the sequence: defendants acquire position → defendants disseminate false or misleading statements timed to reach followers → followers buy induced by those statements → price rises → defendants sell into inflated market → defendants realize profits. Each link should be supported with documentary, testimonial, or expert evidence.

Materiality will ordinarily be a jury question unless the alleged statement is pure opinion, vague puffery, or otherwise incapable of misleading a reasonable investor. Intent can be shown by circumstantial evidence: prior misrepresentations, coordination, inconsistent explanations, sudden sales after posts, concealment of holdings, and internal communications indicating an intent to profit.

## Common defenses and responsive strategies

Defendants in influencer cases commonly raise a predictable set of defenses. Anticipating them in drafting and proof helps prosecutors and shapes defense strategy.

- Puffery and opinion. Statements framed as vague, hyperbolic, or subjective predictions (e.g., “This company could be huge!”) are classic defenses. Courts treat such statements as non‑actionable opinion or puffery unless the speaker presents them as statements of present fact or claims a factual basis knowingly false. To meet this defense, allege and prove that defendants presented statements as factual assertions about holdings, trading intent, or proprietary knowledge (e.g., “I own 100k shares,” “I was told by management that…”) rather than as mere bullish commentary.

- Predictions versus present fact. A prediction about future price movements is often non‑actionable, but a prediction becomes actionable if the speaker misstates an existing fact or fabricates a present event to give the prediction a false appearance of veracity (e.g., claiming to have a current position when no position exists). Draft and prove the contemporaneous factual statements underlying the prediction.

- First Amendment challenges. Defendants may argue that prosecutions chill speech. Courts require that criminal statutes be applied to false statements of fact or material omissions in a securities context; protected opinion and core political speech are not criminalized. Post‑Constantinescu, the First Amendment does not immunize knowingly false, material statements offered in connection with the purchase or sale of securities.

- Scienter and good‑faith defenses. The required mens rea is intent to defraud. Defendants commonly assert a good‑faith belief in their statements, or that they were negligent rather than fraudulent. Documentary evidence of knowing falsity (e.g., internal communications, fabricated trading records) or conscious avoidance can undermine good‑faith claims. Absence of contemporaneous corroborating data (e.g., manufactured screenshots, inconsistent trade histories) supports scienter in a prosecutor’s case.

- Causation and reliance. Defendants may contend followers purchased securities for other reasons or would have purchased notwithstanding the statements. Reliance is not a formal element of § 1348 like it is in some civil claims, but showing temporal proximity, messaging reach, and that specific followers purchased after exposure is persuasive. Where reliance is disputed, case‑specific evidence (testimony from purchasers, platform analytics, and patterns of sudden volume increases following posts) is decisive.

- Fragmented audiences and attenuated harms. Defense may argue that posts were not targeted to investors, or that victims received market‑value securities or even benefited. Kousisis and Constantinescu blunt these defenses: an indictment need only allege that the scheme sought to obtain money/property; proof that victims received something of equal value does not negate the offense.

Defending counsel should, however, exploit factual ambiguities: attack authenticity of screenshots, challenge the temporal or causal link between posts and trades, forecast reasonable investor arguments to the jury, and press on scienter as a core fact question.

## Jury instructions after Kousisis and Constantinescu

The post‑Kousisis/Constantinescu landscape requires jury instructions that clearly define: (1) the nature of the scheme (fraudulent inducement rather than right‑to‑control), (2) the requisite intent, (3) materiality, and (4) the object of the scheme being money or property. Practical guidance:

- Define “scheme to defraud” as a plan to deceive by means of false statements or omissions with the intent to obtain money or property. Avoid language that suggests deprivation of an information right alone qualifies.

- Explain “intent to defraud” as two components: an intent to deceive and an intent to obtain money or property as the object of the deception. Include Kousisis language that the defendant need not have intended to leave the victim economically worse off; it is sufficient that the defendant intended to obtain money/property.

- Define “material” along reasonable‑investor lines: a statement is material if a reasonable investor would consider it important in making an investment decision. But explain to jurors that materiality depends on context and is generally a fact question.

- Explain “object of the scheme” with specific choices: the jury must find that obtaining money or property was among the objects of the scheme. This avoids conflating the right‑to‑control theory with the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

- Provide examples and tie them to the evidence: show sample statements alleged to be false, exhibit trading timelines, and instruct jurors on how to weigh circumstantial evidence of intent.

A model instruction should be succinct but carefully incorporate Kousisis’s key holdings (that net loss is not required) while ensuring the jury focuses on whether the defendant sought money or property through deception.

## Remedies and sentencing: forfeiture, restitution, and loss calculations

Remedies in criminal securities prosecutions involve both statutory criminal penalties and ancillary relief—forfeiture, restitution, and guideline sentencing enhancements. Constantinescu, by affirming the fraudulent‑inducement theory as viable, preserves typical remedial outcomes, but their application can be complex when victims acquired market‑value securities.

- Forfeiture. Criminal forfeiture targets the proceeds traceable to the offense—defendants’ realized gains from the scheme. If the prosecution can trace deposits, transfers, or converted assets to profits realized from sales following the scheme, forfeiture is a viable enforcement tool regardless of whether victims retained securities. Robust accounting and trace evidence are thus important.

- Restitution. Restitution under the Mandatory Victims Restitution Act requires identifiable victims and compensable losses. Kousisis clarified that lack of net pecuniary loss does not negate criminal liability, but restitution is a separate remedial question. If victims can be shown to have lost money because they sold at a loss or overpaid relative to subsequent price movements, restitution is appropriate. Where victims simply hold market‑value securities purchased at inflated prices but have not realized a loss, restitutive awards are legally and practically more difficult.

- Sentencing and loss calculations. Sentencing enhancements under the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines (especially §2B1.1) hinge on “loss” (actual or intended) and the amount of gain or loss attributable to the offense. There is often litigation over whether intended loss (anticipated or potential market harm) or actual loss (realized losses by victims) should drive the enhancement. Post‑Kousisis, courts may have to reconcile that an offense can be established without net victim loss while sentencing requires quantifiable loss. Prosecutors should compile evidence of realized losses by purchasers, the monetary magnitude of defendants’ gains, and the intended scope of the scheme. Defenders should contest speculative loss figures and push for attribution only of concretely established losses.

- Ancillary civil exposure. Convicted defendants often face SEC civil enforcement for Rule 10b‑5 violations and shareholder or class actions. Evidence developed in the criminal case (admissions, trading records) is frequently used in civil litigation.

For both sides, meticulous financial forensics early in the case are essential to support or dispute forfeiture, restitution, and guideline loss arguments.

## Litigation practices: evidence preservation and pretrial motion practice

Influencer cases present unique evidentiary problems. Posts can be deleted, accounts suspended, and messaging ephemeral. Best practices include:

- Immediate preservation letters and Rule 17(c) subpoenas to platforms. Seek post content, metadata, deletion logs, ad purchase records, and platform analytics without delay.

- Court orders to preserve accounts and devices. Ask for device imaging and counsel for early forensic analysis.

- Authentication foundations for social‑media evidence. Preserve chain of custody, IP logs, and corroborative testimony showing an account belongs to the defendant.

- Early custodial discovery for co‑conspirators and witnesses. In multi‑actor campaigns, communications among promoters, payment records for paid promotion, and contracts with brokers or market makers often reveal coordination.

- Motion practice on materiality and First Amendment issues. Expect defense motions to dismiss or for acquittal arguing purely opinion statements and protected speech. These motions are often unsuccessful as materiality is a fact question, but targeted challenges to specific statements can succeed if they are pure puffery.

- Expert disclosure challenges. Anticipate Daubert or Frye challenges to market‑impact or investor‑behavior experts; be prepared with methodological foundations.

- Preservation of Brady and Giglio materials. Early demands for exculpatory evidence and witness impeachment material are routine.

Vigilant early evidence preservation often decides whether a prosecution can substantiate the necessary elements.

## Broader implications: regulation, inter‑circuit alignment, and unresolved questions

Constantinescu reflects an emerging doctrinal consensus that criminal liability for securities deception survives in the social‑media context when prosecutions allege fraudulent inducement to obtain money/property. The decision tightens the pleading requirements that Ciminelli imposed while embracing Kousisis’s endorsement of fraudulent‑inducement liability.

Broader implications include:

- Regulation of online touting. Constantinescu underscores that prosecutors and civil authorities (SEC) can pursue influencers who fabricate holdings or knowingly push securities for personal profit. This may spur platforms to adopt more aggressive transparency or enforcement policies (disclosures for paid promotions, verified holdings attestations).

- Inter‑circuit alignment. Other circuits have applied Kousisis in similar contexts (see post‑Kousisis decisions involving contract procurement and procurement‑type fraud). Constantinescu contributes to an emerging multi‑circuit approach that separates right‑to‑control claims (invalid) from fraudulent‑inducement claims (valid) and that does not require net victim loss for liability. Nonetheless, circuits differ on the quantum of proof required to connect speech to trading gains, and future cases will refine the boundaries.

- Unresolved issues for the Supreme Court. Potential areas for future clarification include the relationship between criminal fraud liability and restitution when victims receive market‑value securities, precise limits of materiality in short, viral posts to large yet diverse follower bases, and the interplay between the First Amendment and criminal sanctions for persuasive online content that straddles opinion and factual presentation.

Constantinescu leaves in place two important guardrails: fraud convictions cannot be premised solely on depriving people of information, but they can be sustained when a defendant’s deception causes transfers of money or property—even if the victims receive something of value. Practitioners should therefore structure their cases around that distinction.

## Concluding practical checklist

For prosecutors:
- Charge § 1348 where securities are at issue; include conspiracy and aiding‑and‑abetting counts as needed and consider mail/wire fraud counts as fallback.
- Draft indictments to allege the object of the scheme was money/property—tie communications to purchases/sales and to realized profits.
- Preserve social‑media evidence and metadata immediately and develop trading and financial traces.
- Prepare expert testimony on market impact and investor behavior.

For defenders:
- Check whether the indictment relies on a right‑to‑control theory; press Ciminelli where applicable.
- Attack authenticity of social‑media materials, and push for specificity in allegations of trading causation and scienter.
- Challenge causation and materiality early when statements are puffery or opinion.
- Develop evidence of good‑faith belief, lack of profit motive, or alternative explanations for timing.

Constantinescu reframes the battleground: craft the criminal theory around inducement to obtain money/property, not control of information; prove the temporal and financial link; and litigate the edges where speech, opinion, and persuasion meet deception.
