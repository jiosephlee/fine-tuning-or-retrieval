Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 1: # Foundations of Federal Criminal Securities Fraud

This chapter explains the federal criminal law of securities fraud as embodied in 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and its common-law casing in the courts, with particular attention to how courts borrow and adapt doctrines developed under the mail and wire fraud statutes. The aim is to give you a usable doctrinal framework: the statutory elements prosecutors must plead and prove, how courts treat materiality, intent, and the required nexus to a security, and how conspiracy and aiding-and-abetting liability fit into a securities-fraud prosecution. United States v. Constantinescu, decided by the Fifth Circuit in 2025, provides a compact recent illustration of these principles in the context of social‑media “pump and dump” schemes and of the post‑Ciminelli/Kousisis doctrinal landscape.

## Statutory architecture: § 1348, § 1349, and § 2

Congress criminalized securities fraud in 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Broadly stated, § 1348 (like the mail and wire fraud statutes) forbids (1) schemes to defraud “any person in connection with” any security, and (2) schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” The statute therefore has two interlocking focal points: a scheme to defraud (with deception and materiality questions) and the object of the scheme (obtaining money or property), situated “in connection with” a security.

Two companion statutory provisions commonly appear in securities‑fraud indictments. 18 U.S.C. § 1349 criminalizes conspiracy to commit offenses such as § 1348; it requires proof of an agreement to commit the offense and an overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy, together with the conspirators’ criminal intent. 18 U.S.C. § 2 makes principals criminally liable for aiding and abetting: a defendant who intentionally associates with the criminal venture, participates, and seeks to make it succeed (by conduct that substantially assists) can be charged as a principal. Thus prosecutions typically pair substantive § 1348 counts with conspiracy charges under § 1349 and, where appropriate, with aiding‑and‑abetting allegations under § 2 or charged as a separate count.

## Relationship to mail and wire fraud doctrines

Courts, including those in the Fifth Circuit, treat § 1348 as importing many of the same conceptual building blocks developed under the mail and wire fraud statutes. The “scheme to defraud” concept, materiality and deception analysis, and the mens rea vocabulary (an intent to deceive and an intent to obtain money or property) derive from mail/wire fraud precedents and are applied analogously when the misconduct involves securities. The Fifth Circuit has acknowledged this borrowing and frequently cites mail/wire fraud caselaw for interpretive guidance (see, e.g., United States v. Greenlaw; United States v. Evans).

That borrowing is useful but not unlimited. Because § 1348 is focused on securities, courts require a nexus “in connection with” the purchase or sale of a security. The mail/wire fraud lineage governs the proof of scheme, materiality, and intent, but courts remain attentive to the distinct policy and doctrinal contours of securities regulation.

## § 1348 versus Section 10(b)/Rule 10b‑5

It is essential to distinguish criminal § 1348 prosecutions from civil and administrative enforcement under the Securities Exchange Act’s § 10(b) and SEC Rule 10b‑5. Both regulatory regimes target deceptive practices in securities markets, and both ask similar questions about material misrepresentations and reliance. But they are different tools with different elements, burdens, and remedies.

Section 10(b)/Rule 10b‑5 is primarily a civil (and administrative) enforcement regime, though the Department of Justice sometimes brings criminal charges adjunctively. Elements of a private § 10(b) claim typically include a material misrepresentation or omission, scienter (traditionally satisfied by intent or recklessness, depending on the circuit and context), reliance by investors, a purchase or sale of securities, and loss causation for damages. Criminal § 1348, by contrast, focuses on a scheme to defraud and a defendant’s intent to obtain money or property by deception. Criminal proof demands the government show the defendant’s culpable state of mind beyond a reasonable doubt; criminal statutes also permit proof under fraudulent‑inducement theories that do not require a showing that the victim suffered a net economic loss. Prosecutorial choice (civil SEC action vs. criminal § 1348 charge) reflects these differences: criminal prosecution adds the prospect of imprisonment and requires a higher burden of proof, but can be attractive where the facts show deliberate deception designed to obtain money or property.

## Core elements under § 1348

To structure charges and proof, break § 1348 into its constituent elements: (1) a scheme to defraud; (2) intent to defraud; (3) a nexus to a security; and (4) that the scheme sought to obtain money or property by means of deceptive, material misrepresentations or omissions.

Scheme to defraud. The scheme element requires a plan, device, or course of conduct designed to deceive others. Deception is central: the government must allege and later prove a material misrepresentation, omission, or deceptive practice intended to deceive. Courts evaluate the alleged statements or acts in context to determine whether they convey materially false or misleading impressions.

Intent to defraud. The mens rea commonly described in the Fifth Circuit requires that the defendant acted with an intent to deceive and an intent that some harm (or deprivation of money/property) result. The Supreme Court’s recent cases refine how to understand the “harm” piece: it is sufficient that the defendant intended to obtain money or property from the victim (the fraudulent‑inducement theory), and the government need not prove that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. Thus intent is proved by direct evidence of deceptive purpose (statements, admissions) or by circumstantial evidence (patterns of conduct designed to mislead and to extract money or property).

Nexus to a security. Section 1348 applies when the scheme is “in connection with” the purchase or sale of any security. This nexus is factual: the deception must relate to the securities transaction so that the deception influenced the victim’s decision to buy or sell. In pump‑and‑dump cases, allegations that the defendant’s false statements induced followers to purchase shares suffice to establish the nexus.

Materiality and deception. Materiality remains a critical legal threshold. A misstatement or omission is “material” if a reasonable investor would consider it important in making an investment decision—that is, if it had a natural tendency to influence the decision. Materiality is a question of law for the court, though often informed by record evidence. Courts require more than puffery or vague opinion; they require misrepresentations of fact or deceptive statements reasonably susceptible of verification when materiality is contested.

## Fifth Circuit guideposts: Greenlaw and Evans

The Fifth Circuit has articulated guideposts that are now routinely deployed to analyze § 1348 prosecutions. In United States v. Greenlaw the court set out a two‑part framework: the government must prove (1) a scheme to defraud, and (2) an intent to defraud, with the scheme involving a material misrepresentation intended to deceive and the object of the fraud to obtain money or property. United States v. Evans clarified that the “scheme to defraud” element requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value” and elaborated on the proof of intent as encompassing an intent to deceive and an intent that harm result. These Fifth Circuit formulations align with the Supreme Court’s later treatment of fraudulent‑inducement in Kousisis and explain how courts evaluate allegations in indictments and at trial.

## Mens rea and proof structure: criminal fraud versus civil securities fraud

Criminal prosecutions require proof beyond a reasonable doubt of the defendant’s mental state. For § 1348, prosecutors typically must show intent to deceive—that the defendant knew the statements were false or was recklessly indifferent to the truth and made them to mislead—and an intent to obtain money or property. In civil Rule 10b‑5 suits, “scienter” may be established by proof of intent or, in some circuits, reckless conduct that is highly unreasonable and demonstrates an extreme departure from standards of ordinary care. The criminal standard practically and constitutionally demands more: negligence or simple mistakes are not criminal; deliberate or recklessly indifferent misrepresentations designed to procure money or property are.

Another important distinction is causation and loss. In civil securities actions, plaintiffs must prove reliance and loss causation to recover damages—i.e., that the misrepresentation caused the investor’s loss. In contrast, the Supreme Court in Kousisis clarified a doctrinal limit on criminal fraud: the fraudulent‑inducement theory targets the deprivation of money or property that results from being induced to enter a transaction under false pretenses, without requiring proof that the defendant intended to or did cause a net pecuniary loss to the victim. The fraud is complete when the defendant obtains money or property by materially false pretenses. Thus in criminal prosecutions the government can proceed even when the defendant provided something of value or when market‑value share transfers leave the victim superficially “whole” at certain points in time.

## Charging theories and count design: substantive fraud, conspiracy, aiding-and-abetting

A practical prosecution strategy commonly combines several counts and theories to capture the full sweep of alleged misconduct.

Substantive counts under § 1348. Each count should allege the elements: the scheme to defraud (with specific misrepresentations), the intent to defraud (including intent to obtain money/property), the nexus to a security (how the deception related to purchase or sale), and identification of victims, dates, and losses where known. Pleading specifics—who made what statements, when, where, and to whom—will matter both to fair notice and to later trial proof.

Conspiracy under § 1349. A conspiracy count requires an agreement to commit the substantive fraud and an overt act furthering it. Conspiracy liability is useful where multiple actors coordinate in time‑staggered roles—creator of false narratives, platform operators, and sellers who dump. Conspiracy allows the government to attribute co‑conspirators’ acts to one another when those acts advance the conspiracy and are reasonably foreseeable.

Aiding and abetting under § 2. When a defendant did not personally make misrepresentations but knowingly and substantially assisted the fraudulent scheme (for example by manipulating trading accounts, coordinating messaging, or providing technical assistance to conceal sales), an aiding‑and‑abetting theory can reach that conduct. Proof requires knowledge of the underlying criminal objective and the provision of assistance that substantially facilitates the offense.

Count design should also anticipate multiplicity and double‑jeopardy issues. Indictments normally include separate counts for separate victims, transactions, or discrete misrepresentations, provided each count alleges distinct elements and is not merely a duplicative charging tactic. The indictment must, as the Fifth Circuit reiterates, allege each material element, fairly inform the defendant of the charges, and enable the defendant to plead former jeopardy in a subsequent prosecution (United States v. Gaytan; United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo).

## Indictment sufficiency and pleadings: standard of review

When a court tests the sufficiency of an indictment, it accepts the indictment’s factual allegations as true and asks whether those allegations, if proved, would constitute an offense (United States v. Fontenot; United States v. Rafoi). The indictment need not prove the case; it need only plead facts sufficient to establish the offense’s elements. The pleading should be sufficiently specific to give defendants notice and to bar future prosecutions for the same conduct. Courts have rejected hypertechnical challenges that rest on whether the indictment uses a particular label (e.g., “fraudulent‑inducement” vs. “right‑to‑control”) so long as the indictment alleges the operative facts that constitute an element of the offense.

Constantinescu illustrates these principles. There the indictment alleged that defendants, who had large social‑media followings and represented themselves as skilled traders, purchased securities, then posted false and misleading information to induce followers to buy and thereby inflate the price, and then secretly sold (dumped) the securities for profit. The district court dismissed, concluding the indictment alleged only deprivation of economic information rather than a property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed: taking the indictment’s allegations as true, the court held it sufficiently pleaded a fraudulent‑inducement theory—that defendants used materially false statements to induce followers to purchase securities and thereby to obtain their money or property. The Fifth Circuit relied on the Supreme Court’s work in Ciminelli and Kousisis to distinguish the rejected right‑to‑control theory (Ciminelli) from the permissible fraudulent‑inducement theory (Kousisis), and to explain that criminal liability can attach even if the defendant did not intend to leave the victim net economically worse off (Kousisis). Constantinescu thus confirms that courts will look to the substance of allegations—did the defendant induce a transfer of money or property by deception?—rather than to the label applied by the indictment.

## Practical considerations for prosecutors and defenses

Prosecutors must decide whether the facts better support a criminal § 1348 case or a civil enforcement action under § 10(b)/Rule 10b‑5, or both. Criminal prosecution is appropriate where the evidence supports an inference of deliberate deception with the object of obtaining money or property. Drafting an effective indictment requires alleging specific misrepresentations, the audiences to whom they were made, the mechanism by which the deception induced purchase or sale (e.g., social‑media posts inducing purchases), and the defendant’s intent to profit.

Defendants have several lines of defense. A truthful‑statement defense—showing the statements were accurate, opinion-based puffery, or not reasonably susceptible of verification—can defeat materiality or deception. A lack‑of‑intent defense focuses on the mens rea: arguing negligence, mistake, or lack of knowledge. Post‑Ciminelli, defendants may seek to recast alleged misconduct as an attempt to control others’ discretionary choices (a “right‑to‑control” theory) to argue that the alleged deprivation is only of information and thus outside the fraud statutes; but as Constantinescu shows, courts will reject that framing when the government alleges fraudulent inducement that led to transfers of money or property. Other defenses attack the nexus to securities or the sufficiency of particular allegations.

## Conclusion

18 U.S.C. § 1348 sits at the intersection of criminal fraud law and securities regulation. Its application depends on classic fraud inquiries—deception, materiality, and intent to obtain money or property—adapted to the securities context with a required nexus to purchase or sale. The Supreme Court’s recent work in Ciminelli and Kousisis has clarified the permissible scope of criminal liability: deprivation of mere economic information cannot sustain a fraud conviction, but fraudulent inducement that causes a transfer of money or property can. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu shows how these doctrines operate in modern fact patterns—social‑media pump‑and‑dump schemes are prosecutable under § 1348 when the indictment pleads the requisite misrepresentations, intent, and nexus to securities. As always, the precise pleading and proof matter: prosecutors must allege the operative facts that establish the elements, and defendants may litigate materiality, intent, and the transactional nexus at indictment, pretrial, and trial.
