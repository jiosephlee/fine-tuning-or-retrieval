Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the scope of § 1348’s “in connection with” requirement and do social-media posts satisfy it without a transactional relationship?
Question:
The statute requires a scheme to defraud “in connection with” the purchase or sale of a security. Here, the alleged misstatements were public social-media posts rather than direct solicitations to identified buyers. How tight must the nexus be? Is it sufficient that the posts were intended to induce trading activity generally, or does the government need to allege a closer link to specific purchases or sales?
Answer:
Short answer
United States v. Constantinescu holds that § 1348’s “in connection with” requirement is satisfied when the charged misrepresentations are alleged to have been made to induce others to buy securities — even when those misrepresentations are public social‑media posts and there is no allegation of a direct transactional relationship between the defendant and specific buyers. What matters is that the scheme fraudulently induced the transfer of money or property (the purchase), not that the government identify particular contracting counterparties.

Why — doctrinal basics from Constantinescu
- § 1348 is analyzed like mail/wire fraud: the crime requires a scheme to defraud (a material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value) and an intent to defraud. Constantinescu, slip op. § III (citing Greenlaw and Evans).
- Constantinescu distinguishes two theories the Supreme Court has discussed:
  - The “right‑to‑control” theory (rejected in Ciminelli) — charging fraud for merely depriving someone of valuable economic information — cannot support conviction because that theory does not protect a traditional property interest. Constantinescu III.A (discussing Ciminelli).
  - The “fraudulent‑inducement” theory — charging fraud where a defendant uses material falsehoods to trick victims into contracts that require handing over money or property — does protect money/property and can support a § 1348 charge. Constantinescu III.A (discussing Kousisis).
- Under Kousisis (as applied in Constantinescu), the government need not show the defendant intended to leave victims worse off; it is sufficient that the defendant sought to “obtain” money or property by inducing purchases. Constantinescu III.B (discussing Kousisis).

What the opinion says about social‑media posts and the “nexus”
- The court upheld the indictment because it alleged defendants bought shares, posted false and misleading information on social media to induce followers to purchase the securities, and then sold for a profit — i.e., a classic “pump and dump.” Indictment ¶¶ 13–14; Constantinescu III.
- The court treated public posts intended to induce trading generally as adequate to plead that the scheme was “in connection with” the purchase of securities: the posts allegedly caused followers to part with money by buying the securities (a fraudulent‑inducement), so the statutory nexus is met even without a direct sales relationship between defendant and each buyer. Constantinescu III.A–B.

Limits the opinion recognizes
- The opinion reiterates Ciminelli’s core limit: allegations that merely the receipt or denial of economic information was deprived do not suffice to charge fraud under the right‑to‑control theory. Constantinescu III.A. So public posts that only (if proved) withheld or distorted information without inducing purchases would risk dismissal.
- The government still must plead the usual elements: material misrepresentation, intent to deceive, and that obtaining money/property was an object of the scheme. Constantinescu § III (citing Evans, Kousisis).

Practical takeaway for pleading
- To satisfy “in connection with” where fraud involves social media, plead (as the government did here):
  1. concrete allegations that defendants posted material falsehoods,
  2. those posts were intended to and were likely to induce recipients to purchase the security, and
  3. the defendant obtained money/property as a result (e.g., sold shares at inflated prices).
- Mere allegations of depriving the public of information, without an allegation that the deception induced purchases (the transfer of money/property), will not meet the statute under Ciminelli.

Bottom line: public social‑media posts can satisfy § 1348’s “in connection with” requirement so long as the indictment alleges a fraudulent‑inducement — i.e., that the posts were made to induce purchases (the transfer of money/property) and that obtaining that money/property was the object of the scheme. Constantinescu reverses dismissal on precisely that theory.
