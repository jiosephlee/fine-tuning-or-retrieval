Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What exactly must an indictment allege under Rule 12 to survive a motion to dismiss for failure to state an offense?
Question:
The court emphasizes taking the indictment’s allegations as true and only checking for the material elements. In practical terms, could defendants argue at this stage that the followers got fair value or that posts were not actually misleading, or are those purely trial issues? How specific must the indictment be about the mechanics of the scheme (who bought from whom, timing of trades, which posts were false) to provide fair notice and double-jeopardy protection under Fifth Circuit standards?
Answer:
Short answer
At the Rule 12(b)(6) pleading stage the court asks only whether the indictment, taken as true, alleges each material element of the charged offenses with enough factual particularity to give fair notice and protect against double jeopardy. Defendants cannot normally prevail on a motion to dismiss by inviting the court to resolve factual disputes (e.g., that followers received “fair value” or that the posts weren’t misleading). Those are defenses or factual issues for trial (or for a motion for acquittal), unless the indictment itself fails to allege an essential element.

What the opinion requires (doctrine and standards)
- Standard for pleading sufficiency: the court takes the indictment’s allegations as true and reviews de novo whether the indictment “allege[s] each prima facie element of the charged offense,” and fairly inform[s] the defendant and provide[s] a double-jeopardy defense. (United States v. Rafoi; Fontenot; Gaytan; Guzman‑Ocampo, cited at II of the opinion.)
- Elements for § 1348 securities fraud (as used by the court): (1) a “scheme to defraud” (a material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain something of value), and (2) an “intent to defraud” (intent to deceive and to cause harm to result from the deceit). The court relied on Greenlaw and Evans for that framing. (See III.)
- Interaction with Ciminelli and Kousisis: Ciminelli rejects a “right‑to‑control” theory that treats deprivation of valuable economic information alone as a protected property interest. By contrast, the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory—alleging that the defendant tricked victims into handing over money or property (e.g., inducing purchases)—does allege deprivation of money/property and is a valid basis for fraud charges. Kousisis confirms the government need not allege that the victim suffered net pecuniary loss; it is enough that the defendant sought to obtain money or property by deception. (See III.A–B.)

Applying these points to your practical questions
- Can defendants argue at the pleading stage that followers got fair value or that posts were not actually misleading?
  - Generally no. On a Rule 12 motion the court does not resolve factual disputes or credit arguments that contradict the indictment’s pleaded facts. As the Fifth Circuit explained, the court must accept the indictment’s allegations (here: buying a security, posting false and misleading information to induce purchases, and secretly selling for profit) and ask whether those allegations, if true, allege the material elements of § 1348. Challenges that the victims actually received fair value or that the statements were not misleading are factual defenses for trial or for a post‑trial motion for acquittal—unless the indictment itself contains no allegation of a material misrepresentation or intent to obtain money/property. (See II, III.A–B.)
- How specific must the indictment be about the mechanics (who bought from whom, timing of trades, which posts were false)?
  - The Fifth Circuit requires that an indictment allege “each material element” and give fair notice and double‑jeopardy protection (Gaytan; Guzman‑Ocampo). But it does not require minute transactional detail at the pleading stage. In Constantinescu the court found adequate an indictment that alleged the scheme’s essential mechanics: defendants purchased securities, posted false/misleading information to induce others to buy (thereby inflating price), and then sold for profit (alleging a $114 million profit). Those allegations were enough to allege a fraudulent‑inducement scheme and intent to obtain money/property.
  - Practical rule of thumb from the opinion: the indictment should identify the nature of the scheme, the role of each defendant, the means used to deceive (e.g., posting false/misleading information), and the object (inducing purchases / obtaining money or property), plus sufficient temporal and contextual detail to put the defendant on notice and to prevent reprosecution for the same conduct. It need not list every individual post, every purchaser, or every trade timestamp to survive a 12(b)(6) attack—so long as the core elements and factual context are pleaded rather than mere boilerplate.
  - Conversely, an indictment that is purely conclusory (alleging fraud in the abstract without alleging a material misrepresentation, a deceptive means, or an intent to obtain money/property) risks dismissal. That is the line drawn between inadequate pleading (dismissible) and a sufficient factual indictment (for trial).

Takeaway
At the pleading stage the Fifth Circuit asks whether the indictment, accepted as true, alleges the material elements of a fraudulent‑inducement scheme to obtain money or property. Arguments that victims got fair value or that statements were not misleading are typically defensive/factual and belong at trial (or in an evidence motion), unless the indictment itself fails to allege an essential element (material misrepresentation, intent to obtain money/property). The indictment need not plead every minutia of mechanics, but it must include sufficient factual detail to allege the elements and to give defendants fair notice and double‑jeopardy protection—as the court found the superseding indictment did in Constantinescu.
