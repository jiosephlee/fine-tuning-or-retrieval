Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# From Right-to-Control to Fraudulent-Inducement: Constantinescu Maps Post‑Ciminelli Terrain for Section 1348

## Why this case matters
United States v. Constantinescu is the Fifth Circuit’s first substantial § 1348 (securities fraud) decision after Ciminelli and Kousisis, and it matters because it shows how lower courts will distinguish the Supreme Court’s Ciminelli bar on “right‑to‑control” theories from garden‑variety fraudulent‑inducement frauds in the social‑media era. The decision reverses a district‑court dismissal of a multi‑defendant “pump and dump” indictment and holds that allegations that influencers misrepresented their trading positions to induce followers to buy securities adequately plead that the defendants sought to obtain money or property under § 1348. For prosecutors, defense counsel, and markets, Constantinescu signals that the combination of Ciminelli’s limitation and Kousisis’s endorsement of fraudulent inducement will not insulate social influencers who allegedly trick followers into parting with cash.

## What the Fifth Circuit did
The indictment alleged a classic pump‑and‑dump: influencers purchased securities, posted false and misleading trading claims that induced followers to buy and inflate prices, and then sold for profits—allegedly about $114 million in proceeds. The district court dismissed, concluding the alleged deception deprived followers only of valuable economic information (a right‑to‑control theory) rather than a traditional property interest, and thus ran afoul of Ciminelli.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It applied well‑worn indictment sufficiency principles—taking allegations as true and asking whether all material elements of § 1348 were pled—and framed the government’s theory as fraudulent inducement, not a right‑to‑control theory. Relying on Kousisis, the court emphasized that a fraudulent‑inducement theory targets the taking of money or property (the follower’s purchase funds), and that Ciminelli does not prohibit prosecutions where the fraud induces a victim to hand over money or property. The court also rejected the defendants’ argument that intent to profit—without an intent to leave victims worse off—failed to allege intent to defraud, again citing Kousisis for the proposition that net pecuniary loss to the victim is not a required element.

## How Constantinescu reads Ciminelli and Kousisis together
The opinion performs a relatively straightforward doctrinal triage. Ciminelli stands for the narrow proposition that the fraud statutes do not protect a “right to valuable economic information” standing alone. Kousisis clarifies that a fraudulent‑inducement theory, by contrast, is focused on money or property obtained through deception and is therefore a proper predicate for a fraud charge even if the victim ends up with something of market value (e.g., a contract performance, securities) and does not necessarily suffer a net loss.

Constantinescu treats those holdings as complementary rather than conflicting: Ciminelli rejects a property theory that treats access to information as the property; Kousisis confirms that inducing a party to part with money or other property by material misrepresentations is prosecutable regardless of whether the fraudster intended merely to enrich himself rather than to wreck the victim’s finances. The Fifth Circuit therefore reads the indictment’s allegations of induced purchases as pleading the requisite “money or property” that § 1348 protects.

## What counts as “money or property” under § 1348 after Constantinescu
Constantinescu reinforces a pragmatic reading: the money used by followers to buy securities is the paradigmatic “money or property” the statute protects. The opinion also reiterates two important corollaries drawn from Kousisis and prior wire/mail fraud cases. First, a fraudulent inducement is not defeated by the fact that the victim receives something of value in exchange; the crime is complete when a defendant obtains money or property by materially false pretenses. Second, it is unnecessary at the indictment stage to plead that victims suffered net pecuniary loss—the government need only allege that the fraudulent inducement caused victims to part with money or property.

That said, Constantinescu does not expand § 1348 into protecting every type of informational loss. The court was careful to identify the allegation as a straight inducement to purchase (money transfers), not an attempt to control access to confidential information or the right to make a discretionary choice divorced from a transfer of property—a scenario Ciminelli condemned.

## Practical implications for prosecutors and defense counsel
For prosecutors, Constantinescu offers a clear roadmap for drafting indictments in influencer‑led market‑manipulation cases post‑Ciminelli. To avoid the right‑to‑control pitfall, indictments should expressly allege:
- that the defendant made material misrepresentations or omissions,
- that those statements were intended to induce followers to purchase securities (i.e., to hand over money or property),
- that the defendants actually sought to and did obtain money or property as a result (traceable proceeds help), and
- indicia of intent such as coordinated timing of purchases and posts, secret sales (“dumps”), and profits derived from sales.

Concrete allegations tying deceptive statements to followers’ purchases and to defendants’ proceeds will distinguish fraudulent inducement from a theory premised only on informational control.

For defenders, Constantinescu highlights the contours of viable pretrial attacks. A successful dismissal under Ciminelli still remains possible where an indictment truly rests only on a right‑to‑control theory—where the government’s theory is that the defendant deprived a victim only of information or influence without alleging that the victim transferred money or property. After Constantinescu, motions should press factual insufficiency (causation between posts and purchases), challenge the specificity of alleged misstatements and materiality, and explore factual predicates for scienter (good‑faith belief in trading positions, lack of deceptive intent). First Amendment arguments—couched as protecting opinions or puffery—remain available in appropriate cases, though Constantinescu makes clear they do not automatically immunize alleged fraudulent inducements.

## Downstream effects and open questions
Constantinescu is likely to encourage more aggressive prosecutorial targeting of social‑media‑based manipulation, particularly where indictments trace follower purchases to misleading posts and alleged seller profits. Lower courts will now have to parse factual pleadings carefully to determine whether a given case is a bona fide fraudulent inducement (permissible) or a disguised right‑to‑control claim (forbidden by Ciminelli). That fact‑sensitive line‑drawing could produce more motions to dismiss and interlocutory appeals in closely framed cases.

Several open questions remain. First, how will courts deal with cases where alleged misstatements are of opinion or “puffery” rather than concrete factual claims about holdings and trades? Second, what showing of causation between public posts and discrete purchases will suffice—especially given the diffuse and rapid nature of social‑media trading? Third, how will courts handle cases involving coordinated promotion where some purchasers may have been sophisticated traders who nonetheless relied on the defendants’ posts? These issues will shape the contours of enforcement going forward.

## Bottom line
Constantinescu does not rewrite fraud law; it applies Ciminelli and Kousisis pragmatically to modern market manipulation. The Fifth Circuit makes clear that when social‑media influencers are alleged to have lied to induce followers to buy securities—and thereby obtain their money—a § 1348 indictment can survive a motion to dismiss. The decision provides a useful blueprint for prosecutors seeking to avoid Ciminelli’s snares and a reminder to defense counsel that factual particularity, causation, and scienter will be the battlegrounds in the next wave of influencer‑driven securities‑fraud litigation.
