Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# Finfluencers in the Dock: Material Misstatements on Social Media After Constantinescu

Why this matters: The Fifth Circuit’s reversal in United States v. Constantinescu brings a high‑stakes, social‑media reality check for “finfluencers.” The court held that a traditional securities‑fraud theory — fraudulent inducement to obtain money or property — adequately supports an indictment alleging that influencers “pumped” microcap stocks by posting false or misleading social‑media statements and then “dumped” positions for profit. That, not the more attenuated “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in Ciminelli, is the hook the government must plead and prove. The decision signals that prosecutors can pursue social‑media traders under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 so long as their allegations point to concrete inducement to buy (i.e., the transfer of money or property), and not merely to the withholding of useful information.

What the opinion does (and doesn’t) say: The Fifth Circuit reviewed the indictment de novo, accepted the allegations as true for pleading purposes, and held that the superseding indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent to defraud. The court relied chiefly on the Supreme Court’s recentClarifications in Ciminelli (rejecting right‑to‑control as a standalone theory) and Kousisis (accepting the fraudulent‑inducement theory and holding that a defendant need not intend to leave the victim worse off overall). Because Constantinescu alleged that the influencers posted materially false statements to induce their followers to purchase securities — and that the defendants covertly sold into the price run‑up, realizing roughly $114 million — the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment pleaded the deprivation of a protected property interest (money/property) rather than only the loss of informational control.

Parsing the doctrinal lines: opinion and puffery, fact and intent

- Puffery and opinion remain conceptually distinct from actionable misstatements. The court’s logic follows long‑standing fraud doctrine: statements of present fact about holdings, past trades, or present intent (e.g., “I own X shares” or “I will not sell”) are closer to verifiable factual claims and therefore more readily actionable when they are alleged to be false. By contrast, expression of generalized optimism, unverifiable “to the moon” price calls, or hyperbolic endorsements are the classic examples of puffery and nonactionable opinion. Constantinescu does not redraw these general lines, but it illuminates where prosecutors will try to draw them in the social‑media context.

- The special potency of misstatements about present holdings or intent to sell. The indictment’s core allegations — that defendants misrepresented their trading positions and price prospects while secretly selling — are the paradigmatic fraudulent‑inducement scenario: an influencer allegedly tells followers to buy while simultaneously offloading shares. Those sorts of affirmative, contemporaneous factual assertions are inherently capable of inducing purchases and thus of obtaining money or property. Constantinescu underscores that lies about what you currently hold, have bought, or plan to do with your position are legally significant because they bear directly on the audience’s decision whether to part with money to buy a security.

- Omissions and “opinion” that becomes misleading. Although Constantinescu focuses on affirmative misstatements, established fraud principles (as applied in securities and wire/mail‑fraud contexts) caution that an ostensibly opinionated statement can be rendered fraudulent by omissions that make it misleading. A boast that “I’m bullish and holding” coupled with the omission that the speaker has already sold, or that the speaker is being paid to tout the stock, can convert an opinion into a materially misleading communication if that omission is the kind of information a reasonable investor would consider important. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion implicitly points in that direction by emphasizing deceit that induced purchases; whether omissions will support liability in any given case will turn on materiality and the surrounding context.

Intent to defraud and the absence of a required net loss: Kousisis supplied the court’s helpful doctrinal backbone. The Supreme Court there made clear that fraudulent inducement targets money/property even if the victim does not ultimately end up economically worse off overall. Constantinescu leans on that principle to reject the defense argument that an influencer who intended to profit (and perhaps even provided buyers with market‑value shares) lacked the intent element. For prosecutors, that removes a potential pleading obstacle: allege inducement to spend money, allege deceptive intent, and liability can follow even if the defendant gave something of value back or the victim’s net position is not worse in a zero‑sum sense.

What this means for influencers, platforms, and regulators

- For influencers: The practical takeaway is straightforward and stark. Publicly asserting concrete facts about your positions, trades, or intentions while secretly trading against your audience invites legal risk if those assertions are false or materially misleading. Vague optimism and non‑specific commentary are safer from a fraud‑prosecution perspective, but not immune: the more an influencer styles a claim as fact — or omits material realities that would defeat the impression of a bona fide recommendation — the closer it comes to actionable fraud. Disclosure of positions and conflicts of interest is not merely good practice; it is a critical risk‑mitigation measure.

- For platforms and compliance officers: Expect heightened scrutiny. Platforms that host securities commentary may face pressure (regulatory and reputational) to develop clearer policies around paid promotions, position disclosures, and rapid coordination that can produce pump‑and‑dump dynamics. Civil and criminal enforcers alike will look for patterns of coordinated posting, contemporaneous sales, and evidence of intent to profit.

- For prosecutors and plaintiffs’ counsel: Constantinescu confirms a path forward under § 1348: plead a fraudulent‑inducement theory with allegations that the speaker made materially false statements or omissions intended to induce purchases, and that the purchases resulted in the transfer of money or property that the defendant sought to obtain. The Fifth Circuit’s approach sets a pleading standard that is workable at the indictment stage, but conviction will still require proof beyond a reasonable doubt of materiality, causation, and mens rea.

Downstream challenges and unresolved questions

- Materiality in the social‑media ecosystem. Determining materiality on platforms like Twitter, TikTok, or Discord may be messy. Courts will have to grapple with whether concise, ephemeral posts plausibly contain facts that would have significantly altered the total mix of information available to a reasonable investor. Context will matter: who the influencer is, how followers use the information, and whether the posts conveyed verifiable factual claims versus mere enthusiasm.

- Causation and market dynamics. At trial, defendants will press causal defenses: the market price may move for many reasons, and proving that an individual influencer’s posts induced particular purchases (or the flow of funds the defendant obtained) can be factually difficult, especially in liquid or widely followed securities. Pump‑and‑dump cases will be strongest in microcap or thinly traded stocks where a few voices can move price and where contemporaneous sales by the promoter are traceable.

- First Amendment friction. Criminal prosecutions for false statements about securities implicate free‑speech concerns, but the fraud statutes have long been construed to reach materially deceptive commercial speech. Constantinescu does not resolve any broader constitutional challenge; it simply affirms that materially false or misleading commercial assertions used to induce purchases fall within the fraud statutes. Challenges grounded in overbreadth or vagueness may arise in close cases — for example, penalizing hyperbolic or low‑verifiability statements — which will likely be litigated as these facts work through the courts.

Practical litigation pointers

Defendants are likely to attack indictments on familiar grounds: contesting that the charged statements were mere opinion or puffery, arguing a lack of specific intent to deceive, disputing materiality and causation, and pointing to contemporaneous disclosures or market evidence undermining inducement. Prosecutors will try to assemble digital timelines showing posts coincident with purchases, trace the defendants’ sales, and leverage payment or coordination evidence. Because Constantinescu was decided at the pleading stage, watch for how lower courts treat evidentiary development on these fronts.

Conclusion: A narrower but meaningful tool

Constantinescu does not make every bullish tweet a crime. Rather, it confirms that classical fraudulent‑inducement law — not the disfavored “right‑to‑control” theory — is the proper vehicle for prosecuting pump‑and‑dump schemes executed through social media. The ruling sharpens the risk picture for influencers who make concrete, factlike assertions about positions and intentions while secretly monetizing the resulting buying pressure. For lawyers, platforms, and policymakers, the case frames the task ahead: delineating the boundary between actionable deception and protected expression in an era when a single post can move markets.
