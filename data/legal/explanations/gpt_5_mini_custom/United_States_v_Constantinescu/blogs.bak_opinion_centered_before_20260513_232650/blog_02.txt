Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# No Harm, Still Foul: Profit Motive Satisfies Section 1348’s Intent After Kousisis

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is the Fifth Circuit’s quick, clarifying answer to a contemporary question about securities fraud in the age of social-media “influencers.” At base, the panel held that an indictment charging a classic “pump-and-dump” scheme survives a motion to dismiss so long as it alleges a deceptive scheme that sought to obtain money or property through fraudulent inducement — even if victims ultimately received market-value securities and even if the defendants sought profit rather than to leave investors worse off. The ruling operationalizes two recent Supreme Court decisions — Ciminelli (drawing a bright line against right-to-control/information-only theories) and Kousisis (confirming that fraud need not be aimed at leaving a victim economically worse off) — and signals that § 1348 prosecutions of traders and online promoters remain viable where the indictment alleges that deception induced purchases.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The government’s superseding indictment alleged that defendants with large social-media followings bought thinly traded stocks, posted false or misleading messages to induce followers to buy (thereby inflating price), and then sold into that artificially inflated market — realizing about $114 million in profits. The district court dismissed, concluding the indictment alleged only an intent to deprive followers of valuable economic information rather than any traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed.

The panel’s analysis turned on two doctrinal touchstones. First, Ciminelli forbids a conviction based solely on the deprivation of a nontraditional “right to valuable economic information” — the so-called right-to-control theory — because information itself is not a traditional property interest. Second, Kousisis makes clear that fraud under the federal statutes can be grounded in the fraudulent-inducement theory: a defendant who uses material misstatements to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property can be guilty even if the scheme does not leave the victim economically worse off. Applying those precedents, the Fifth Circuit read the indictment as alleging fraudulent inducement — i.e., the defendants’ misrepresentations induced followers to hand over money to buy securities — and therefore as alleging deprivation of money or property sufficient to state an offense under § 1348. The court also relied on Baker and related precedents to reject any “mirror-image” requirement that a defendant’s gain must precisely equal the victim’s loss.

## Why the panel’s approach is persuasive

The Fifth Circuit’s posture is careful and doctrinally consistent. It honors Ciminelli’s limit on information-based theories while giving full effect to Kousisis’s recognition that the object of fraud is obtaining money or property, not necessarily producing net pecuniary loss. That alignment matters: prosecutors must plead that deception induced a transfer of money or property (fraudulent inducement), but they need not show at the pleading stage that the victim suffered net economic harm after accounting for what was received in return. This is the pragmatic reading of the statutes — fraud targets wrongful acquisition by deceit, and the acquisition itself is the statutory evil even if the victim receives something nominally of value in return.

The decision also correctly treats the indictment in the light most favorable to the government, as the law requires. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, whether followers in fact relied or whether the market ultimately reflected fair value are factual questions for trial, not pleading-stage dispositives. The court thus preserves the government’s pathway to prove a classic “pump-and-dump” theory at trial while still leaving room for robust factual and legal defenses.

## Doctrinal implications and likely downstream effects

Constantinescu has a number of practical and doctrinal consequences.

First, it reaffirms that post-Ciminelli prosecutions cannot rest on information-property theories but that they can proceed where the government alleges that lies induced purchases (fraudulent inducement). That narrows, but does not hollow out, prosecutorial options: prosecutors must craft indictments that allege deceptive communications and that those communications were intended to, and did, induce tangible transfers (money spent to buy securities).

Second, the decision underscores Kousisis’s substantive point that intent to “harm” the victim is not a statutory prerequisite. Proof that the defendant had money or property in mind (i.e., intended to obtain money through deception) suffices for the mens rea element. That will be particularly important in the social-media context, where promoters often claim they did not intend to leave followers worse off — they sought profit, which Kousisis and now Constantinescu indicate is a sufficient object for a fraud prosecution.

Third, the ruling foreshadows increased supervisory scrutiny of how courts assess allegations about inducement, reliance, and causation at the pleading stage. Although Constantinescu clears a pleading hurdle, it does not eliminate later defenses. Expect defense strategies to press on particularity (Rule 9(b)), factual reliance, causation (did the misrepresentations actually cause followers to buy?), and scienter (did the speaker actually know a statement was false or act recklessly?). Prosecutors will need to allege the details of posts, timing, and contemporaneous trades with sufficient specificity to survive motions to dismiss and to persuade juries.

Fourth, there are practical market implications. Social-media-driven trading has been an enforcement priority for years, and Constantinescu gives prosecutors a clearer doctrinal path to pursue influencers and microcap promoters who orchestrate price runs. The ruling should encourage charges in similar fact patterns, and appellate courts — and perhaps the Supreme Court — may be asked to refine the contours of “inducement” and how closely proximate causation must be tied to deceptive speech.

Finally, the decision brushes up against free-speech sensibilities. The court properly stayed within fraud doctrine, focusing on false and misleading statements made to induce purchases. But as prosecutions increase, courts will need to continue distinguishing protected opinion or market commentary from actionable false statements made with deceptive intent. That line will be hotly litigated and fact-intensive.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu is a reminder that the modern fraud statutes protect the integrity of transfers — not just victims’ informational prerogatives. After Ciminelli closed the door on information-as-property theories, Kousisis opened a robust window for fraudulent-inducement prosecutions, and the Fifth Circuit has walked through that window here. The upshot is straightforward: an objective to obtain money or property by deception is sufficient mens rea under § 1348, even when victims receive market-value securities or when defendants claim they only sought profit. But the decision does not end the debate; it simply moves it from the pleadings phase to the trial phase, where fact, particularity, and proof will determine how far criminal securities enforcement can — and should — go in policing influencer-driven markets.
