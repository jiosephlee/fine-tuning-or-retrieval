Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# Indictments, Not Trials: The Fifth Circuit Tightens the Standard for Pretrial Dismissals

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is a timely reaffirmation of a basic, but often forgotten, principle of federal criminal procedure: at the dismissal-on-the-pleadings stage, courts must accept the allegations of an indictment as true and confine their review to whether those allegations, if accepted, state the material elements of the charged offense. The Fifth Circuit reversed a district court that had dismissed a securities‑fraud indictment against a group of social‑media traders accused of a “pump and dump,” reasoning that the district court impermissibly asked for more than Rule 12 allows. In doing so, the panel navigated the post‑Ciminelli/Kousisis landscape—holding that allegations that defendants induced followers to buy securities by false statements adequately plead a fraudulent‑inducement scheme under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, even though the victims received securities that were, at least nominally, worth something.

The decision matters for two overlapping audiences. For prosecutors, it preserves the ability to charge schemes that exploit influencer platforms to induce purchases. For defense counsel, it signals that early dismissal is a steep hill: indictments alleging fraudulent inducement will survive facial attack so long as they allege deception and an object of obtaining money or property. The practical knock‑on: many fights that defendants used to try to win on pleadings will now have to be litigated later—through discovery, motions in limine, Rule 29 motions, and at trial.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis is tidy and doctrinally anchored. It began with the familiar pleading sufficiency rubric—Rafoi’s de novo review of indictments, the command to accept indictment allegations as true (Fontenot), and the basic content requirements for indictments (Gaytan; Guzman‑Ocampo). Against that procedural baseline, the panel confronted the doctrinal knots left by two recent Supreme Court decisions.

First, Ciminelli held that the federal fraud statutes do not reach schemes that deprive victims only of "valuable economic information" (the “right‑to‑control” theory). The Fifth Circuit acknowledged Ciminelli’s limit, but explained that Ciminelli does not invalidate the fraudulent‑inducement theory—where a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into handing over money or property. The indictment in Constantinescu alleged precisely that: defendants posted false and misleading information to induce followers to buy securities, and then sold into those inflated markets. Those allegations, the panel held, describe a scheme to cause followers to part with money or property, not merely to withhold information.

Second, Kousisis clarified that fraudulent‑inducement prosecutions do not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim worse off financially; it is enough that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the fraud. The Fifth Circuit relied on Kousisis to reject a related defense contention here—that the indictment fails because defendants intended only to profit for themselves rather than to harm followers. Under Kousisis, intent to obtain money (and a scheme to induce the victim to hand it over) suffices at the pleading stage.

On that footing, the panel concluded the indictment alleged both the scheme and the requisite intent to defraud and reversed the district court.

## Putting the opinion in context: what it permits—and what it forbids

Constantinescu performs two useful gatekeeping functions. First, it enforces the structural rule that Rule 12 dismissal is not a substitute for a pretrial merits inquiry. When an indictment alleges elements of deception and an object of obtaining money or property, courts should not try to evaluate competing inferences about the facts on a motion to dismiss. Second, it clarifies that Ciminelli and Kousisis are complementary: Ciminelli draws a line against right‑to‑control theories where no money or property is alleged to change hands; Kousisis confirms that where the fraud aims to induce the transfer of money or property, the government need not show an intent to cause net loss.

But the opinion is not a blank check. The Fifth Circuit takes care to say that Ciminelli’s exclusion of right‑to‑control theories remains binding; prosecutions that rest solely on an allegation that defendants deprived victims of valuable information without alleging any scheme to obtain money or property will not survive. That is the limiting principle that preserves a meaningful boundary for fraud law and addresses some First Amendment anxieties: false speech that merely withholds or manipulates information cannot be equated to taking a property interest unless the indictment alleges that the speech was used to procure money or property.

## Practical consequences for defense strategy

For defense counsel, Constantinescu should prompt a recalibration of expectations and tactics. The decision nudges several fights later in the case lifecycle.

First, front‑loading dismissal motions based on interpretive arguments about the statute or Ciminelli will be less likely to succeed where the indictment charges fraudulent inducement. Appreciable chances remain for Rule 12 dismissal only where the indictment facially relies on the right‑to‑control theory or otherwise plainly fails to allege one of the statutory elements.

Second, the action shifts to the discovery and evidence phases. If dismissal is unlikely, defense teams should invest in aggressive pretrial discovery and evidentiary work: early, focused requests for particularity and Brady material; motions to compel; targeted Daubert and evidentiary challenges; and motions in limine designed to control the narrative the government may present to a jury. The issue of whether the government can prove fraudulent inducement—whether misleading statements were material, whether followers actually relied, and whether the defendants had the requisite intent—are quintessential merits issues suitable for a trial (or a Rule 29 motion after the government rests).

Third, defense counsel should be prepared to press on jury instructions and preserved appellate arguments. Because the statutory contours remain shaped by Ciminelli and Kousisis, careful instruction drafting that highlights the constitutional and doctrinal limits of the fraud statutes will be vital. Where appropriate, defense teams should preserve arguments that the evidence fails to show that defendants sought to obtain money or property—as opposed to merely inflating prices through speech—or that the alleged misrepresentations were not material to a reasonable investor.

Finally, a practical adjunct: plea discussions. When an indictment survives a Rule 12 motion, the calculus of proceeding to trial—particularly in complex, expensive securities prosecutions—changes. Defendants and counsel should evaluate whether early negotiated resolutions are preferable to protracted, high‑stakes litigation, while bearing in mind the preservation of appellate issues.

## Broader doctrinal and policy implications

Constantinescu will likely be cited for the proposition that post‑Ciminelli challenges do not allow courts to collapse indictment review into a mini‑trial. Circuits already interpreting the post‑Ciminelli/Kousisis framework may find in the Fifth Circuit’s opinion a useful formulation: Ciminelli establishes what is off the table (rights‑to‑control claims); Kousisis clarifies what remains on the table (fraudulent inducement to obtain money or property), and indictment sufficiency simply asks whether the indictment, taken as true, pleads the latter.

The opinion also signals continued prosecutorial appetite for addressing securities misconduct driven by social‑media platforms and influencers. Prosecutors will see Constantinescu as encouragement that an indictment that alleges pumping and dumping through false or misleading social‑media posts is likely to survive early dismissal and reach fact development. That, in turn, may produce more cases—forcing courts to grapple at trial with hard questions about causation, reliance in a market context, and reliance proxies in mass‑market schemes.

At the same time, the decision preserves a guardrail for speech‑related prosecutions by reaffirming that mere manipulation of information—if it does not intentionally induce the transfer of money or property—remains outside the fraud statutes. Whether that guardrail will prove sufficiently protective in practice depends on how carefully trial courts instruct juries and how rigorously appellate courts police the line between protected speech and inducements to part with property.

## Conclusion

United States v. Constantinescu reads like a useful corrective: it reminds trial courts to honor the modest, element‑focused role of a Rule 12 dismissal and to leave disputed factual inferences for later stages. For defendants in modern, media‑driven securities cases, the ruling reduces the probability of winning on pleadings and increases the importance of constructing persuasive evidentiary and jury‑instruction strategies. For prosecutors, it affirms that the fraudulent‑inducement path to conviction remains viable even where victims receive marketable securities. The case does not erase Ciminelli’s limits, but it does insist that those limits be applied in a way that preserves the ordinary, fact‑bound processes of criminal adjudication.
