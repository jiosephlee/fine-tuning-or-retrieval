Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# Section 1348’s Coming of Age: Borrowing Mail and Wire Fraud Concepts to Police Market Manipulation

## Why Constantinescu matters

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Constantinescu (Oct. 2, 2025) is a clear signal that federal criminal securities enforcement can—and will—reach modern market manipulation conducted through social media under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The panel reversed a district court that had dismissed a pump‑and‑dump indictment on the ground that the alleged conduct only deprived followers of “valuable economic information,” not a traditional property interest. By leaning on mail‑ and wire‑fraud principles and the Supreme Court’s recent guidance in Ciminelli and Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit held that an indictment alleging fraudulent inducement—misrepresentations that cause investors to hand over money to buy securities—sufficiently pleads a scheme and intent to defraud. That ruling preserves a potent criminal tool for prosecuting influencer‑driven manipulation where the government can allege direct inducement to part with money or property.

## What the court said and how it reasoned

The facts alleged in the superseding indictment are familiar: well‑followed social‑media traders purchase thinly traded stocks, tout them with false and misleading posts to induce followers to buy, inflate prices, then sell for a profit—allegedly yielding roughly $114 million in gains. The district court dismissed, reasoning that the conduct allegedly targeted only followers’ access to information rather than a traditional property interest.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It framed § 1348’s elements in terms used in its prior caselaw: a scheme to defraud (a material misrepresentation intended to obtain something of value, such as money) and an intent to defraud (an intent to deceive and to cause harm to result from the deceit). The panel relied on two Supreme Court decisions that have recently reshaped the contours of criminal fraud doctrine. Ciminelli rejected the so‑called “right‑to‑control” theory—you cannot criminalize depriving someone of valuable economic information alone because that right is not a traditional property interest. Kousisis, by contrast, upheld the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant who uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property can be prosecuted even if the defendant did not intend to leave the victim worse off overall.

Applying that framework, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment here did more than allege mere informational withholding. It charged that defendants intentionally used false posts to induce followers to buy securities—that is, to part with money or property. Under Kousisis, that allegation suffices to plead a scheme to obtain money or property by fraud even if the victims ultimately received marketable securities or were not left net poorer. Thus the dismissal could not stand.

## Doctrinal takeaways

Constantinescu illustrates several doctrinal points worth noting.

First, § 1348 remains a malleable criminal statute largely interpreted through the analogies to mail and wire fraud. The Fifth Circuit explicitly leans on that lineage (and the circuit’s prior wire/mail fraud decisions) to supply the definitional contours of “scheme” and “intent.” That approach gives courts doctrinal tools developed over decades of mail/wire fraud jurisprudence to apply in securities contexts, while the Supreme Court’s recent pruning of certain theories (notably Ciminelli’s rejection of a pure right‑to‑control theory) imposes important limits.

Second, the decision underscores the durable vitality of the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Post‑Kousisis, prosecutors can pursue criminal charges where the heart of the allegation is a misrepresentation that causes victims to hand over money or property—even where the fraudster gives something of value in return or the victim does not suffer an ultimate net loss. That squarely fits the classic pump‑and‑dump scenario: the victims buy the security in reliance on false claims, and the fraudster pockets gains when prices spike.

Third, Constantinescu confirms that the constitutional and common‑law worry that fraud law should not criminalize mere speech remains meaningful—Ciminelli’s limitation survives—but it does not immunize misleading market talk that actually induces purchases. The doctrinal line the courts will enforce is between deceptive inducement (actionable) and mere suppression or manipulation of information (not actionable as property fraud by itself).

## Practical consequences for prosecutors and defense counsel

For prosecutors, the ruling is a validation of § 1348 as a go‑to criminal vehicle in social‑media‑driven manipulation cases. Section 1348 is criminal, operates nationwide, and—importantly—does not require the SEC’s administrative apparatus or reliance on Rule 10b‑5’s civil regime. Where the government can plausibly allege that influencer posts were material misrepresentations aimed at inducing purchases, § 1348 provides a straightforward path to indictment.

That said, the prosecution’s path is not unfettered. The government must craft indictments that allege fraudulent inducement—victims were induced to part with money or property—not merely that defendants distorted the information environment. Thoughtful pleading will emphasize concrete transactions tied to specific misstatements and the defendant’s profit motive, as the Fifth Circuit found sufficient here.

Defense counsel will, predictably, press several lines. One is the Ciminelli line: challenge whether alleged conduct actually deprived anyone of a traditional property interest. Another is First Amendment and overcriminalization concerns—contending that prosecutions that rest on hyperbole, opinionated market commentary, or puffery risk chilling protected speech. Beyond these constitutional claims, defendants will seek narrow jury instructions and dismissal where indictments fail to tie misstatements to particular purchases. The post‑Kousisis landscape also invites factual fights over whether the defendant truly intended to “obtain” money or merely offered something of value in exchange.

Procedurally, Section 1348 prosecutions may offer advantages—criminal penalties, arrest power, and the ability to pursue accessories and conspirators—while also creating risks for prosecutors (jury skepticism about prosecuting speech, potential appellate scrutiny about where the line between speech and fraud lies).

## Broader implications for securities enforcement

Constantinescu will likely accelerate criminal scrutiny of influencer‑based market manipulation. Social media created low‑friction channels for coordinated touting and cross‑promotion; DOJ has already shown interest in policing these dynamics, and this Fifth Circuit ruling removes a doctrinal excuse for dismissal where the indictment ties misstatements to induced purchases.

We should expect several downstream developments. First, prosecutors will lean into careful bridge‑building between specific misstatements and transactional harm—pleading concrete buys tied in time and causation to particular posts. Second, courts will receive more contested motions arguing the boundary between protected commentary and criminal inducement. That is fertile ground for appellate development: how specific and direct must the inducement be, how proximate the causal link, and how to instruct juries about “property” and “intent” in a digital, information‑rich market?

Third, the decision will spur dialogue (and perhaps case law) about appropriate jury instructions and evidentiary limits to prevent criminal liability from swallowing legitimate market discourse. The Supreme Court’s Ciminelli and Kousisis guardrails remain crucial here; prosecutors must not try to recast private informational advantages as property. The tension—protecting markets from fraud while preserving robust speech—will shape litigation posture in the coming years.

Finally, Constantinescu highlights the interplay between criminal enforcement under § 1348 and the SEC’s civil remedies under Rule 10b‑5. The statutes serve complementary roles: the SEC can pursue broad remedial and disgorgement relief; the DOJ can bring criminal charges where the facts permit a fraud‑by‑inducement theory. Expect continued coordination, but also strategic choices by enforcement agencies based on evidentiary posture, deterrent goals, and the need to secure convictions.

## Conclusion

Constantinescu does not open the floodgates to criminalizing mere market commentary. But it does mark an important doctrinal development: courts will treat social‑media pump‑and‑dump schemes as classic objects of § 1348 prosecutions when indictments plead fraudulent inducement—i.e., misrepresentations that caused followers to hand over money or property. In an era when online influencers can move prices quickly, the decision equips prosecutors to use a long‑standing federal fraud framework against modern methods of market manipulation, while preserving the Ciminelli guardrail that mere informational control is not a species of property fraud. Expect hard factual battles over causation, intent, and the line between puffery and deception; those battles will define the contours of criminal securities enforcement in the social‑media age.
