Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 6: # Separate Opinion: The § 1522 Dispute and Its Implications

## Agreement on the entry-suspension issue (§ 1182(f))
Judge Lee begins from common ground with the other members of the panel: the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend classes of admissions is entitled to substantial deference. Both the majority and Judge Lee accept that, under the Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Hawaii and the text of § 1182(f), the President may suspend refugee admissions and that courts should not perform a searching review of the President’s determinations about national interests. That concession narrows the dispute on appeal: Lee does not challenge the stay of the district court’s injunction ordering an end to the admissions suspension; rather, his disagreement is focused entirely on whether the federal government has a statutory duty, separate from the admission decision, to provide domestic reception-and-placement services to refugees under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

## Core disagreement: § 1522 as permissive authorization, not a mandate
At the heart of Judge Lee’s partial dissent is a textualist argument about statutory form and signal words. He emphasizes that § 1522’s heading—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—is itself a textual clue. “Authorization,” as Lee quotes Black’s Law Dictionary, connotes permission, not compulsion. That framing, he argues, is reinforced throughout the substantive subsections: subsections (b) through (e), which set out particular resettlement programs and services, repeatedly state that the Director or Secretary “is authorized” to take certain actions. For Lee, the prevalence of permissive phrasing—“is authorized”—shows Congress intended to grant the executive branch discretion to design and fund refugee-resettlement programs, not to command that those programs must be operated in all circumstances.

## Reading § 1522(a): “shall” is conditional on choosing to fund
The majority rests part of its conclusion on § 1522(a), which uses the mandatory verb “shall” to describe certain duties of the Director “in providing assistance under this section.” Lee reads that provision differently: he treats § 1522(a)’s “shall” as a clause of conditions and considerations that governs how programs must operate if the government elects to provide assistance under the discretionary authorities of subsections (b)–(e). In his view, the statutory sequence matters: the mandatory language is prefaced by “In providing assistance under this section,” which ties the “shall” obligations to the decision to provide assistance (i.e., to exercise the discretionary authorities later set out). Put simply, for Lee the “shall” creates required operating rules for funded programs, not an independent, freestanding spending mandate that obligates the executive branch to run programs or maintain contractual mechanisms irrespective of its policy choices or appropriations constraints. The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” further reinforces, to his reading, that Congress contemplated executive discretion tied to funding availability.

## Qualified subsections: (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) preserve discretion
Judge Lee addresses two specific provisions the majority highlights—§ 1522(d)(2)(B), concerning temporary legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), concerning cash and medical assistance projects. He reads both as qualified obligations that leave significant room for executive judgment. Subsection (d)(2)(B) requires the Director to assume certain responsibilities “if necessary” and authorizes the Director to make “necessary” decisions—language that, Lee argues, is explicitly contingent and deferential to executive assessment of necessity. Subsection (e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to “develop and implement” projects “as needed,” again signaling that the duty to act pertains to identified needs and is governed by programmatic discretion. For Lee, these qualifiers undercut the majority’s view that § 1522 imposes an enforceable duty to maintain contractual reception-and-placement mechanisms at all times.

## Remedial consequence: no judicially compelled reinstatement of cooperative agreements
Because Lee reads § 1522 as authorizing programs rather than mandating them, he reaches a straightforward remedial conclusion: the district court erred in ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements and enjoining the government from suspending the statutory program. On Lee’s view, the court lacks the statutory predicate to compel execution or funding of resettlement contracts when the executive has chosen to suspend or reprioritize those programs. Practically, this means he would have granted a complete stay of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal—allowing the government to keep cooperative agreements terminated and to withhold reception-and-placement funding during the appeal—whereas the majority preserves a limited carveout that obligates the government to reinstate agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services to refugees already admitted.

## What to watch for in the forthcoming opinion
Judge Lee flags several issues that the full opinion will need to resolve, and these are the points that will shape the precedential significance of the case:

- Definitive construction of § 1522. Will the court accept Lee’s textualist account that § 1522 is predominantly permissive, or will the majority adopt a reading that treats some or all of § 1522 as imposing enforceable duties even when the executive has suspended program operations? How the panel resolves the interplay between the section title, repeated “is authorized” language, the “shall” language in subsection (a), and the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification will control the overall statutory posture.

- APA reviewability and the “contrary to law” question. If § 1522 is permissive, the termination of cooperative agreements is likely committed to agency discretion and therefore not “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” If § 1522 is mandatory, termination could be a final agency action “contrary to law” under the Administrative Procedure Act. The panel’s resolution will determine whether plaintiffs can succeed on APA claims.

- Scope of judicial authority to compel contractual mechanisms. Even if the court finds some statutory duty, it must decide whether and how a court may fashion equitable relief to compel the government to enter or reinstate contracts and cooperative agreements with private partners in order to fulfill that duty. Lee’s concurrence suggests skepticism that courts should compel the mechanics of program delivery where Congress did not plainly require them.

- Harmonization of entry power and post-admission obligations. The case implicates a structural question: how does the President’s broad authority to restrict entry under § 1182(f) sit alongside statutory obligations relating to refugees after admission? If the executive can suspend admissions but Congress has required certain post-admission services, courts may face competing commands. Lee’s position treats the post-admission statute as granting discretion that avoids a direct collision; the forthcoming opinion will need to decide whether a genuine conflict exists and, if so, how to reconcile or prioritize the statutes and the branch prerogatives they reflect.

Conclusion
Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent advances a tight, text-focused argument that § 1522 authorizes—rather than compels—domestic refugee resettlement programs. That interpretive stance leads him to oppose the district court’s remedy that would have reinstated cooperative agreements and required program operations during the appeal. The forthcoming merits opinion will have to choose between Lee’s permissive construction and the majority’s view that certain statutory duties are enforceable, and that choice will determine both the parties’ fate on the APA claims and the scope of judicially enforceable obligations for refugee resettlement programs.
