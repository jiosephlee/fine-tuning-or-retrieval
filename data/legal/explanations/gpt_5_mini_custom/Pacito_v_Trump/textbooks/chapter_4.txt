Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # Interim Merits Assessment: Statutory Duties to Provide Post-Admission Services

This chapter explains why the Ninth Circuit panel concluded, in its interim order, that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge—namely, that the executive branch violated statutory duties to provide certain post‑admission refugee services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522—and how that conclusion produced a narrow exception to the court’s stay of the district court injunctions. The discussion stays close to the panel’s reasoning: it identifies the specific statutory provisions the panel relied on, summarizes the implementing regulations and appropriations context that supply the “availability” predicate, shows how cooperative agreements have historically delivered those services, reviews the relevant record facts (especially termination of agreements and the government’s representations), and explains the tailored relief the panel ordered while the appeal proceeds.

## Statutory mandates identified by the panel

The panel anchored its APA concern in three statutory provisions within 8 U.S.C. § 1522 that, taken together, create substantive duties to provide certain reception and placement services for refugees once they are admitted to the United States. First, § 1522(a)(1)(A) directs that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement “shall,” to the extent of available appropriations, make available core resources—employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance structured not to discourage self‑sufficiency, and gender equality protections. The panel treated the use of “shall” in § 1522(a)(1)(A) as imposing a mandatory obligation on the executive when appropriations are available.

Second, § 1522(d)(2)(B) requires that, during an interim period while an unaccompanied minor refugee is in the United States or in transit and before placement, the Director “shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary,” and is “authorized to make necessary decisions” for the child’s immediate care. The panel read this language as creating at least a conditional mandatory duty (triggered when assumption of responsibility is “necessary”) that cannot be ignored if the condition is present.

Third, § 1522(e)(7)(A) commands that the Secretary “shall develop and implement alternative projects” providing interim support, medical services, and case management for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty‑six months “as needed.” The panel viewed this as another statutory command to furnish certain services for recently admitted refugees, subject to the “as needed” qualification but nonetheless mandatory when the need exists.

Taken together, these provisions supply the statutory substantive baseline that underlies the plaintiffs’ APA claim: that the executive may not abandon statutorily required reception and placement functions for refugees already admitted to the United States, particularly where appropriations are available and needs exist.

## Implementing regulations: 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.

The statute’s operational content is reinforced by the implementing regulations in 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which describe the federally funded refugee services that state and national resettlement agencies have historically provided. Those regulations define program components—case management, cash and medical assistance coordination, employment and language training, and other reception and placement functions—that map onto the statutory duties identified in § 1522. The panel relied on that regulatory framework to show that Congress did not write hollow directives: the statute and regulations together describe the types of services the executive has been expected to furnish to facilitate refugees’ integration and care after admission.

## Appropriations context: availability of funds in FY2025

Section 1522 repeatedly conditions duties on the availability of appropriations (“to the extent of available appropriations”), so the availability of funds is a crucial legal predicate. The panel pointed to the FY2025 appropriations for migration and refugee assistance: Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance (in the cited statutes), of which $800 million was later rescinded. The panel treated those enacted appropriations and the partial rescission as relevant record facts supporting the conclusion that funds existed at the time the government terminated cooperative agreements—i.e., that the “to the extent of available appropriations” condition was satisfied, so the statutory “shall” language could be operative. The panel thus considered appropriations context in assessing whether the government could lawfully stop providing services without running afoul of § 1522.

## Delivery mechanism: cooperative agreements under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)

Section 1522 explicitly contemplates that reception and placement services may be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” Historically, the federal government has relied on cooperative agreements with national and local voluntary resettlement agencies to carry out the § 1522 duties. The panel emphasized this established delivery mechanism because it bears on what it is reasonable to order in a preliminary remedy: if cooperative agreements have been the usual vehicle for delivering federally‑mandated services, and if the government abruptly terminates those agreements while funds remain available, a court may require temporary reinstatement of those agreements to preserve statutorily required care for already‑admitted refugees during the pendency of judicial review.

## Record facts: termination, the RFP, and uncertainty at argument

The record facts drove the panel’s practical concern. On February 26, 2025—after the district court’s initial preliminary injunction—the State Department terminated every cooperative agreement for reception and placement services in the United States and nearly all overseas processing cooperative agreements. On March 10, 2025 the government told the district court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency” to provide those services. But at oral argument before the panel the government could not say whether any substantive progress had been made toward securing replacement agencies. The panel viewed that sequence—termination of the existing agreements, a promise to solicit replacements, and uncertainty about whether replacements were near— as strengthening the plaintiffs’ APA claim that the government’s termination left refugees admitted to the United States without the statutorily required services, at least in the short term.

In short, the panel did not need to resolve the ultimate legal question on the merits at this stage; it concluded that the factual record showed a credible risk that, absent interim relief, the executive branch would not be meeting the statutory duties the plaintiffs invoked.

## Why the panel concluded the government is not likely to prevail on the APA challenge

Under the APA, a plaintiff can prevail on a claim that an agency action was “contrary to law” if the action is inconsistent with a statutory mandate. The panel identified that risk here based on three linked points: (1) § 1522 contains categorical “shall” commands to make certain services available to refugees, (2) appropriations for refugee assistance existed in FY2025 (so the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification did not foreclose relief), and (3) the government had terminated the practical means of delivering those services and could not credibly show replacement capacity. Given those considerations, the panel determined that the government was not likely to succeed on an APA defense that its terminations and defunding were lawful because § 1522 either did not impose a duty or because appropriations were unavailable. The panel therefore treated the plaintiffs’ APA claim as sufficiently compelling on the merits to weigh against a blanket stay of the district court’s injunctions.

Importantly, the panel separated this APA result from its view on the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions. It found the government likely to prevail on the § 1182(f) issue (and therefore stayed the district court’s injunction to the extent it blocked a presidential suspension of admissions), but concluded the government was unlikely to prevail on plaintiffs’ statutory/APA challenge concerning services owed to refugees already admitted.

## Targeted relief: reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees

As a result of the foregoing, the panel issued narrowly tailored interim relief while the appeal proceeds: it stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions in their entirety except for one carveout. The government was directed to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The panel described those services as “the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements,” which ties the required relief to the statutory duties and the implementing regulations (rather than imposing novel obligations).

Two aspects of this remedy are important to keep in mind. First, the relief is targeted to refugees already admitted — the panel left intact the government’s ability, subject to other injunction limits, to pause future admissions under the president’s § 1182(f) authority. Second, the remedy is pragmatic and limited: it seeks to restore the preexisting operational relationships (cooperative agreements) the government used to fulfill § 1522 duties while the appellate process runs its course, rather than prescribing a new permanent structure or adjudicating in full whether § 1522 creates an unfetterable duty in all circumstances.

## Takeaway for students

At the preliminary‑stage, courts frequently must balance competing legal questions and practical consequences. Here, the panel applied that approach: it accepted the government’s strong arguments (and precedent) about the President’s broad § 1182(f) authority but at the same time recognized a separate statutory channel—§ 1522 together with its regulations and the appropriation record—through which Congress required the executive to provide certain services to refugees already admitted. The combination of statutory “shall” language, an implementing regulatory scheme, available appropriations, an established delivery mechanism (cooperative agreements), and the government’s record of terminating those agreements without demonstrable replacements persuaded the panel that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA claim concerning post‑admission services. That determination justified a limited, practical judicial remedy: temporary reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to carry out the usual reception and placement services for admitted refugees while the appeal proceeds. The panel signaled it will explain the full merits reasoning in an opinion to follow.
