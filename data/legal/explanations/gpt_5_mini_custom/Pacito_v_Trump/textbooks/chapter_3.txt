Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 3: # Interim Merits Assessment: Authority to Suspend Refugee Admissions

This chapter explains the Ninth Circuit’s interim assessment that the government is likely to prevail on its defense of Executive Order No. 14163 under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and how that assessment shaped the court’s decision to stay the district court’s injunction against the suspension of refugee admissions. The opinion relies principally on the text of § 1182(f), Supreme Court precedent emphasizing judicial deference in immigration and national-security contexts (most notably Trump v. Hawaii), and the four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder. Reading the court’s reasoning requires attention to what § 1182(f) actually says, how courts treat presidential “findings” under that provision, and how likelihood of success on the merits interacts with the other stay factors.

## Textual breadth of § 1182(f): suspension of entry of any class of aliens based on presidential detriment finding

Section 1182(f) authorizes the President to act whenever he “finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The statute then permits the President, “by proclamation,” to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate.” The panel’s interim order reads this language broadly: Congress plainly gave the President authority to suspend entry of “any class” of aliens, and the provision’s operative condition—the President’s finding of detriment—is the “sole prerequisite” spelled out in the text. The court therefore treats § 1182(f) as a grant of sweeping power to suspend entry, subject to the single textual requirement that the President make the requisite finding.

For students, the key takeaway is that statutory language matters here: the statute speaks in universal terms (“all aliens or any class of aliens”) and places the triggering decision squarely with the President (“whenever the President finds”). The panel relied on those textual features to conclude that Executive Order 14163 falls within the statute’s plain scope.

## Limits on judicial review: no “searching inquiry” into the President’s justifications per Trump v. Hawaii

Because § 1182(f) vests the determinative “finding” in the President, the panel limited the scope of judicial inquiry into the Executive’s justification. The court quoted Trump v. Hawaii for the principle that courts should not engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Trump underscored that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and vests “ample power” in him to impose entry restrictions. The Ninth Circuit adopted that posture in this interim order: the question for the stay analysis is not whether the President’s policy is politically appealing or factually optimal, but whether the statutory prerequisites are satisfied and whether the statute’s text bounds the President’s action.

Practically, this means the court’s preliminary review focuses on two simple legal checks: did the President make the formal finding that entry “would be detrimental,” and does the order fall within the textual authority conferred by § 1182(f)? The court expressed that the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the statutory finding here and that the executive order does not exceed “any textual limit” on the President’s authority. The court therefore declined to require a searching evidentiary record or to second-guess the substance of the President’s national-security or foreign-policy judgments at the preliminary stage.

## Application to E.O. 14163: the President made the requisite detriment finding; the order fits § 1182(f)’s scope

Applying those principles to Executive Order 14163, the panel concluded that the President satisfied § 1182(f)’s formal condition. The order expressly states a presidential finding that refugee entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and directs suspension of refugee admissions pending further findings. That language tracks the statutory trigger; accordingly, the court treated the President’s declaration as adequate for the purposes of § 1182(f).

The panel also found the order’s substance to be within § 1182(f)’s scope. Because the statute authorizes suspension of the “entry of . . . any class of aliens,” the court read the refugee class described in EO 14163 to be a class that § 1182(f) covers. In short, under the court’s preliminary legal assessment, (1) the Executive made the finding § 1182(f) requires, and (2) the operational step taken—suspending entry of a class of aliens (refugees)—is the very remedial power the statute confers. That conclusion provided the foundation for the court’s view that the government is “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.”

Students should note the limited nature of this assessment: the panel made a preliminary, likelihood-of-success determination for stay purposes, not a final merits disposition. The court signaled that a fuller opinion will elaborate the merits later, but the interim reasoning relied on the straightforward textual fit and on deference principles from Trump v. Hawaii.

## Balancing the Nken factors: domestic and foreign policy interests, irreparable harm to the government, and public interest favor a stay of the injunction against the entry suspension

A federal court deciding whether to stay a district-court injunction pending appeal applies the four-factor Nken test: (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) likelihood of irreparable harm to the movant absent a stay, (3) potential injury to other interested parties from granting the stay, and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken makes the first two factors “the most critical.”

Because the panel concluded the government is likely to succeed on the § 1182(f) claim, that critical first factor weighed strongly in favor of a stay. The court then linked that conclusion to the second factor—irreparable harm—by invoking the national-security, foreign-relations, and immigration policy interests that § 1182(f) empowers the President to protect. The panel characterized those interests as substantial and as matters where the President is afforded “broad discretion.” In that context the court viewed the government as likely to suffer irreparable injury if the injunction remained in force and prevented the President from implementing a suspension tied to those policy judgments.

For the remaining factors, the panel concluded that neither the harm to plaintiffs nor the public interest counseled against a stay. Although the district court had granted an injunction to protect plaintiffs’ asserted statutory and APA rights, the panel weighed those interests against the government’s interest in preserving its chosen immigration and foreign-policy posture while the appeal proceeds. Because these decisions often implicate foreign relations and national security, the court viewed the public interest as favoring the maintenance of executive flexibility and cautioned that courts should be reluctant to obstruct such executive acts at the preliminary stage.

Putting the Nken factors together, the Ninth Circuit found that the government had shown both a strong likelihood of success (based on the text of § 1182(f) and deference doctrines) and a risk of irreparable harm if the stay were denied. Those two central factors, combined with considerations about competing harms and the public interest, justified a stay of the district court’s injunction insofar as it barred implementation of the entry suspension.

Conclusion

In sum, the panel’s interim order treats § 1182(f) as a broad statutory grant allowing the President to suspend the entry of a class of aliens so long as he makes the statutory “detriment” finding. Drawing on Trump v. Hawaii, the court limited judicial inquiry into the substance of that finding and, on that basis, concluded the government is likely to prevail on the merits of its § 1182(f) defense. Because likelihood of success and the prospect of irreparable injury favor the government, those core Nken factors supported issuing a stay of the district court’s injunction against the refugee-entry suspension while the appeal proceeds.
