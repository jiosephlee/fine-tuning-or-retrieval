Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 5: # Operative Order: What Is Stayed and What Must Proceed

This chapter explains precisely what the Ninth Circuit did in its interim order: which prior appellate stays it vacated, which district-court injunctions it put on hold, and the narrow exception the court left in place requiring the federal government to restore certain resettlement services for refugees already in the United States. It also notes the disposition of the government’s emergency motion and the procedural posture going forward.

## Vacatur of prior appellate stays

The panel began by withdrawing the temporary stays it had earlier entered. Two categories of prior appellate action were vacated: an administrative stay the court issued on July 18, 2025, and an earlier stay issued March 25, 2025 (as later amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025). Vacatur of those orders means they no longer have any operative effect; the new interim order replaces them. For a student, the key point is that vacatur here does not resolve the merits of the underlying dispute — it simply removes those earlier temporary stays from the record and substitutes a fresh stay tailored to the court’s present ruling.

## Global stay of the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions (with one exception)

The core operative move in the order is a stay pending appeal of the district court’s two written preliminary injunctions dated February 28 and March 24, 2025. Those district-court injunctions had (1) prohibited implementation of substantial portions of President Trump’s Executive Order 14163 suspending refugee admissions and had (2) required the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements that had been terminated to provide reception and placement services to refugees. The Ninth Circuit’s order now stays those district-court injunctions “in their entirety,” meaning that, as a general matter, the district court’s work is paused while the appeal proceeds and the government may not be constrained by those injunctions during the appeal.

It is important to read that sweeping language with the next section in mind: the stay is comprehensive except for a deliberately narrow carveout. The panel’s reasoning for granting the stay rests on the familiar four-factor framework for stays pending appeal from Nken v. Holder: likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, balance of equities, and public interest — with the first two being most critical. The panel concluded (as previewed in the order) that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, and that the equities and public-interest factors favor the government on that question. That conclusion is why the injunctions that would have halted the suspension of admissions are stayed.

## Carveout and mandate: reinstating cooperative agreements to provide § 1522 reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted

Although the district-court injunctions are stayed generally, the Ninth Circuit left intact one narrow but concrete obligation: the government must reinstate those cooperative agreements that are necessary to provide the reception and placement services required by statute to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The order directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States,” and it specifies that such services “shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.”

Two features of that carveout merit careful attention.

First, who is covered. The court’s order applies only to refugees who have already been admitted into the United States. It does not require the government to resume overseas processing or to admit refugees who remain outside the United States; it does not resurrect any obligation to process conditionally approved applicants abroad. The difference between “admitted” refugees and those eligible or conditionally approved but still outside the country is important: the court confined the reinstatement mandate to persons already present in the United States and to services that assist their domestic resettlement.

Second, what services are covered. The panel tied the injunction’s carveout to the statutory scheme in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement and assistance and authorizes (and in some subsections instructs) certain reception-and-placement services. The order does not catalogue every item but expressly requires the government to restore the “usual and customary services” under the prior cooperative agreements. In the district-court briefing and in the appellate opinion the panel flagged illustrative statutory provisions — such as employment training and placement, English-language instruction, cash and medical assistance and case management for recently resettled refugees, and specific authority concerning unaccompanied refugee children — as the kinds of programs § 1522 contemplates. Practically, therefore, the government must reestablish the contractual relationships (cooperative agreements) needed to deliver the core reception-and-placement services that historically were provided under those agreements for refugees who are already in the United States.

The order’s wording also limits the scope in a second way: the government must reinstate only the cooperative agreements that are “necessary to provide” the § 1522 services. That language signals that the court expects the government to restore the functionality needed to deliver statutory services, rather than to reopen every programmatic element of the prior agreements regardless of need. The panel’s direction responds to the record evidence that the State Department had terminated cooperative agreements and that, at oral argument, the government could not identify whether a replacement network of providers was in place.

Finally, the panel’s carveout reflects a legal split in the court. The majority concluded that plaintiffs are likely to succeed on at least one of their APA-based challenges tied to § 1522 (hence the mandate to provide services), whereas it concluded the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the executive’s authority under § 1182(f) to suspend admissions (hence the stay of the broader injunctions). Judge Lee filed a partial concurrence and partial dissent taking a different view of § 1522’s scope (reading it as permissive rather than mandatory), but the operative order is the majority’s.

## Emergency motion (July 17) denied as moot

The government had filed an emergency motion on July 17, 2025, seeking relief concerning enforcement or clarification of the existing stay regime. Because the panel’s new order now supersedes the prior administrative stay and replaces the earlier March 25 stay with the present stay-and-carveout order, the court disposed of that emergency motion as moot. In other words, the panel addressed the underlying issues by issuing this interim order, making the separate emergency motion unnecessary.

## Case submitted after expedited argument; full merits opinion to follow

Procedurally, the Ninth Circuit heard expedited oral argument and, by this order, submitted the case for decision. The panel expressly stated that a full written opinion on the merits will issue in due course. That means two things for readers. First, this stay order is interim and limited to preserving the parties’ positions while the appeal proceeds; it does not finally resolve the substantive statutory and administrative-law questions. Second, the order telegraphs the court’s preliminary conclusions on two main legal points: (1) substantial deference accompanies the President’s invocation of § 1182(f) and the court is inclined to uphold the suspension of admissions; and (2) the court perceives stronger plaintiffs’ claims under the APA tied to § 1522’s statutorily defined reception-and-placement duties, which is why the court compelled the government to restore services for admitted refugees. The forthcoming opinion will develop the full legal analysis that undergirds those tentative conclusions.

Conclusion

In short, the Ninth Circuit vacated its earlier temporary stays and issued a new interim stay that suspends the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions pending appeal, except for a narrow, concrete requirement: the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services required by § 1522 for refugees already admitted to the United States, restoring the usual and customary services previously provided under those agreements. The emergency motion is moot, the case has been submitted after expedited argument, and a full merits opinion will follow.
