Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## Hawaii, reheated: The Ninth Circuit’s broad deference to presidential power under §1182(f) now reaches refugees

Why this matters — in short: the Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump reprises the core holding of Trump v. Hawaii and applies it to the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. §1182(f). The panel makes clear that, under Hawaii, courts should not undertake a “searching inquiry” into the executive’s rationale once the President has “found” that entry of a class of aliens would be “detrimental.” At the same time, the order marks an important limit: the court concluded (at least for interim relief) that the administration is unlikely to prevail on plaintiffs’ APA-based challenge to the abrupt termination of statutory reception-and-placement programs under 8 U.S.C. §1522, and it directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements to provide services for refugees already admitted. That split — broad deference on admissions, narrower review when domestic statutory obligations are implicated — will be the key doctrinal battleground going forward.

What the panel did (and did not) hold

The Ninth issued a stay pending appeal of the district court’s preliminary injunctions that had blocked large parts of Executive Order 14163 (the refugee-suspension order) and ordered the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements. Applying the four-factor stay standard from Nken, the panel concluded that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of its defense of the suspension under §1182(f). Relying on Trump v. Hawaii, the panel repeated the core proposition from that case: §1182(f) “exudes deference,” the “sole prerequisite” is that the President “find[]” the entry of a class of aliens would be detrimental, and courts should not subject the President’s justification to a searching review. Accordingly, the court found a strong showing of likely success as to the executive’s authority to suspend refugee admissions.

But the panel also recognized that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims tied to the Refugee Act’s domestic resettlement provisions. The court parsed §1522 and related regulatory authorities governing reception and placement services and concluded that the statutory scheme contemplates concrete duties and delivery mechanisms (including cooperative agreements with nonprofit resettlement agencies). Because the State Department had terminated those cooperative agreements and the government could not assure the court that alternative arrangements were in place, the panel directed reinstatement of the agreements needed to provide reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent encapsulates the competing statutory readings: while the majority treats §1522 as imposing enforceable obligations, Lee reads the section as largely permissive authorization that grants the executive discretion to design programs “to the extent of available appropriations.”

Why the court’s approach matters doctrinally

At the doctrinal level the order does two things that will reverberate in future litigation.

First, it extends the Hawaii framework to refugees in a straightforward way: refugees are “a class of aliens,” and the President’s finding under §1182(f) is, per Hawaii, entitled to substantial deference. That means that an executive suspension of admissions — even when it collides with a detailed statutory refugee admissions apparatus — will receive a formidable presumption of validity in the courts unless challengers can point to some textual limit on §1182(f) or to other compelling statutory or constitutional constraints. Practically speaking, the Ninth’s interim ruling signals that routine suits attacking a presidential admissions moratorium will face a steep uphill climb where the government invokes §1182(f) and makes an express finding of detriment.

Second, and crucially, the order also recognizes that Congress did not leave the domestic resettlement regime purely to executive whim. By insisting on reinstatement of cooperative agreements for reception and placement services for refugees already admitted, the panel draws a line between the President’s control over entry and the executive’s statutory obligations to people already in the country. That is an important doctrinal carve-out: Hawaii governs entry restrictions, but it does not (or should not) immunize the executive from complying with other federal statutes that create duties and programs directed at persons within the United States.

Where the real fights will be — and what litigants will press next

Several legal flashpoints follow predictably from this order.

One line of attack will be statutory displacement. Plaintiffs can (and likely will) argue that the Refugee Act’s structure and specific mandates — annual admission determinations, consultation requirements, and the resettlement-service provisions — create a statutory scheme that implicitly limits the President’s §1182(f) power, at least to the extent that the suspension would undermine effectuating those congressional policies. The government will answer that §1182(f) is a broad, freestanding authority that permits the President to suspend entry of any class of aliens, and that Congress’s failure to cabin that power in the Refugee Act means the President’s finding governs.

A close textual fight is inevitable over §1522. The Ninth’s interim view treats §1522 as imposing enforceable obligations that can be vindicated via APA claims (an approach that undergirds the remedial direction to reinstate cooperative agreements). Judge Lee’s dissent, by contrast, emphasizes the numerous statutory signals of permissive “authorization” (and the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification). The resolution of that interpretive dispute will determine whether courts can order the executive to operationalize refugee programs after admissions have begun, and to what degree budgetary adjustments affect those duties. The Ninth’s opinion already references Congress’s appropriations for migration and refugee assistance and a rescission, flagging that funding-level disputes will also be front-and-center.

A second line of attack will be framed in administrative-law terms: abrupt termination of long-standing cooperative agreements and the failure to follow ordinary notice-and-comment or reasoned-decisionmaking procedures may be vulnerable to arbitrary-and-capricious review. The Ninth’s interim disposition suggests such APA claims have real traction, at least where the agency cannot point to a reasoned plan to replace the operational infrastructure for admitted refugees.

A third battleground will be constitutional and separation-of-powers doctrines. Although Hawaii counsels deference in the immigration-entry context, litigants may press the “major questions” frame (arguing that far-reaching policy changes affecting a statutory humanitarian program require clear congressional authorization) or invoke appropriations prerogatives as a limit on unilateral executive retrenchment. The Ninth did not resolve those issues in the interim order; they remain available to litigants on appeal and to the Supreme Court if the case reaches it.

Practical consequences and political feedback loops

Practically, the Ninth’s order creates a split effect: the government may get broad latitude to stop new admissions, but it cannot unilaterally abandon services to refugees already brought to the United States without exposing itself to judicial remedies. For resettlement agencies, the immediate effect is operational: reinstatement of cooperative agreements (if the district court’s injunction is ultimately sustained) or continued uncertainty if the government layers on new procurement processes. For refugees, the ruling preserves access to the domestic services Congress contemplated — at least while litigation proceeds — even as new admissions may be halted.

Politically, the decision is likely to intensify pressure on Congress. If the executive uses §1182(f) to pause USRAP admissions repeatedly — and the judiciary continues to treat that power as plenary with respect to entry — Congress may face incentives to clarify the statutory architecture, tighten appropriations, or more explicitly condition the President’s §1182(f) authority. Conversely, the Ninth’s willingness to enforce domestic statutory duties may encourage litigants and resettlement providers to press statutory and APA claims where executive policy decisions disrupt on-the-ground programs.

What to watch next

The Ninth has pledged a full merits opinion; that forthcoming opinion will be consequential both for refugee law and for the broader reach of Hawaii’s deferential approach. Key questions to watch in that opinion (and on further appeal) include how the court delineates the boundary between entry restrictions and domestic statutory obligations, how it construes §1522’s “to the extent of available appropriations” language in practice, and whether it engages Machiavellian (or minimalist) readings of Hawaii or seeks to cabin that precedent in the unique statutory context of the Refugee Act.

In short: Pacito reasserts Hawaii’s strong presumption of deference to presidential findings under §1182(f), bringing refugees within the same analytic frame as other classes of aliens. But it also signals that the executive’s power over admissions is not a universal veto over all statutory regimes Congress has erected for people once they are on U.S. soil. That tension — enormous in policy consequence and manageable only through sustained litigation or legislative clarification — is likely to animate the next chapters of this dispute.



# Mandatory or Merely Authorized? The Panel Split on Whether §1522 Imposes Enforceable Duties to Serve Admitted Refugees

## Why this matters
The Ninth Circuit’s emergency order in Pacito v. Trump puts a spotlight on a recurring, high-stakes tension: how far may the executive reach when it both restricts entry under broad immigration powers and simultaneously tries to zero out the programs that support people already admitted? On the one hand the panel gives the government wide deference to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. §1182(f). On the other hand, the court—in language that stopped short of a final merits opinion—has already concluded plaintiffs are likely to prevail on an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. §1522 and has required the government to reinstate cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services to refugees already in the United States. That split in reasoning — deference about who gets in, compulsion about how those admitted must be served — raises doctrinal and practical questions that could shape agency behavior, litigation strategies, and congressional responses going forward.

## What the panel did and why the order signals a statutory duty
The court issued a stay of the district court’s broader injunctions against implementation of President Trump’s refugee-suspension order, accepting the government’s contention that §1182(f) gives the President far-reaching authority over entry restrictions. But the panel also concluded—again preliminarily and without a final merits opinion—that the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims: that the government unlawfully failed to provide statutorily required reception-and-placement services for refugees under 8 U.S.C. §1522. On that basis the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.

The court’s tentative reading of §1522 rests on statutory text and context the panel highlighted: §1522 contains multiple provisions that use “shall” (including, notably, subsections dealing with unaccompanied minor refugees and with developing projects for cash and medical assistance) and also ties some duties to appropriations while Congress has in fact appropriated substantial funds for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025. The panel emphasized that §1522 authorizes use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies and that the government had terminated those cooperative agreements without being able to point to any alternative capable of delivering the statutorily mandated services. The court also pointed to statutory reporting and accountability requirements (e.g., §1523) as further evidence that Congress contemplated an ongoing program of services and oversight.

## Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent: a competing textualist baseline
Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and partial dissent frames a contrasting, textualist argument: §1522’s title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the repeated phrasing in subsections (b)–(e) that the Director “is authorized” to provide particular kinds of assistance, signal a permissive, not mandatory, statutory regime. From that perspective, the “shall” language in §1522(a) simply prescribes how the agency must act "in providing assistance under this section" — that is, it imposes conditions and standards if and when the executive chooses to fund or operate programs, rather than creating a judicially enforceable obligation to operate them at all. Lee further stresses the common limiting formula “to the extent of available appropriations,” reading that phrase as decisive: if Congress has not made funds available, the executive cannot be compelled to run the programs.

Judge Lee’s view is familiar to courts that treat statutory “authorization” language and appropriation-linked commands as signals of executive discretion. His position puts a premium on titles, the “is authorized” phrasing, and the conditionality created by appropriations language.

## What’s at stake under the APA
The practical and doctrinal hinge is whether §1522 imposes a non‑discretionary duty that a court may enforce under APA §706(1) — the provision that authorizes a court to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.” If §1522 imposes mandatory duties (the majority’s working assumption), then terminating cooperative agreements that implement those duties can be actionable agency action unlawfully withheld or otherwise arbitrary and capricious. If §1522 is merely permissive (the dissent’s reading), then Heckler‑style deference to agency enforcement and resource allocation discretion likely protects the government from judicial compulsion.

Two features make the majority’s preliminary view plausible. First, the statutory text contains unqualified “shall” provisions tied to specific programmatic duties (for example, taking legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children “if necessary” and developing projects for cash and medical assistance “as needed”). Second, Congress did appropriate (and then partially rescind) funding for migration and refugee assistance in the relevant fiscal year—so the “to the extent of available appropriations” caveat is not an empty shelter for the government here unless it can show appropriations are truly unavailable for these services. The panel explicitly relied on the government’s apparent uncertainty about replacement arrangements when ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements: courts are less likely to accept a unilateral executive shuttering of programs where statutory structure, appropriations, and oversight requirements point toward ongoing obligations.

## The practical consequences for agencies and litigants
If the Ninth Circuit’s tentative approach is sustained, agencies will face limits when they attempt to “zero‑out” programs by terminating cooperative agreements or grants without a clear statutory basis. Two practical consequences follow.

First, agencies that want to pause or wind down services tied to statutory programs will need to demonstrate (ideally in contemporaneous administrative records) either that their interpretation of the statute is reasonable (if the statute is ambiguous) or that appropriations truly do not cover the programs in question. Mere policy preference or executive instruction to cut funding will be a weak record if Congress has appropriated funds and the statute contains mandatory‑sounding directives.

Second, plaintiffs will be encouraged to bring APA claims that target terminations of implementing arrangements — not just claims about admissions policy — because a successful §706(1) claim can produce an order compelling the agency to reinstate programs or otherwise comply with statutory duties. That dynamic changes the stakes of immigration‑oriented litigation: even when a court gives the executive broad latitude to control entry under §1182(f), it may still police the domestic obligations that attend those who are already admitted.

## Broader doctrinal implications and likely paths forward
Pacito sharpens at least three doctrinal lines of inquiry that other courts will soon confront. One is the perennial “shall” vs. “is authorized” interpretive problem: when does “shall” create a mandatory, judicially enforceable duty and when is it a hortatory or conditional term embedded in a discretionary statutory regime? Courts look at the whole statutory scheme, related provisions (including reporting and appropriation language), and practical indicators of congressional intent; this order signals that the Ninth Circuit will not treat the title and the “authorized” phraseology as dispositive where the statute also contains specific “shall” prescriptions and where appropriations exist.

Second, Pacito returns the focus to the interface between national‑security/immigration deference and ordinary administrative law. Trump v. Hawaii gives the executive considerable leeway on who may enter the country, but it does not automatically immunize domestic programmatic decisions affecting those who are in the United States from APA review. That doctrinal split — wide executive power over entry, narrower room to avoid statutory duties for individuals already present — may produce more compartmentalized litigation strategies and, potentially, more limited remedies targeted at domestic implementations rather than entry policy.

Third, the order underscores the significance of appropriations and Congress’s fiscal choices. Agencies trying to rely on “to the extent of available appropriations” need to show the funds do not exist or are otherwise unavailable for the covered purposes. Where Congress appropriates funds and requires reporting, courts may be skeptical that the statutory language was meant to confer sink-or-swim discretion to executive officers.

The next steps to watch are obvious. The court promised a full opinion, which should elaborate the interpretive framework for §1522 and the analytical path from text to preliminary relief. If the Ninth Court ultimately affirms the view that §1522 imposes mandatory duties, that decision may produce a circuit split with courts inclined to read similar statutory regimes as permissive. That split could attract en banc review or certiorari, particularly given the national ramifications for refugee resettlement and the intersection with executive immigration authority.

## Bottom line
Pacito is a reminder that the courts will treat two questions differently: who the President may exclude, and what duties the government owes once it admits people into the United States. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order signals that, at least preliminarily, statutory provisions that look to create programmatic frameworks with “shall” language and appropriations behind them can be read as imposing enforceable duties under the APA — even when the executive concurrently invokes sweeping discretion over entry. Judge Lee’s forceful countertextualism will have traction in other settings, but for now the court’s order narrows the practical effect of the refugee suspension by protecting the resettlement infrastructure that serves refugees already admitted. The final merits opinion, and any further appeals, will determine whether that protective line becomes durable law.



# An Unusual Stay with Mandatory Relief: Appellate Tailoring, Nken’s Factors, and Refugee Services

## Why this matters

The Ninth Circuit’s administrative order in Pacito v. Trump is procedurally and doctrinally noteworthy. On an expedited record, the court stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions that blocked President Trump’s suspension of refugee admissions and related actions, reaffirming broad deference to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). At the same time, and unusually, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. That combination — an appellate stay of an injunction together with affirmative, mandatory relief for a narrow class of plaintiffs — raises important questions about how courts balance competing claims and the institutional limits of appellate stays when statutory duties are alleged.

## What the Ninth Circuit did

The panel vacated earlier administrative and partial stays and issued a new stay pending appeal of the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions, but carved out a mandatory duty: the government must reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” The court explicitly relied on the Nken stay factors and on Trump v. Hawaii for its view that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and that the President has “undoubtedly fulfilled” the statutory prerequisite of making a finding that entry of a class of aliens would be detrimental to U.S. interests. But the court also made a separate assessment on statutory and APA grounds, concluding that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge tied to the statutory scheme for post‑arrival refugee services under § 1522.

The panel therefore stayed the injunctions that would have restrained the President’s suspension of refugee admissions, while preserving — indeed ordering — certain relief for admitted refugees pending resolution of the merits.

## How the court reasoned

The court framed its analysis around Nken’s four-factor stay test, emphasizing the two “most critical” factors: likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury. On the first, it treated the § 1182(f) claim as likely to favor the government, citing Trump v. Hawaii and reading the statute’s “finds” language and expansive text as giving the President broad authority to suspend entry. That conclusion carried weight on the stay: it justified staying the district court’s injunction insofar as it curtailed the President’s ability to impose a moratorium on admissions.

But the panel separated the claims. It concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on APA challenges tied to the resettlement services statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the court read as imposing substantive obligations for reception-and-placement services and related duties (including directed attention to specific subsections and the existing appropriation picture). Given the government’s February 2025 termination of cooperative agreements, its representation that it was preparing requests for proposals, and its apparent uncertainty at argument about progress toward replacement providers, the panel found a real risk that statutorily required services would not be provided absent judicially ordered relief. Balancing the injury and public-interest factors accordingly produced the hybrid result: broad stay of the injunctions as to admission policy, plus a judicially compelled reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees so that statutory services continue while the appeal proceeds.

Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent underscores the flashpoint. Lee agreed with the panel that the President likely has authority to suspend admissions but disagreed with the panel’s reading of § 1522 as imposing a mandatory duty. Lee insisted that § 1522’s title (“Authorization for programs…”) and repeated statutory “authoriz[ation]” language point toward permissive, not mandatory, executive action, and would have stayed the district court injunctions in full.

## Why the mix — stay plus affirmative relief — is striking

Appellate courts routinely tailor stays to protect the status quo and to avoid irreparable harm, and Nken authorizes a flexible, factor-driven approach. What makes Pacito unusual is that the court did not simply leave the district court’s order in place for some narrow group; it affirmatively ordered the executive to take (or restore) contractual steps to deliver services. That is, the panel issued relief that looks a lot like a mandatory injunction at the appellate stage: an order that requires the government to reinstate cooperative agreements and to provide “usual and customary services” to admitted refugees under § 1522.

Two features make that move consequential. First, it reflects a bifurcated likelihood-of-success analysis: deference controls the immigration/suspension claim, but statutory mandates control the APA claim. The court’s approach treats the stay as a tool for fine‑grained equity rather than an all-or-nothing block. Second, ordering affirmative performance by the executive—directing the reinstatement of contracts or cooperative agreements—moves the court into an operational supervisory role, with attendant separation-of-powers and feasibility concerns. The panel tried to cabin that step by limiting relief to services for refugees already admitted and by referencing statutory text and appropriations, but the practical implementation will raise operational questions (which agencies, which organizations, precisely what services and scope), and likely further litigation about enforcement and scope.

## Institutional and doctrinal implications

Pacito has several downstream consequences worth watching. First, it underscores that an appellate court assessing a stay may perform a claim-by-claim, factor-by-factor balancing, yielding hybrid orders tailored to competing statutory regimes. That is within Nken, but it tests how far a reviewing court may go in directing affirmative government performance without a final merits decision.

Second, the decision highlights a recurring tension between broad executive power over entry and more constrained statutory obligations once individuals have entered. The court’s reading — that the President’s suspension authority under § 1182(f) is highly discretionary, while § 1522 can cabin that discretion by imposing duties toward admitted refugees — signals that even when the Executive wins on immigration policy, statutory protections for those already here may remain enforceable. That doctrinal pinning could encourage more challenges that press statutory-service claims alongside broader immigration-policy suits.

Third, the opinion throws the “authorization” versus “mandatory” question about benefits statutes into relief. Judge Lee’s textualist counterargument — that § 1522 is an authorization, not a mandate — will likely animate further briefing and possibly create scope for en banc review or certiorari if circuits split on whether § 1522 creates judicially enforceable duties. The presence of the appropriations caveat (“to the extent of available appropriations”) complicates the matter; appropriations history and the Rescissions Act reductions the panel noted will be a live factual and legal battleground on the merits.

Finally, there are practical ramifications. The panel expressly noted the government’s own uncertainty about lining up replacement providers and relied on the existing cooperative agreement infrastructure as the means to preserve statutory services. Reinstatement orders will require the government to reengage with former partners, and disputes over what “necessary” and “usual and customary” mean will likely spawn additional litigation and supervision (recall that the district court had appointed a magistrate to oversee a framework). That supervisionpotency is not doctrinally novel, but applied at the appellate stay stage it is operationally significant.

## What to watch next

The court promised a full merits opinion to follow; that opinion will need to reconcile the tension the panel identified and will answer two central questions. First, how robust is the President’s § 1182(f) power in the post‑Trump v. Hawaii landscape when measured against statutory and administrative law constraints? Second, does § 1522 impose judicially enforceable duties that survive executive restructuring or defunding choices, particularly in a tight appropriations environment? The forthcoming opinion will also likely elaborate the panel’s view on remedies and the proper scope of court-ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements.

For now, Pacito is a reminder that stays are not merely binary rubber stamps. Appellate courts can — and in the Ninth’s view here, must — weigh discrete claims and craft tailored relief to protect statutory rights for those currently within U.S. borders even while respecting executive latitude over admissions. That hybrid posture resolves immediate risks to newly admitted refugees, but it invites more doctrinal skirmishing about statutory meaning, judicial authority to order affirmative government action, and the practical bounds of court supervision of executive programs while an appeal remains pending.



# Appropriations as Constraint: The Ninth Circuit’s Pacito stay and what it means for defunding USRAP, §1522, and the APA

## Why Pacito matters
The Ninth Circuit’s emergency stay in Pacito v. Trump, issued September 12, 2025, is more than an interlocutory skirmish over refugee policy. The panel split the difference: it accepted the Executive’s broad authority to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (following Trump v. Hawaii), but it balked at permitting the Administration to abandon the domestic resettlement apparatus for refugees already admitted to the United States. Practically, the court’s interim directive — requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” — marks a judicial willingness to enforce statutory resettlement duties even while deferring to the President on border-entry decisions. Doctrinally, the decision raises a key question: when a statute uses both mandatory phrasing (“shall”) and qualifying language (“to the extent of available appropriations”), what limits do appropriations principles place on executive flexibility to halt programs? And what Administrative Procedure Act (APA) problems arise when the government abruptly terminates cooperative agreements without a clear plan to honor statutory obligations?

## What the Ninth Circuit ordered and why
The panel granted a stay of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal except for one substantive carve-out: the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted. The court reasoned that, while the President likely has authority under §1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, the government was unlikely to prevail on at least one APA challenge grounded in 8 U.S.C. §1522. Section 1522, the court emphasized, contains specific provisions (and implementing regulations) that require the federal government, through the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) and related mechanisms, to make various services available to refugees — employment assistance, English-language training, cash and medical programs, and special responsibilities for unaccompanied children. Faced with the State Department’s February 2025 termination of cooperative agreements that historically delivered these services, the court concluded there was a real risk that the Executive had not lawfully complied with §1522’s statutory duties. Against that backdrop, the court ordered reinstatement of agreements necessary to meet §1522 obligations for refugees who are already in the United States.

Judge Lee concurred in part and dissented in part, taking the opposite view on §1522’s character: he reads the statute (and its title, “Authorization for programs…”) as permissive, not mandatory, arguing the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification and repeated use of “authorized” signal discretion. The panel’s majority, however, treated at least some of §1522’s provisions as creating enforceable obligations when appropriations exist — an interpretive posture the stay reflects.

## How appropriations language and statutory “shall” interact
At the heart of the dispute is a familiar interpretive tension: statutory prescriptions framed as duties (often using “shall”) that are simultaneously qualified by “to the extent of available appropriations.” That qualification ordinarily means that funding constraints limit a statutory program — Congress must appropriate money before an agency may spend it. But it does not automatically render every “shall” a nullity.

Two points flow from the Ninth Circuit’s interim approach. First, when Congress has actually appropriated funds for the statutory program, a “to the extent of available appropriations” clause will not necessarily give the Executive free rein to zero out services unilaterally through an internal policy decision or an unrelated executive order. The opinion notes Congress’s FY2025 appropriation for migration and refugee assistance (and an $800 million rescission), suggesting the court was attentive to whether appropriated resources remained available to satisfy §1522 responsibilities. If Congress has provided funds specifically for the relevant programs in a fiscal year and those funds have not been lawfully rescinded or reprogrammed, an agency abandon­ment of statutory duties may be reviewable and enjoinable.

Second, the qualification matters for scope. The government’s ability to change program structure or prioritize spending may be broader when appropriations are limited or have been lawfully rescinded. But where appropriated funds remain and the statute lays out discrete responsibilities (especially for vulnerable populations, such as unaccompanied children), courts are more likely to treat certain statutory commands as operational constraints that the Executive must follow, and to require a reasoned explanation under the APA for any abrupt termination of services.

In short: “to the extent of available appropriations” is a real limit on enforcement only insofar as appropriations are in fact unavailable; where appropriations exist, the “shall” language can be operationally binding.

## APA risks in terminating cooperative agreements
The court’s order rests on an APA theory: the Termination of longstanding cooperative agreements that implement §1522 services may be arbitrary and capricious if the Executive fails to account for statutory obligations and to provide a reasoned plan for continuity of services for refugees already admitted. Several legal levers are relevant:

- Final agency action. The termination of a cooperative agreement that affects the legal rights and duties of recipients and beneficiaries can constitute final agency action subject to judicial review under the APA. Plaintiffs can and did press that theory here.

- Arbitrary-and-capricious review. When an agency changes course on an established policy or program that serves statutory mandates, it must supply a reasoned explanation showing that it considered the relevant statutory scheme and the reliance interests at stake. A blanket termination that ignores demonstrable statutory duties (e.g., legal responsibility for unaccompanied minors; cash and medical assistance programs required under §1522(e)) risks being vacated as arbitrary or procedurally deficient.

- Procedural obligations. Some cooperative agreements may be contract-like; others implicate grant or regulation regimes that require notice, opportunity to respond, or adherence to statutory procedures before termination. Abrupt termination that leaves beneficiaries without statutorily contemplated services raises procedural APA questions as well.

The Ninth Circuit’s direction to reinstate “necessary” cooperative agreements reflects concern that the government did not adequately account for these APA obligations before slashing funding or canceling agreements.

## The interagency wrinkle: HHS duties versus State’s cooperative agreements
Pacito also highlights a messy bureaucratic mismatch. Section 1522 vests the Director of ORR (within HHS) with responsibilities for many resettlement functions. Operationally, however, the State Department has historically managed cooperative agreements with resettlement agencies to handle reception-and-placement work on the ground. When State — perhaps acting under a different executive directive — terminates those agreements, it may jeopardize HHS’s ability to meet statutory obligations.

The Ninth Circuit’s order implicitly recognizes that an interagency split cannot be used to sidestep statutory duties. If HHS has a statutory obligation under §1522 and the only practical delivery mechanism is State-managed cooperative agreements, the Executive branch must ensure continuity — whether by keeping agreements in place, transferring funds and responsibility formally to HHS, or otherwise establishing a lawful alternative. Absent a clear restructuring accompanied by reasoned administrative action, courts are likely to insist on the status quo ante for services already in motion.

## Doctrinal implications and likely downstream effects
Pacito’s interim posture portends several doctrinal and practical consequences:

- Splitting admissions from services. Courts may be inclined to leave the Executive broad authority over who enters the country while policing statutory obligations owed to persons already admitted. That bifurcation reduces the practical bite of an admissions moratorium in protecting resident beneficiaries.

- Appropriations are not an escape hatch by default. Executive attempts to rely on internal policy pronouncements to nullify congressionally created programmatic duties — absent a clear congressional rescission or reprogramming of funds — face uphill APA review. Agencies must document not only policy justifications but also how appropriations realities shape their actions.

- Interagency coordination will matter more. Agencies that depend on another department’s contracting machinery to satisfy statutory duties will need clear delegation, funding transfers, or joint plans before one agency unilaterally cancels contracts. Failure to coordinate invites litigation and provisional relief.

- Litigation strategy. Plaintiffs challenging sudden program terminations will press (and courts will scrutinize) whether statutory duties remain operable given appropriations. Plaintiffs may obtain relief limited to individuals already admitted or covered by pending cooperative agreements even if courts defer to the Executive on future admissions.

- Congress’s leverage. If the Executive wishes to make the changes permanent, it can — and Congress can — do so more cleanly through appropriations and statutory amendment rather than through executive orders that fray statutory implementation and invite APA challenges.

## Bottom line for agencies and litigants
For agencies: when you tinker with programs that implement statutory duties, don’t treat appropriations language as a one-way ticket to instant suspension. If appropriations exist, you need a reasoned administrative record explaining how statutory obligations will be met (or lawfully ceased), and you must coordinate across agencies when program delivery mechanisms live in another department.

For litigants: Pacito illustrates a promising pathway. Even if courts defer to the Executive on border-entry policy under §1182(f), they will enforce statutory service obligations under §1522 where funds and longstanding reliance interests exist. Emergency relief to restore services for already-admitted refugees remains a live and effective remedy.

The Ninth Circuit’s forthcoming opinion on the merits will be the real test of the boundaries the stay sketches. But the stay itself already signals that appropriations phrasing and statutory “shall” obligations are not mere semantic theater: courts will parse them to determine whether the Executive has lawfully suspended programs or simply walked away from congressionally structured humanitarian duties.
