Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the practical and legal effect of vacating prior stays and issuing a new stay pending appeal?
Question:
The Ninth Circuit vacated its March 25 stay (as amended) and its July 18 administrative stay, then issued a new stay pending appeal that keeps most of the district court's injunctions stayed except for a directive to reinstate cooperative agreements for reception and placement services. How do administrative stays differ from stays pending appeal, and why would vacating prior stays make the government's July 17 emergency motion "moot"? Also, what authority does an appellate panel have to tailor a new stay to include affirmative obligations (like reinstating agreements) while generally staying the injunction?
Answer:
Short answer
- An “administrative stay” is a temporary, internally-managed pause the court issues to preserve the status quo while it considers a motion or schedules argument; a “stay pending appeal” is an adjudicated interlocutory stay grounded in the Nken factors and intended to remain in effect while an appeal is decided. The Ninth Circuit here vacated its earlier temporary/administrative stay(s) and then issued a new, reasoned stay under the Nken standard.  
- Vacating the earlier stay(s) meant there was no longer any live order for the government’s July 17 emergency motion to enforce/clarify/amend to operate against, so that motion was moot.  
- An appellate panel has broad equitable authority to craft a stay that is practical and tailored: it may stay some parts of a district-court order while leaving other parts operative (including affirmative obligations) where the Nken factors and the equities warrant that carving-out. In Pacito the panel concluded the government was likely to prevail on most issues but not on the APA claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, so it stayed the district court’s injunctions generally but retained the district court’s directive that cooperative agreements providing reception-and-placement services be reinstated.

Explanation and support from the opinion

1. Administrative stay v. stay pending appeal
- The opinion labels the July 18 order an “administrative stay” and then says the court “vacate[s] our administrative stay of July 18.” That language signals the distinction: the administrative stay was a stop-gap measure while the court prepared for full consideration. By contrast the court then says “this Order shall constitute a stay pending appeal and further order of this court,” and sets out the Nken standard. In short, the administrative stay is a temporary procedural device; the stay pending appeal is a substantive interim ruling under the stay/preservation-of-the-status-quo standard (Nken) that the panel explains and applies. (See Order ¶¶ 1–3 & “The standards for issuing a stay are well established…”.)

2. Why vacating the prior stays made the July 17 emergency motion moot
- The government’s July 17 motion asked the court to “enforce, clarify or amend the stay order” issued earlier. The panel expressly “vacate[d]” the earlier administrative and March 25 stays and then issued a new stay. Because the target of the July 17 motion (the earlier stay order) no longer exists, there is no longer a live controversy about enforcement or amendment of that earlier order — the relief sought by the emergency motion has been superseded by the court’s new, controlling stay. The opinion disposes of the emergency motion by saying it is “denied as moot.” (See Background ¶¶ re July 17 motion; Order ¶: “We hereby vacate our administrative stay … We vacate our prior stay … The government’s emergency motion of July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108) is denied as moot.”)

3. Authority to tailor a stay and to include affirmative obligations
- The panel sets out the governing stay test — the four Nken factors, with particular emphasis on likelihood of success and irreparable injury — and applies them to the record. A stay is an equitable, interlocutory remedy whose scope is shaped by those factors and by the public interest. The panel concluded the government is “likely to prevail” on the statutory authority question under §1182(f) and that several of the Nken factors favor the United States, but that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (the statutory provisions requiring reception-and-placement services). Because the panel reached different conclusions about different legal claims, it tailored relief accordingly: it stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions “in their entirety except as specified,” and then expressly directed the government to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.” (See Order ¶¶ setting out Nken test and the panel’s conclusions; specific directive quoted in the Order.)

- That tailoring follows from the panel’s equitable powers and the Nken framework: the court can preserve the status quo and protect interests that would be irreparably harmed absent limited, affirmative relief even while staying other parts of a district court order that the government is likely to prevail on. Here the need to prevent statutory noncompliance and harm to refugees admitted to the United States under § 1522 was the basis for leaving the affirmative reinstatement obligation intact while staying the rest of the injunction. The concurrence/dissent (Judge Lee) illustrates the opposite view (would have stayed everything), which underscores that tailoring is a discretionary, judgment-based exercise tied to the court’s assessment of the merits and equities. (See the panel’s discussion of §1522 and the concluding directive; see Lee, J., concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.)

Practical effect (how this plays out)
- Practically, vacating the earlier stays removes any reliance on those specific orders; the new stay is the operative interim order. Under the new stay: most of the district court’s injunctions are not being enforced while the appeal proceeds (so the government avoids immediate compliance on those points), but the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to deliver reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted (so the court preserves statutory protections the panel thinks plaintiffs are likely to win on under the APA claim regarding §1522). The court’s order therefore splits the difference in light of its assessment of the legal claims and the competing harms.

Bottom line
- Administrative stays are temporary procedural pauses; stays pending appeal are substantive interim rulings under the Nken factors. Vacatur of the earlier administrative/stay orders removes the legal basis for motions that sought relief tied to those orders, making such motions moot. And an appellate panel, exercising equitable discretion under the stay standard, may craft a tailored stay that keeps most of an injunction on ice while maintaining specific affirmative obligations where the equities and likelihood of the plaintiffs’ success on particular claims justify doing so — which is exactly what the Pacito panel did with respect to §1522.
