Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 mandate refugee services or merely authorize them, especially given the title "Authorization" and the use of "shall"?
Question:
The majority reads § 1522 as imposing mandatory duties (citing § 1522(a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)), while Judge Lee argues the statute is primarily permissive: the title says "Authorization," subsections (b)-(e) use "is authorized," and the "shall" language in § 1522(a) is just a condition that applies only if the government chooses to fund programs. How do courts interpret the interplay between a section title signaling authorization and operative text using "shall" with qualifiers like "to the extent of available appropriations," "if necessary," and "as needed"? Are there canons or precedents specific to reconciling these mixed signals within § 1522?
Answer:
Short answer
Courts do not treat a section title (“Authorization”) as dispositive. When text contains both permissive phrases (“is authorized”) and mandatory verbs (“shall”) qualified by provisos (e.g., “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary,” “as needed”), courts reconcile them by reading the statute as a whole: the operative “shall” language ordinarily creates duties the executive must follow, but those duties are often expressly conditional on appropriations or necessity. Pacito illustrates the split: the Ninth Circuit majority read §1522’s operative “shall” provisions as imposing enforceable duties (and ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements), while Judge Lee viewed the same provisions as authorizations conditioned on funding and thus permissive.

How courts actually reconcile the mixed signals (doctrinal roadmap, grounded in Pacito)
1) Titles are interpretive only.  
- The opinion records Judge Lee’s reliance on the section title “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” and on Black’s definition of “authorization” to argue permissive intent. But the court (and standard interpretation practice reflected in the opinion) treats titles as subordinate to the operative text — titles can inform meaning but do not override clear statutory commands. Pacito demonstrates that a court will look beyond the title to the statute’s operative clauses.

2) “Shall” vs. “is authorized” — look to context and structure.  
- Pacito highlights both formulations. The majority emphasized that §1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director, “to the extent of available appropriations,” “to . . . make available sufficient resources” for specified resettlement services (the panel quotes §1522(a)(1)(A) and then directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements). That shows the court read the mandatory “shall” language as imposing duties.  
- Judge Lee, by contrast, reads the statutory architecture as a whole: subsections (b)–(e) state the Director “is authorized” to run programs and §1522(a) contains “conditions and considerations” that apply “in providing assistance under this section,” so the “shall” language operates only if the executive chooses to fund programs.

3) Qualifiers matter — “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary,” “as needed.”  
- The opinion quotes those exact qualifiers (§1522(a): “to the extent of available appropriations”; §1522(d)(2)(B): “if necessary”; §1522(e)(7)(A): “as needed”). These phrases are read as limiting language: they indicate Congress conditioned performance on funding or factual necessity. The majority nonetheless concluded the statutory scheme (including reporting requirements in §1523 and the historical practice of cooperative agreements) supported an enforceable obligation when appropriations exist or when the situation requires (hence the district court’s injunction as to reinstatement of agreements). Judge Lee viewed those qualifiers as strong evidence that Congress authorized discretionary programs and did not compel continuous operation.

4) Enforcement depends on whether appropriations exist and the statutory command is clear.  
- Pacito expressly notes Congress appropriated funds for FY2025 (and the rescission) when discussing §1522. The majority relied on that factual backdrop to conclude the government had statutory obligations it could not simply abandon. Judge Lee’s position makes clear that absent appropriations (or if appropriations are cut), the “shall” obligations may be ineffectual because Congress has not funded them.

Practical rule of thumb from Pacito
- Read the whole statute. Titles and “is authorized” language point to permissive grants of authority; mandatory verbs (“shall”) directed to officials normally create duties; qualifying phrases (appropriations/necessity/as-needed) limit the scope and timing of those duties. A court will enforce the statutory duties where the operative text unambiguously imposes them and the appropriations or factual conditions are met (majority), but a judge may resist treating authorization language and express funding-conditions as transforms of “shall” into mere advisory guidance (Judge Lee). Pacito therefore exemplifies the borderline cases where statutory structure, contemporaneous appropriations, and congressional accountability provisions (like §1523 reporting) tilt a court toward recognizing enforceable obligations.

Bottom line
- There is no magic canon that overrides all others here. The title “Authorization” is helpful but not controlling; the presence of “shall” ordinarily signals a duty, but that duty is meaningfully qualified by language tying performance to appropriations or necessity. Pacito shows courts will enforce §1522 duties when the text, structure, and appropriations indicate Congress intended actionable obligations; but reasonable judges (like Judge Lee) can and do read the same mixed text as primarily permissive when the statute repeatedly frames programs as “authorized” and conditions performance on funding.
