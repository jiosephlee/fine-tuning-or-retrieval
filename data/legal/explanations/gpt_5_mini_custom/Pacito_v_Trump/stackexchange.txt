Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the practical and legal effect of vacating prior stays and issuing a new stay pending appeal?
Question:
The Ninth Circuit vacated its March 25 stay (as amended) and its July 18 administrative stay, then issued a new stay pending appeal that keeps most of the district court's injunctions stayed except for a directive to reinstate cooperative agreements for reception and placement services. How do administrative stays differ from stays pending appeal, and why would vacating prior stays make the government's July 17 emergency motion "moot"? Also, what authority does an appellate panel have to tailor a new stay to include affirmative obligations (like reinstating agreements) while generally staying the injunction?
Answer:
Short answer
- An “administrative stay” is a temporary, internally-managed pause the court issues to preserve the status quo while it considers a motion or schedules argument; a “stay pending appeal” is an adjudicated interlocutory stay grounded in the Nken factors and intended to remain in effect while an appeal is decided. The Ninth Circuit here vacated its earlier temporary/administrative stay(s) and then issued a new, reasoned stay under the Nken standard.  
- Vacating the earlier stay(s) meant there was no longer any live order for the government’s July 17 emergency motion to enforce/clarify/amend to operate against, so that motion was moot.  
- An appellate panel has broad equitable authority to craft a stay that is practical and tailored: it may stay some parts of a district-court order while leaving other parts operative (including affirmative obligations) where the Nken factors and the equities warrant that carving-out. In Pacito the panel concluded the government was likely to prevail on most issues but not on the APA claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, so it stayed the district court’s injunctions generally but retained the district court’s directive that cooperative agreements providing reception-and-placement services be reinstated.

Explanation and support from the opinion

1. Administrative stay v. stay pending appeal
- The opinion labels the July 18 order an “administrative stay” and then says the court “vacate[s] our administrative stay of July 18.” That language signals the distinction: the administrative stay was a stop-gap measure while the court prepared for full consideration. By contrast the court then says “this Order shall constitute a stay pending appeal and further order of this court,” and sets out the Nken standard. In short, the administrative stay is a temporary procedural device; the stay pending appeal is a substantive interim ruling under the stay/preservation-of-the-status-quo standard (Nken) that the panel explains and applies. (See Order ¶¶ 1–3 & “The standards for issuing a stay are well established…”.)

2. Why vacating the prior stays made the July 17 emergency motion moot
- The government’s July 17 motion asked the court to “enforce, clarify or amend the stay order” issued earlier. The panel expressly “vacate[d]” the earlier administrative and March 25 stays and then issued a new stay. Because the target of the July 17 motion (the earlier stay order) no longer exists, there is no longer a live controversy about enforcement or amendment of that earlier order — the relief sought by the emergency motion has been superseded by the court’s new, controlling stay. The opinion disposes of the emergency motion by saying it is “denied as moot.” (See Background ¶¶ re July 17 motion; Order ¶: “We hereby vacate our administrative stay … We vacate our prior stay … The government’s emergency motion of July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108) is denied as moot.”)

3. Authority to tailor a stay and to include affirmative obligations
- The panel sets out the governing stay test — the four Nken factors, with particular emphasis on likelihood of success and irreparable injury — and applies them to the record. A stay is an equitable, interlocutory remedy whose scope is shaped by those factors and by the public interest. The panel concluded the government is “likely to prevail” on the statutory authority question under §1182(f) and that several of the Nken factors favor the United States, but that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (the statutory provisions requiring reception-and-placement services). Because the panel reached different conclusions about different legal claims, it tailored relief accordingly: it stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions “in their entirety except as specified,” and then expressly directed the government to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522.” (See Order ¶¶ setting out Nken test and the panel’s conclusions; specific directive quoted in the Order.)

- That tailoring follows from the panel’s equitable powers and the Nken framework: the court can preserve the status quo and protect interests that would be irreparably harmed absent limited, affirmative relief even while staying other parts of a district court order that the government is likely to prevail on. Here the need to prevent statutory noncompliance and harm to refugees admitted to the United States under § 1522 was the basis for leaving the affirmative reinstatement obligation intact while staying the rest of the injunction. The concurrence/dissent (Judge Lee) illustrates the opposite view (would have stayed everything), which underscores that tailoring is a discretionary, judgment-based exercise tied to the court’s assessment of the merits and equities. (See the panel’s discussion of §1522 and the concluding directive; see Lee, J., concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.)

Practical effect (how this plays out)
- Practically, vacating the earlier stays removes any reliance on those specific orders; the new stay is the operative interim order. Under the new stay: most of the district court’s injunctions are not being enforced while the appeal proceeds (so the government avoids immediate compliance on those points), but the government must reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to deliver reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted (so the court preserves statutory protections the panel thinks plaintiffs are likely to win on under the APA claim regarding §1522). The court’s order therefore splits the difference in light of its assessment of the legal claims and the competing harms.

Bottom line
- Administrative stays are temporary procedural pauses; stays pending appeal are substantive interim rulings under the Nken factors. Vacatur of the earlier administrative/stay orders removes the legal basis for motions that sought relief tied to those orders, making such motions moot. And an appellate panel, exercising equitable discretion under the stay standard, may craft a tailored stay that keeps most of an injunction on ice while maintaining specific affirmative obligations where the equities and likelihood of the plaintiffs’ success on particular claims justify doing so — which is exactly what the Pacito panel did with respect to §1522.

### Can the President suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) despite the Refugee Act’s specific admissions framework?
Question:
The panel relies on Trump v. Hawaii to conclude the President is likely to prevail on the legality of suspending refugee admissions under § 1182(f), saying courts cannot engage in a searching inquiry into his justifications. But the Refugee Act (8 U.S.C. § 1157 et seq.) sets out a detailed process for determining refugee admissions and consultations with Congress. How do courts reconcile the broad § 1182(f) "suspend entry" power with this specific statutory scheme? Is there any argument that the Refugee Act implicitly cabins § 1182(f) when it comes to refugee admissions, or is Hawaii read to foreclose that?
Answer:
Short answer
Pacito treats §1182(f) as a very broad grant of authority to suspend entry and follows Trump v. Hawaii in refusing to engage in a searching inquiry into the President’s justifications for a suspension. But Pacito also makes clear that §1182(f) does not automatically nullify the Refugee Act’s programmatic obligations: the court ordered (and plans to explain in full) that statutory duties under 8 U.S.C. §1522 concerning reception-and-placement services must be respected for refugees already admitted. So Hawaii gives the President wide power to suspend future admissions, but it does not foreclose arguments—grounded in ordinary canons of statutory construction and in the Refugee Act’s text—that the Refugee Act’s specific framework limits what the Executive can do (or what it must continue to do) in other respects.

Why and how courts reconcile the two provisions
1) Two distinct questions. Courts separate (a) the validity of a Presidential suspension of entry under §1182(f) (Hawaii governs this) from (b) whether other statutes require or forbid particular actions that flow from the suspension (e.g., funding or services under the Refugee Act). Pacito follows this separation: it applies Hawaii to find the government likely to prevail on the suspension itself, but it also holds that the government is not likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ APA claim regarding failure to provide services mandated by §1522 and directs reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already in the United States.

2) Statutory-construction principles remain available. Courts still apply ordinary canons—specific statutes control general ones; implied repeal is disfavored; a later statute will not be read to repeal an earlier one absent a clear statement. Pacito’s treatment reflects this: the panel quotes Hawaii that §1182(f) “exudes deference” and permits entry restrictions “in addition to those elsewhere enumerated,” but the panel nevertheless enforces the Refugee Act’s textually grounded obligations ( §1522 ) where they appear to impose duties on agencies. In short, a broad §1182(f) suspension is read to coexist with, not automatically override, specific statutory programs unless there is a clear conflict.

3) The limits of Hawaii. Hawaii constrains judicial inquiry into the President’s motives and the persuasiveness of his finding under §1182(f): Pacito repeats Hawaii’s point that “the sole prerequisite” is the President’s finding that entry “would be detrimental” and that courts “cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” But that limitation concerns judicial review of the presidential finding under §1182(f) itself; it does not say federal courts cannot enforce other statutory requirements (e.g., funding, reporting, or programmatic duties) that Congress has enacted in the Refugee Act.

What this means for the “implicit-cabining” argument
- A viable argument that the Refugee Act cabins §1182(f) is to press that Congress, by creating a detailed admissions-and-resettlement regime (with statutory duties, appropriations language, reporting requirements, and programmatic structures), manifested an intent that the Executive cannot use §1182(f) to nullify those specific obligations. Pacito shows that courts will take that argument seriously where the Refugee Act’s text appears to impose mandatory duties (the panel reads §1522 as imposing duties “to the extent of available appropriations” and directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements).
- Hawaii does not foreclose that argument. Hawaii reduces judicial scrutiny of the President’s finding under §1182(f), but it does not hold that §1182(f) repeals or negates other statutory rights and duties across the INA or elsewhere. Pacito illustrates both points: deference on the suspension itself, but enforcement of statutory duties under the Refugee Act for admitted refugees.

A practical outline for litigation
- Focus on textual hooks in the Refugee Act that look mandatory (e.g., “shall,” specific duties tied to appropriations, reporting and oversight provisions). Pacito relied on §1522’s text when ordering relief for admitted refugees.
- Press the specific-over-general canon and the presumption against implied repeal: if §1182(f) is broad, Congress’s more specific provisions for refugee resettlement still govern unless the text of §1182(f) clearly and expressly displaces them.
- Accept Hawaii’s limitation on probing motives for a §1182(f) finding, but preserve challenges that rest on statutory commands that the Executive has failed to follow (as Pacito did under the APA in relation to §1522).

Bottom line
Hawaii gives the President broad, highly deferential authority to suspend entry under §1182(f), and Pacito follows Hawaii on that core point. But neither Hawaii nor Pacito says §1182(f) is a free pass to ignore other statutory obligations; the Refugee Act’s specific provisions can and (in Pacito’s view) do cabin certain effects of executive action—at least insofar as those provisions create enforceable duties for refugees already admitted or for the administration of resettlement programs.

### Are the State Department’s terminations of refugee cooperative agreements reviewable under the APA, and can a court order reinstatement?
Question:
Plaintiffs challenge the defunding and termination of cooperative agreements as violating the APA. The order suggests the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one APA claim tied to 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and directs reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide services to admitted refugees. My confusion: (1) Is the EO itself reviewable under the APA, or must plaintiffs target discrete "final agency action" like State's termination notices? (2) What APA theories fit here—contrary to law (if § 1522 mandates services), arbitrary and capricious for failing to consider reliance or statutory factors, or procedural defects? (3) Is reinstatement of terminated agreements an available APA remedy, or does that cross into nonreviewable contract/procurement discretion?
Answer:
Short answer
- Challenge the Department of State’s terminations (the funding/termination notices) — not the President’s Executive Order — when bringing APA claims. The panel treated the EO under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as presidential action entitled to extreme deference, and it held the government “is likely to prevail” on the EO challenge. Pacito v. Trump, Order at 6–7 (9th Cir. Sept. 12, 2025).
- The opinion identifies two APA theories that fit the record: (1) statutory illegality (agency action “contrary to law”) if § 1522 imposes a duty to provide reception-and-placement services, and (2) arbitrary-and-capricious review tied to the government’s failure to account for its ability to satisfy § 1522 obligations (the panel emphasized the government’s admitted uncertainty about securing a replacement provider). Pacito Order at 8–12.
- Reinstatement is an available remedy here: the Ninth Circuit expressly directed the State Department “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States,” and defined those services as “the usual and customary services” under the prior agreements. Pacito Order at 11–12. The concurrence/dissent disagrees, but the majority issued the reinstatement.

More detail and doctrinal map

(1) What to sue: the EO vs. discrete agency action
- The opinion treats Executive Order No. 14163 (the suspension of refugee admissions) as presidential action under § 1182(f) that receives the kind of deference discussed in Trump v. Hawaii; the panel says the government “is likely to prevail” on the EO challenge because “the President ‘find[]’” the relevant predicate and § 1182(f) “exudes deference.” Pacito Order at 6–7. That suggests an APA challenge aimed at the EO itself will face a steep uphill battle.
- By contrast, the panel squarely entertained APA claims tied to the Department of State’s operational steps — in particular, the February 2025 termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of USRAP funding. The court’s relief and its legal reasoning focused on those agency actions (the terminations and defunding) and on what § 1522 requires for admitted refugees. Pacito Order at 8–12.
- Practical implication: plead and press challenges to “final agency actions” like termination notices, funding suspensions, and any binding State Department determinations about cooperative agreements; the EO is better characterized as a background presidential policy that the court viewed as largely nonreviewable in this context.

(2) Which APA theories fit?
The panel’s reasoning points to two principal theories (and explains why each is plausible on the record):

A. Contrary to law (agency action unlawful if it violates § 1522)
- The court identified § 1522’s substantive provisions (e.g., § 1522(a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), (e)(7)(A)) and concluded that those provisions can impose duties to provide reception-and-placement services “to the extent of available appropriations.” Pacito Order at 8–10. The panel found plaintiffs likely to succeed on at least one APA challenge grounded in § 1522. That is a straightforward “contrary to law” theory: if § 1522 imposes mandatory obligations for admitted refugees, then terminating the cooperative agreements that implement those obligations is reviewable and can be set aside as unlawful agency action.
- Note: the opinion records a disagreement (Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent) that § 1522 is merely an “authorization” rather than a mandate. Pacito Order, Lee, J., concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part (explaining counter‑argument). The majority, however, treated § 1522 as at least plausibly imposing duties for admitted refugees.

B. Arbitrary and capricious (failure to consider statutory obligations and reliance)
- The court pointed to the government’s own representation that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” but its admission at argument that it was “unaware whether any progress” had been made toward a replacement provider. The panel relied on that uncertainty to justify preliminary relief and to conclude plaintiffs are likely to succeed on an APA claim tied to § 1522. Pacito Order at 10–11.
- That factual posture gives rise to an arbitrary-and-capricious claim: an agency must consider relevant statutory obligations and the practical consequences of terminating programs that implement those obligations. The opinion’s directive to reinstate the agreements follows from that concern.

C. Procedural APA claims
- The opinion does not dwell on notice‑and‑comment or other procedural APA defects; the court’s reasoning centers on statutory duty and the substance of the terminations. Procedural theories are not foreclosed by the opinion, but the majority’s ground for relief is statutory interpretation and substantive review.

(3) Can a court order reinstatement of cooperative agreements?
- Yes — at least on the preliminary-injunction record here. The Ninth Circuit expressly ordered reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States,” and specified that “such services shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” Pacito Order at 11–12.
- The court treated reinstatement as an appropriate interim remedy to ensure compliance with § 1522 while the appeal is decided. The majority’s command is narrowly framed: reinstatement of the prior cooperative agreements to the extent necessary to fulfill statutory reception-and-placement duties for admitted refugees.
- Counterpoint in the opinion: Judge Lee would not have ordered reinstatement, viewing § 1522 as permissive and worrying that courts are intruding into executive discretion over contracts and program operation. Pacito (Lee, J., concurring/dissenting). So remedial availability is contested on the panel, but the controlling order here did direct reinstatement.

Bottom line for litigation strategy and pleadings
- If you want APA relief in this factual context, focus on final agency actions (defunding and termination notices) and develop a § 1522-based claim that (a) § 1522 imposes enforceable obligations for admitted refugees and (b) the terminations/funding suspension are arbitrary and capricious because the agency failed to account for those duties and cannot show a viable plan to replace performance. Use the record evidence the panel relied on (the terminations, the government’s representations about RFPs, and the statutory text cited in Pacito) to make both the statutory-violation and A&C arguments.
- Be prepared to confront the significant counter-argument (expressed by Judge Lee and the panel’s treatment of the EO) that § 1522 can be read as authorizing discretionary programs “to the extent of available appropriations,” and that courts should be cautious about micromanaging contract/procurement choices. Pacito Order at 8–12 (and Lee, J., concurring/dissenting).

The Ninth Circuit’s full merits opinion will explain its reasoning more fully; for now the Order makes three points clear: the EO itself is highly deferential and likely to survive APA-style attack under § 1182(f); the State Department’s terminations and defunding are properly the focus of APA claims tied to § 1522; and a court may (and here did) order reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to carry out statutory reception‑and‑placement duties for refugees already admitted.

### Does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 mandate refugee services or merely authorize them, especially given the title "Authorization" and the use of "shall"?
Question:
The majority reads § 1522 as imposing mandatory duties (citing § 1522(a)(1)(A), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)), while Judge Lee argues the statute is primarily permissive: the title says "Authorization," subsections (b)-(e) use "is authorized," and the "shall" language in § 1522(a) is just a condition that applies only if the government chooses to fund programs. How do courts interpret the interplay between a section title signaling authorization and operative text using "shall" with qualifiers like "to the extent of available appropriations," "if necessary," and "as needed"? Are there canons or precedents specific to reconciling these mixed signals within § 1522?
Answer:
Short answer
Courts do not treat a section title (“Authorization”) as dispositive. When text contains both permissive phrases (“is authorized”) and mandatory verbs (“shall”) qualified by provisos (e.g., “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary,” “as needed”), courts reconcile them by reading the statute as a whole: the operative “shall” language ordinarily creates duties the executive must follow, but those duties are often expressly conditional on appropriations or necessity. Pacito illustrates the split: the Ninth Circuit majority read §1522’s operative “shall” provisions as imposing enforceable duties (and ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements), while Judge Lee viewed the same provisions as authorizations conditioned on funding and thus permissive.

How courts actually reconcile the mixed signals (doctrinal roadmap, grounded in Pacito)
1) Titles are interpretive only.  
- The opinion records Judge Lee’s reliance on the section title “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” and on Black’s definition of “authorization” to argue permissive intent. But the court (and standard interpretation practice reflected in the opinion) treats titles as subordinate to the operative text — titles can inform meaning but do not override clear statutory commands. Pacito demonstrates that a court will look beyond the title to the statute’s operative clauses.

2) “Shall” vs. “is authorized” — look to context and structure.  
- Pacito highlights both formulations. The majority emphasized that §1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director, “to the extent of available appropriations,” “to . . . make available sufficient resources” for specified resettlement services (the panel quotes §1522(a)(1)(A) and then directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements). That shows the court read the mandatory “shall” language as imposing duties.  
- Judge Lee, by contrast, reads the statutory architecture as a whole: subsections (b)–(e) state the Director “is authorized” to run programs and §1522(a) contains “conditions and considerations” that apply “in providing assistance under this section,” so the “shall” language operates only if the executive chooses to fund programs.

3) Qualifiers matter — “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary,” “as needed.”  
- The opinion quotes those exact qualifiers (§1522(a): “to the extent of available appropriations”; §1522(d)(2)(B): “if necessary”; §1522(e)(7)(A): “as needed”). These phrases are read as limiting language: they indicate Congress conditioned performance on funding or factual necessity. The majority nonetheless concluded the statutory scheme (including reporting requirements in §1523 and the historical practice of cooperative agreements) supported an enforceable obligation when appropriations exist or when the situation requires (hence the district court’s injunction as to reinstatement of agreements). Judge Lee viewed those qualifiers as strong evidence that Congress authorized discretionary programs and did not compel continuous operation.

4) Enforcement depends on whether appropriations exist and the statutory command is clear.  
- Pacito expressly notes Congress appropriated funds for FY2025 (and the rescission) when discussing §1522. The majority relied on that factual backdrop to conclude the government had statutory obligations it could not simply abandon. Judge Lee’s position makes clear that absent appropriations (or if appropriations are cut), the “shall” obligations may be ineffectual because Congress has not funded them.

Practical rule of thumb from Pacito
- Read the whole statute. Titles and “is authorized” language point to permissive grants of authority; mandatory verbs (“shall”) directed to officials normally create duties; qualifying phrases (appropriations/necessity/as-needed) limit the scope and timing of those duties. A court will enforce the statutory duties where the operative text unambiguously imposes them and the appropriations or factual conditions are met (majority), but a judge may resist treating authorization language and express funding-conditions as transforms of “shall” into mere advisory guidance (Judge Lee). Pacito therefore exemplifies the borderline cases where statutory structure, contemporaneous appropriations, and congressional accountability provisions (like §1523 reporting) tilt a court toward recognizing enforceable obligations.

Bottom line
- There is no magic canon that overrides all others here. The title “Authorization” is helpful but not controlling; the presence of “shall” ordinarily signals a duty, but that duty is meaningfully qualified by language tying performance to appropriations or necessity. Pacito shows courts will enforce §1522 duties when the text, structure, and appropriations indicate Congress intended actionable obligations; but reasonable judges (like Judge Lee) can and do read the same mixed text as primarily permissive when the statute repeatedly frames programs as “authorized” and conditions performance on funding.

### Can the Executive suspend or withhold refugee-related funds that Congress has appropriated, consistent with the Impoundment Control Act?
Question:
The order notes Congress appropriated several billion dollars for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025, with a partial rescission. Yet the State Department suspended all USRAP funding and terminated cooperative agreements, purportedly under an EO reevaluating foreign aid. Is this a lawful "programmatic delay" or does it raise an impoundment issue if the Executive withholds or delays funds for policy reasons? Do different rules apply to State Department foreign assistance funds versus HHS’s ORR funds, and how might those distinctions matter for APA or statutory claims?
Answer:
Short answer
- Pacito makes clear that a presidential suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is likely lawful and receives great deference from the courts. (The Ninth Circuit said the government is “likely to prevail” on that claim, citing Trump v. Hawaii.)
- But the panel also held that the government is not likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ APA/statutory challenge to the shutdown of the operational resettlement program: because Congress has authorized and Congress has appropriated funds for reception-and‑placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the court directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide those statutorily described services to refugees already admitted. In short: suspension of admissions ≠ automatic license to withhold or terminate statutorily required resettlement services. (The court ordered the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements needed to provide §1522 services.)

What the opinion says about the governing legal standards
- Presidential authority over entry. The panel relied on the Supreme Court’s approach in Trump v. Hawaii: § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and the sole statutory prerequisite is that the President “find[]” entry would be detrimental. The Ninth Circuit therefore concluded the government is likely to prevail on the claim challenging the suspension of admissions. (Pacito pp. quoting Hawaii.)
- Statutory duties for reception and placement. The opinion describes § 1522 as creating duties tied to “available appropriations” and identifies specific statutory text requiring the Director of ORR to make resources available “to the extent of available appropriations” for employment, language training, cash and medical assistance, and to assume responsibility for unaccompanied children when necessary. The court found the plaintiffs’ APA/statutory challenge to the defunding of those services likely to succeed and thus ordered reinstatement of the cooperative agreements that provide those services. (Pacito pp., discussion of §1522 and the court’s directive to reinstate agreements.)
- Internal disagreement on scope. Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasized that §1522 is titled an “Authorization for programs…” and repeatedly uses “is authorized,” arguing the provision is permissive rather than mandatory. The majority and the concurrence thus frame competing statutory readings—one treating §1522 as imposing enforceable obligations for services to admitted refugees (the majority), the other treating it as discretionary (Judge Lee).

How that bears on the Impoundment Control Act (ICA) and programmatic delays
- Pacito does not rule on the ICA. The panel’s interim order resolves the specific preliminary‑injunction issues presented: it upholds broad presidential deference over admissions but enjoins the government’s elimination of the operational resettlement infrastructure insofar as necessary to satisfy §1522 obligations. The opinion does not analyze or decide ICA principles or the specific statutory requirements for deferral/rescission under the ICA.
- Practically speaking (what Pacito shows you can argue): if the Executive withholds or terminates appropriated funding in a way that frustrates statutory duties (as the court viewed here with respect to §1522 services), courts are willing to enjoin that withholding under APA/statutory authority. By contrast, the panel treats the pure policy decision to stop admissions as a different animal—one where judicial inquiry is limited. That contrast is the key lesson: where appropriations are tied to a statutory duty to provide particular services, courts will scrutinize agency action that prevents those services irrespective of deference on admission policy.

State Department foreign‑assistance funds v. HHS/ORR funds — why the distinction mattered in Pacito
- HHS/ORR funds: §1522 assigns (or at least contemplates) responsibilities for reception and placement to ORR/the Director, and the Ninth Circuit concluded that the government cannot simply walk away from providing those services when (1) Congress has appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance and (2) the Executive terminated the cooperative agreements that have historically delivered the §1522 services. The court therefore ordered reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to carry out §1522 services to admitted refugees.
- State Department cooperative agreements and foreign‑assistance instruments: the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements was the mechanism that prevented delivery of the §1522 services. Pacito does not hold that all State Department foreign‑assistance funds are immune from programmatic suspensions; rather, it ties the State Department’s contractual decisions to HHS’s statutory duties. If a State Department funding decision defeats a separate statute’s mandatory duties, a court can—and here did—force restoration of the programmatic apparatus needed to fulfill those duties. If a foreign‑assistance appropriation funds purely discretionary foreign policy programs with no separate statutory duty to a domestic beneficiary, different (and generally more deferential) rules may apply—but Pacito does not resolve that broader question.

Implications for APA or statutory claims
- APA/§ 706(2)(A) (arbitrary and capricious) and statutory‑interpretation claims were the vehicle used here. The Ninth Circuit found plaintiffs likely to succeed on such a claim with respect to the resettlement services. That shows a successful path for plaintiffs who can show that an agency’s withholding or termination of funds frustrates clear statutory responsibilities or lacks a reasoned explanation.
- Where the statute uses mandatory language (or creates a discrete statutory duty tied to appropriations), courts will scrutinize executive action that disables performance of that duty even if the underlying policy (e.g., suspending admissions) is highly deferential.

Bottom line (for your exam or brief)
- Under Pacito: the President has broad, deferential authority to suspend refugee admissions under §1182(f), but that authority does not automatically permit the Executive to cut off the operational funding and contracts that Congress and statute have tied to delivery of reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The Ninth Circuit ordered reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to deliver §1522 services and indicated plaintiffs are likely to prevail on their APA/statutory challenge to the funding termination. Pacito does not resolve ICA questions; if you need to argue ICA issues you must note that the opinion did not address them and focus instead on the statutory‑duty and APA theories the court relied upon.

### Which agency’s programs fall under § 1522, and does that statute actually govern State Department reception and placement agreements?
Question:
The opinion cites § 1522 and ORR obligations (HHS), but the historical "Reception and Placement" program (R&P) is run by the State Department’s Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration under the Migration and Refugee Assistance appropriation. The panel then orders reinstatement of State’s cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522." Is § 1522 properly read to govern State’s R&P cooperative agreements, or does it primarily govern ORR’s domestic assistance programs? If State’s R&P is under separate authority, what is the legal hook for compelling State to reinstate those agreements based on § 1522?
Answer:
Short answer
- Textually §1522 is an ORR/HHS statute: it names the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement and sets out duties and programs administered through HHS (see §1522(a)(1)(A) and the provisions quoted in the opinion).  
- But §1522 also expressly contemplates that the required “reception and placement” services may be delivered “through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements” with nongovernmental agencies. The Ninth Circuit majority therefore treated State Department cooperative agreements as one of the lawful mechanisms by which the government satisfies §1522 and ordered reinstatement of those State agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522.”  
- The panel was not unanimous: Judge Lee (concurring/dissenting) argued §1522 is primarily authorizing (discretionary) rather than a command to spend, and so would not permit a court to force operation of programs the executive declines to run.

Why the court tied §1522 to State’s R&P agreements (the court’s reasoning, grounded in the opinion)
1. The statute names HHS/ORR as the office charged with providing reception and placement services (the opinion cites §1522(a)(1)(A) and related subsections describing ORR’s responsibilities and programs).  
2. Crucially, the statute also provides that those services “may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with ‘public or private nonprofit agencies’” (the opinion quotes §1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) and observes the historical use of cooperative agreements). The court relies on that clause as the statutory vehicle that permits the government to meet its §1522 obligations by contracting/cooperating with outside resettlement agencies.  
3. Because the State Department had terminated the cooperative agreements that historically delivered those reception-and-placement services, and the government acknowledged uncertainty about replacing them, the majority concluded reinstatement of the State agreements was necessary to ensure the statutory reception-and-placement services described in §1522 would be provided to refugees already admitted. The panel therefore directed the government to reinstate “such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in §1522.”

What this means for your question
- If you read only the statutory text cited in the opinion, §1522 establishes HHS/ORR’s programmatic responsibilities but authorizes delivery of services through cooperative agreements. The Ninth Circuit majority adopted a practical view: even if HHS/ORR is the statutory “owner” of the program, the State Department’s R&P cooperative agreements were part of the government’s existing delivery system for §1522 services, and the court ordered reinstatement of those agreements to vindicate the statutory scheme.  
- That outcome is not uncontroverted: the concurrence presses a statutory-interpretation argument that §1522 is permissive (an “authorization”) and thus does not impose a judicially enforceable duty to operate the programs or to keep particular cooperative agreements in place. The opinion records both positions.

Bottom line
Under Pacito v. Trump (as the majority framed it), §1522 is an HHS/ORR statute that contemplates and permits use of cooperative agreements to deliver the statutorily described reception-and-placement services — and the Ninth Circuit used that statutory provisioning clause as the legal hook to require reinstatement of State’s R&P cooperative agreements necessary to carry out §1522 services for refugees already admitted. The point is contested on the panel, however: one judge would read §1522 as merely authorizing (discretionary) and would not have compelled reinstatement.

### Do refugees abroad and resettlement organizations have standing and fall within the zone of interests to challenge a § 1182(f) suspension and funding terminations?
Question:
Plaintiffs include refugees admitted to the U.S., refugees conditionally approved but still abroad, and organizations with cooperative agreements. For those still outside the U.S., do they have standing to challenge entry suspensions after Trump v. Hawaii? For the organizational plaintiffs, does the APA’s zone-of-interests test allow them to invoke § 1522 or related provisions to challenge termination of their agreements, or are such claims too attenuated or contractual in nature?
Answer:
Short answer

- Refugees who have been conditionally approved for resettlement but remain abroad: yes — the Ninth Circuit treated them differently from the general class barred by § 1182(f) and preserved the district-court injunction as to those individuals. The panel’s stay orders show the court found their claims sufficiently concrete and redressable to avoid a stay even while recognizing the broad deference afforded the President under Trump v. Hawaii. That does not mean the refugees have won the merits; the panel said the government is likely to prevail generally under § 1182(f), but it expressly declined to stay relief for conditionally approved applicants. (Pacito order, March 25, 2025 stay order quoted in the panel order.)

- Organizational plaintiffs with terminated cooperative agreements: yes — the court treated § 1522 as supplying a legally cognizable claim under the APA and directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception-and-placement services required by § 1522 for refugees already admitted. The opinion grounds this on § 1522’s substantive provisions (which refer expressly to using “public or private nonprofit agencies” and enumerate duties for the Director) and on the concrete injury caused by termination of those agreements. A dissenting judge argued the statutory text is permissive, but the majority (in the interim order) concluded the government is not likely to prevail on the APA claim and ordered reinstatement. (Pacito order, discussion of § 1522 and district-court relief.)

Why — the legal doctrine and how the opinion applied it

1. Article III standing (injury, causation, redressability)
- Injury-in-fact and imminence: conditionally approved refugees abroad had applications adjudicated and were in the pipeline; the court’s refusal to stay the injunction as to that subgroup reflects a view that their injury was concrete and imminent enough to satisfy Article III. The motions panel explicitly “denied the motion for a stay… to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by [USCIS] before January 20, 2025.” That carveout implies the panel saw a different standing calculus for those with conditional approval than for the general class of non‑admitted aliens.
- Causation and redressability: the panel’s carveout and the district court’s injunction (kept in place for conditionally approved applicants) show the court concluded an injunction could alleviate the plaintiffs’ injury (i.e., allow their resettlement to proceed), so redressability was satisfied as to them.

2. Zone‑of‑interests under the APA (5 U.S.C. § 702 / test from Match–Zone cases)
- The APA’s zone‑of‑interests test asks whether the plaintiff’s interests are arguably within the interests Congress sought to protect in the statute invoked. The panel’s treatment of § 1522 supports a straightforward fit for the organizational plaintiffs: § 1522 expressly contemplates that reception-and-placement services “may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with ‘public or private nonprofit agencies’.” The organizations at issue were exactly such partners and suffered immediate contractual and programmatic harms when the State Department terminated the agreements.
- The panel concluded the government was “not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” described the statutory duties in § 1522 (and its subsections) and, based on uncertainty about replacement arrangements, directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That relief confirms the court viewed the organizational plaintiffs’ interests as falling within § 1522’s zone.

What the opinion does (and does not) resolve
- The panel repeatedly cites Trump v. Hawaii to stress that § 1182(f) vests broad presidential authority and says the government is “likely to prevail” on challenges to the suspension of refugee admissions under that provision. So for the broad class of aliens the President has suspended, the merits are uphill after Hawaii.
- But the panel preserved preliminary relief for a narrow subset — refugees conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025 — and left intact the district court’s reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted. Those rulings show the court is willing to recognize standing and statutory/APA claims where the plaintiffs have been admitted or have a more concrete entitlement (conditional approval + imminent entry) or where Congress’ statutory scheme explicitly contemplates third‑party nonprofit providers.
- Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores the counterargument: § 1522’s title and many subsections use “authorize,” and he reads the statute as permissive rather than mandatory. The interim order does not finally resolve that textual debate; it signals the majority will view § 1522 as imposing sufficient legal duty (at least for preliminary relief) to support the organizations’ APA claims.

Practical takeaways for briefing or litigation
- Emphasize imminence and concrete planning for any refugee plaintiff still abroad (conditional USCIS approval, tickets, port-of-entry arrangements) to strengthen injury/redressability.
- For organizational plaintiffs, emphasize the close textual fit: § 1522 contemplates cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies and enumerates programmatic duties (employment training, language, cash/medical assistance, care for unaccompanied children). That alignment supports both standing and the APA zone‑of‑interests.
- Be ready to address the Lee dissent’s statutory‑interpretation argument — the government will press that § 1522 is discretionary and that appropriations language limits duty. The panel’s interim order shows that, at least at the preliminary‑injunction stage, the majority found those counterarguments insufficient.

Bottom line
Pacito’s orders indicate (1) the Ninth Circuit treats conditionally approved refugees abroad as having a stronger Article III case than the general class of aliens barred under § 1182(f) (the panel preserved injunction relief for that subgroup), and (2) the court found organizational plaintiffs injured and within the APA’s zone‑of‑interests with respect to § 1522‑based claims, directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees. The panel also signaled that the government remains likely to prevail on the general § 1182(f) challenge under Trump v. Hawaii, and a vigorous textual dispute over § 1522’s mandatory vs. permissive character remains an important counterargument.

### What is the legal basis for the panel’s earlier carveout for conditionally approved refugees and the district court’s appointment of a magistrate to oversee compliance?
Question:
Earlier, the motions panel carved out from the stay individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before Jan. 20, 2025, and the district court then set an enforcement framework and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee it. On what authority can an appellate court tailor a stay with such a carveout, and what standard governs district court managerial orders (like appointing a magistrate or special master) to administer that carveout while the merits are on appeal? Does such supervision risk contravening the stay or exceed ancillary enforcement powers?
Answer:
Short answer
- The motions panel relied on its equitable authority under the familiar stay framework (Nken’s four-factor test) to tailor the stay and leave a narrow “carveout” in place for refugees conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025. See Pacito order (quoting Nken; March 25 order language quoted in the opinion).
- The district court’s appointment of a magistrate to implement and police that limited injunction is an ordinary exercise of a trial court’s managerial and equitable authority to enforce injunctions and to administer the relief it has ordered — provided the appointment is (1) necessary to implement the un-stayed relief, (2) limited in scope, and (3) not used to expand relief that the appellate court has stayed. The panel treated the district court’s enforcement order as the kind of interlocutory enforcement activity that can be administratively stayed, and it ultimately left the carveout intact while vacating broader relief. See Pacito (describing the July 14 enforcement order, the panel’s administrative stay, and its later vacatur of earlier stays while preserving the carveout).
- Supervision does not automatically contravene the stay so long as it is confined to administering the portion of the injunction the appellate court left in effect; it risks contravening the stay only if the district court’s managerial measures (or a magistrate’s actions) reach beyond the carveout and attempt to implement relief the panel has stayed.

Explanation and textual support from the opinion
1) Appellate authority to tailor a stay (the carveout)
- The panel framed its decision in the Nken stay framework: it explicitly applied the four Nken factors (likelihood of success; irreparable injury; balance of harms; public interest) and called the first two “the most critical.” Pacito at “The standards for issuing a stay are well established.” (citing Nken, 556 U.S. 418).
- Applying that framework, the panel concluded the government was likely to prevail on the merits of its challenge to the Executive Order’s suspension of admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (invoking the deference described in Trump v. Hawaii), but that there was at least one APA-based claim (grounded in § 1522 statutory duties to provide reception-and-placement services) on which the government was not likely to prevail. Pacito explains both holdings: likely success on the § 1182(f) claim, and the court’s view that the government is not likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ §1522/APA challenge.
- Because a stay is an equitable, discretionary interlocutory remedy, the panel exercised that discretion by staying the district court’s injunctions “in their entirety except as specified” and explicitly preserving the injunction’s effect for the subclass of “individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status … before January 20, 2025.” Pacito (quoting the March 25 order and subsequent clarifications). In short, the panel used Nken-guided equitable discretion to tailor the stay to the balance of interests it found.

2) District court managerial orders and the standard that governs them while the merits are on appeal
- The opinion shows the district court responded to the panel’s carveout by entering a July 14 enforcement framework and appointing a magistrate to oversee compliance with the limited injunction. The panel treated that managerial order as enforcement activity that could be administratively stayed pending resolution (it issued an administrative stay of the July 14 order and then revisited the matter).
- The controlling standard, as reflected in the opinion, is practical and functional: a district court may structure and supervise implementation of the relief that remains operative (here, reception-and-placement services for the limited class) — including by appointing a magistrate or other officer to administer that relief — so long as the supervision is directed at effectuating the un-stayed injunction, is reasonably necessary to that end, and does not expand or modify relief that the appellate court has stayed. The panel’s handling of the enforcement order (administrative stay; then vacatur of earlier stays but preservation of the carveout; and eventual direction that the government “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522”) illustrates that the district court’s managerial steps are proper insofar as they implement the surviving scope of relief.
- The opinion also ties the propriety of such supervision to the underlying merits questions it identified: because the court found the government unlikely to prevail on the §1522-based APA claim, it was appropriate to preserve and to order implementation of the statutorily rooted reception-and-placement obligations for those covered by the carveout. That statutory basis limits and justifies managerial oversight aimed at securing compliance with those duties.

3) When supervision risks contravening the stay or exceeding ancillary powers
- The opinion demonstrates the boundary: the panel repeatedly stayed the district court’s injunctions “in their entirety except as specified” and made clear it could and would issue administrative relief (the July 18 administrative stay) to prevent enforcement activity that exceeded the stay. That sequence shows that appellate courts will intervene if a district court’s enforcement apparatus attempts to reach relief that the appellate court has stayed.
- Put plainly: appointing a magistrate to implement the un-stayed carveout is consistent with both the district court’s enforcement/managerial powers and the panel’s carveout. But supervisory measures that attempt to implement or expand the injunction beyond the narrowly preserved subclass would conflict with the stay and invite appellate correction (as the panel’s administrative stay of the July 14 order illustrates).

Bottom line for practitioners
- Appellate courts may and do tailor stays under Nken, including leaving limited portions of a district-court injunction operative for discrete classes when equity and the merits-supporting findings justify that result. Pacito is an example: the panel stayed the injunction broadly but preserved relief for conditionally approved refugees pre‑Jan. 20, 2025.
- District courts retain the ordinary authority to structure and supervise compliance with whatever relief remains in force, including appointing magistrates or other officers to administer that relief, so long as those measures are necessary, limited to effectuating the un‑stayed relief, and do not attempt to vindicate relief the appellate court has suspended — otherwise the appellate court can and will step in. Pacito shows both sides of that authority: the district court’s managerial appointment and the panel’s willingness to stay or vacate enforcement measures to keep the stay’s scope intact.
