Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Mandatory or Merely Authorized? The Panel Split on Whether §1522 Imposes Enforceable Duties to Serve Admitted Refugees

## Why this matters
The Ninth Circuit’s emergency order in Pacito v. Trump puts a spotlight on a recurring, high-stakes tension: how far may the executive reach when it both restricts entry under broad immigration powers and simultaneously tries to zero out the programs that support people already admitted? On the one hand the panel gives the government wide deference to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. §1182(f). On the other hand, the court—in language that stopped short of a final merits opinion—has already concluded plaintiffs are likely to prevail on an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. §1522 and has required the government to reinstate cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services to refugees already in the United States. That split in reasoning — deference about who gets in, compulsion about how those admitted must be served — raises doctrinal and practical questions that could shape agency behavior, litigation strategies, and congressional responses going forward.

## What the panel did and why the order signals a statutory duty
The court issued a stay of the district court’s broader injunctions against implementation of President Trump’s refugee-suspension order, accepting the government’s contention that §1182(f) gives the President far-reaching authority over entry restrictions. But the panel also concluded—again preliminarily and without a final merits opinion—that the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims: that the government unlawfully failed to provide statutorily required reception-and-placement services for refugees under 8 U.S.C. §1522. On that basis the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.

The court’s tentative reading of §1522 rests on statutory text and context the panel highlighted: §1522 contains multiple provisions that use “shall” (including, notably, subsections dealing with unaccompanied minor refugees and with developing projects for cash and medical assistance) and also ties some duties to appropriations while Congress has in fact appropriated substantial funds for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025. The panel emphasized that §1522 authorizes use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies and that the government had terminated those cooperative agreements without being able to point to any alternative capable of delivering the statutorily mandated services. The court also pointed to statutory reporting and accountability requirements (e.g., §1523) as further evidence that Congress contemplated an ongoing program of services and oversight.

## Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent: a competing textualist baseline
Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and partial dissent frames a contrasting, textualist argument: §1522’s title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the repeated phrasing in subsections (b)–(e) that the Director “is authorized” to provide particular kinds of assistance, signal a permissive, not mandatory, statutory regime. From that perspective, the “shall” language in §1522(a) simply prescribes how the agency must act "in providing assistance under this section" — that is, it imposes conditions and standards if and when the executive chooses to fund or operate programs, rather than creating a judicially enforceable obligation to operate them at all. Lee further stresses the common limiting formula “to the extent of available appropriations,” reading that phrase as decisive: if Congress has not made funds available, the executive cannot be compelled to run the programs.

Judge Lee’s view is familiar to courts that treat statutory “authorization” language and appropriation-linked commands as signals of executive discretion. His position puts a premium on titles, the “is authorized” phrasing, and the conditionality created by appropriations language.

## What’s at stake under the APA
The practical and doctrinal hinge is whether §1522 imposes a non‑discretionary duty that a court may enforce under APA §706(1) — the provision that authorizes a court to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.” If §1522 imposes mandatory duties (the majority’s working assumption), then terminating cooperative agreements that implement those duties can be actionable agency action unlawfully withheld or otherwise arbitrary and capricious. If §1522 is merely permissive (the dissent’s reading), then Heckler‑style deference to agency enforcement and resource allocation discretion likely protects the government from judicial compulsion.

Two features make the majority’s preliminary view plausible. First, the statutory text contains unqualified “shall” provisions tied to specific programmatic duties (for example, taking legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children “if necessary” and developing projects for cash and medical assistance “as needed”). Second, Congress did appropriate (and then partially rescind) funding for migration and refugee assistance in the relevant fiscal year—so the “to the extent of available appropriations” caveat is not an empty shelter for the government here unless it can show appropriations are truly unavailable for these services. The panel explicitly relied on the government’s apparent uncertainty about replacement arrangements when ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements: courts are less likely to accept a unilateral executive shuttering of programs where statutory structure, appropriations, and oversight requirements point toward ongoing obligations.

## The practical consequences for agencies and litigants
If the Ninth Circuit’s tentative approach is sustained, agencies will face limits when they attempt to “zero‑out” programs by terminating cooperative agreements or grants without a clear statutory basis. Two practical consequences follow.

First, agencies that want to pause or wind down services tied to statutory programs will need to demonstrate (ideally in contemporaneous administrative records) either that their interpretation of the statute is reasonable (if the statute is ambiguous) or that appropriations truly do not cover the programs in question. Mere policy preference or executive instruction to cut funding will be a weak record if Congress has appropriated funds and the statute contains mandatory‑sounding directives.

Second, plaintiffs will be encouraged to bring APA claims that target terminations of implementing arrangements — not just claims about admissions policy — because a successful §706(1) claim can produce an order compelling the agency to reinstate programs or otherwise comply with statutory duties. That dynamic changes the stakes of immigration‑oriented litigation: even when a court gives the executive broad latitude to control entry under §1182(f), it may still police the domestic obligations that attend those who are already admitted.

## Broader doctrinal implications and likely paths forward
Pacito sharpens at least three doctrinal lines of inquiry that other courts will soon confront. One is the perennial “shall” vs. “is authorized” interpretive problem: when does “shall” create a mandatory, judicially enforceable duty and when is it a hortatory or conditional term embedded in a discretionary statutory regime? Courts look at the whole statutory scheme, related provisions (including reporting and appropriation language), and practical indicators of congressional intent; this order signals that the Ninth Circuit will not treat the title and the “authorized” phraseology as dispositive where the statute also contains specific “shall” prescriptions and where appropriations exist.

Second, Pacito returns the focus to the interface between national‑security/immigration deference and ordinary administrative law. Trump v. Hawaii gives the executive considerable leeway on who may enter the country, but it does not automatically immunize domestic programmatic decisions affecting those who are in the United States from APA review. That doctrinal split — wide executive power over entry, narrower room to avoid statutory duties for individuals already present — may produce more compartmentalized litigation strategies and, potentially, more limited remedies targeted at domestic implementations rather than entry policy.

Third, the order underscores the significance of appropriations and Congress’s fiscal choices. Agencies trying to rely on “to the extent of available appropriations” need to show the funds do not exist or are otherwise unavailable for the covered purposes. Where Congress appropriates funds and requires reporting, courts may be skeptical that the statutory language was meant to confer sink-or-swim discretion to executive officers.

The next steps to watch are obvious. The court promised a full opinion, which should elaborate the interpretive framework for §1522 and the analytical path from text to preliminary relief. If the Ninth Court ultimately affirms the view that §1522 imposes mandatory duties, that decision may produce a circuit split with courts inclined to read similar statutory regimes as permissive. That split could attract en banc review or certiorari, particularly given the national ramifications for refugee resettlement and the intersection with executive immigration authority.

## Bottom line
Pacito is a reminder that the courts will treat two questions differently: who the President may exclude, and what duties the government owes once it admits people into the United States. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order signals that, at least preliminarily, statutory provisions that look to create programmatic frameworks with “shall” language and appropriations behind them can be read as imposing enforceable duties under the APA — even when the executive concurrently invokes sweeping discretion over entry. Judge Lee’s forceful countertextualism will have traction in other settings, but for now the court’s order narrows the practical effect of the refugee suspension by protecting the resettlement infrastructure that serves refugees already admitted. The final merits opinion, and any further appeals, will determine whether that protective line becomes durable law.
