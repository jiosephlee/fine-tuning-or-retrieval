Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# When ‘Similar’ Must Stay Similar: The Federal Circuit Demands Consistency Across DuPont Factors

## Why this case matters

In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the Federal Circuit reasserts a simple—but too-often overlooked—principle in trademark opposition practice: once the Board defines the relevant universe of similar goods or services in its analysis of one DuPont factor, it cannot contract that universe without explaining why a different scope is justified for another factor. The court’s decision to affirm the Board’s finding that Apex’s banking services and CC Serve’s credit-card services are highly similar, while vacating and remanding the Board’s narrow treatment of third-party “Aspire” uses under DuPont factor six (and, as consequential, its treatment of factor one) drives home the point that consistency in defining the competitive landscape matters to the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion determination.

Why practitioners should care: many opposition and cancellation cases turn on how broadly the Board defines “similar goods/services.” That definition colors not only the second DuPont factor (similarity of goods/services) but also the sixth (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods) and the first (appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression). Apex underscores that you can’t have it both ways—find a broad overlap for confusion reasons and then selectively shrink the comparison pool to evade evidence of third-party use or consumer familiarity.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The dispute began with CC Serve’s long-standing registration for ASPIRE (credit card services) and Apex’s intent-to-use ASPIRE BANK applications for “banking and financing services.” The TTAB found the services highly similar—indeed, “legally identical, in part”—and gave that substantial weight under DuPont factor two. Apex appealed two particular strands of the Board’s analysis: (1) that the services are highly similar, and (2) that CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark was entitled to a normal scope of protection because third-party uses of “Aspire” were not sufficiently numerous to show weakness.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board on factor two (substantial evidence supports the finding of high similarity), but identified a logical error in the Board’s analysis of factor six. Having already concluded that the services align closely, the Board then limited its survey of third-party “Aspire” uses to marks specifically tied to “credit card services,” excluding dozens of other third-party uses of “Aspire” in the broader banking and financial-services space. The court held this narrowing was inconsistent with the Board’s own earlier finding: once the goods/services universe is defined broadly (or found to be highly similar), third-party evidence must be evaluated against that same scope. Because the sixth factor bears directly on the mark’s commercial strength—and commercial strength informs the perception and commercial impression of the mark under factor one—the court vacated both factors one and six and remanded for reconsideration.

Put another way: you cannot find significant overlap between services for purposes of confusion and then decline to consider third-party use that falls within that overlap. The Board’s selective narrowing skewed its assessment of how “weak” or crowded the field is for the ASPIRE-formative marks, and that error required reconsideration.

## Analytical perspective: consistency is a structural necessity in DuPont analysis

A central virtue of the DuPont framework is that it forces the factfinder to make and connect a series of factual findings that support a legal conclusion about likelihood of confusion. That method only works if those factual findings use coherent and consistent definitions. Apex reminds us that defining the relevant consumer market and the scope of competing goods or services is not an ad hoc exercise where the Board can expand or contract the relevant universe depending on whether a particular factor favors one party.

This ruling proceeds from a modest and defensible premise: similarity is similarity. If you determine two services are similar enough that consumers could confuse source, you cannot then treat third-party uses for the same broadly defined services as irrelevant for assessing mark strength. Doing so invites a distortive analysis: a mark might appear strong simply because the Board ignored competing uses that are plainly relevant to consumers who encounter those similar services.

The court’s approach also aligns with prior Federal Circuit precedent recognizing that third-party use need not be for identical goods—only similar ones—to be probative. Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation were cited for that proposition, and Apex applies that logic practically. The Board’s mistake was not a novel misreading of precedent; it was an inconsistent application of its own factual findings.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Apex will likely have ripple effects in several ways:

1. More rigorous linkage between factors. Boards and courts will need to be careful to carry forward the definitions and market scopes adopted at factor two when assessing factors that logically depend on that scope—particularly factor six (third-party use) and factor one (commercial impression). Expect more remands where the Board’s internal definitions are incoherent.

2. Increased importance of third-party evidence across the broader goods/services class. Parties seeking to show a mark’s relative weakness will have stronger footing to introduce third-party registrations, uses, and marketplace evidence that fall within a broadly defined field of financial services even if the owner’s registration recites a narrower subset (e.g., “credit card services”). Likewise, trademark owners who obtain favorable findings on similarity must be prepared to rebut third-party evidence across that same breadth.

3. Careful evidentiary framing in prosecutions and oppositions. Examining attorneys, opposers, and applicants will need to anticipate that an early definitional choice will have downstream consequences. The Trademark Office and TTAB may be prompted to state more explicitly how they are defining relevant goods and services at different stages of analysis to avoid reversible inconsistency.

4. Possible tactical shifts in identification drafting. Applicants will continue to draft narrow identifications to avoid conflict, but the Apex decision signals that a narrow recitation may not insulate a mark from third-party evidence that covers related services. Conversely, registrants hoping to assert broad protection should expect challengers to marshal third-party uses across the larger field.

## Practice pointers

For practitioners representing applicants, registrants, or challengers, Apex suggests some practical moves:

- Define and document your market early. When litigating or opposing, make an explicit record of how you define the relevant goods/services and the relevant consumer groups. If you argue for a broader overlap (to support confusion), recognize you will have to address any third-party uses within that breadth.

- Be consistent in advocacy. If you argue for high similarity to prove confusion, don’t then argue that the field is too narrow to admit third-party evidence. Conversely, if you argue the services are distinct, make that argument up front and explain why third-party uses across the broader industry are irrelevant.

- Collect broad third-party evidence preemptively. If you represent an owner of a senior mark, collect and analyze third-party uses not only in the exact recited services but in related services—and be ready to argue why those uses are (or are not) relevant. If you represent an applicant/opposee, build a wide net of third-party applications, registrations, and marketplace evidence showing proliferation of similar formative marks.

- Tie commercial strength to the defined marketplace. Because commercial strength is contextual, explicitly connect third-party use evidence to the relevant consumer’s likely experience—what do consumers of “banking and financing services” encounter? How often do they see “Aspire” marks in the broader field?

- Anticipate remand issues. If you win on one DuPont factor that depends on a definitional choice (e.g., services are highly similar), expect the Board to reexamine other factors consistent with that choice. Prepare for that domino effect.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is not a flashy doctrinal pivot. It is, instead, a correction of an internally inconsistent DuPont analysis that had the practical effect of insulating a senior mark from third-party evidence where that evidence plainly fell within a scope the Board had already found relevant. The Federal Circuit’s message is straightforward: once you choose your market lens, you must use it consistently across the relevant DuPont inquiries. For practitioners—whether prosecuting, defending, or opposing marks—the decision is a reminder that definitional discipline matters, and that the interdependence of DuPont factors can turn a single definitional choice into the linchpin of the entire likelihood-of-confusion analysis.



## Counting ‘Aspires’: Third-Party Use Evidence After Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin

Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve — vacating portions of a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board decision and remanding for further consideration — is a useful reminder that the DuPont likelihood-of-confusion analysis must be coherent across factors. The court didn’t upset the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar (credit card services versus banking and financing services), but it rejected the Board’s narrowing of the relevant universe of third‑party “Aspire”-formative marks when assessing the sixth DuPont factor (the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods). That holding brings into focus two recurring questions in Lanham Act practice: how many third‑party uses are enough to demonstrate marketplace crowding (and thus a comparatively weak mark), and how should tribunals authenticate and contextualize that evidence — particularly in the financial services space where services overlap and brands proliferate.

What the court said (and why it matters doctrinally)

Two strands of precedent frame the Federal Circuit’s ruling. On the one hand, cases like Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin made clear that a field saturated with similar marks can justify a narrower scope of protection for a challenger’s mark; courts can point to “a considerable number” of third‑party uses (Juice Generation cited 26), or “voluminous evidence” (Jack Wolfskin discussed fourteen notable examples among many) to show weakness by dilution of distinctiveness in the marketplace. On the other hand, older decisions hold that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand identical goods or services — only similar ones. The Board’s error was procedural and conceptual: having concluded (in its second‑factor analysis) that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services are highly similar (even partially legally identical), it then considered only third‑party marks used on credit card services and excluded marks used on other banking/finance services. The Federal Circuit sensibly rejected that narrowing. If the services are effectively in the same commercial orbit, one cannot arbitrarily constrict the field of third‑party evidence to a subcategory of services when assessing whether the mark is crowded.

This is more than a quibble about labels. The better (and required) practice is internal consistency: findings about the relationship between goods/services should inform — not be divorced from — the DuPont factors that follow. Otherwise a Board may overstate the strength of an earlier mark by selectively parsing the third‑party landscape.

Quantity versus quality: what it takes to show crowding

Apex’s submission included nine third‑party marks for credit card services and other submissions showing a further 42 Aspire‑formative marks for broader banking and finance services. The Board treated the nine as insufficiently numerous to demonstrate weakness and dismissed the rest as irrelevant because they were not limited to “credit card services.” The Federal Circuit’s remand invites the Board to reassess the third‑party evidence with the correct scope — including related banking/finance uses — but it leaves open how many and what kinds of third‑party uses will suffice.

From the post-Juice and Jack Wolfskin landscape, a few practical principles emerge:

- Pure count matters, but so does salience. Courts have been swayed by both large numbers of third‑party uses and by a set of examples that together show a pattern of widespread use. There is no fixed numeric threshold. Juice Generation’s 26 and Jack Wolfskin’s references to “voluminous” evidence are illustrative, not prescriptive. A handful of third‑party registrations or uses can be persuasive if they are prominent, national, long‑standing, and illustrative of a crowded marketplace; conversely, many obscure uses limited to a narrow geographic or niche market may matter less.

- Services must be meaningfully related. Third‑party uses tied to services that customers would reasonably associate with the same source as the opposed services carry more weight. That is precisely the Federal Circuit’s point here: because banking/finance and credit card services overlap in commerce (and in the Board’s own second‑factor ruling), marks used across those categories are relevant to whether the Aspire mark has been diluted by competitive use.

- Evidence of consumer sophistication and marketplace context can cut both ways. In some markets, numerous similar marks may show that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing brands (supporting a finding of non‑confusion); in others, proliferation may simply mean commoditization, raising the bar for distinctiveness. The financial sector — with many subbrands (cards, bank divisions, program names) and intermediated relationships (banks partnering with processors or program managers) — often presents the ambiguous middle ground where context and provenance matter a great deal.

Authentication and contextualization of third‑party evidence

A recurring practical pinprick in Board and court decisions is the quality of third‑party evidence. Registrations, screenshots, advertising copies, press releases, and domain records all have different evidentiary weight. The Apex remand underscores how a Board must not only collect a sufficiently broad set of marks when warranted by the similarity finding, but also must authenticate and contextualize them.

- Authentication: Third‑party use evidence should be supported by reliable source material — archival web captures, contemporaneous marketing material, printouts with URL/meta data and capture dates, or sworn declarations from witnesses with knowledge. The PTO and TTAB accept a variety of exhibit types, but the trier of fact must be able to evaluate whether an item reflects genuine commercial use and when it was used.

- Contextualization: Not all “uses” are created equal. The Board on remand should assess geographic scope (local vs. national), duration, intensity of use (heavy national advertising versus passive domain holding), and channels (same channels of trade as the opposed services?). For financial services, where white‑label, co‑brand, and programmatic offerings are common, it also matters whether the third party used the mark as the primary source indicator or as a program/product name under a larger corporate house mark. The difference affects how consumers might perceive source.

- Consumer perception evidence: If the factual record permits, showing whether consumers actually perceive Aspire‑formative marks as signaling a common source — via surveys, testimony, or market studies — can be dispositive, though such evidence is costly and often unnecessary where the aggregate documentary record is compelling.

Implications for financial services marks

The financial services sector is a crowded and brand‑rich environment. Banks, processors, card networks, fintech platforms, and white‑label partners often create program names that migrate across institutions. That marketplace reality both complicates and informs DuPont inquiries.

Practitioners should take away three practical points. First, when opposing or defending mark applications in finance, be prepared to cast a wide net of third‑party evidence if the services overlap: program or product names across banking and card services are probative. Second, anticipate battles about proof — don't rely on bare lists of registrations or isolated screenshots; substantiate the evidence and explain its commercial relevance. Third, tailor the narrative: a challenger seeking to show crowding should emphasize national/sustained uses and consumer exposure; an applicant seeking to criticize third‑party evidence should be ready to show narrow use, limited reach, or different channels of trade.

Downstream doctrinal effects

The Federal Circuit’s remand is narrowly tailored — it does not announce a new rule of law — but it reinforces an important structural principle: DuPont factors must be analyzed consistently. A board’s conclusion about the relatedness of services should not be used to narrow the scope of evidence considered under other factors unless there is a reasoned basis to do so. The decision therefore nudges tribunals toward more holistic DuPont narratives and will likely result in more careful, cross‑factor reasoning in future TTAB opinions.

Practically, we should expect litigants to press harder for inclusion of seemingly adjacent categories of services when marshaling third‑party evidence, and to litigate the evidentiary foundation for such inclusions. Expect also more emphasis on contextual evidence in the financial space: the presence of many “Aspire” uses alone may not be dispositive, but their number, prominence, and commercial reach — properly authenticated and contextualized — could tip the sixth DuPont scale toward crowding and a narrower scope of protection.

Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is a useful course correction: when the Board concludes services are closely related, it cannot then cherry‑pick a narrow slice of third‑party uses to defeat a showing of marketplace crowding. The decision preserves the Board’s finding of service similarity, but insists on coherent treatment of third‑party evidence across the DuPont analysis. For practitioners in the crowded financial sector, the ruling reinforces the importance of both casting an appropriately broad evidentiary net and of carefully documenting and contextualizing every third‑party use you rely upon.
