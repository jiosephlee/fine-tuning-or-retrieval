Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 1: # Foundations of Trademark Registration and Likelihood of Confusion

This chapter explains how the Lanham Act’s prohibition against confusingly similar marks (Section 2(d)) is applied at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) and reviewed by the Federal Circuit. It uses Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 WL ___ (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025), as the doctrinal touchstone for several recurring issues: how to define the goods or services, the proper use of third‑party evidence, the interdependence of the DuPont factors, and the standards of review on appeal. The goal is to give you a practical, principled method for analyzing likelihood of confusion disputes and for preserving issues for appeal.

## Section 2(d): Purpose and the Policy Against Consumer Confusion

Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), forbids registration of a mark when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” The statutory policy is straightforward: the federal registration system should not sanction marks that are likely to cause consumers to mistake the source of goods or services. Likelihood of confusion litigation or opposition is thus not a vehicle to vindicate abstract rights of stylistic preference; it is a consumer‑protection inquiry rooted in marketplace perception.

Two corollaries flow from that purpose. First, the inquiry is pragmatic and fact‑intensive: courts and the Board ask whether consumers are likely to be misled, not whether lawyers or academics find two marks elegant or ugly. Second, the analysis is source‑oriented. The operative question is whether the marks, as used on the specific goods or services at issue, are likely to make consumers believe that the products or services come from the same source (or are affiliated).

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. illustrates these principles. CC Serve opposed registration of Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks for banking and financing services on the ground they would cause confusion with CC Serve’s existing ASPIRE registration for credit card services. The Board and the Federal Circuit framed the dispute as whether consumers would likely mistake the two ASPIRE‑formative marks for the same source of financial services. The Court emphasized that this is a holistic, consumer‑facing inquiry informed by the DuPont factors discussed below.

## TTAB Practice: Ex parte Examination, Letters of Protest, Oppositions, and the Record on Appeal

The modern path to litigation at the TTAB typically begins at the PTO examining attorney. After an application is filed, an examiner reviews it for statutory compliance and conflicts with registered marks. Opponents may try to influence the examiner by filing a “letter of protest” or similar submissions, but such filings do not substitute for a formal opposition. If the examiner approves an application for publication, third parties then have the statutory window to oppose registration.

If a formal opposition is filed, the TTAB conducts an adversarial proceeding that resembles a bench trial: parties take discovery, submit evidence (testimony and exhibits), and present arguments. The Board’s decision rests entirely on the administrative record compiled during that proceeding; documents that were not part of the record cannot be considered on appeal. Thus, if a party wants a piece of evidence considered by the Board, that evidence must be introduced into the TTAB record at the opposition proceeding.

In Apex, CC Serve filed a letter of protest during prosecution and later brought a formal opposition after the mark published. The Board treated the materials properly introduced during the opposition as the evidentiary record. On appeal to the Federal Circuit, the Court reviewed the Board’s written decision and the trial record, applying the familiar administrative review standards discussed below.

Practical point: if you are advising a client at the application stage, consider both ex parte submissions (letters of protest) and the more extensive—but more powerful—opposition procedure. Make sure any facts you will later rely on are developed and entered into the TTAB record during the opposition.

## The DuPont Multi‑Factor Test: Origin, Content, Weighting, and Interdependence

Since Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973), courts and the TTAB evaluate likelihood of confusion under a flexible, multi‑factor test. The original DuPont opinion articulated thirteen factors; later cases have distilled and reordered them, but the factors remain the analytical toolbox:

- Similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression (DuPont factor 1).
- Similarity or relatedness of the goods or services and the established, likely channels of trade (factor 2).
- The conditions under which and buyers to whom sales are made (care exercised by consumers) (factor 3).
- The fame of the prior mark (factor 4).
- The number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods (factor 6).
- Evidence of actual confusion (factor 7).
- The length and exclusivity of concurrent use (factor 10).
- Applicant’s intent in adopting the mark (factor 11).
- The quality of the applicant’s goods or services (factor 12).
- Any other established fact probative of the effect of use (catch‑all).

The DuPont factors are not a checklist that must be mechanically applied; the Board need only consider those factors that are “relevant and of record.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Communications, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006). But two methodological constraints are important.

First, the DuPont factors are interdependent. Findings under one factor can and often should inform findings under another. For example, if the Board finds that the parties’ services are highly similar (factor 2), that should inform the scope of third‑party marks that are relevant under factor 6 and the analysis of commercial impression under factor 1. Apex Bank demonstrates this interdependence: the Board found the services “highly similar” or partially legally identical under factor 2, yet then—erroneously, in the Federal Circuit’s view—narrowed the universe of third‑party marks under factor 6 to only those covering “credit card services,” excluding marks used in other banking and financial contexts that the Board had already deemed similar. The Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s factor‑6 analysis and remanded because the Board’s inconsistent scopes undercut the DuPont interdependence principle.

Second, DuPont is flexible about weight. No single factor is dispositive in every case. Courts have frequently said that the marks’ similarity (factor 1) and the goods’ relatedness (factor 2) are often central; actual confusion (factor 7), when it exists, is highly probative. But the particular factual mix—fame of the mark, crowdedness of the field, and consumer sophistication—determines weight in the individual dispute.

A structured way to apply DuPont on a record:
1. Define the goods or services precisely as they appear in the registrations or applications. If definitions are ambiguous, develop the record (e.g., through dictionary evidence or the Trademark ID Manual).
2. Identify the relevant consumer group and channels of trade.
3. Analyze marks’ similarity (appearance, sound, meaning, commercial impression).
4. Assess relatedness of goods/services and overlap of trade channels.
5. Evaluate third‑party evidence and strength of the mark (conceptual and commercial strength).
6. Consider other factors (actual confusion, intent, consumer care) and weigh everything holistically to answer whether confusion is likely.

## Evidence Commonly Used Before the TTAB

The Board and courts rely on several common categories of evidence. Understanding the evidentiary value and limitations of each is critical.

- Third‑party registrations. Registrations show that the PTO has allowed other parties to register similar marks for particular goods/services. Registrations are persuasive evidence that goods or services can emanate from a single source under a single mark, and that the mark in question is not unique in the field. But registrations do not by themselves prove actual marketplace use or consumer recognition; they merely reflect registration outcomes. Apex’s record included multiple third‑party registrations using ASPIRE or Aspire‑formative marks. The Board counted such registrations but then—contrary to the Court’s view—narrowed consideration improperly when assessing weakness.

- Third‑party uses (web pages, catalogs, screenshots, business records). Actual use evidence (sales, advertising, websites) is stronger than registrations because it shows marketplace exposure and helps measure commercial strength. The Board may treat such evidence as demonstrative of how consumers experience marks.

- Evidence of actual confusion (witness testimony, declarations, misdirected communications). Actual confusion is highly probative but rare. The Board looks for contemporaneous, thoughtful evidence (dates and specifics), not speculative inferences.

- Dictionary definitions and industry trade publications. These can help define terms in identifications of goods and services, or show the ordinary meaning of words used in marks. In Apex, because the PTO’s ID Manual had removed an entry for “credit card services,” the Board relied on definitional materials to characterize CC Serve’s services comprehensively.

- Internet evidence (screenshots, press releases). These are admissible if properly authenticated, and they help show channels of trade, extent of advertising, and third‑party uses. Because the TTAB record is the basis for appeal, parties must authenticate and enter online evidence in the proceeding.

- The USPTO Trademark ID Manual and TESS. The ID Manual is a tool for identifying pre‑approved descriptions of goods and services; it sometimes defines coverage that an examiner will accept without amendment. But the ID Manual is not dispositive of whether services are identical or related for DuPont purposes; it is one piece of guidance, and the Board will interpret identifications in the context of the record.

Practical evidence strategy: favor actual use evidence to show market presence and consumer exposure, and calibrate the geographic and trade channel overlap. If you rely on registrations, include the registration certificates, dates, and relevant descriptions so the Board can evaluate their relevance.

## Conceptual Strength vs. Commercial Strength; Inherent Distinctiveness and Scope of Protection

A mark’s protectable scope depends on both conceptual strength (inherent distinctiveness) and commercial strength (market recognition and fame).

- Conceptual strength is categorical. Marks fall along the classic spectrum: generic (no protection), descriptive (protection only upon acquired distinctiveness), suggestive (inherently distinctive), arbitrary and fanciful (strongest inherent distinctiveness). A mark’s inherent distinctiveness affects how much protection it receives: arbitrary/fanciful marks enjoy the broadest scope.

- Commercial strength (market strength) measures marketplace renown: consumer recognition, market share, advertising expenditures, and length of exclusive use. Famous marks (e.g., Vuitton, Coke) enjoy broader protection even against dissimilar marks.

The sixth DuPont factor—“the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”—is the principal vehicle for analyzing weakness or crowdedness. If a field is crowded with similar marks, courts treat a particular mark as relatively weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection: consumers accustomed to many similar marks will be better able to distinguish them. Apex demonstrates that when the Board finds the parties’ goods/services to be highly similar, it must consider third‑party marks across the whole range of similar services, not an artificially narrowed class. In Apex the Board initially found the services highly similar (suggesting a broad class of related financial services), but then limited factor‑6 analysis to only registrations that explicitly stated “credit card services.” The Federal Circuit vacated that approach because the DuPont interdependence principle requires consistent scope.

Another important point concerns standard character marks (also called word marks) versus stylized word and design marks. A standard character mark registration claims rights in the word irrespective of font, style, or design. That confers a broader claim to the word element universally; design elements of a later applicant may be less probative to avoid confusion if the prior registrant has a strong standard character mark. In Apex, CC Serve owned a standard-character ASPIRE registration, which carries the presumption of exclusive use of the word ASPIRE in the registered services and gives the Board a starting presumption about the mark’s strength and scope (subject to rebuttal by third‑party evidence).

## Burdens of Proof, Presumptions, and Priority

In TTAB oppositions, the party asserting likelihood of confusion (the opposer) bears the burden of proving that confusion is likely by a preponderance of the evidence. A prior federal registration confers a bundle of presumptions in favor of the registrant: prima facie validity, ownership, and a right to exclude others within the registered goods/services. These presumptions shift the practical battleground: a challenger who attempts to register a mark must overcome the presumption of registrability afforded to the prior registrant’s mark.

Priority of use is also central in contested proceedings. A registrant’s filing and registration dates can establish constructive nationwide priority as of the date of filing or registration, and the registrant’s actual date of first use may establish earlier common‑law rights. In Apex, CC Serve’s registration had an effective priority date of October 17, 1996, a clear advantage to CC Serve on the priority question.

That said, a prior registration does not automatically doom an applicant. The Board still asks whether confusion is likely, applying the DuPont factors. The registrant’s presumptions are probative and powerful, but they do not replace the multi‑factor inquiry.

## Standards of Review on Appeal to the Federal Circuit

Two basic standards govern Federal Circuit review of TTAB decisions:

- Legal conclusions by the Board are reviewed de novo. The ultimate determination of likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion that rests on subsidiary factual findings; therefore, the Court reviews legal error without deference.

- Factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence means “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). The Federal Circuit will uphold Board factual findings if supported by substantial evidence in the record, even if the court might have reached a different conclusion.

In practice, the Court often asks whether the Board applied the correct legal standards (e.g., proper definition of the relevant product universe) and then examines whether the Board’s fact findings are supported by substantial evidence. Apex followed this pattern. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on DuPont factor 2 (relatedness of services) because substantial evidence supported it. But the Court held the Board made a legal error by applying an inconsistent scope when evaluating factor 6, i.e., using a narrow universe of third‑party marks despite having found the parties’ services to be highly similar. The Court therefore vacated the factor‑6 and factor‑1 analyses and remanded for reconsideration.

Jurisdiction over appeals from TTAB final decisions lies in the Federal Circuit under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B). The appellate record is the TTAB record; the Federal Circuit does not take new evidence.

Practical appellate point: when presenting to the TTAB, make clear, well‑documented factual findings with record citations. If you want the Federal Circuit to review a legal error, preserve the issue in the Board and frame it as a legal question.

## Practical Guidance for Applying the Doctrine (Checklists and Common Pitfalls)

1. Define the goods and services precisely. The starting point is the identifications in the registrations or application. If a term is ambiguous or absent from the ID Manual, introduce dictionary definitions, industry materials, or other explanatory evidence during the TTAB proceeding.

2. Identify the relevant consumer. The relevant public can vary with the services at issue. Business buyers who exercise high care may be less likely to be confused; mass‑market consumers may be more susceptible. But don’t let an artificial narrowing of the consumer class defeat consideration of otherwise relevant third‑party evidence. Apex instructs that the Board must keep the same scope when moving among DuPont factors.

3. Present third‑party evidence carefully. Prefer evidence of actual use (web pages, sales) over mere registrations. If relying on registrations, explain their scope and why they are probative for the relevant range of services. Be sure to authenticate internet evidence and to enter it into the record.

4. Distinguish conceptual and commercial strength. If a mark is descriptively weak but enjoys strong market recognition, the Board must account for both. Conversely, a suggestive mark with many third‑party uses may be commercially weak even if conceptually strong.

5. Address likely channels of trade and classes of consumers. Show overlapping channels (same websites, same bundling of services, partnerships) to support relatedness under factor 2.

6. Preserve arguments about DuPont interdependence. If you rely on a broad relatedness finding under factor 2, explain why the same breadth must apply when assessing third‑party weakness under factor 6 and commercial impression under factor 1. Apex shows the Court will insist on internal consistency.

7. Authentication and contemporaneousness matter. For actual confusion, contemporaneous misdirected payments, consumer statements, or internal communications are far more persuasive than after‑the‑fact assertions.

8. When defending a registration, exploit presumptions attached to the prior registration (priority, validity, exclusive rights) but prepare to rebut third‑party evidence and to show lack of overlap in channels or consumer class where appropriate.

## Concluding Lessons from Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Apex Bank exemplifies recurring TTAB and appellate themes:

- Likelihood of confusion is a legal determination grounded in factual DuPont findings; Federal Circuit review is de novo for legal errors and substantial‑evidence for facts.

- The Board must apply DuPont factors flexibly but consistently. If the Board finds goods/services highly similar under factor 2, it must not then narrow the third‑party mark universe under factor 6 to exclude categories of similar services it previously deemed relevant.

- Third‑party evidence is critical to assessing the relative strength of a mark, but the scope of that evidence must match the scope of the goods/services at issue.

- Standard character registrations (word marks) and early priority dates give the prior registrant powerful presumptions, yet those presumptions are part of the overall DuPont mosaic rather than an automatic victory.

For practical advocacy before the TTAB or on appeal, build a record that (1) defines the class of goods/services and the relevant consumers, (2) authenticates use and registration evidence, (3) ties third‑party evidence to the defined scope consistently across DuPont factors, and (4) anticipates the Federal Circuit’s two‑tier review of legal conclusions and factual findings. Apex Bank demonstrates the cost of inconsistency: it can force a remand even where some findings (here, the relatedness of services) are solid under substantial evidence review.
