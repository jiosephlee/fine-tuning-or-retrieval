Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 2: # Relatedness of Services in Financial Brands: The Second DuPont Factor

The second DuPont factor—whether the goods or services are related—often decides a trademark dispute before the other factors meaningfully bite. In financial-brand cases, this factor frequently tilts in favor of likelihood of confusion because the industry contains many overlapping activities (issuing credit, servicing accounts, branding and co-branding, program management, and full-service banking). Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., a 2025 Federal Circuit decision, provides a clear illustration of how identifications, dictionary evidence, and third‑party registrations are used to define the scope of services and to establish that financial services are “related,” and in some respects “legally identical.” This chapter explains the applicable rules and tests, how they were applied in Apex, and practical rules for practitioners drafting and litigating identifications in the financial-services context.

## The controlling principle: compare the identifications, not commerce

A basic and recurring principle in trademark oppositions and cancellations is that the comparison of goods or services is governed by the identifications in the registration and the application—not by the actual products or services in the marketplace. Courts and the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) evaluate relatedness based on what the parties have claimed they will offer (or have registered), not on how they currently operate in the real world. This rule flows from the premise that a registration confers rights to the mark for the goods or services as they are identified.

Apex underscores that principle. CC Serve’s registration identified “credit card services” and Apex’s applications identified “banking and financing services.” The Board and ultimately the Federal Circuit started from those identifications; they did not restrict the analysis to the particular contractual arrangements in which CC Serve participated (where CC Serve acted as a program manager and partnered with banks to issue co‑branded cards). The Federal Circuit affirmed that the Board appropriately compared the scope of the parties’ identifications rather than their present business models, and that substantial evidence supported the Board’s finding of high similarity between the services. The lesson: when examining relatedness, the parties are judged by the labels they chose for their filings unless the identifications themselves require construction.

## Construe identifications when ID Manual entries change: the role of plain meaning and dictionaries

When a USPTO Identifications Manual entry (now commonly called the ID Manual) changes or a short, ambiguous identification appears, the Board and courts must construe the identification to determine what the words encompass. Apex presented exactly that situation: the entry for “credit card services” had been deleted from the ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration issued, so the Board needed to define the term’s ordinary meaning.

The Board used dictionary definitions and other authoritative sources to determine the plain, ordinary meaning of “credit card services,” “banking,” and “finance.” It concluded that those terms overlap because dictionaries and commonly accepted definitions show that banking and finance often include extending credit or providing funds—activities that may be accomplished through credit cards. The Federal Circuit accepted that approach: where an identification is unclear or the ID Manual no longer provides direct guidance, the Board may turn to dictionaries and other extrinsic evidence to determine the ordinary meaning of the terms used in the parties’ identifications.

Practical point: applicants should avoid vague, umbrella terms when a precise scope is intended; conversely, opponents should be prepared to submit dictionary and industry evidence to establish ordinary meanings when an identification must be construed.

## “Legally identical, in part”: what that phrase means and why it matters

Apex and CC Serve’s conflict resulted in the Board finding the services to be “legally identical, in part.” That formulation reflects a common scenario: the identifications are not fully identical word-for-word, but some of the identified services overlap such that a portion of the services covered by one party is the same as a portion covered by the other. When an overlap exists, courts treat that overlapping portion as legally identical.

Why does partial legal identity matter? Because when services are legally identical (even only in part), there is often a presumption that relatedness has been demonstrated. The practical consequence is that the opponent need not produce additional marketplace evidence to show relatedness; the overlap in the written identifications suffices to support a finding that the services are related for likelihood-of-confusion purposes. Once the overlap is established, the analysis moves on to the other DuPont factors (similarity of marks, channels of trade, commercial impression, strength of the mark, etc.), but the second factor itself will usually weigh in favor of confusion.

In Apex the Board concluded—and the Federal Circuit found supported by substantial evidence—that because “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” can encompass issuing credit cards and related account‑management services, the parties’ services overlapped in at least some respects. That overlap was sufficient to warrant calling them “highly similar” and “legally identical, in part.”

## Relatedness in the financial sector: program managers, co‑brand issuers, and banks

Financial services present particular challenges in relatedness analysis because the functions in the industry are frequently split among different actors and the same consumer often encounters different actors in a single product experience. Several configurations commonly appear:

- Traditional bank issuer: a bank issues a credit card and holds the account.
- Program manager / servicer: a nonbank firm (a card servicer or processor) provides program management, processing, and servicing for a card program while a bank technically issues the card.
- Co‑branding arrangements: multiple brands appear on the card (bank brand, retail brand, network mark).
- Full-service banking: a bank offers checking, savings, loans, mortgages, and sometimes credit cards.

In Coach Services v. Triumph Learning, the Federal Circuit emphasized that identical or closely related descriptions of services are sufficient to show relatedness even when services are not identical in business practice. Apex follows that logic. The fact that CC Serve’s business model involved partnering with banks to issue ASPIRE‑branded cards did not immunize its registration from a claim of relatedness with Apex’s “banking and financing services.” The relevant inquiry is whether consumers would plausibly perceive the services as emanating from a common source, not who technically issues payment instruments in contractual terms.

This is particularly important in the era of outsourcing and co-branded programs: a bank customer encountering an ASPIRE card and an ASPIRE BANK online banking portal might well perceive a common commercial source, even if different legal entities perform issuance vs. servicing. Hence the Board’s and the Federal Circuit’s focus on the ordinary meaning of the service terms that consumers encounter and whether those ordinary meanings overlap.

## Third‑party registrations: admissible but limited evidence that services commonly emanate from a single source

Third‑party registrations and examples of use are a regular feature of second-DuPont-factor proof. Such evidence is relevant to show that in the marketplace it is common for a single source to offer both categories of services, or that certain services are offered under the same mark by multiple proprietors. In Apex the Board relied on third‑party registrations showing marks that covered both credit‑card services and banking/financing services to support relatedness.

Two cautions about third‑party registrations:

- Registrations are not conclusive proof of use: they show that the USPTO once allowed those identifications and that registrants claimed use (or intent to use) for those services; they do not in themselves prove marketplace use or consumer perception. Nevertheless, they tend to show that registration practice recognizes overlap between categories and that other parties have claimed both services under single marks.
- The number and nature of third‑party marks matter: a handful of registrations that combine two fields may be persuasive evidence that the services can emanate from one source; an extensive body of third‑party registrations and demonstrated marketplace use can support a finding that a mark is conceptually or commercially weak in that field and entitled to a narrower scope of protection.

Apex also highlights an important interplay with the sixth DuPont factor (the number and nature of similar marks in use). Because the Board found the services highly similar on the second factor, the Federal Circuit held that the Board must consider third‑party marks across the full scope of the services it has found similar—not artificially restrict the universe to only a narrow subcategory. If the services are legally similar across banking and credit card services, evidence of Aspire‑formative marks in broader banking and finance contexts is relevant to show whether the mark is weak or how consumers discriminate among marks.

## Consequences for the overall likelihood-of-confusion analysis

When the second factor favors the senior user because the services are identical or highly similar, other DuPont factors often follow naturally. A legally identical or highly related service means:

- Channels of trade and classes of purchasers are more likely to overlap; the Board can presume that the channels and purchasers identified in the application and registration coincide unless the identifications include limiting language.
- Consumers may reasonably believe a common source exists for differently labeled services (e.g., a card and a bank account bearing the same dominant term).
- The senior user need not produce abundant marketplace pairing evidence; overlapping identifications and supporting third‑party registrations can be enough.

However, the strength of the senior user’s mark, third‑party use of similar marks, and differences in commercial impression still play crucial roles. A finding that services are related does not compel a finding of likelihood of confusion if other DuPont factors significantly favor the junior user (for example, if the senior mark is shown weak through extensive third‑party use, or the marks are dissimilar in appearance and sound).

Apex demonstrates this balanced approach: the Court affirmed the Board’s finding of high similarity for the second DuPont factor, but it vacated and remanded analysis of the first and sixth factors so the Board could re-evaluate the number and nature of third‑party Aspire marks across the full scope of services it had found similar, and then reconsider the marks’ overall commercial impressions.

## Practical drafting and prosecution strategies

How parties draft identifications and handle prosecution can materially affect a later confusion analysis. The financial‑services context offers several tactical choices:

- Tailor identifications precisely. If you are an applicant seeking a registration for a single activity (e.g., “account servicing for merchant credit card programs”), avoid broad umbrella terms like “banking services” or “financial services” unless you intend to claim them. Narrower identifications limit overlap and reduce exposure to oppositions.
- Use limiting language. If your services are restricted to particular customers, channels, or activities, state those limits explicitly (e.g., “credit card account servicing for supermarket co‑branded credit card programs”). The TTAB will usually respect clear limitation language when comparing identifications.
- Consider disclaimers. If a word in a mark is descriptive and conflicts with a senior user’s formative term, a disclaimer may help obtain a registration, though disclaimers do not necessarily avoid confusion claims.
- Try to secure consent/coexistence agreements. A consent from a prior user (with clear geographic or channel limitations) can be persuasive to the PTO and the Board and may be recorded with the USPTO to prevent later arguments about the proper scope of coexistence.
- Be strategic about amendments. Amending an identification to narrow scope during prosecution can reduce conflict with existing registrations. But be mindful: amendments may not cure prior oppositions if the opposer persuades a tribunal that confusion still remains.
- Preserve and submit industry evidence when needed. If the identifications require construction, be ready to present reliable dictionary definitions, industry standards, trade publications, or regulatory definitions to establish ordinary meaning.
- When litigating, remember the presumption rule: courts and the Board compare identifications as written; if those identifications are broad and overlap, a party claiming distinctness bears the burden of showing that the ordinary consumer would not perceive a relationship between them.

## Applying these rules to Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications for ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “banking and financing services.” CC Serve relied on a 1998 registration for ASPIRE for “credit card services.” The Board concluded that the services were highly similar and in part legally identical because common definitions of “banking” and “finance” include extending credit via credit cards. The Board supplemented the definitional evidence with third‑party registrations covering both credit card and banking/finance services. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s conclusion under the second DuPont factor, finding substantial evidence supported the Board’s construction of the identifications and its determination that the services were highly similar.

At the same time, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s analysis of the sixth DuPont factor because, although the Board found the services highly similar, it limited its search for third‑party Aspire‑formative marks to the narrower credit‑card‑services category rather than to the broader field of banking and finance. The Circuit directed the Board on remand to consider third‑party marks across the full scope of services it had already found similar, and to reassess the marks’ overall commercial impression (first DuPont factor) in light of any new findings about the mark’s strength or weakness.

The takeaways from Apex are concrete: (1) identifications control; (2) when ID Manual guidance is no longer available, dictionaries and ordinary meaning can be used to construe service terms; (3) partial legal identity is sufficient to weigh the second DuPont factor strongly in favor of relatedness; and (4) once services are found related, third‑party registration evidence must be considered in the correct scope when evaluating mark strength and commercial impression.

## Checklist for students and practitioners

- Begin any perception of relatedness analysis with the parties’ identifications. Do not recast the analysis around actual business models unless the identifications themselves are ambiguous.
- If the ID Manual does not resolve the meaning of an identification, be prepared to introduce dictionary, industry, or regulatory materials to establish the ordinary meaning.
- If one identification encompasses an activity included in the other identification (even if only partially), call the overlap “legally identical, in part”; treat the second DuPont factor as strongly favoring relatedness absent persuasive contrary evidence.
- Use third‑party registrations and marketplace examples to show that services commonly emanate from the same source, but note their evidentiary limits (registrations ≠ confirmed use).
- When the second factor favors relatedness, ensure the sixth and first factors examine third‑party evidence and commercial impression at the same scope—otherwise the Board’s analysis may be reversed or remanded (as in Apex).
- Draft identifications narrowly and with limiting language where possible to avoid unnecessary overlap; consider consent agreements or coexistence arrangements when overlap is unavoidable.

Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful, contemporary statement of how courts apply the second DuPont factor in the financial‑services context. It reinforces the control of written identifications, permits the use of dictionary and industry evidence to construe ambiguous terms, recognizes that partial legal identity is sufficient to establish relatedness, and requires consistent scope when considering third‑party evidence and commercial impression. These rules matter especially in finance, where the same consumer experience often spans issuance, branding, servicing, and banking.
