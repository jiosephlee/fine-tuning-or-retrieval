Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 4: # Comparing the Marks: Commercial Impression and the First DuPont Factor

This chapter explains how tribunals compare marks “in their entireties” for appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression—the first DuPont factor—and how that comparison interacts with the mark’s strength under the sixth DuPont factor. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is the contemporary vehicle: the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are highly similar, but vacated and remanded the Board’s analyses of the sixth and first DuPont factors because the Board improperly limited the universe of third‑party marks it considered. The lesson is threefold. First, mark comparison is holistic and marketplace‑focused, not an academic side‑by‑side parsing of words. Second, the commercial strength or weakness of a mark—shown through third‑party use and registration evidence—alters how similar two marks must be before confusion is likely. Third, error in assessing the field of third‑party use often requires rethinking the marks’ commercial impressions on remand.

## The totality standard: sight, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression

The first DuPont factor requires courts and the TTAB to compare the marks “in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. That comparison is not a mechanical checklist or a paper comparison of strings of letters. Instead, tribunals must ask whether, taking the marks as consumers encounter them in the marketplace, the overall commercial impressions are likely to lead purchasers to believe the goods or services come from the same source.

Three practical guideposts follow:

- Focus on consumer perception. The inquiry asks what message a mark conveys to the relevant public, given the nature of the goods or services and the circumstances of their marketing. The test is whether confusion as to source is likely, not whether the marks could be distinguished on close inspection by an expert.

- Consider all dimensions together. Sight, sound, and meaning are evaluated in combination. Two marks that differ visually may still sound alike and have the same commercial implication, producing a confusing overall impression.

- Resist over‑literal parsing. Minor differences—added or disclaimed terms, punctuation, or small design elements—often do not prevent confusion when the dominant commercial impression remains the same.

I.AM.Symbolic, LLC v. Fitbug, Inc. (In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC) reiterates that this factor is about the overall commercial impression. Even if differences can be articulated, the question is whether a reasonable consumer would be confused in the marketplace.

When applying this totality standard in new fact patterns, identify the mark as it will appear to consumers (standard character, word+design, stylization), imagine typical encounters with the mark (ads, product packaging, signage), and then ask whether those encounters would likely produce source confusion.

## Shared dominant terms and added matter: effect of descriptive additions

A common issue is marks that share a prominent term but differ by an added descriptive or generic word—e.g., ASPIRE v. ASPIRE BANK. The courts recognize that the presence of an added generic or descriptive term reduces but does not eliminate the risk of confusion. The deletion or addition of such matter will be weighed, but the dominant term often controls the commercial impression.

Key principles:

- The dominant element doctrine. When a multi‑word mark includes a distinctive word plus a descriptive word, the distinctive word will often dominate the commercial impression. For example, consumers are likely to focus on “Aspire” rather than the generic descriptor “Bank.”

- Descriptive additions are not dispositive. Even if a registrant disclaims a descriptive element or the applicant adds a descriptive term, that does not automatically prevent a finding of likelihood of confusion. Disclaimers affect the registerability and exclusivity of the disclaimed term but are not a shield against confusion findings.

- Context matters. If the descriptive term alters the product category or clarifies a different source (for example, by signaling a subsidiary line or a geographically restricted operation), it can reduce confusion. But where goods/services overlap substantially—as the Board found in Apex—the added matter’s ability to distinguish is limited.

Thus, in hypothetical disputes between marks like ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK, courts will ask whether adding “BANK” meaningfully changes what consumers perceive about the source. If consumers encountering both marks in the same marketplace would still think a single source might be behind both, the added word will not save the junior mark.

## Standard character marks versus composite word‑and‑design marks; dominance of words

Marks may be claimed as standard character marks (pure words, no claimed design) or as composite word‑and‑design marks. The functional difference matters for the first DuPont factor because a standard character registration protects the word in any stylization, and a word element typically dominates the commercial impression of a composite mark.

Practical consequences:

- Words generally control. Where a composite mark consists of a prominent word plus minor design elements, the word will usually be the dominant source identifier. Courts will compare the word elements even when one party has a design mark.

- Standard character registrations are broad. A standard character mark gives the registrant rights in the word across fonts and stylizations, which tends to strengthen the word element’s claim to being the core commercial impression.

- Design elements can matter when they are distinctive and prominent. If a logo’s design is large, unique, and prominent, it may materially change the commercial impression despite identical words.

In Apex, CC Serve’s ASPIRE is a standard character registration, while Apex sought ASPIRE BANK in word and design formats. That factual posture emphasizes the weight of the shared word component: a standard character ASPIRE registration arguably gives CC Serve a strong claim to the word itself across contexts, which is why the Board’s and court’s attention to consumer impression is pivotal.

## How mark strength calibrates similarity analysis

Mark strength is a central modifier in likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis. Strength operates both conceptually (is the mark inherently distinctive or merely descriptive?) and commercially (is the mark widely used by others in the field?). The sixth DuPont factor—number and nature of similar marks in use—tests the field’s crowdedness and thereby the mark’s relative strength.

Two effects of strength are particularly important:

- Strong marks receive broader protection. An inherently distinctive mark that is not diluted by third‑party use is entitled to a wider radius of protection; even modest differences between marks may be insufficient to avoid confusion.

- Weak marks get narrower protection. Where a mark is conceptually weak (descriptive or suggestive) or is commercially weak because many competitors use similar marks, courts permit closer coexistence. Evidence of multiple third‑party uses of the same or similar term in related goods/services implies that consumers have been educated to distinguish among those marks, reducing the likelihood that a single similarity will lead to confusion.

The sixth factor is therefore not a sidebar to the first factor but an input that changes how the first factor should be weighed. A crowded field around “Aspire” in banking and finance makes it more plausible that ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK can coexist; a sparsely used, distinctive ASPIRE strengthens CC Serve’s claim that ASPIRE BANK will cause confusion.

## I.AM.Symbolic and the role of commercial impression in the first factor

In I.AM.Symbolic the Federal Circuit emphasized that the first DuPont factor is about commercial impression. The opinion reminds practitioners that the comparison should be realistic and consumer‑oriented, and that the overall impression may be informed by other evidence (e.g., how the marks are actually used, advertising, and channels of trade). The case is useful doctrine for two points: first, it reiterates the holistic focus described above; second, it shows that a later re‑evaluation of the mark’s strength or marketplace context can and should lead to a new first‑factor analysis.

I.AM.Symbolic supports the appellate approach in Apex: because the Board’s flawed treatment of third‑party use affected the perceived strength of ASPIRE, the Board could not reliably conclude what the commercial impression of ASPIRE v. ASPIRE BANK would be in the marketplace. Where strength shifts, so may the overall commercial impression and, with it, the first factor.

## Application to Apex: why the Federal Circuit vacated the first‑factor analysis and plausible outcomes on remand

Apex supplies a concrete example of the interdependence between the sixth and first factors. The Board found the parties’ services highly similar—indeed partially legally identical—and then, in its sixth‑factor analysis, limited third‑party Aspire marks to those used strictly for “credit card services.” Because the Board had already found similarity across banking and credit card services, that narrowing discounted relevant third‑party uses in banking and finance. The Federal Circuit held that narrowing was error: if services are highly similar across banking and credit card offerings, the universe of third‑party marks to be considered should include those used in both fields. Because the sixth factor speaks directly to commercial strength, and because commercial strength influences the overall commercial impression (the first factor), the court vacated and remanded both factors together.

On remand the Board must consider all third‑party evidence of ASPIRE/Aspire‑formative marks across relevant banking and financial services, reassess whether ASPIRE is relatively weak or strong, and then reexamine whether ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK convey sufficiently distinct commercial impressions. Two outcomes are plausible:

- If the Board finds ASPIRE to be weak (many third‑party uses of Aspire in banking and closely related financial services), it will likely construe CC Serve’s protection narrowly. Under that narrower scope, the addition of the descriptive term “BANK” could be enough—especially in combination with any stylization—to reduce the likelihood of confusion. The first factor would weigh less strongly for CC Serve because consumers are accustomed to different Aspire marks within the same field.

- If the Board finds ASPIRE to be relatively strong (few or no relevant third‑party Aspire marks in banking/finance), CC Serve’s ASPIRE will have broader protection. In that event, the shared dominant term “ASPIRE” will likely carry more weight, and ASPIRE BANK may be viewed as producing a similar commercial impression likely to cause source confusion, particularly given the parties’ highly similar services.

Either way, the Board’s next round of analysis must be consistent: the scope used to define similar services (the second factor) should be mirrored in the scope of third‑party marks considered for the sixth factor, and the revised strength assessment must inform the first‑factor comparison.

Conclusion

The Apex decision reinforces two enduring lessons. First, the first DuPont factor is a totality inquiry about how consumers perceive marks in context; courts should not permit narrow semantic parsing or overreliance on disclaimers to defeat that inquiry. Second, a mark’s strength—shaped by third‑party use in the relevant field—modulates how much difference between marks is required to avoid confusion. Practitioners evaluating or litigating mark similarity should therefore (1) define carefully the relevant marketplace and goods/services, (2) assemble comprehensive third‑party evidence across that defined scope, and (3) conduct the first‑factor comparison with the assessed strength in mind. Apex is a remedial lesson: an erroneous narrowing of the field of comparison can vitiate the commercial‑impression analysis and require reconsideration of whether the marks, in their entireties, are likely to confuse.
