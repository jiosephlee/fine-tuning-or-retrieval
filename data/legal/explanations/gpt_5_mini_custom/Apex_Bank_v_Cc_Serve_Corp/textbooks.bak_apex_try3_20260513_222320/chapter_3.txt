Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 3: # Crowded Fields and Mark Strength: The Sixth DuPont Factor

One of the most consequential yet often misunderstood DuPont factors is the sixth: the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. That factor is the primary vehicle by which an accused infringer or defendant can demonstrate that a plaintiff’s mark is relatively weak and therefore entitled to a narrower scope of protection. This chapter explains the legal role of the sixth factor, how courts evaluate third‑party evidence, the required link between the definition of “similar goods/services” used across DuPont analyses, and practical evidentiary considerations—illustrated and shoe‑horned to the Federal Circuit’s treatment of the Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. appeal.

## Purpose of the sixth DuPont factor: showing weakness through crowded fields

The Lanham Act’s likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis asks courts to weigh multiple factors to decide whether consumers are likely to confuse two marks. The sixth DuPont factor—“the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”—asks whether the marketplace already contains other marks similar to the plaintiff’s such that the plaintiff’s mark is commercially weak. When many third parties use similar marks on similar goods or services, courts infer that consumers have learned to distinguish those similar marks, and therefore the plaintiff’s mark should receive a narrower scope of protection. Put differently: a crowded field diminishes exclusivity.

Cases make two basic points about the sixth factor. First, third‑party evidence is relevant to commercial strength, not dispositive: substantial third‑party use suggests weakness; little evidence suggests strength. Second, the inquiry evaluates similarity of marks used on similar goods or services—identity is not required. Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772 and later decisions underscore that registrations and evidence of use can be probative of weakness, and Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin show how courts treat the quantity and quality of that evidence when deciding whether a mark is “crowded.”

Apex illustrates these principles. CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration was contested by Apex Bank, which submitted evidence of multiple third‑party uses of “Aspire” and “Aspire‑formative” marks in the financial services field. The Board recognized some third‑party uses but concluded they were not “considerable” and therefore did not meaningfully reduce CC Serve’s mark strength. The Federal Circuit did not reject the Board’s ultimate resolution on strength; rather, it identified a legal error in the scope of the third‑party universe the Board examined. That error—treating the sixth factor as if it required a narrower goods/services match than the Board had already accepted for the second DuPont factor—undermined the analysis and required remand.

## Qualitative and quantitative showings: what counts as “considerable” or “ubiquitous”

No fixed numeric threshold determines when third‑party evidence makes a mark “weak.” Past decisions refer to “voluminous,” “considerable,” or “ubiquitous” evidence as sufficient to show a crowded field, but the number attached to those adjectives varies with context.

- Juice Generation treated 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number” in a fairly narrow field (juice/smoothie retail). Jack Wolfskin found that a dozen‑plus examples of paw‑print usage constituted voluminous evidence of a crowded field when coupled with registrations and use. By contrast, a handful of third‑party uses often will not defeat protection for an inherently distinctive mark.
- Courts assess both quantity and quality. A small number of third‑party marks may suffice if they are prominent, national in scope, longstanding, and clearly target the same consumers through the same channels. Conversely, many obscure or local uses may carry less weight.
- Context matters: the narrower and more specialized the goods or services, the fewer examples needed to demonstrate crowding. For broad, heterogeneous fields (e.g., “financial services” generally), more examples will be required to show that consumers have been exposed to similar marks on similar offerings.

Apex underscores that courts must evaluate third‑party evidence in the context of the relevant goods/services and public. The Board found nine Aspire‑formative marks in the credit‑card subfield and concluded that nine examples were not “considerable.” That factual judgment is not categorically wrong, but the Federal Circuit required that the Board consider additional Aspire uses in banking and financing fields once it had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar. Whether the additional evidence will push the tally into “considerable” territory is a fact question for the Board on remand.

## Registrations versus marketplace use: the evidentiary roles

Third‑party registrations and evidence of use play overlapping but distinct roles.

- Registrations: Third‑party registrations (or applications) can suggest that many others seek to use similar marks in the market. Registrations are admissible as evidence and can support a finding of crowding, but they are weaker than proof of actual marketplace presence because a registration alone does not show consumer exposure or commercial significance.
- Actual use: Evidence of marketplace use—screenshots of websites, marketing materials, press releases announcing national rollouts, transaction records, or other proof showing consumers have actually encountered the mark—carries greater weight. Use demonstrates that consumers have been (or are being) exposed to those marks and thus are more likely to have learned to distinguish them.
- Together: A combination of many registrations and significant use examples is most persuasive. Where registrations are numerous but show only small, localized, or non‑overlapping uses, courts will discount their effect.

Apex is instructive: the Board considered some third‑party examples but excluded many Aspire registrations and uses in related banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit required reconsideration because the Board’s exclusion meant it did not fully assess the marketplace presence of Aspire‑formative marks across the services it had found relevant.

## Defining “similar goods/services” and the requirement of consistency across DuPont analyses

A recurring source of error is treating “similar” differently across DuPont factors. The courts require internal consistency: the definition of the relevant goods or services used to evaluate similarity under factor two should inform the sixth‑factor inquiry.

- The second DuPont factor asks whether the parties’ goods/services are related such that consumers might believe they come from the same source. That inquiry can produce a factual finding that the services are highly similar or even legally identical in part.
- Once the Board (or court) has defined the commercial scope of the services relevant to the likelihood‑of‑confusion dispute, it cannot then narrow the scope for the sixth factor without justification. If the parties’ services are found highly similar or overlapping, third‑party marks used on any services within that same defined scope are potentially probative of crowding.
- Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation reinforce that “similar” does not mean “identical,” and the Federal Circuit in Apex makes the point explicit: when the Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar (banking/finance and credit‑card services), it erred by confining its sixth‑factor review to credit‑card marks only.

Practical effect: the Board must first define the relevant goods and the relevant consumer/public, based on its factor‑two analysis and the record. Then, when counting and qualifying third‑party marks, it should use that same definition unless record evidence or legal reasons suggest a narrower or broader scope is appropriate.

## Avoiding errors in defining the relevant public: adjacent services matter

Closely related to the scope of services is the identity of the relevant consumer or “relevant public.” The audience whose perception matters for both similarity and strength is the class of consumers who encounter the goods/services. The Board erred in Apex by treating the relevant public as only consumers of credit‑card services after finding the parties’ services broadly overlapping with banking and financing services.

Guidance:

- If the parties’ services reach the same end users (e.g., retail banking customers, consumers who use credit products) or travel through the same channels (e.g., online banking portals, co‑branded credit cards, bank branch networks), that shared audience is the relevant public for the sixth factor.
- Even if one party functions as a service provider to institutions (e.g., back‑end processing for bank‑issued credit cards) and the other interacts directly with retail customers, third‑party marks targeting the shared end users or marketed in the same channels can be probative of crowding.
- In financial services, adjacency is frequent: credit cards, checking and savings accounts, payment processing, loan origination, and account servicing often coexist in the same consumer minds and in the same marketing channels (banks, fintech portals, co‑brand partnerships). Excluding adjacent subfields without record support risks undercounting relevant third‑party usage.

Apex teaches that the Board must define the relevant public consistently and include adjacent services that its own factor‑two findings show are materially similar.

## Evaluating evidentiary weight: specimens, web pages, and marketplace context for financial brands

When preparing or evaluating a sixth‑factor record, counsel should gather and present evidence calibrated to the features courts value:

- Consumer‑facing materials showing actual use: website screenshots (with timestamps), mobile app listings, advertisements, social media profiles, press releases describing consumer rollouts, and consumer complaint or review pages demonstrate exposure.
- Business documents that show breadth of use: filings (SEC, state banking authorities), annual reports, bank press releases announcing co‑branding partnerships (e.g., a bank issuing an Aspire‑branded card through a servicing company), and merchant acceptance announcements all show marketplace presence.
- Geographical and channel coverage: evidence that a mark is used nationally (or within the same local market) and through the same channels as the parties’ services strengthens the showing. Localized or pilot uses are weaker.
- Duration and prominence: long‑standing brands and high‑profile national campaigns carry more weight than short‑lived or minor uses.
- Third‑party registrations: include them, but pair registrations with use evidence where possible. Registrations alone can be helpful in demonstrating how many others have sought to use a term, but courts will look for commercial exposure to conclude that consumers have actually learned to distinguish marks.

For financial services, particular attention should be paid to partnerships and co‑branding arrangements (banks issuing cards under a brand developed by a merchant or servicer), because such arrangements show overlapping commercial channels and a likelihood that consumers associate brands across those channels.

## Application to Apex: what the Federal Circuit required and why

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a compact example of the nexus between the second and sixth DuPont factors:

- The Board found that Apex’s proposed marks for “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s earlier registration for “credit card services” were highly similar and even partially legally identical in operation. That finding defined a broad universe of overlapping services and a relevant consumer public.
- When the Board then considered the sixth DuPont factor, it limited its review to third‑party marks used only for credit‑card services and discarded Aspire‑formative marks used in broader banking and financing contexts. The Federal Circuit held this inconsistent: once the Board established the high similarity of services, it could not narrow the field of third‑party marks for the sixth factor compared to the definition used for factor two.
- The Circuit vacated the Board’s sixth‑factor finding and remanded for reconsideration of third‑party evidence in light of the Board’s own finding about the parties’ services. Because the sixth‑factor result may affect the commercial impression analysis under the first DuPont factor, the Court also remanded factor one for reconsideration.

The lesson for practitioners and exam writers is clear: the Board must (1) make a principled, record‑based definition of the relevant goods/services and relevant consumer for factor two; (2) employ that same definition when gathering and weighing third‑party evidence under factor six (unless there is a reasoned basis to adjust); and (3) evaluate third‑party registrations in tandem with evidence of actual marketplace use to decide whether the field is sufficiently crowded to erode a mark’s strength.

## Practical advice for litigators and exam takers

- Build the record holistically. If you plan to argue crowdedness, collect registrations, but prioritize contemporaneous use evidence—web archives, screenshots, press releases, consumer notices, and partnership announcements. For financial services, show co‑branding, bank partner materials, and consumer‑facing product descriptions.
- Define the relevant public early and consistently. If you win on a broad similarity finding under factor two, be prepared to resist narrowing under factor six or explain why a narrower subfield is appropriate.
- Quantify thoughtfully but remember there are no bright‑line counts. Emphasize quality (national, consumer‑facing, long‑running uses) as much as quantity.
- Anticipate cross‑effects among DuPont factors. A successful sixth‑factor showing can shrink the plaintiff’s market exclusivity and influence analysis under the first (similarity of marks) and overall likelihood‑of‑confusion balance.

In short: the sixth DuPont factor is a contextual inquiry into market crowding. Courts look for convincing evidence that consumers have been repeatedly exposed to similar marks in the same commercial space. Apex illustrates the procedural and doctrinal rule that the scope of “similar goods/services” for the sixth factor must align with the Board’s earlier determinations about the scope of the parties’ services—an alignment required to ensure a coherent, fair evaluation of a mark’s commercial strength.
