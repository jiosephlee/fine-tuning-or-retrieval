Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How many third-party uses are enough to show a mark is weak, and do registrations without proof of use count?
Question:
The Board found nine Aspire-formative uses for credit card services and deemed that insufficient to show commercial weakness, contrasting cases like Juice Generation (26 third-party marks) and Jack Wolfskin (voluminous evidence). Is there any quantitative benchmark for 'considerable' or 'ubiquitous' third-party use, or is it context dependent? Also, does the evidence have to show actual marketplace use, or can third-party registrations alone meaningfully weaken a mark? On remand, if the TTAB includes broader banking/financing uses, what quality and type of third-party evidence typically moves the needle from 'normal' to 'weak' commercial strength?
Answer:
Short answer
- There is no fixed numerical benchmark. The Federal Circuit in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. treated the question as fact‑ and context‑dependent: nine third‑party Aspire uses for credit‑card services were held insufficient by the Board, while earlier cases called 26 marks “a considerable number” (Juice Generation) or relied on “voluminous” evidence (Jack Wolfskin). See Apex, J.A.47 (discussing nine uses and comparing Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin).
- Registrations can be considered (the Board used third‑party registrations to show the kinds of services that may emanate from a single source, J.A.33–34), but the persuasive weight comes from evidence of actual marketplace use and consumer exposure. In Apex the Board relied on exhibits showing third‑party uses (J.A.40–46); the Federal Circuit’s discussion treats use/market presence as the relevant indicia of commercial weakness (citing Palm Bay, Jack Wolfskin, Juice Generation).

Why there’s no bright line
- The opinion explicitly treats the sixth DuPont factor as a mixed quantitative/qualitative inquiry and compares the particular factual showings in different cases. Juice Generation referred to 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number”; Jack Wolfskin relied on “voluminous evidence” and highlighted multiple notable examples; by contrast the Board here found nine credit‑card uses inadequate (J.A.47). The court therefore applies a context‑sensitive test rather than a numeric rule.

What courts/boards look for (the relevant doctrinal considerations)
- Number of independent third‑party users/marks in the relevant market (quantity matters, but what counts as “considerable” depends on the field). Apex rejected the Board’s myopic counting of only credit‑card listings when it had already found the services highly similar; the Board should consider third‑party marks across banking/financing if the services are highly similar (J.A.33–34, 47).
- Nature and quality of use: evidence that consumers encounter the third‑party marks in commerce—websites, specimens, advertising, product labels, press coverage, long‑standing use—supports a finding of commercial weakness (Apex relied on exhibits of third‑party use, J.A.40–46; the opinion cites Palm Bay, Jack Wolfskin, and Juice Generation approvingly for the proposition that evidence of consumer education and “crowded” fields reduces protection).
- Breadth of channels, geographic reach, and longevity—showing multiple, independent market actors using the same formative mark across the same or closely related services weighs more heavily than a handful of isolated or fleeting uses.
- Distinctiveness of the formative element in context—if numerous third parties use the same formative in similar services, consumers are more likely to distinguish among sources, reducing a senior mark’s commercial strength (the Apex opinion explains this theory and the relevance of third‑party use, J.A.47 (citing Palm Bay and Juice Generation)).

Do registrations without proof of use count?
- The opinion shows that registrations were considered for assessing whether services may emanate from a single source (the Board looked at third‑party registrations in analyzing the second DuPont factor, J.A.33–34). But when the inquiry is commercial weakness under the sixth DuPont factor the opinion emphasizes actual third‑party use evidence (Apex compiled exhibits showing third‑party uses, J.A.40–46), and the cases the court cites (Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin) derive their force from demonstrations that the field is crowded in consumers’ experience. In short: registrations can be relevant to some DuPont inquiries, but the stronger way to show commercial weakness is proof that third parties actually use the marks in the marketplace and that consumers have been exposed to them.

What to submit on remand to move the needle from “normal” to “weak”
Based on Apex and the cases it cites, the TTAB (and a reviewing court) will be persuaded by a mix of quantity and quality. On remand, if the Board expands its universe to include broader banking/financing uses, Apex should consider submitting:
- More examples—many independent third parties using Aspire/Aspire‑formative marks across banking/financing services (not just credit cards), approaching the scale courts have treated as “considerable” (cf. Juice Generation’s 26; Jack Wolfskin’s “voluminous” record).
- Clear, consumer‑facing specimens of actual use for each third party (web pages, card images, account statements, advertising, trade‑press articles) showing the mark in its marketplace context.
- Evidence of channel overlap and common consumers (show that the same customers would encounter those third‑party marks in the same purchasing or decision contexts).
- Geographic and temporal breadth (longstanding or widespread uses, not single small local uses).
- Documentation of independent ownership and independent commercial presence (to avoid a showing of a single family of related users).
- If available, consumer survey or testimony showing that consumers have encountered multiple Aspire marks and distinguish them—this is not required but can be highly persuasive where the question is whether the field is “crowded” and consumers have been educated to differentiate marks.

Bottom line
- No numeric bright line; the inquiry is contextual. Apex confirms that the Board must consider third‑party marks beyond a narrow subcategory when services have been found highly similar (J.A.27–28, 33–34, 47), and that a relatively small cluster of uses (nine here) may be insufficient where other cases treated larger, more robust showings as dispositive. Registrations help show classes of services, but the persuasive power for weakening a mark lies in demonstrable marketplace use and consumer exposure across the relevant field.
