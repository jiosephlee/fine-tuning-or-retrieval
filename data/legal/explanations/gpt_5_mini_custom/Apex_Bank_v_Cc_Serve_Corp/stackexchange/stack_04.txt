Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How can the TTAB define 'credit card services' after the ID Manual entry was deleted, and what sources govern interpreting legacy identifications?
Question:
The Board noted that 'credit card services' was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s 1998 registration, then relied on dictionary definitions to construe the scope of 'credit card services' (issuing cards, processing payments, authorization, monitoring, account management, etc.). What is the legal basis for construing identification language when the ID Manual entry no longer exists or has changed—does the TTAB look to ordinary meaning at the time of registration, current ordinary meaning, the TMEP, or other interpretive sources? Are there precedents on interpreting legacy identifications when official taxonomy has shifted?
Answer:
Short answer
The TTAB may (and here did) construe an identification even if the Trademark ID Manual entry that once tracked that term has been deleted or changed. In Apex Bank v. CC Serve the Board, faced with a deleted ID Manual entry for “credit card services,” defined the phrase by reference to dictionary definitions and to evidence of industry practice in third‑party registrations, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that factual approach as supported by substantial evidence for the DuPont relatedness inquiry. (See J.A. 25, 27–28, 33–34.)

What the opinion establishes (legal doctrine and standards)
- Role of the Board: The Board must determine the scope of the applicant/registrant’s identified goods or services as a factual predicate to the DuPont relatedness analysis. Because CC Serve’s ID Manual entry had been deleted after its 1998 registration, the Board “first determined the meaning of the identified services” (J.A. 25) and then assessed relatedness under the second DuPont factor (J.A. 24–28).
- Permissible interpretive sources: In doing so the Board relied on ordinary‑language sources (dictionary definitions) to construe “credit card services” (the Board’s definition appears at J.A. 25), and it also relied on third‑party registrations and evidence of industry practice showing that credit‑card and banking/finance services may emanate from a single source (J.A. 33–34). The Federal Circuit treated those items as proper evidentiary bases for the Board’s factual finding and affirmed the Board’s relatedness conclusion because it was supported by substantial evidence (see review standards at J.A. 2–3 (citing M2 Software and Consolidated Edison) and the affirmance of the second DuPont factor at pp. 4–5).
- Appellate review: The court reiterates the familiar standard — legal conclusions de novo, factual findings for substantial evidence (J.A. 2–3) — so the Board’s choice of interpretive aids will be upheld if they are adequate evidentiary support for its factual conclusions.

What the opinion does not hold (and therefore what it does not decide)
- Timing of “ordinary meaning”: The opinion records that the entry was deleted after registration and then shows the Board using dictionary definitions, but it does not announce a rule that meaning must be fixed as of the date of registration (or, alternatively, as of the date of the proceeding). The opinion therefore does not adopt a time‑of‑registration requirement nor say that the Board must use only contemporaneous dictionaries or TMEP guidance.
- Reliance on the TMEP or ID Manual as dispositive: The opinion does not cite the TMEP or treat ID Manual entries as conclusive. Instead the Board looked to other evidentiary sources (dictionaries, third‑party registrations); the Federal Circuit accepted that approach in this case. The opinion does not establish that the TMEP/ID Manual controls where the Manual has been changed after issuance of a registration.

Practical implications / how this plays out in practice
- If an ID Manual entry has been deleted or altered, expect the Board to construe the identification using ordinary‑language evidence (dictionaries), contemporaneous industry evidence (third‑party registrations, evidence of use), and other admissible evidence of how the services are understood and marketed. Apex Bank shows that such sources are probative and can support a finding of relatedness (J.A. 25, 27–28, 33–34).
- Because the Board’s factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence, parties who want a narrower (or broader) reading of a legacy identification should introduce direct, contemporaneous evidence of meaning or usage (e.g., definitions in use at the time of registration, contemporaneous industry materials, specimen evidence, third‑party use) to counter dictionary or third‑party registration evidence.
- Interpretive consequences carry through the DuPont analysis: how the Board defines the services will affect what third‑party marks it considers (the Federal Circuit vacated and remanded the sixth‑factor analysis here because the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party marks based on its service definition — see J.A. 47, 33–34, 6–8).

Bottom line
Apex Bank shows that when the ID Manual no longer supplies a usable taxonomy, the TTAB may (and did) construe the legacy identification using ordinary‑language definitions and evidence of industry practice and third‑party registrations. The Federal Circuit accepted that approach on substantial‑evidence review for the DuPont relatedness question, but the opinion does not lay down a categorical rule about whether meaning must be fixed at the date of registration or about mandatory reliance on the TMEP/ID Manual.
