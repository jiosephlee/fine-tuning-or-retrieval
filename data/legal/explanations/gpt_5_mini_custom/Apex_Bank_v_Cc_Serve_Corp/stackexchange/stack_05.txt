Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Does adding the word 'BANK' to ASPIRE materially change the commercial impression for the first DuPont factor?
Question:
Apex’s marks are ASPIRE BANK (word and design) versus CC Serve’s ASPIRE. The first DuPont factor analysis was vacated because the sixth factor could affect assessments of commercial strength and overall impression. Independently of strength, how much weight does the addition of a descriptive or generic term like 'BANK' typically carry in comparing marks? Would the presence of 'BANK' be discounted as merely descriptive of services, or could it meaningfully alter appearance, sound, connotation, or commercial impression in the financial-services context?
Answer:
Short answer: The addition of the word “BANK” is not inert — it is part of the marks’ “entireties” and can change appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression — but it is not dispositive by itself. Under Apex Bank v. CC Serve the Board must evaluate “ASPIRE” versus “ASPIRE BANK” in context (including the parties’ overlapping services and evidence of third‑party use), and whether “BANK” should be given diminished weight as descriptive is a factual determination that bears on the first DuPont factor.

Why (grounded in the opinion)
- The opinion reiterates the governing standard for the first DuPont factor: compare the marks “in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” (Slip op. § III.C (citing DuPont).) That requires treating “BANK” as part of Apex’s asserted mark when assessing overall commercial impression.
- At the same time the court vacated the Board’s first‑factor analysis because the sixth factor (third‑party uses) might change assessment of the mark’s commercial strength and thus the overall impression. The court explained that third‑party evidence may show a mark is weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection, and that a different finding on factor six could affect the first factor on remand. (Slip op. § III.B & § III.C.)
- The opinion therefore frames two interrelated points you should keep in mind: (1) “BANK” must be considered in the marks’ entireties, and (2) the weight given to “BANK” depends in part on the mark’s commercial strength as developed by the record.

How that plays out, practically
- If “BANK” is truly descriptive/generic of the services (i.e., denotes the nature of the services to the relevant consumers), the Board may give it less source‑identifying weight and focus more on “ASPIRE” when comparing the marks’ commercial impressions. The opinion’s remand of the first factor in light of the sixth factor invites precisely this kind of inquiry: whether third‑party use of “Aspire” has made “ASPIRE” weak or commonplace such that the generic/descriptive matter (“BANK”) should be discounted.
- If, however, “BANK” contributes a distinct commercial message (for example, it steers the consumer’s expectation toward a bank’s services, or signals a different source relationship), it can meaningfully alter appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The opinion’s factual background is instructive: Apex is a retail bank and plans to use “ASPIRE BANK” for banking/financing services, while CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark is used on credit‑card/credit services in partnership with banks. Given the court’s finding that the services are highly similar, a consumer could reasonably perceive ASPIRE BANK as a banking arm or brand related to CC Serve’s ASPIRE credit‑card program — so the addition of “BANK” might not avoid confusion and could in fact reinforce a common commercial impression.
- The bottom line from the opinion: whether “BANK” is discounted or regarded as significant is a factual judgment the Board must make on remand after considering third‑party uses and the breadth (or weakness) of CC Serve’s claim in the marketplace. The court’s vacatur signals that this assessment can materially change the first‑factor outcome.

What the Board should examine on remand (directed by the opinion)
- Treat “ASPIRE” and “ASPIRE BANK” as whole marks for the appearance/sound/connotation analysis, but ask how much consumer perception is driven by the generic/descriptive element “BANK.”
- Reassess the sixth DuPont factor across the full, properly scoped universe of similar financial/banking/credit‑card marks (the court faulted the Board for narrowing to credit‑card marks only). If “ASPIRE” is shown to be common/weak, the Board may narrow its protection and discount generic matter more heavily in the first‑factor comparison.
- Consider the parties’ actual uses and the overlap of services (the court found the services “highly similar”), because close service overlap affects whether adding “BANK” will meaningfully distinguish the commercial impressions to relevant consumers.

Concise conclusion
The presence of “BANK” can be given less weight if it is simply descriptive/generic, but it cannot be ignored — it may still alter the marks’ commercial impression (positively or negatively) depending on the record. Apex Bank v. CC Serve requires the Board to make those factual determinations (particularly after reexamining third‑party use) because they materially affect the first DuPont factor.
