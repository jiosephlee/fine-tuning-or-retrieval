Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why did the Federal Circuit label the sixth-factor problem a 'legal flaw' instead of a substantial-evidence issue?
Question:
The court vacated because the Board used a too-narrow legal definition of 'similar' goods under factor six, rather than because the record lacked substantial evidence. What’s the boundary between legal error (misstating the test or defining the wrong marketplace) and factual error (miscounting or misweighing uses), and how does that distinction control the remedy (vacate vs. affirm)?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the Federal Circuit concluded the Board applied the wrong legal test when it narrowed the universe of third‑party marks to “credit card services” only, that was a legal error (reviewed de novo) rather than a dispute about whether the record contains enough evidence to support a factual finding (substantial‑evidence review). A misstatement or narrowing of the applicable legal standard requires vacatur and remand so the Board can reapply the correct test to the record; a mere dispute about the weight or sufficiency of competing evidence is a factual issue decided on substantial evidence.

Why the court treated factor six as a legal flaw (with references to the opinion)
- The opinion sets out the governing review rules up front: “We review the [Board]’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” (Slip op. §II (citing M2 Software)). That control means mistakes about which legal standard applies are legal errors, not factual ones.
- The court described the Board’s problem as an error in the legal definition of the relevant universe of “similar” marks. The Board had already found in its analysis of factor two that the parties’ services are “highly similar, in fact, partially legally identical” (J.A. 27–28). Yet when the Board reached factor six it “restricted the universe of marks it considered to only those relating to credit card services and excluded marks related to other banking and financing services.” The court stated plainly: “That was an error.” (Slip op. §III.B).
- The court grounded that conclusion in precedent and doctrine: factor six looks to “similar” (not identical) goods or services (citing Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation). Because the Board’s narrowing imposed “a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor,” the Court vacated that portion of the decision and remanded (J.A. 7–8).

Where the line is between legal error and a factual/substantial‑evidence issue
- Legal error: the Board misstated, misapplied, or imposed a stricter/ different legal standard than the one governing the factor (for example, requiring identity when the law asks only for similarity; or defining the relevant market wrongly for the factor). Those are questions of law reviewed de novo and, if present, require the Court to set aside the Board’s conclusion so the Board can apply the correct legal standard to the facts.
- Factual error/substantial‑evidence issue: the Board properly states and applies the legal test but the appellant argues the record does not contain enough evidence (or the Board misweighed evidence). Those are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard: if “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept” supports the Board’s factual findings, the court will uphold them (Consol. Edison quoted at §II).

How that distinction controls the remedy (vacate vs. affirm or remand)
- When the court finds a legal error in the Board’s analysis (as here), the proper remedy is vacatur of the affected findings and remand for reconsideration under the correct legal standard. That is what the Federal Circuit did for factor six (and, because factor six could affect commercial strength, for factor one as well): “We thus vacate the Board’s finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reconsideration” (Slip op. §III.B–C).
- When the issue is only whether the Board’s factual finding is supported by substantial evidence, the court will affirm if the record meets the substantial‑evidence test and will reverse/remand only if the evidence is legally insufficient. In other words, insufficient evidence can also lead to reversal/remand, but that result follows from application of the substantial‑evidence standard to the facts rather than from correcting an erroneous legal test.

Takeaway in this case
- The Board’s error was legal: it narrowed the class of third‑party marks contrary to its earlier factual finding of highly similar services and to the legal principle that factor six requires consideration of similar (not identical) goods/services. Because the Board used the wrong legal definition of “similar” goods for factor six, the court vacated that portion of the decision and remanded so the Board can reconsider third‑party marks using the correct scope.
