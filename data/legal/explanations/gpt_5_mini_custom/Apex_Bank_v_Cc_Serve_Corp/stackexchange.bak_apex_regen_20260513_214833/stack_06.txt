Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Are third-party registrations without marketplace use evidence probative under DuPont factor six?
Question:
The Board relied on third-party registrations to show relatedness (factor two), but seemed to focus on actual third-party uses for factor six. Do courts draw a doctrinal line between registrations (conceptual weakness) and marketplace uses (commercial weakness) under the sixth DuPont factor? If so, how should boards weigh each type of evidence when assessing the mark’s scope of protection?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — courts and the Federal Circuit treat registrations and marketplace use as different kinds of evidence under DuPont factor six, and they must be weighed accordingly. Registrations (or registrations plus isolated uses) tend to show conceptual weakness (crowding on paper); actual marketplace use shows commercial weakness (consumer exposure and ability to “live with” similar marks). Crucially, when the Board has already defined the relevant goods/services broadly under DuPont factor two, it must use that same scope when considering third‑party marks under factor six. That is the specific error the Federal Circuit corrected here.

Why and where the opinion says this
- The opinion explains the governing principle: “Evidence of third‑party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” (citing Palm Bay Imps., J.A. 46–47 (opinion § III.B)). The court cites Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin for the point that marketplace use educates consumers and thus tends to show lack of commercial strength (J.A. 46–47).
- The Board relied on third‑party registrations to support its finding that the services are related under factor two (J.A. 33–34). But when it reached factor six it “restricted the universe of marks it considered to only those relating to credit card services and excluded marks related to other banking and financing services.” The Federal Circuit held that was error because the Board had already found the parties’ services “highly similar” and even “partially legally identical” under factor two, so it must retain the same scope for factor six (J.A. 47–48).
- The court vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding and remanded so the Board can reconsider the “number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods” in light of its factor‑two finding (J.A. 51–52).

How courts generally treat registrations vs. marketplace use under DuPont factor six (doctrinal guidance grounded in the opinion)
- Distinct evidentiary flavors:
  - Registrations (and registrations alone) tend to show conceptual or “on‑paper” crowding: many parties like the term enough to register it. The Board here used third‑party registrations to help define relatedness under factor two (J.A. 33–34).
  - Demonstrated marketplace uses (evidence that third parties actually use similar marks in commerce) show commercial weakness: consumers see multiple marks in the marketplace and learn to distinguish sources (J.A. 46–47 citing Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin).
- Relative probative value:
  - Marketplace use is generally more probative of weakness than mere registrations, because factor six asks about “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” The opinion treats “use” as central to the factor (J.A. 46).
  - Registrations are not irrelevant, but they are weaker proof of consumer habituation; courts will want to know how many marks are actually used, how prominent they are, and whether consumers of the defined relevant market will encounter them.
- Consistent scope rule:
  - If the Board finds goods/services are similar (factor two), it must use that same definition of the relevant market when deciding which third‑party marks to count under factor six. The Federal Circuit rejected the Board’s narrowing of the universe (J.A. 47–48).

Practical guide for Boards (and practitioners) based on the opinion
- First, define the relevant goods/services under factor two and keep that scope when assessing factor six (the error the court fixed; J.A. 27–28, 47–48).
- When presenting third‑party evidence for factor six:
  - Distinguish registrations from demonstrated commercial use. Label exhibits as “registrations only” vs “evidence of use” and explain the geographic reach, dates, sales/prominence for uses.
  - For registrations, explain why the registered goods/services are sufficiently similar to the parties’ services (but be prepared for lower weight).
  - For marketplace uses, provide concrete indicia of consumer exposure (e.g., advertising, sales figures, duration, channels, market share) because those are the kinds of facts courts treat as showing consumers have been “educated” to distinguish similar marks (J.A. 46–47).
- If only a few third‑party uses exist (as the Board found here—nine uses), explain why that number either is or is not “considerable” in the context of the defined market; courts compare the extent of third‑party evidence to examples of “voluminous” or “considerable” evidence in prior cases (see Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation cited at J.A. 47).

What the Federal Circuit did not decide
- The court did not quantify how many third‑party uses make a mark weak. It vacated and remanded because the Board applied an inconsistent scope rather than because a specific number was per se insufficient (J.A. 47–52). Thus, the weight of registrations versus uses remains fact‑specific.

Takeaway
- Registrations matter but are less persuasive than actual marketplace uses for DuPont factor six. The Board must consider both in light of the same goods/services scope adopted under factor two; when it fails to do so, the Federal Circuit will vacate and require reconsideration (as the court did here, J.A. 47–52).
