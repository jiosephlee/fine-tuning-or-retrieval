Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### How should tribunals evaluate the 'number and nature' of third-party uses—do raw counts like 9 vs. 26 control?
Question:
The Board discounted Apex’s showing because nine Aspire-formative credit card uses were not as 'considerable' as in Juice Generation or Jack Wolfskin. The Federal Circuit didn’t say nine is enough or not, but vacated for using too narrow a marketplace. On remand, what should matter more: the raw number of uses, the similarity of the goods/services to the parties’ identifications, the extent of consumer exposure (sales, advertising, geography), or a combination? How have courts articulated this assessment?
Answer:
Short answer: No, raw counts like “9 vs. 26” do not control. On remand the Board must evaluate the sixth DuPont factor holistically — using the same scope of goods/services it adopted for the second factor — and weigh (1) the similarity of the third‑party uses to the parties’ identified goods/services, (2) the extent and nature of consumer exposure to those third‑party uses (sales, advertising, distribution/geography, marketplace prominence), and (3) the quality of the evidence (registrations vs. actual use, contemporaneity, probative examples). The Court in Apex requires a combined, context‑sensitive inquiry rather than a mechanical count.

Why and how the Board should do this (grounded in the opinion)

- Use the same relevant market as the second DuPont factor. The Federal Circuit held the Board erred by narrowing the universe of third‑party uses to only “credit card services” after it had already found the parties’ services “highly similar” (indeed “partially legally identical”) in its second‑factor analysis. See J.A. 27–28, 33–34, 47–48. The court instructed that the Board “should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors.” J.A. 7 (opinion at III.B).

- Don’t treat raw numbers as dispositive. The opinion explicitly rejects reliance on a bare numerical threshold: the Board compared nine uses to the “considerable” 26 uses in Juice Generation and 14 “notable examples” in Jack Wolfskin (cited by the Board), but the Federal Circuit vacated because the Board’s scope was too narrow. See J.A. 47 (discussing Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin) & J.A. 7–8 (explaining error). The court therefore signals that numbers are context‑dependent: examples in past cases are persuasive illustrations, not bright‑line rules.

- Focus on commercial strength and consumer education. As the opinion and the cases it cites make clear, third‑party use is probative because a crowded field can weaken a mark (Palm Bay; Juice Generation; Jack Wolfskin). The Board should assess whether third‑party uses show the public is “educated” to distinguish marks — which depends on how visible and pervasive those uses are in the relevant marketplace (sales, advertising, geographic reach), not just how many marks exist. See J.A. 6 (discussing Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin) and J.A. 51 (Board’s ultimate statement that commercial strength/weakness affects scope of protection).

- Assess quality and comparability of third‑party evidence. Courts distinguish mere registrations from actual marketplace use (Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation). The Board should give more weight to third‑party marks that (a) are used in commerce (not just registered), (b) operate on the same or highly similar goods/services the Board already identified, and (c) reach the same consumer class/geography that matters for likelihood of confusion. See J.A. 33–34 (Board relied on third‑party registrations in second‑factor analysis) and the court’s instruction that the Board consider “similar marks for similar goods” (J.A. 7).

Practical checklist for the remand analysis
- Keep the same scope of products/services used in the second DuPont factor when identifying third‑party marks to consider (J.A. 27–28, 7).
- For each third‑party use, record (a) whether it’s actual use or just registration, (b) the goods/services identified, (c) sales/advertising evidence, (d) geographic reach, and (e) consumer‑facing prominence.
- Explain how the pattern of uses affects CC Serve’s mark’s commercial strength: do the uses show a crowded field that would educate consumers (favoring a narrower scope of protection), or are uses limited/isolated (supporting normal protection)?
- Avoid a mechanical count; tie the weight of the evidence to consumer exposure and marketplace reality (not merely the number of entries).

Conclusion
The Board must perform a contextual, evidence‑driven inquiry into the “number and nature” of third‑party uses. Mere raw counts are not controlling; what matters is how similar those uses are to the parties’ services (using the same scope as the second factor), how much consumer exposure and marketplace prominence the third‑party uses have, and the evidentiary quality of those uses. The Apex opinion directs precisely that integrated analysis (see J.A. 27–28, 47–51, and the cited Juice Generation/Jack Wolfskin authorities).
