Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

## Policing TTAB Consistency Across DuPont Factors: A Substantial‑Evidence Case That Turned on Legal Framing

Why this matters — Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is another reminder that while the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s factual findings receive substantial‑evidence review, the Federal Circuit will step in where the Board shifts its legal framing from one DuPont factor to another. The panel here affirmed the Board’s key factual finding — that CC Serve’s credit‑card services and Apex’s banking/finance services are highly similar — but vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth factor and, consequentially, its analysis of the first factor. The error: the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party “Aspire” uses it would consider under DuPont 6 to a slice of the services even though it had already concluded the parties’ services overlapped broadly under DuPont 2. That mismatch turned what might have been a run‑of‑the‑mill likelihood‑of‑confusion opinion into a Federal Circuit remand.

What the court did and why

The Board sustained CC Serve’s opposition to Apex’s ASPIRE BANK application under Section 2(d), largely on the premise that the services were highly similar and the marks close enough to create a likelihood of confusion. On appeal the Federal Circuit applied the familiar standard: legal conclusions de novo, factual findings for substantial evidence. It affirmed the Board’s DuPont 2 holding — the services are highly similar (in part legally identical) — because the Board’s parsing of the parties’ identifications and supporting evidence was supported by record citations.

The Court’s reversal targeted a legal inconsistency. Having found the services highly similar for DuPont 2, the Board then confined its DuPont 6 inquiry to third‑party uses tied narrowly to “credit card services,” excluding Aspire uses in other banking/finance contexts. The Federal Circuit flagged that approach as erroneous: DuPont 6 asks about “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods,” not only identical goods, and the Board’s earlier DuPont 2 finding defined the relevant scope of similarity. Once the Board decided the services were highly similar, it could not impose a different, narrower definition of similarity for DuPont 6 without legal error. Because DuPont 6 bears on a mark’s commercial strength and thus on the overall impression relevant to DuPont 1, the Court vacated both factors and remanded for the Board to reassess the universe of third‑party Aspire uses and then revisit the marks’ overall commercial impression.

Context and doctrinal line

Apex Bank fits into a steady line of Federal Circuit decisions policing how the TTAB treats third‑party evidence and how the Board defines the “relevant public” or scope of goods/services when applying DuPont. The court has repeatedly emphasized that the Board must be coherent in its legal framing: it cannot, for example, find goods to be related for purposes of confusion yet ignore that relatedness when considering the breadth of third‑party use. Decisions such as Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin have long guided that DuPont 6 is about relative weakness and the practical marketplace reality that consumers might distinguish crowded marks; Apex reinforces that the universe of potentially crowding uses must be set consistently with the Board’s own DuPont 2 findings.

Two discrete themes are worth underscoring. First, DuPont factors are interlocking; factual findings under one factor often bear on the legal import of evidence under another. A Board determination that services are highly similar should generally expand — not contract — the set of third‑party uses that are “similar” enough to inform DuPont 6. Second, the Board’s purposive inquiry into commercial strength cannot be artificially narrowed by technical pinning of a third‑party mark to a single inventory description when the Board itself has said the marketplace overlap is broader.

Practical takeaways for litigants and the Board

For litigants: preserve your legal objections to the Board’s framing of the relevant market and the scope of “similarity.” Apex shows the Federal Circuit will correct legal inconsistencies even where factual findings stand. If the Board finds goods or services related under DuPont 2, press to ensure that any third‑party evidence you submit is considered in that same, consistent scope under DuPont 6. If opposing, be ready to show why third‑party uses should be limited (e.g., materially different channels, customer classes), not merely to argue numbers.

For the TTAB (and practitioners drafting decisions): Apex is a drafting reminder. When the Board makes a substantive finding about the nature and scope of goods/services, it should explicitly carry that scope into related-factor analyses or explain why a narrower lens is appropriate. If the Board intends to confine DuPont 6 to more specific subcategories, it should ground that limitation in record facts (channel distinctions, separate classes of consumers, service differences) rather than in a conclusory exclusion. Doing so reduces reversal risk and gives appellate courts a clear chain from facts to legal conclusions.

Downstream effects

Apex is unlikely to change the basic playbook: parties will still marshal third‑party registrations and marketplace uses to argue for relative weakness or strength. But the decision reinforces an allocation of tasks between factfinder and court: the Board gets broad leeway on mixed factual assessments, but its legal framing — especially when it shifts during an analysis of interconnected factors — is fair game for correction. Expect litigants to cite Apex when urging broader consideration of third‑party evidence once relatedness is established, and expect the Board to be more explicit about the universe of marks it treats as “similar” so its DuPont narrative is coherent across factors.

Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve exemplifies a modest but important supervisory role the Federal Circuit plays over TTAB methodology. The core mistake was not necessarily the outcome the Board reached, but the Board’s inconsistent legal framing across DuPont factors. By insisting that the scope of similarity found under DuPont 2 carry through to DuPont 6 (unless justified by record distinctions), the court protects the coherence of the likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis and limits opportunistic narrowing that can mask a mark’s true commercial strength. Practitioners and the Board would do well to take that coherence seriously in both record development and opinion writing.
