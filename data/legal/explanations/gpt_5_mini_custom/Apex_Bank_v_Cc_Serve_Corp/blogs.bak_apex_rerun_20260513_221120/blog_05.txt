Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# Legacy Identifications and Deleted Terms: Interpreting “Credit Card Services” in 2025

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. 2025) is a useful waypoint for litigants and practitioners wrestling with “legacy” trademark identifications—terms that were valid when a registration issued but have since been altered or removed from the Trademark ID Manual. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board in Apex Bank confronted just that problem: CC Serve’s registration identified “credit card services,” a term later deleted from the ID Manual. The Board nonetheless construed the legacy identification using dictionary definitions and marketplace context, and the Federal Circuit, while rejecting part of the Board’s reasoning on other DuPont factors, accepted the Board’s construction of the services for the second DuPont factor (similarity of goods/services). The decision underscores how courts and the Board will treat aging or deleted identifications and signals practical consequences for both registrants trying to preserve older filings and applicants seeking clarity about the scope of prior rights.

## How the Board and Federal Circuit approached a deleted ID

At issue was whether CC Serve’s plain-text registration for ASPIRE (issued in 1998) should be read to cover a range of activities embodied by the phrase “credit card services.” Because “credit card services” had been deleted from the ID Manual subsequent to CC Serve’s registration, the Board did not rely on a current ID Manual definition. Instead it turned to dictionary definitions and market-context evidence to spell out what “credit card services” encompassed—issuing cards, processing transactions, account management, credit counseling, fraud alerts, and the like. The Board then compared that construction to Apex’s proposed banking and financing services and concluded the services were highly similar, even partially legally identical.

On appeal Apex argued that CC Serve’s services were not the same as Apex’s (particularly because CC Serve partners with banks to service card programs rather than itself “being a bank”). The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding under the second DuPont factor, stating that the Board had carefully tied the services it construed to the written identification and that substantial evidence supported the conclusion of high similarity. The court therefore accepted the Board’s methodology in defining the legacy phrasing for purposes of the services-similarity analysis.

The court did, however, vacate the Board’s analysis under DuPont factors six (third‑party use) and one (mark similarity), not because of the definition itself but because the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party “Aspire” uses to only those expressly labeled “credit card services.” Having already found the parties’ services highly similar or even partially legally identical, the court held that the Board should have applied the same (broader) conception of similarity when assessing third‑party marks and the marks’ overall commercial impression.

## What this means doctrinally

Apex Bank reinforces several legal touchstones and clarifies their application in the context of legacy IDs:

- Legacy identifications can and will be construed by the Board and courts using extrinsic sources when the ID Manual no longer defines the term. The Board is not confined to the current ID Manual if the registration predates a change.
  
- The governing question remains what the identification, viewed in context, reasonably encompasses. The Board’s use of dictionaries and marketplace evidence to flesh out the meaning is consistent with the general principle that an identification defines the legal scope of the registration.

- Substantial-evidence review governs. Because the court affirmed the Board’s factual finding that the services are highly similar, parties will face a high bar to overturn such constructions absent record‑deficient reasoning.

- Consistency across DuPont factors matters. Once a particular scope of goods/services is adopted in one part of the analysis (here, factor two), that scope should inform consideration of other factors—especially those that turn on the field of use (e.g., third‑party marks under factor six or commercial impression under factor one). The Federal Circuit corrected the Board for applying inconsistent scopes.

## Practical implications and strategies

For registrants with older registrations
- Legacy registrations remain valuable. Even if an ID term was later deleted from the Manual, a registration’s plain-language identification can be construed to include a range of services so long as the Board or a court finds that construction supported by evidence.
- Preserve evidentiary support for marketplace meaning. Registrants should develop and maintain contemporaneous evidence that what the written identification covered at issuance corresponds to industry practices—advertising, catalogs, agreements with partners, contemporaneous dictionaries, and trade publications can all help cement a broad reading later.

For applicants and challengers
- Don’t assume deletion from the ID Manual narrows preexisting registrations. An applicant can expect the Board to consider dictionary and marketplace context when evaluating legacy IDs. Attackers should be prepared to rebut proposed constructions with evidence showing a narrower historical understanding or differences in function between the old term and the applicant’s services.
- Attack the factual foundation. When facing a legacy ID construed broadly on dictionaries and market usage, challenge whether the Board’s particular factual findings have substantial evidentiary support. Absent weak factual findings, reversal is unlikely.

For both sides during prosecution and litigation
- Use extrinsic evidence proactively. The Apex Bank opinion validates using dictionaries, third‑party registrations, industry materials, and testimony to define the operative scope of an identification. Parties should marshal such evidence early.
- Be consistent in scope across arguments. If you succeed (or fail) in establishing a particular scope for factor two, expect that same scope to be relevant to factors that ask whether the field is crowded or how the marks are perceived commercially. Courts will not tolerate “moving the goalposts” by expanding or narrowing the relevant universe between factors.

## Final thoughts

Apex Bank offers a practical lesson: deletions from the ID Manual change the guidance the PTO gives going forward, but they do not erase the legal life of earlier identifications. The Board and courts will look beyond the Manual to dictionaries and marketplace realities to understand what a legacy identification meant and whether that meaning overlaps with an applicant’s goods or services. That approach is defensible on the merits—language evolves, and the Trademark Act ties rights to the registration’s identification, not to a shifting online manual—but it also injects uncertainty. Practitioners should therefore treat identifications as living documents: preserve usage evidence, use extrinsic materials strategically, and be prepared to insist on consistent treatment of scope across the DuPont inquiry.
