Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 9: # Case Study: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

This chapter uses the Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. to illustrate how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) and reviewing courts apply the multifactor DuPont likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The opinion presents a clean example of (1) how the Board may find high similarity or partial legal identity of services under the second DuPont factor; (2) why consistency in defining the “relevant” services and consumers matters when assessing other DuPont factors; and (3) how third‑party use evidence bears on a mark’s commercial strength under the sixth DuPont factor. We also consider standards of review and practical lessons for trademark practitioners in the financial-services field.

## Factual setup: credit card services versus planned online banking under ASPIRE BANK

At the center of this dispute are two parties operating in the financial-services space and competing over the right to use ASPIRE-formative trademarks. CC Serve owns a federal registration, issued in 1998 and with a priority date in 1996, for the standard-character mark ASPIRE covering “credit card services.” CC Serve’s business model is not a typical retail bank: it partners with banks so that the bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and accounts, but CC Serve and affiliates provide substantial program-management services—servicing accounts, processing transactions, monitoring, and other credit‑card‑related functions. Apex Bank is a brick-and-mortar and retail bank that does not at the time offer credit cards. Apex sought federal registration, via intent‑to‑use applications, for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “banking and financing services,” intending to use the marks for an internet banking brand.

The parties’ applications and the record produced two important initial facts. First, CC Serve’s registration predates Apex’s filings by many years, giving CC Serve the presumptive priority to oppose Apex’s marks. Second, Apex’s identified services—“banking and financing services”—overlap with the range of activities the Board concluded fall within the ordinary meaning of “credit card services” (issuing cards, processing transactions, account management, credit counseling, etc.). Those facts frame the DuPont inquiry: priority favors CC Serve; the central question is whether Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks would likely confuse the ordinary consumer as to source given the parties’ overlapping services and the marketplace use of “Aspire” by other entities.

## Factor 2 affirmed: high similarity and partial legal identity of services

The second DuPont factor asks whether the parties’ goods or services are related such that concurrent use of similar marks would create a likelihood of confusion. This inquiry does not require literal identity; instead courts and the TTAB ask whether the services are “related in some manner and/or the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” That legal standard—derived from long-standing precedent such as Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC—focuses on the nature of the services and how they are presented to consumers.

In Apex, the Board carefully parsed the ordinary meanings of the parties’ pleaded identifications of services. Because the Trademark ID Manual entry for “credit card services” had been removed after CC Serve’s registration, the Board examined the descriptive scope of both parties’ services. It concluded that “credit card services” includes not only the issuance of cards but also account management, processing of transactions, credit monitoring, and related financial-account services—all activities that overlap with Apex’s broadly phrased “banking and financing services.” Given that overlap, the Board found the services to be “highly similar” and “legally identical, in part.” The Federal Circuit affirmed that conclusion, emphasizing two propositions students should internalize.

First, an applicant’s and registrant’s services need not be identical for the second DuPont factor to weigh in favor of confusion; analogous or complementary functions suffice. Financial services frequently illustrate that point: credit cards, deposit accounts, loan servicing, and payment processing are distinct service labels but occupy the same marketplace space and are frequently offered under a single brand by banks or financial‑service conglomerates. The Board’s exercise of statutory interpretation of the listed services—reading “banking,” “finance,” and “credit card services” in light of commercial reality—was found by the Federal Circuit to be supported by substantial evidence.

Second, the breadth of a party’s identification of services matters. When a registration or application uses broad terms (e.g., “banking and financing services”) the Board may reasonably read those terms to encompass a range of activities, including services one party believes are outside the other party’s scope. A registrant’s actual business model (CC Serve’s partnering with banks) does not immunize it from a finding that its registered services overlap with a bank’s offerings if the terms in the registration or application naturally encompass such overlap. For students, that underscores the importance of precise class descriptions and the risks of broad identifications in prosecution and opposition practice.

## Factor 6 vacated: improper narrowing to credit card services despite broader similarity finding

The sixth DuPont factor examines the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods and services. Evidence of third‑party registration and use is relevant under this factor because it can show a mark is relatively weak and entitled to narrower protection. The legal principle is that “a crowded field” of similar marks teaches consumers to distinguish among similar marks and diminishes each mark’s exclusive scope.

The Board in Apex considered third‑party evidence of ASPIRE‑formative marks. Apex submitted numerous exhibits showing third‑party uses of “Aspire” or Aspire‑formative marks across the financial services sector. But the Board—having earlier concluded the parties’ services were highly similar—narrowed its consideration under factor six to third‑party marks used for “credit card services” alone, excluding marks used for other banking and financial services. The Board concluded that only nine Aspire marks were relevant and that that number was not “considerable” enough to demonstrate weakness. The Federal Circuit held this narrowing to be an error.

The doctrinal point is critical: DuPont factor assessments must be consistent in how the relevant goods or services are defined. If the Board’s analysis of factor two finds that banking and credit‑card services overlap or are partially legally identical, the scope of “similar goods or services” for the sixth factor cannot be truncated to a narrower subset (credit card services only) without justification. Precedent the court cited—Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation—makes plain that third‑party marks need not be on literally identical goods; marks used on similar goods or services are probative. The Board’s inconsistent scope effectively insulated CC Serve’s mark from evidence that might demonstrate that “Aspire” is widely used across the broader financial-services field. Because the breadth of services considered under factor two was broader, the sixth factor should have considered third‑party marks used in that broader space.

Students should draw two lessons. One, the Board must define the “relevant” goods or services and then apply that definition consistently when comparing marks and when evaluating third‑party use. Two, when arguing factor six, practitioners should frame third‑party use evidence to match the full scope of services the Board has found comparable under factor two. On remand, the Board is required to reassess factor six using the broader, more realistic scope of similar services it already found, considering all Aspire‑formative marks used on banking and financing services that are similar to the parties’ services.

## Factor 1 vacated: commercial impression must be reassessed after strength is recalibrated

The first DuPont factor analyzes the marks in their entireties with respect to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. This is an inherently comparative and context‑sensitive inquiry: a mark’s potential to cause confusion depends not only on literal similarity but on how consumers perceive and mentally process the marks in the marketplace.

In Apex the Board initially evaluated the marks and concluded that the first factor weighed in favor of the registrant. But because the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s factor six analysis, the court also required reconsideration of factor one. Why? Because third‑party use evidence can materially affect the commercial impression of a mark. If the Board, on remand, finds that ASPIRE is widely used across similar financial services and thus commercially weak, that finding could alter the weight the first factor gives to the marks’ similarities. A mark that is conceptually or commercially weak receives narrower protection; the same degree of literal similarity between marks may be less likely to support a finding of confusion when the registered mark is one among many similar marks in a crowded field.

This interdependence between DuPont factors is a practical point about trademark analysis: findings under one factor—particularly those about the nature of services and a mark’s strength—can and should inform the analysis under other factors, including the core mark‑to‑mark comparison. The Federal Circuit’s vacatur of the Board’s factor one finding reinforces that the Board must revisit mark similarity with fresh eyes after any recalibration of commercial strength under factor six.

For students, the teaching is methodological: when preparing or reviewing a likelihood‑of‑confusion case, think holistically. If you plan to introduce evidence that a mark is weak (through third‑party use, registrations, or marketplace descriptive use), explain how that evidence changes the commercial impression and thus the first factor. Conversely, if defending a mark, show that third-party uses are limited or materially distinguishable so that the mark retains a broad scope of protection.

## Standards of review applied: substantial evidence and legal error

Understanding how appellate courts review TTAB decisions is essential for litigating and predicting outcomes. The Federal Circuit applies two complementary standards. Findings of fact—such as the Board’s determinations about the scope of a party’s services, the number and nature of third‑party uses, and the circumstances of marketing—are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence requires that a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate to support the Board’s findings; it is not a de novo reweighing. Legal conclusions, including the application and construction of legal standards and the weighing of DuPont factors as questions of law grounded on subsidiary fact findings, are reviewed de novo.

In Apex, the court affirmed the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar because substantial evidence supported that conclusion. But the court found legal error in how the Board applied that finding to its consideration of factor six (a legal error in scope and consistency), which warranted vacatur and remand. This mixed approach—defer to factual findings so long as supported by evidence, but correct legal misapplication—is typical and instructive: a successful appeal will often hinge on identifying not an absence of evidence but an inconsistency in legal analysis or the wrongful application of legal standards to the facts the Board did find.

Students should therefore scrutinize TTAB decisions for two types of reversible mistakes: (1) insufficient evidentiary support for a factual finding; and (2) legal errors in applying principled standards (for example, inconsistent definitions of the relevant goods or consumers across factors). The Apex decision exemplifies the second category—an application error—rather than mere lack of evidence.

## Remand instructions: reconsider scope of third-party marks and mark comparison

The Federal Circuit’s mandate to the Board was precise in scope and instructive as to how the Board must proceed on remand. The Board must:

- Reassess DuPont factor six by considering third‑party marks used on all services that the Board previously found to be similar to the parties’ services. Because the Board had already found Apex’s “banking and financing services” highly similar (and partially legally identical) to CC Serve’s “credit card services,” the universe of third‑party marks eligible for consideration should include Aspire and Aspire‑formative marks used for banking, financing, payment processing, and other related financial services—not only literal “credit card services.”

- Reevaluate the commercial strength of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark, taking into account the extent, nature, and consumer recognition of third‑party uses in the now‑properly defined service space. The Board must distinguish conceptual strength (inherent distinctiveness) from commercial strength (market recognition), and weigh evidence that a mark is descriptive or commonly used by third parties.

- Reexamine DuPont factor one—similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—in light of any altered finding about CC Serve’s mark strength. If ASPIRE is shown to be commercially weak, the Board must explain whether the apparently similar elements between ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK are likely to confuse a typical consumer despite the crowded field.

- Maintain consistent definitions of the relevant goods, services, and consumers across all factors. Where the parties’ services are found to be similar or overlapping, the Board should apply that same scope when defining the consumer audience and when comparing marks or evaluating competing marketplace uses.

Practically, the Board’s reconsideration must be reasoned and make clear why particular third‑party uses are or are not probative. It must explain the relevance of each piece of evidence to a mark’s strength and to consumer perception. If the Board discounts categories of third‑party evidence, it should articulate the reasons: temporal remoteness, different channels of trade, different consumer sophistication, or clear material differences in the services offered under the third-party marks.

## Practical implications for financial services branding strategies

Apex provides several actionable lessons for brand owners and practitioners in the financial-services sector.

First, choose marks that are distinctive in both concept and marketplace practice. The word “Aspire” is a laudatory verb—a kind of aspirational term that may be conceptually weak for services where aspiration is a common marketing theme. Particularly in industries like finance, where motivational words are popular, applicants should anticipate third‑party use and the probable limitation of trademark scope.

Second, prosecutions should use precise identifications of services. Broad identifications like “banking and financing services” can encompass many activities and make an applicant vulnerable to oppositions where prior users have registrations covering particular niches (e.g., credit‑card program management). If you wish to avoid conflict with existing registrations, consider narrowing the application identification (within the constraints of the applicant’s intended use) or providing evidence distinguishing channels of trade, classes of customers, or methods of marketing.

Third, collect and present third‑party use evidence early and comprehensively. If you are defending against an opposition, demonstrate that the prior mark is used in a narrow niche or that third‑party uses are few and materially different. If you are attacking a registration, marshal evidence showing a crowded field—registrations, websites, advertising, and third‑party branding across the full suite of relevant services. In Apex the breadth and nature of third‑party Aspire uses across financial services were central. Presenting them bundled by service type, channels of trade, and contemporaneous marketplace recognition will be persuasive.

Fourth, account for business models that involve partnerships and co‑branding. CC Serve’s model—managing credit‑card programs while banks issue the consumer‑facing cards—illustrates that functional distinctions in corporate structure do not necessarily determine the DuPont analysis. When a program manager or payments processor operates under a mark in conjunction with a bank, that mark may be treated as a source-identifying mark in the same marketplace as a bank’s retail brands.

Fifth, consider consumer sophistication evidence and consumer surveys when appropriate. Financial services range in consumer sophistication from routine retail banking customers to sophisticated institutional clients. Evidence that the relevant consumers are pro‑sophisticated professionals may reduce the likelihood of confusion; evidence of low consumer attention or habitual behavior in online banking may support the opposite.

Finally, keep uses and registrations current: pre‑1998 registrations like CC Serve’s may retain strong priority, but marketplace realities and subsequent third‑party adoption can erode exclusivity. Trademark owners should monitor and, where necessary, police third‑party uses; potential challengers should document and present the market’s evolution.

Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful case for understanding how the TTAB’s DuPont analysis should be coherent and consistent across factors. The second DuPont factor’s finding of high similarity between banking and credit‑card services was supported by substantial evidence and affirmed. But the Federal Circuit correctly required the Board to apply that finding uniformly when assessing third‑party use evidence under factor six and to revisit the first factor once commercial strength is recalibrated. For students and practitioners, the decision highlights operational rules: define the marketplace carefully, present third‑party evidence that matches that definition, and recognize the interdependence of DuPont factors—especially how a finding about a mark’s strength ripples through the mark‑to‑mark comparison.
