Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and marks
This dispute arises between CC Serve Corp. and Apex Bank over the use and registration of marks containing the word “ASPIRE.” CC Serve owns U.S. Registration No. 2,126,948 for the standard-character mark ASPIRE for “credit card services.” That registration issued in 1998 and carries a priority date of October 17, 1996. Apex Bank sought federal registration of two intent-to-use (ITU) marks—an ASPIRE BANK word mark and an ASPIRE BANK word-and-design mark—intended to identify banking and financing services offered under the ASPIRE BANK brand.

## Nature of the services
The parties’ businesses and the services identified in the applications differ in form but overlap in the financial-services sphere. CC Serve does not itself issue credit cards as a bank; rather, it partners with banks to operate branded credit-card programs. Under CC Serve’s model, a bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts, and CC Serve (and its affiliates) provides account servicing, transaction processing, monitoring, and related services in connection with those cards and accounts. Apex Bank is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank with 18 branches that offers deposit accounts, mortgages, and consumer and business loans. Apex did not offer credit cards at the time of the proceedings but planned to launch an internet banking service under the ASPIRE BANK brand.

## Prosecution history at the USPTO
Apex filed its ITU applications in August 2019 to register the ASPIRE BANK marks for “banking and financing services.” During prosecution CC Serve submitted a letter of protest asserting likely confusion with its prior ASPIRE registration. Despite that protest, the examining attorney approved Apex’s ASPIRE BANK applications for publication, and the marks published in the Official Gazette on December 17, 2019. Publication started the statutory window during which third parties could file oppositions.

## TTAB opposition and Board decision
CC Serve filed an opposition at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, alleging that Apex’s ASPIRE BANK applications were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s existing ASPIRE registration. The Board sustained the opposition and refused Apex’s registrations, concluding that consumer confusion was likely. In reaching that conclusion the Board applied the familiar DuPont factors to evaluate likelihood of confusion and made key factual findings about the similarity of the services and the marks’ competitive landscape.

## Appeal to the Federal Circuit and jurisdiction
Apex appealed the TTAB’s decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit has appellate jurisdiction over TTAB decisions refusing trademark registration under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B). On appeal, Apex challenged the Board’s likelihood-of-confusion analysis—particularly the Board’s treatment of certain DuPont factors—and the Federal Circuit reviewed the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and its factual findings for substantial evidence. The court’s judgment ultimately affirmed the Board in part, vacated parts of its analysis, and remanded for further proceedings.
