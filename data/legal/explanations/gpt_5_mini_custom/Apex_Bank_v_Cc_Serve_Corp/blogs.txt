Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# One Definition of “Similarity” to Rule Them All: Federal Circuit Aligns DuPont Factors in Apex Bank v. CC Serve

## Why this matters
The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a short but important corrective for how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) must treat “similarity” across the multi‑factor DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry. Trademark disputes often turn on how broadly the Board defines the relevant goods or services at the outset, and how consistently it applies that definition when weighing marketplace crowding and commercial strength. Apex clarifies that once the Board has found the parties’ services to be “highly similar” (or even partially legally identical) under the second DuPont factor, it cannot then narrow the universe of third‑party uses considered under the sixth factor to a tighter subset of services—third‑party evidence may be drawn from the same broad class of similar services. The decision reinforces earlier teaching in Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin that the sixth DuPont inquiry looks to similar, not strictly identical, goods or services.

## The court’s reasoning in brief
CC Serve owns a prior registration for the mark ASPIRE for credit‑card services; Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” The Board found the parties’ services highly similar and concluded that confusion was likely, but when it evaluated the sixth DuPont factor—whether there are many similar marks in use on similar services—it confined its review to marks tied expressly to “credit card services,” excluding Aspire‑formative marks used in other banking and financing contexts. The Federal Circuit held that this narrowing was legal error. Because the Board had already determined the parties’ services were highly similar (and even partially legally identical) under factor two, the same broader scope of similarity must govern consideration of third‑party uses under factor six. The court vacated the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis and, because the commercial‑strength inquiry under factor one can turn on the number and nature of third‑party uses, remanded factor one as well. The court affirmed the Board’s finding on factor two and left the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion result to be revisited in light of a corrected analysis.

## Doctrinal harmony across DuPont factors
Apex’s key doctrinal point is internal consistency: DuPont is a composite inquiry, and the Board should not re‑define “similar” on a factor‑by‑factor basis in a way that produces analytical tensions. The Federal Circuit relied on existing precedents—Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin—to restate a core principle: the sixth factor requires examination of marks used on similar, not necessarily identical, goods or services. That principle matters because the sixth factor is one of the principal ways courts and the Board measure a mark’s commercial strength. Limiting third‑party evidence artificially shrinks the universe of competing uses and can overinflate the prior mark’s scope of protection.

This harmonization also tracks the allocation of review: factual findings (e.g., what services are similar) are reviewed for substantial evidence, while legal conclusions (e.g., the proper legal standard for evaluating third‑party uses) are reviewed de novo. Where the Board’s legal framing of the sixth factor was inconsistent with its own factual finding under factor two, the Federal Circuit was right to correct course.

## Practical fallout for litigants and the TTAB
Practitioners should take away two immediate lessons. First, when you win a broad similarity finding on factor two, expect that the Board must apply that breadth when assessing third‑party marketplace crowding under factor six. Applicants and challengers alike should plan third‑party evidence accordingly: once services are found similar, third‑party uses from the full swath of related services are fair game to show weakness (or lack thereof). Second, registrants who want to prevent such evidence from being considered must litigate the contours of similarity early and with precision—showing distinctive trade channels, distinct consumers, or other limits on overlap can justify a narrower universe of comparison.

For Board practice, Apex signals that inconsistent scope‑setting will be an easy reversal point. The TTAB should explain, when it finds similarity on factor two, why it would nevertheless be appropriate to confine third‑party evidence for factor six to a narrower niche—otherwise the Board risks vacatur and remand.

## Limits and open questions
Apex does not rewrite the law to require identical treatment of every DuPont factor in every case. The Federal Circuit was careful: similar goods are sufficient for the sixth factor; the Board still has discretion to find that a particular third‑party use is irrelevant because it is meaningfully different in trade channels, consumers, or branding. The decision also does not guarantee registration for Apex—broadening the set of third‑party uses could cut either way, weakening CC Serve’s claim of distinctiveness or showing that “Aspire” is common across the financial services landscape and thus less likely to confuse.

Finally, the case underscores that many DuPont battles are evidentiary ones. The Board’s characterization of services, and the parties’ framing of the relevant consumer, remain pivotal. Apex simply requires that the Board not have it both ways: it cannot adopt a wide definition of similarity for the services‑overlap inquiry and then cherry‑pick a narrow universe when counting comparable third‑party marks.

In short, Apex reasserts a coherent approach to “similarity” across the DuPont matrix. Practitioners should expect the TTAB—and later courts—to pay close attention to the scope choices made early in an opposition or cancellation, because those choices will shape what evidence is admissible and how commercial strength is measured on remand.



# Counting “ASPIREs”: How Many Third‑Party Uses Make a Mark Weak?

## Why this case matters
Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful reminder that DuPont’s sixth factor—the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services—is not a math problem with a magic cutoff. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) tossed out 42 third‑party “Aspire” uses because they were not described as “credit‑card services,” leaving only nine to weigh against CC Serve’s registered ASPIRE mark. The Federal Circuit reversed that portion of the Board’s decision, vacating the sixth‑factor finding (and, for good measure, the first factor) and remanding for reconsideration consistent with its earlier finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. The court’s ruling reinforces two important principles: (1) the relevant marketplace/scope of services must be defined consistently across DuPont factors, and (2) tribunals should not treat numerical counts of third‑party uses as talismans divorced from their commercial context.

Put simply, this appeal matters because it clarifies how much (and what kind of) third‑party evidence a tribunal must consider when deciding whether a mark is comparatively weak and therefore entitled to a narrower scope of protection.

## What the Federal Circuit did and why
The Board concluded the parties’ services—CC Serve’s credit‑card services and Apex’s banking/financing services—were highly similar (indeed, partly legally identical) under the second DuPont factor. Yet when it came to the sixth factor, the Board restricted its view of third‑party “Aspire” uses to marks specifically tied to “credit card services,” excluding dozens of other Aspire‑formative marks used in the banking and finance space. The Federal Circuit held that was inconsistent and legally erroneous: if the Board treats the services as highly similar in the second‑factor analysis, it must use the same scope when searching for similar third‑party marks under factor six. The Court vacated the Board’s sixth‑factor finding and remanded—also vacating the first‑factor analysis (similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression)—because a reassessment of commercial strength on remand could change the overall commercial impression of the marks.

The opinion does not invent new law; it simply applies settled precedent (Olde Tyme Foods, Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin, etc.) to emphasize consistent scope and to caution against relying on raw counts without attention to the nature and commercialization of the third‑party uses.

## How this fits within DuPont and the case law on third‑party evidence
Three lines of precedent animate the Court’s reasoning and should guide tribunals:

- Palm Bay (396 F.3d 1369) and cases like Jack Wolfskin (797 F.3d 1363) recognize that third‑party registrations and uses can show relative weakness of a mark, but they must be substantial and probative. Mere isolated or limited uses do not automatically erode a registrant’s rights.

- Juice Generation (794 F.3d 1334) underscores that a “crowded” field—many contemporaneous users of similar marks for related goods—can undermine a mark’s exclusivity because consumers learn to distinguish among similar marks.

- Olde Tyme Foods (961 F.2d 200) supports the proposition that the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods; similar goods or services suffice.

Taken together, these precedents require analysis of both conceptual and commercial weakness. Conceptual weakness means the mark is inherently common or descriptive (e.g., a widely used dictionary word). Commercial weakness means the marketplace is populated with similar brands actually used in connection with the relevant goods or services, narrowing the senior user’s practical exclusivity.

The Federal Circuit’s point—that you can’t narrow the universe of relevant third‑party marks after having broadened the services for the second factor—is rooted in this analytic coherence: once you have found the services overlap, the pool of potentially weakening third‑party uses should reflect that overlap.

## What the ruling cautions against: numbers without context
The TTAB’s treatment of the 42 excluded Aspire uses reveals two common missteps:

1. Inconsistent scope: Defining the relevant public narrowly for some DuPont factors but broadly for others invites reversible error. If the Board decides the services overlap for source‑confusion purposes, it should not cabin the sixth‑factor inquiry to a narrower slice of services that undermines that factual finding.

2. Counting as a substitute for analysis: The Board suggested that nine third‑party uses did not amount to a “considerable” number. The Federal Circuit’s remand implicitly reiterates that there is no bright‑line count. Whether a given number of third‑party users is persuasive depends on who is using the mark, how they use it, how visible those uses are, and whether consumers encounter and distinguish among them in the marketplace.

In other words, quality and context matter as much as quantity.

## Practical implications for practitioners and tribunals
For petitioners seeking to show a mark is weak, and for registrants defending against dilution of rights, this decision suggests several best practices.

- Build the marketplace record, not just the register. Third‑party registrations are useful, but evidence of actual use—web pages, ads, press coverage, customer testimonials, screenshots, and sales/market‑share data—carries more weight. Registrations without proof of use are weaker proof of commercial presence.

- Be consistent in defining the relevant services and the consumer. If you argue the services are similar or overlap for likelihood‑of‑confusion reasons, be prepared to demonstrate the breadth of that overlap when you seek to prove widespread third‑party use. Conversely, if a registrant argues the services are dissimilar to minimize third‑party evidence, the tribunal will assess that position against other factor findings.

- Describe the nature of the third‑party uses. Are the third‑party “Aspire” marks national brands with payment processing or online banking footprints that consumers commonly encounter? Or are they niche, local, or dormant registrations and small‑scale uses? Evidence about advertising spend, geographic reach, duration of use, and channels (e.g., co‑branded programs, bank partnerships, mobile apps) will help the factfinder decide whether consumers have been “educated” to distinguish among marks.

- Show contemporaneity and priority where relevant. The usefulness of third‑party uses depends in part on timing—longstanding co‑existence and overlap at relevant times is more probative than fleeting or temporally remote uses.

- Use surveys prudently. Well‑designed consumer surveys can help show whether ordinary consumers encounter and distinguish among similar marks in the relevant marketplace, though surveys must be crafted to reflect the correct universe of consumers and services.

For the TTAB, the lesson is administrative as well as doctrinal: maintain internal consistency in defining the marketplace across DuPont factors, and avoid treating a raw count as dispositive. Explain why particular third‑party uses are dispositive (or not), tying that explanation to consumer exposure and the nature of the services.

## Downstream effects and likely outcomes on remand
What will happen when the Board reconsiders? Vacating the sixth factor does not guarantee a finding of weakness; it simply forces the Board to look at the full set of potentially relevant Aspire uses across banking and finance services and to explain how those uses affect CC Serve’s commercial strength. If the Board finds many Aspire uses that are similar and visible in the same marketplace, CC Serve’s mark may be entitled to a narrower scope of protection, which could weigh against a likelihood of confusion. But if the broader set includes many nominal or remote uses, or if the uses are not meaningfully similar in channels or consumer perception, the Board could still find the mark commercially strong despite more numerically numerous Aspire marks.

The remand also illustrates a practical truth of trademark litigation: outcomes often turn on the depth and texture of the evidentiary record. A party that invests in demonstrating real marketplace overlap and consumer exposure will fare much better than one that relies on a string of registrations or a headcount of marks alone.

## Conclusion
Apex Bank v. CC Serve is a crisp reminder that the sixth DuPont factor is about more than arithmetic. Courts and boards must assess third‑party evidence against the full backdrop of the services and consumers at issue. Numerical tallies of marks can be persuasive, but only if they are tied to the nature and visibility of third‑party use in the relevant marketplace. For practitioners, the bottom line is straightforward: document the marketplace, show how consumers actually encounter brands, and insist on consistency in how you define the services and the relevant public across all DuPont factors.



# Banking on Overlap: Federal Circuit Affirms Broad Similarity Between Credit Cards and “Banking and Financing Services”

## Why this case matters
Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a reminder that in the financial‑services space, labels on a specimen or an applicant’s current business model are not dispositive: courts and the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board will look to how consumers understand services and to industry practice. On appeal the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s core holding that “credit card services” substantially overlap with “banking and financing services,” rejecting an effort by an applicant that does not itself issue cards to escape a prior mark covering ASPIRE. That holding matters for fintechs, internet banks, and other financial‑services applicants who sit adjacent to card issuance or payment servicing through partnerships: even if you don’t issue a card today, an identification that sweeps into “banking and financing” may expose your mark to opposition from earlier users in the credit‑card lane.

## What the court did
The Federal Circuit reviewed the Board’s Section 2(d) likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis. It affirmed the Board’s factual finding under the second DuPont factor that Apex’s identified “banking and financing services” are highly similar to CC Serve’s registered “credit card services.” The court accepted the Board’s construction of “credit card services” (which the Board read broadly to include issuing cards, managing card accounts, processing payments, authorization, monitoring, and related services) and relied on dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations to show the overlap between card services and broader banking/finance activities. The court emphasized that services need not be identical to be “related” under DuPont; a demonstrated market overlap or industry practice linking the services is enough.

However, because the Board had limited its DuPont six (third‑party use) analysis to only those third‑party marks tied to “credit card services,” the Federal Circuit found that error. Having already concluded the services are highly similar (indeed partially legally identical), the Board should have cast a correspondingly broader net when assessing whether numerous third‑party “Aspire” marks weaken CC Serve’s registration. The court therefore vacated the Board’s analysis of DuPont six and, because commercial strength feeds the first DuPont factor, remanded the first‑factor analysis as well.

## Why the deleted ID Manual entry didn’t save Apex
Apex tried to rely on the fact that the PTO’s ID Manual no longer contains a specific entry for “credit card services.” That deletion, the court and the Board showed, does not cabin what a reasonable consumer would understand the services to include. The Board reconstructed the meaning of “credit card services” from dictionary definitions and functionally descriptive evidence and concluded the term encompasses many of the same activities one associates with banking and finance. The Federal Circuit accepted that methodology: absent a limiting, negotiated identification, applicants cannot assume that an ID Manual deletion will shrink the universe of services that a term denotes in DuPont analysis. What matters is the services as identified in the application, how the relevant consuming public perceives them, and the industry context—not the presence or absence of a particular prose entry in the PTO’s Manual.

## How outsourcing and partnership models collapse perceived distinctions
A key factual premise supporting the Board’s conclusion (and endorsed by the Federal Circuit) was industry reality: companies like CC Serve often provide card‑program management and servicing while partnering with chartered banks that formally “issue” the cards. That outsourcing or co‑branding arrangement means the consumer’s impression of who provides “credit card services” blurs into the bank/financing space. The DuPont analysis asks whether marketing or industry practices could create a mistaken belief of common source; where servicers and issuing banks routinely join forces and co‑brand, that risk is real. Thus, an applicant that positions itself as an “internet bank” or other banking service—even if it does not issue cards in‑house—cannot ignore the risk that consumers will associate its services with credit‑card offerings under the same or a similar mark.

## Practical implications for practitioners
Apex offers a few concrete lessons for how to draft and clear marks in the financial‑services realm.

First, draft identifications with an eye toward functional overlap. If your client wants to avoid entanglement with credit‑card marks, do not file a broad “banking and financing services” identification without careful qualifiers. Conversely, if you intend to claim a suite of financial functions, be aware that that breadth will invite overlap arguments.

Second, in prosecution and clearance searches look beyond literal terms. Search for marks and registrations in adjacent service descriptions—payment processing, account servicing, card program management, authorization services, and similar phrases—because the Board and the Federal Circuit will treat those services as related for DuPont purposes.

Third, consider industry structure: if your business relies on partnerships (bank issuers, servicers, network processors), collect evidence of how programs are presented to consumers. That evidence cuts both ways. It can support a finding of relatedness (if you’re facing opposition) or be used defensively (if you can show that consumers clearly distinguish the roles or that branding practices keep sources distinct).

Fourth, third‑party registrations and evidence of crowded use matter. The Board’s error—narrowing the third‑party universe to only marks specifically listed under “credit card services”—was corrected on appeal. Practitioners should therefore marshal cross‑industry evidence showing use of a formative term across banking/finance to argue weakness of a prior mark, and conversely be prepared to counter such evidence.

Finally, consider alternative risk‑management tools: negotiated coexistence agreements, consent letters, disclaimers, licensing arrangements, and careful branding strategies (distinctive logos, trade dress, or house marks that reduce overlap) can help shore up clearance or preserve registration prospects in the face of similar earlier marks.

## What the remand might change (and what it won’t)
On remand the Board must re‑examine DuPont six using an expanded set of third‑party marks tied to banking and financing services broadly, not solely to entries titled “credit card services.” That reassessment could show a broader pattern of Aspire‑formative marks across the financial sector, which in turn could diminish CC Serve’s commercial strength and narrow the scope of protection afforded to ASPIRE. A finding of weakness on DuPont six might alter the first factor’s commercial impression analysis and could ultimately affect the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion outcome.

What the remand will not change, at least absent new evidence, is the Federal Circuit’s acceptance that the services overlap: the court affirmed the factual finding of similarity under DuPont two. So an applicant whose identification sweeps into bank/finance functions remains exposed to oppositions from credit‑card actors unless it can produce compelling evidence on remand that the prior mark is weak in practice or that consumers are unlikely to be confused.

## Bottom line
Apex Bank underscores that in financial services, the law follows the market: servicers, issuing banks, and fintechs operate in an intertwined ecosystem, and trademark analysis reflects that reality. Broad identifications like “banking and financing services” invite scrutiny for overlap with credit‑card services; deleting an ID Manual entry for “credit card services” is no panacea. Practitioners should draft identifications deliberately, search laterally across related service terms, and be ready to put up or rebut evidence about industry practices and third‑party use when clearing and defending marks in the payments and banking space.



## Commercial Impression on a Sliding Scale: Why Factor One Can’t Precede Factor Six

Why this case matters: In Apex Bank v. CC Serve, the Federal Circuit reminded the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) that the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry is not a free‑wheeling jumble of factors but a disciplined exercise in sequencing and consistency. When the Board has already found the parties’ services are highly similar, it cannot cabin the universe of third‑party uses for the sixth DuPont factor to a narrower slice of the marketplace and then proceed to fix the “commercial impression” under the first factor without recalibrating that strength assessment. For marks built around aspirational or commonplace words like ASPIRE, the court’s decision underscores that evidence of a crowded field can materially change whether the shared word dominates consumer perception — and thus how the TTAB should analyze similarity of the marks as a whole.

What the court did: The dispute was straightforward on the facts. CC Serve owns a 1998 registration for ASPIRE for credit‑card services. Apex Bank sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services (it does not currently issue credit cards). The TTAB sustained the opposition, finding the services “highly similar” and concluding that the marks were likely to cause confusion. On appeal the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor (services are highly similar), but vacated and remanded the Board’s treatment of the sixth and first DuPont factors.

The court’s analytical point was precise. The Board had found the parties’ services to be highly similar — indeed “partly legally identical” — and yet, when it reached the sixth factor, it limited its inquiry to third‑party uses of ASPIRE only in connection with credit‑card services. The Federal Circuit rejected that maneuver: once the Board defines the relevant services broadly (in the second‑factor analysis), it must apply that same scope when considering the number and nature of similar marks in use under factor six. Because evidence that a term is widely used in the broader field of banking and finance can weaken the proprietor’s exclusive claim to the word and narrow its scope of protection, that evidence must be considered before fixing the marks’ commercial impression under factor one. The court thus vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding and the related factor‑one analysis so the TTAB can reassess third‑party use across the appropriate field and then reexamine whether ASPIRE or the composite ASPIRE BANK is likely to cause consumer confusion.

Why the sequence matters doctrinally: At root, the decision enforces a logical dependency among DuPont factors. Factor one asks whether the marks’ overall appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression are similar — but what a consumer’s “commercial impression” is depends in large part on the relative commercial strength of the elements that compose the marks. If a shared word has been diluted by numerous third‑party uses across the relevant market, consumers are more likely to focus on distinguishing matter (e.g., BANK, stylization, or logos). Conversely, if the shared word is commercially strong and uncommon in the class of services at issue, that word will dominate commercial impression and push the first‑factor analysis toward the shared element. That dynamic is why the Federal Circuit correctly insisted that the Board calibrate market evidence under factor six before finalizing its factor‑one conclusions.

How this plays out in practice: Consider two stylized ASPIRE marks in the financial sphere. If the record shows dozens of independent financial services, card issuers, and banks using ASPIRE or ASPIRE‑formative marks across banking, lending, and payments, the proprietary owner’s claim to exclusivity in the word will be weak; the first‑factor inquiry should then examine whether the composite forms (ASPIRE BANK vs. ASPIRE with a particular logo) contain sufficient distinguishing matter to avoid confusion. By contrast, if third‑party use of ASPIRE is rare or confined to unrelated goods, the shared term may be entitled to a broader scope, and factor one will place greater emphasis on the shared word’s dominance in the composite marks.

Guidance for litigants and brand owners: For parties policing or defending aspirational words in finance, the decision supplies pragmatic rules of thumb. Owners of marks built on common or laudatory words should assemble affirmative evidence of commercial strength if they want a broad scope — advertising spend, sales figures, length and exclusivity of use, and consumer recognition surveys can help. Applicants who rely on composite matter (e.g., a generic or informing term like BANK, or distinctive designs) should proactively develop evidence showing that the shared word is widely used in the relevant field so the TTAB will accord it narrow protection and focus on the distinctive elements. Third‑party use evidence should reflect the full range of services the Board has already found similar; cherry‑picking only identical applications or a narrow subset of services risks reversal.

What the TTAB should do on remand: The Federal Circuit’s remediation is straightforward. The Board must reassess factor six using the scope of goods and services it already found similar under factor two. That means considering all third‑party uses of ASPIRE across banking and finance services that are within the established scope. After that reassessment, the Board must reweigh the commercial strength or weakness of the ASPIRE element and then revisit the first DuPont factor with that calibrated picture in mind. The Board should explicitly explain why, given the breadth (or scarcity) of third‑party use, the consumer’s likely commercial impression either centers on the shared word or pivots to distinguishing matter such as “BANK” or design elements.

Downstream effects and limitations: The opinion does not rewrite DuPont — the Board still has discretion to elect which DuPont factors are relevant and how to weigh them — but it curbs analytic inconsistency. It’s a reminder that findings on one factor cannot be treated as isolated islands; factual findings about the services’ similarity constrain later comparisons of third‑party usage and commercial strength. The ruling will be especially consequential in financial‑services disputes, where aspirational, laudatory, or generic words (aspire, elevate, summit, vault, etc.) are frequently reused across products and providers. Practitioners should expect more rigorous proof contests over the breadth of third‑party use and commercial recognition, and the Board should be ready to reconcile its factor determinations in a single, coherent narrative.

Conclusion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve is a tidy correction with broad implications. It confirms that DuPont is not merely an enumerative checklist; the Board must sequence its inquiry so that market strength (factor six) informs the assessment of what consumers actually take away from a mark (factor one). For brands that depend on common or aspirational words, the lesson is clear: either gather the evidence that proves your term is uniquely associated with you, or accept that the market’s crowdedness will shift the analytic focus to whatever distinctive matter remains.
