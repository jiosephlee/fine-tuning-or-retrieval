Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why did the court vacate the first DuPont factor based on potential changes to commercial impression from the sixth factor?
Question:
The panel ties commercial impression under the first factor to the outcome of the sixth (strength/weakness). Is this linkage doctrinally required? Under what circumstances does evidence of third-party use alter the perceived commercial impression of an otherwise similar mark, and how should the Board articulate that interplay on remand?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel vacated the first DuPont factor because its treatment of the sixth factor (third‑party use) could change the mark’s commercial strength, and commercial strength is part of the “commercial impression” inquiry under the first factor. The linkage is not a rule that the first factor must always be re‑decided whenever the sixth is; rather, it follows sound DuPont doctrine: evidence that a field is “crowded” (or not) affects how distinctively a mark reads to consumers, which in turn can change the weight and result of the first‑factor comparison. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., No. 2023‑2143 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) explains that point and remands the Board to reassess both factors consistently.

Why the court vacated the first factor
- The Sixth DuPont factor asks whether there are “a number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods,” and that evidence is probative of a mark’s commercial strength (weakness or strength). As the opinion explains, “Evidence of third‑party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” (Opinion § III.B (citing Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin).)
- The First DuPont factor asks for the marks’ “appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” The court tied the two because commercial strength (what consumers have been trained to expect and distinguish) is an element of commercial impression. The panel put it plainly: “Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.” (Opinion § III.C.)
- Put simply: if reconsideration of third‑party use shows the mark is weaker (or stronger) than the Board found, that changes how the mark is perceived in the marketplace and thus can change the first‑factor outcome—so both must be re‑examined consistently on remand.

Is the linkage doctrinally required?
- There is no mechanical rule that whenever the sixth factor is reweighed the first factor must automatically be vacated. But doctrinally, it is proper and often necessary to revisit the first factor when the evidence bearing on the mark’s strength changes. The court invoked established precedent that third‑party use bears directly on strength and consumer sophistication/education (Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin), and that commercial impression is what the first factor measures (DuPont, I.AM.Symbolic). When the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis is materially altered, the Board should reassess whether that altered view of strength changes its view of commercial impression and likelihood of confusion.

When third‑party use alters commercial impression (practical guide drawn from the opinion)
- Scope overlap: The third‑party uses must be in the same relevant field or services the Board has found to be similar. The opinion faulted the Board for narrowing the universe to only “credit card services” after it had already found the parties’ services “highly similar” (partially legally identical) in banking/finance. The Board should use the same scope it adopted in factor two when deciding which third‑party marks are “similar.” (Opinion § III.B.)
- Quantity and quality of uses: A few isolated third‑party uses (the Board here counted nine) may not demonstrate a crowded field; many or “voluminous” examples (the opinion cites prior cases treating 14 or 26 examples as significant) can support a finding of weakness. The Board must explain why the number it finds is or is not “considerable.” (Opinion § III.B, citing Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation.)
- Consumer education: If third‑party use is widespread and in overlapping channels, it supports the conclusion that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing similar marks — i.e., the mark has narrower protection and a different commercial impression (less likely to indicate single source).
- Nature of the marks: Similarity in formative word or distinctive elements matters. The opinion addressed “Aspire” and “Aspire‑formative” marks across financial services; the Board should explain which elements of the third‑party marks are probative of strength/weakness.

How the Board should articulate the interplay on remand
- Use a consistent definition of the relevant services/public. Because the Board found the parties’ services “highly similar” in factor two, it must use that same scope in factor six (the court explicitly instructs this). (Opinion § III.B.)
- List and justify the universe of third‑party marks considered. Identify which marks were included or excluded and why (service overlap, nature of use, geographic/consumer channels, bona fide use vs. registrations only).
- Explain both quantity and quality. Say whether the number of similar marks and the nature of their use is sufficient to support a finding of weakness, citing the standard and precedent and explaining how the examples compare to past “considerable” or “voluminous” proofs.
- Connect strength to commercial impression. If the sixth‑factor reassessment changes the Board’s view of CC Serve’s mark as relatively weak or strong, explain concretely how that change affects the first‑factor analysis of appearance, sound, connotation, and—critically—commercial impression and likelihood of confusion. The Board should state whether the apparent similarity between marks is mitigated (or amplified) by the field’s crowdedness and why.
- Make evidentiary findings with reasons. The Board must tie each factual finding to the record (what exhibits show, whether use is for credit cards vs. broader banking, etc.) and then state the legal conclusion about the weight to assign in the DuPont calculus.

Bottom line
The panel’s vacation of the first factor flows from settled DuPont reasoning: third‑party use (sixth factor) is probative of a mark’s commercial strength, and strength is part of the commercial impression the first factor measures. When the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis may materially change the mark’s perceived commercial strength, the Board should reexamine and explain the first factor in light of that revised assessment — using the same scope of services, listing the third‑party marks considered, and tying the strength finding to a clear explanation of how the commercial impression (and thus likelihood of confusion) is affected. The Apex opinion instructs precisely that approach.
