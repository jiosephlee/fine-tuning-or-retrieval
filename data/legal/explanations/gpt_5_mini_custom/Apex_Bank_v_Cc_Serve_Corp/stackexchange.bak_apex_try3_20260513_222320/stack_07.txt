Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Who bears the burden on the sixth DuPont factor to prove a mark’s strength or weakness?
Question:
The Board said CC Serve had not shown particular commercial strength, but also that Apex had not shown the mark was weak, and thus gave ASPIRE the “normal” scope of protection. Is it correct to treat the absence of proof either way as defaulting to normal strength? Do Federal Circuit cases assign a burden of production/persuasion on the opposer or applicant regarding marketplace weakness under factor six?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit in Apex did not adopt a special, litigant‑shifting rule for DuPont factor six. The opinion does not say that either party has the ultimate legal burden to prove marketplace weakness; instead, the Board must evaluate the evidence of third‑party use/registration (the “number and nature of similar marks”) against the properly defined scope of goods/services. If a party wants the mark narrowly construed as weak, it must produce the persuasive third‑party evidence; if neither party presents such evidence the Board may (factually) treat the mark as entitled to a normal scope of protection — but only after analyzing the correct universe of comparable uses. See Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., Section III.B (J.A. 47, 51–52).

What the opinion says about burdens and how to apply factor six
- DuPont factor six asks the trier of fact to consider “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods,” and third‑party use is relevant to showing weakness. Apex cites Palm Bay, Juice Generation, and Jack Wolfskin for this principle (J.A. 46–47).  
- The court treats each DuPont factor as a question of fact (DuPont is a fact‑intensive inquiry) and reviews the Board’s factual findings for substantial evidence (see Part II and DuPont discussion). The Apex opinion does not reallocate a different legal burden of persuasion for factor six; it reviews whether the Board correctly considered the relevant evidence and applied the correct scope of “similar goods/services.” (See J.A. 27–28, 47–48, 51–52.)
- The Board in this case found “CC Serve has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak” and therefore gave the mark the “normal scope of protection” (J.A. 51). The Federal Circuit did not reverse that approach as a per se error; it vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding only because the Board improperly narrowed the universe of third‑party uses to credit‑card services despite having found the parties’ services “highly similar” (and partially legally identical) in its factor‑two analysis (J.A. 27–28, 47–49). The court remanded for reconsideration of factor six with the correct scope of similar goods/services (J.A. 49–52).

Practical rule of thumb (from Apex)
- There is no magic presumption that “no evidence = narrow protection” or vice versa created by Apex. Rather:
  - The party asserting marketplace weakness should present robust third‑party evidence (registrations and evidence of use) covering the properly defined class of goods/services. The court noted that a “considerable” number and nature of such marks (e.g., Juice Generation’s many examples or Jack Wolfskin’s voluminous evidence) support a finding of weakness (J.A. 47–48).  
  - If neither party presents convincing third‑party evidence, the Board may reasonably conclude the mark is entitled to its normal scope — but that conclusion must rest on a correct definition of the relevant market and substantial evidence. Apex vacated the Board’s weight on factor six not because the Board adopted “normal scope” when evidence was sparse, but because the Board applied the wrong universe of comparable uses (J.A. 49–52).

Bottom line
Apex does not reassign a formal burden of persuasion to one side for factor six. The practical burden of producing evidence of weakness falls on the party who wishes to show weakness (typically the later user/applicant), and the Board must weigh whatever third‑party evidence exists against the properly defined scope of goods/services. If the Board errs in defining that scope or ignores relevant third‑party uses, the Federal Circuit will remand for reconsideration (as it did here).
