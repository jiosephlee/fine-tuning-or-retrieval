Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Was it proper for the Board to interpret the legacy identification “credit card services” using dictionaries after the ID Manual entry was deleted?
Question:
Because the Trademark ID Manual entry for “credit card services” was deleted after CC Serve’s 1998 registration, the Board resorted to dictionary sources to define the scope and then found Apex’s services legally identical in part. Is there a standard for construing the scope of legacy identifications—e.g., based on the identification’s ordinary meaning at the time of registration, current meanings, PTO guidance, or extrinsic evidence? Did the court implicitly endorse the Board’s interpretive method by affirming factor two?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — the Board’s use of dictionary definitions (and related evidence) to construe a deleted Trademark ID Manual entry was permissible in this case, and the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s DuPont factor two finding because that construction was supported by substantial evidence. But the court did not announce a categorical rule about which sources (contemporaneous vs. current meanings, PTO guidance, extrinsic evidence) must control when construing “legacy” identifications; its decision is fact‑specific and rests on the Board’s explanation and the evidentiary record.

Why (and what standard the court applied)
- DuPont/factual-legal framework. The likelihood-of-confusion inquiry is legal, based on factual findings under the DuPont factors. Factual findings by the Board are reviewed for substantial evidence; legal conclusions are reviewed de novo. (Opinion §II; citing M2 Software, Consol. Edison.) 
- How the Board construed the ID here. The Board confronted a deleted Trademark ID Manual entry for “credit card services” and “first determined the meaning of the identified services,” relying on dictionary definitions and other evidence to conclude that “credit card services” included issuance, processing, account management, counseling, monitoring, etc. (Op. at J.A. 25–28.) The Board then compared that scope to Apex’s “banking and financing services” and concluded the services were “legally identical, in part.” (J.A. 27–28.)
- Why the court affirmed factor two. The Federal Circuit affirmed because “the Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services, and substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (Op. at §III.A, citing J.A. 27–28 and the Board’s analysis.) In short, the court treated the Board’s construction as a factual finding supported by substantial evidence and accepted the resulting similarity conclusion.

What the opinion does and does not hold about methodology
- What it endorses (implicitly): In this case, using dictionaries and related evidence to give meaning to a deleted ID entry was an acceptable fact-finding method so long as the Board explains its reasoning and the record supports the finding. The court’s affirmation of factor two operates as deference to the Board’s factbound interpretation and evidentiary choices.
- What it does not endorse: The court did not adopt a general rule that dictionary definitions always control, nor did it say whether the controlling meaning must be the ordinary meaning at the time of registration or the current meaning. The opinion is silent on a temporal rule and does not prioritize a particular source (ID Manual, contemporaneous PTO guidance, dictionaries, or extrinsic evidence) as the exclusive correct approach.
- Consistency constraint the court enforced: The court faulted the Board elsewhere for being inconsistent — it found the Board’s narrowing of the universe of third‑party marks under DuPont factor six to be legally flawed because the Board had already found the services “highly similar” under factor two and then limited consideration to only credit‑card services. That underscores that whatever interpretive method the Board uses, it must apply a consistent scope across the DuPont analysis. (See §III.B, J.A. 47–51.)

Practical takeaway for parsing legacy IDs
- The Board may rely on dictionaries, PTO records (including the ID Manual when available), third‑party registrations, and other extrinsic evidence to construe a deleted or otherwise ambiguous identification — but it must (1) explain its reasoning, (2) ground its construction in the record, and (3) apply that construction consistently across DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit will accept that approach when the Board’s findings are supported by substantial evidence, but it will not bless an inconsistent or unexplained methodology.

Bottom line
The court affirmed the Board’s factor two finding because the Board’s dictionary‑based construction of “credit card services” was reasonable and supported by the record. That affirmation is case‑specific deference to the Board’s factual findings; it is not a precedential rule that dictionaries must always be used or that meanings must be fixed at the time of registration.
