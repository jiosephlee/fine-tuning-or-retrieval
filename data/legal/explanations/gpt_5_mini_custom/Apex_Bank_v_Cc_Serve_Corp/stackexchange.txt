Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why was the Board’s narrowing of DuPont factor six treated as a legal error rather than a factual one?
Question:
The opinion says legal conclusions are reviewed de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. Likelihood of confusion is a legal question with factual underpinnings. Here, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s factor-two factual finding (services are highly similar) but vacated factor six because the Board considered only third-party marks for credit card services and excluded other banking/financing uses. Is the mistake characterized as legal because it applied the wrong legal standard for 'similar goods' under factor six, even though the underlying evidence was factual? How do courts decide when an error in the DuPont framework is legal (scope/standard) versus factual (weight of evidence)?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the Board didn’t just weigh the third‑party evidence differently — it applied the wrong legal scope for “similar” goods under DuPont factor six. The Federal Circuit treated that as a legal error (reviewed de novo) because the Board imposed a narrower legal standard on factor six than it had applied under factor two and than governing precedent allows.

Why: the governing review rules and the opinion
- As the opinion restates, likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion based on underlying factual findings under the DuPont factors. Legal conclusions are reviewed de novo; factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382; Consol. Edison, 305 U.S. at 229 (J.A. 12–13).
- Each DuPont factor is usually a factual inquiry, but an appellate court will treat as legal any error that consists of mis-defining or mis-applying the legal standard that governs how those facts are to be considered. See the court’s statement that the Board’s narrowing “was an error” because the sixth factor “does not require identical goods—only similar ones.” (Opinion at J.A. 6–8).

How that applies here (what the Board did and why that was legal error)
- The Board found, as a factual matter under DuPont factor two, that Apex’s “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s “credit card services” are highly similar (indeed “legally identical, in part”). (J.A. 27–28).
- Despite that factual finding, when analyzing factor six the Board limited the universe of third‑party marks it would consider to marks used on “credit card services” only and excluded marks used in other banking/finance contexts. (J.A. 47).
- The Federal Circuit held this was not merely a disagreement over weight or sufficiency of third‑party evidence but a legal mistake: the Board adopted a more stringent legal definition of “similar” for factor six than it applied under factor two and than precedent permits. Because the alleged error was a mis‑application of the legal standard (scope of marks to be considered), the Court vacated factor six and remanded. (J.A. 6–8).

How to decide legal vs factual errors in DuPont contexts (rule of thumb from the opinion)
- If the challenger asserts the Board used the wrong legal standard, applied inconsistent standards across factors, or misinterpreted controlling precedent about what the factor requires — that is a legal issue for de novo review. That is what happened here: an improperly narrowed legal definition of “similar” marks under factor six.
- If the challenger attacks whether the record contains enough third‑party uses, whether particular exhibits were persuasive, or whether a particular factual finding is supported by the evidence — those are factual issues for substantial‑evidence review.
- Put differently: ask whether the purported error is an issue of law (how to define/apply the factor) or an issue of fact (did the evidence support the Board’s application of the factor under the correct legal standard). The opinion treats the Board’s narrowing of the factor as the former, not the latter. (See discussion at J.A. 33–34; J.A. 47–51).

Bottom line: The Court vacated factor six because the Board legally narrowed the universe of “similar” marks it would consider in a way that was inconsistent with its own factual finding of highly similar services and with precedent holding factor six requires only similarity, not identity. That is a legal error (wrong standard/scope), not merely a dispute over the weight of the third‑party evidence.

### Under DuPont factor six, must the TTAB consider third-party "Aspire" marks used for broader banking/financing services, not just credit cards?
Question:
The Board defined the relevant public for factor six as consumers of 'credit card services' and treated other uses of Aspire for banking/financing as 'essentially irrelevant.' The Federal Circuit said that was too narrow, especially since factor two had already found the parties’ services 'highly similar' and even 'legally identical, in part.' Is there a rule that the 'similarity' scope used under factor two should carry over to factor six? In practice, how broadly should 'similar goods and services' be drawn in financial services when the identifications include 'banking and financing services' on one side and 'credit card services' on the other?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Under the Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve, when the Board has already found the parties’ services to be “highly similar” (or partially “legally identical”) under DuPont factor two, it must use that same scope of similarity when deciding which third‑party “Aspire” (or Aspire‑formative) marks are “on similar goods” for DuPont factor six. The sixth factor requires consideration of marks used on similar — not identical — goods, so third‑party uses tied to broader “banking and financing services” cannot be excluded simply because the opponent’s registration is limited to “credit card services.” (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025, No. 2023‑2143.)

Why (the legal rule and doctrine)
- DuPont factor six asks about “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” Evidence of third‑party use shows whether a mark is relatively weak and thus entitled to a narrower scope of protection. The governing principle is that the goods/services need only be similar, not identical. (Apex citing DuPont §6; Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation; Palm Bay.)  
- In Apex the Board had already found the services “highly similar” and partly “legally identical” under factor two (credit card services overlap with “banking”/“finance” insofar as both involve issuing credit, managing accounts, etc.). The Federal Circuit held it was error for the Board then to narrow its factor‑six inquiry to consumers of only “credit card services” and to ignore third‑party Aspire uses in the broader banking/finance field. The Court reasoned there is no reason to apply a different or more stringent similarity standard under factor six than the Board applied under factor two; thus the same scope should be retained. (Apex, J.A. 27–28, 47–51.)

Practical application in financial‑services cases
- Start with the Board’s factor‑two finding: define the universe of “similar” services according to that factual finding. If factor two found credit card services overlap with banking/financing (e.g., issuing cards, account servicing, extending credit), the relevant universe for factor six includes third‑party marks used on those banking/finance services. (Apex, J.A. 25–28, 33–34, 47.)  
- Don’t exclude third‑party registrations/uses merely because they are not labeled exactly as “credit card services.” If the third party’s service falls within the scope the Board already found similar (e.g., credit issuance, account management, payment processing, loans), it is relevant for factor six. (Apex: “we find the Board’s definition of similarity to be too narrow.”)  
- The Board still evaluates the quantity and nature of third‑party uses: it may conclude that a handful of third‑party uses does not show weakness, whereas a crowded field does. Apex vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding so the Board can reconsider the number/nature of third‑party Aspire uses once the proper scope is applied. (Apex, J.A. 47–51; Jack Wolfskin / Juice Generation discussion cited therein.)  
- Because factor six bears on commercial strength, and commercial strength affects the marks’ overall commercial impression, a re‑run of factor six may require reconsideration of factor one as well — exactly what the Court directed in Apex. (Apex, remand instructions.)

Bottom line for counsel and the Board
- If factor two finds substantial overlap between credit‑card services and broader banking/financing services, the Board must treat third‑party uses of the same formative (e.g., “Aspire”) across that broader banking/finance scope as relevant under factor six. The inquiry remains whether those third‑party marks are used on services “similar” to the parties’ services (similar — not identical), and the Board must reassess factor six (and, if necessary, factor one) accordingly. (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., No. 2023‑2143.)

### How many third-party uses are enough to show a mark is weak, and do registrations without proof of use count?
Question:
The Board found nine Aspire-formative uses for credit card services and deemed that insufficient to show commercial weakness, contrasting cases like Juice Generation (26 third-party marks) and Jack Wolfskin (voluminous evidence). Is there any quantitative benchmark for 'considerable' or 'ubiquitous' third-party use, or is it context dependent? Also, does the evidence have to show actual marketplace use, or can third-party registrations alone meaningfully weaken a mark? On remand, if the TTAB includes broader banking/financing uses, what quality and type of third-party evidence typically moves the needle from 'normal' to 'weak' commercial strength?
Answer:
Short answer
- There is no fixed numerical benchmark. The Federal Circuit in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. treated the question as fact‑ and context‑dependent: nine third‑party Aspire uses for credit‑card services were held insufficient by the Board, while earlier cases called 26 marks “a considerable number” (Juice Generation) or relied on “voluminous” evidence (Jack Wolfskin). See Apex, J.A.47 (discussing nine uses and comparing Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin).
- Registrations can be considered (the Board used third‑party registrations to show the kinds of services that may emanate from a single source, J.A.33–34), but the persuasive weight comes from evidence of actual marketplace use and consumer exposure. In Apex the Board relied on exhibits showing third‑party uses (J.A.40–46); the Federal Circuit’s discussion treats use/market presence as the relevant indicia of commercial weakness (citing Palm Bay, Jack Wolfskin, Juice Generation).

Why there’s no bright line
- The opinion explicitly treats the sixth DuPont factor as a mixed quantitative/qualitative inquiry and compares the particular factual showings in different cases. Juice Generation referred to 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number”; Jack Wolfskin relied on “voluminous evidence” and highlighted multiple notable examples; by contrast the Board here found nine credit‑card uses inadequate (J.A.47). The court therefore applies a context‑sensitive test rather than a numeric rule.

What courts/boards look for (the relevant doctrinal considerations)
- Number of independent third‑party users/marks in the relevant market (quantity matters, but what counts as “considerable” depends on the field). Apex rejected the Board’s myopic counting of only credit‑card listings when it had already found the services highly similar; the Board should consider third‑party marks across banking/financing if the services are highly similar (J.A.33–34, 47).
- Nature and quality of use: evidence that consumers encounter the third‑party marks in commerce—websites, specimens, advertising, product labels, press coverage, long‑standing use—supports a finding of commercial weakness (Apex relied on exhibits of third‑party use, J.A.40–46; the opinion cites Palm Bay, Jack Wolfskin, and Juice Generation approvingly for the proposition that evidence of consumer education and “crowded” fields reduces protection).
- Breadth of channels, geographic reach, and longevity—showing multiple, independent market actors using the same formative mark across the same or closely related services weighs more heavily than a handful of isolated or fleeting uses.
- Distinctiveness of the formative element in context—if numerous third parties use the same formative in similar services, consumers are more likely to distinguish among sources, reducing a senior mark’s commercial strength (the Apex opinion explains this theory and the relevance of third‑party use, J.A.47 (citing Palm Bay and Juice Generation)).

Do registrations without proof of use count?
- The opinion shows that registrations were considered for assessing whether services may emanate from a single source (the Board looked at third‑party registrations in analyzing the second DuPont factor, J.A.33–34). But when the inquiry is commercial weakness under the sixth DuPont factor the opinion emphasizes actual third‑party use evidence (Apex compiled exhibits showing third‑party uses, J.A.40–46), and the cases the court cites (Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin) derive their force from demonstrations that the field is crowded in consumers’ experience. In short: registrations can be relevant to some DuPont inquiries, but the stronger way to show commercial weakness is proof that third parties actually use the marks in the marketplace and that consumers have been exposed to them.

What to submit on remand to move the needle from “normal” to “weak”
Based on Apex and the cases it cites, the TTAB (and a reviewing court) will be persuaded by a mix of quantity and quality. On remand, if the Board expands its universe to include broader banking/financing uses, Apex should consider submitting:
- More examples—many independent third parties using Aspire/Aspire‑formative marks across banking/financing services (not just credit cards), approaching the scale courts have treated as “considerable” (cf. Juice Generation’s 26; Jack Wolfskin’s “voluminous” record).
- Clear, consumer‑facing specimens of actual use for each third party (web pages, card images, account statements, advertising, trade‑press articles) showing the mark in its marketplace context.
- Evidence of channel overlap and common consumers (show that the same customers would encounter those third‑party marks in the same purchasing or decision contexts).
- Geographic and temporal breadth (longstanding or widespread uses, not single small local uses).
- Documentation of independent ownership and independent commercial presence (to avoid a showing of a single family of related users).
- If available, consumer survey or testimony showing that consumers have encountered multiple Aspire marks and distinguish them—this is not required but can be highly persuasive where the question is whether the field is “crowded” and consumers have been educated to differentiate marks.

Bottom line
- No numeric bright line; the inquiry is contextual. Apex confirms that the Board must consider third‑party marks beyond a narrow subcategory when services have been found highly similar (J.A.27–28, 33–34, 47), and that a relatively small cluster of uses (nine here) may be insufficient where other cases treated larger, more robust showings as dispositive. Registrations help show classes of services, but the persuasive power for weakening a mark lies in demonstrable marketplace use and consumer exposure across the relevant field.

### How can the TTAB define 'credit card services' after the ID Manual entry was deleted, and what sources govern interpreting legacy identifications?
Question:
The Board noted that 'credit card services' was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s 1998 registration, then relied on dictionary definitions to construe the scope of 'credit card services' (issuing cards, processing payments, authorization, monitoring, account management, etc.). What is the legal basis for construing identification language when the ID Manual entry no longer exists or has changed—does the TTAB look to ordinary meaning at the time of registration, current ordinary meaning, the TMEP, or other interpretive sources? Are there precedents on interpreting legacy identifications when official taxonomy has shifted?
Answer:
Short answer
The TTAB may (and here did) construe an identification even if the Trademark ID Manual entry that once tracked that term has been deleted or changed. In Apex Bank v. CC Serve the Board, faced with a deleted ID Manual entry for “credit card services,” defined the phrase by reference to dictionary definitions and to evidence of industry practice in third‑party registrations, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that factual approach as supported by substantial evidence for the DuPont relatedness inquiry. (See J.A. 25, 27–28, 33–34.)

What the opinion establishes (legal doctrine and standards)
- Role of the Board: The Board must determine the scope of the applicant/registrant’s identified goods or services as a factual predicate to the DuPont relatedness analysis. Because CC Serve’s ID Manual entry had been deleted after its 1998 registration, the Board “first determined the meaning of the identified services” (J.A. 25) and then assessed relatedness under the second DuPont factor (J.A. 24–28).
- Permissible interpretive sources: In doing so the Board relied on ordinary‑language sources (dictionary definitions) to construe “credit card services” (the Board’s definition appears at J.A. 25), and it also relied on third‑party registrations and evidence of industry practice showing that credit‑card and banking/finance services may emanate from a single source (J.A. 33–34). The Federal Circuit treated those items as proper evidentiary bases for the Board’s factual finding and affirmed the Board’s relatedness conclusion because it was supported by substantial evidence (see review standards at J.A. 2–3 (citing M2 Software and Consolidated Edison) and the affirmance of the second DuPont factor at pp. 4–5).
- Appellate review: The court reiterates the familiar standard — legal conclusions de novo, factual findings for substantial evidence (J.A. 2–3) — so the Board’s choice of interpretive aids will be upheld if they are adequate evidentiary support for its factual conclusions.

What the opinion does not hold (and therefore what it does not decide)
- Timing of “ordinary meaning”: The opinion records that the entry was deleted after registration and then shows the Board using dictionary definitions, but it does not announce a rule that meaning must be fixed as of the date of registration (or, alternatively, as of the date of the proceeding). The opinion therefore does not adopt a time‑of‑registration requirement nor say that the Board must use only contemporaneous dictionaries or TMEP guidance.
- Reliance on the TMEP or ID Manual as dispositive: The opinion does not cite the TMEP or treat ID Manual entries as conclusive. Instead the Board looked to other evidentiary sources (dictionaries, third‑party registrations); the Federal Circuit accepted that approach in this case. The opinion does not establish that the TMEP/ID Manual controls where the Manual has been changed after issuance of a registration.

Practical implications / how this plays out in practice
- If an ID Manual entry has been deleted or altered, expect the Board to construe the identification using ordinary‑language evidence (dictionaries), contemporaneous industry evidence (third‑party registrations, evidence of use), and other admissible evidence of how the services are understood and marketed. Apex Bank shows that such sources are probative and can support a finding of relatedness (J.A. 25, 27–28, 33–34).
- Because the Board’s factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence, parties who want a narrower (or broader) reading of a legacy identification should introduce direct, contemporaneous evidence of meaning or usage (e.g., definitions in use at the time of registration, contemporaneous industry materials, specimen evidence, third‑party use) to counter dictionary or third‑party registration evidence.
- Interpretive consequences carry through the DuPont analysis: how the Board defines the services will affect what third‑party marks it considers (the Federal Circuit vacated and remanded the sixth‑factor analysis here because the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party marks based on its service definition — see J.A. 47, 33–34, 6–8).

Bottom line
Apex Bank shows that when the ID Manual no longer supplies a usable taxonomy, the TTAB may (and did) construe the legacy identification using ordinary‑language definitions and evidence of industry practice and third‑party registrations. The Federal Circuit accepted that approach on substantial‑evidence review for the DuPont relatedness question, but the opinion does not lay down a categorical rule about whether meaning must be fixed at the date of registration or about mandatory reliance on the TMEP/ID Manual.

### Does adding the word 'BANK' to ASPIRE materially change the commercial impression for the first DuPont factor?
Question:
Apex’s marks are ASPIRE BANK (word and design) versus CC Serve’s ASPIRE. The first DuPont factor analysis was vacated because the sixth factor could affect assessments of commercial strength and overall impression. Independently of strength, how much weight does the addition of a descriptive or generic term like 'BANK' typically carry in comparing marks? Would the presence of 'BANK' be discounted as merely descriptive of services, or could it meaningfully alter appearance, sound, connotation, or commercial impression in the financial-services context?
Answer:
Short answer: The addition of the word “BANK” is not inert — it is part of the marks’ “entireties” and can change appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression — but it is not dispositive by itself. Under Apex Bank v. CC Serve the Board must evaluate “ASPIRE” versus “ASPIRE BANK” in context (including the parties’ overlapping services and evidence of third‑party use), and whether “BANK” should be given diminished weight as descriptive is a factual determination that bears on the first DuPont factor.

Why (grounded in the opinion)
- The opinion reiterates the governing standard for the first DuPont factor: compare the marks “in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” (Slip op. § III.C (citing DuPont).) That requires treating “BANK” as part of Apex’s asserted mark when assessing overall commercial impression.
- At the same time the court vacated the Board’s first‑factor analysis because the sixth factor (third‑party uses) might change assessment of the mark’s commercial strength and thus the overall impression. The court explained that third‑party evidence may show a mark is weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection, and that a different finding on factor six could affect the first factor on remand. (Slip op. § III.B & § III.C.)
- The opinion therefore frames two interrelated points you should keep in mind: (1) “BANK” must be considered in the marks’ entireties, and (2) the weight given to “BANK” depends in part on the mark’s commercial strength as developed by the record.

How that plays out, practically
- If “BANK” is truly descriptive/generic of the services (i.e., denotes the nature of the services to the relevant consumers), the Board may give it less source‑identifying weight and focus more on “ASPIRE” when comparing the marks’ commercial impressions. The opinion’s remand of the first factor in light of the sixth factor invites precisely this kind of inquiry: whether third‑party use of “Aspire” has made “ASPIRE” weak or commonplace such that the generic/descriptive matter (“BANK”) should be discounted.
- If, however, “BANK” contributes a distinct commercial message (for example, it steers the consumer’s expectation toward a bank’s services, or signals a different source relationship), it can meaningfully alter appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The opinion’s factual background is instructive: Apex is a retail bank and plans to use “ASPIRE BANK” for banking/financing services, while CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark is used on credit‑card/credit services in partnership with banks. Given the court’s finding that the services are highly similar, a consumer could reasonably perceive ASPIRE BANK as a banking arm or brand related to CC Serve’s ASPIRE credit‑card program — so the addition of “BANK” might not avoid confusion and could in fact reinforce a common commercial impression.
- The bottom line from the opinion: whether “BANK” is discounted or regarded as significant is a factual judgment the Board must make on remand after considering third‑party uses and the breadth (or weakness) of CC Serve’s claim in the marketplace. The court’s vacatur signals that this assessment can materially change the first‑factor outcome.

What the Board should examine on remand (directed by the opinion)
- Treat “ASPIRE” and “ASPIRE BANK” as whole marks for the appearance/sound/connotation analysis, but ask how much consumer perception is driven by the generic/descriptive element “BANK.”
- Reassess the sixth DuPont factor across the full, properly scoped universe of similar financial/banking/credit‑card marks (the court faulted the Board for narrowing to credit‑card marks only). If “ASPIRE” is shown to be common/weak, the Board may narrow its protection and discount generic matter more heavily in the first‑factor comparison.
- Consider the parties’ actual uses and the overlap of services (the court found the services “highly similar”), because close service overlap affects whether adding “BANK” will meaningfully distinguish the commercial impressions to relevant consumers.

Concise conclusion
The presence of “BANK” can be given less weight if it is simply descriptive/generic, but it cannot be ignored — it may still alter the marks’ commercial impression (positively or negatively) depending on the record. Apex Bank v. CC Serve requires the Board to make those factual determinations (particularly after reexamining third‑party use) because they materially affect the first DuPont factor.

### On remand, can the TTAB reopen the record to accept new evidence on third-party use and commercial strength?
Question:
The Federal Circuit vacated the TTAB’s findings on DuPont factors six and one and remanded for reconsideration. Is the Board limited to the evidentiary record as it stood, or may it accept additional evidence (e.g., more third-party uses or marketplace context) on remand in an inter partes opposition? What rules govern reopening the record after a Federal Circuit remand in a Section 2(d) case, and does the nature of the identified legal error (scope of similarity) affect whether new evidence is permitted or required?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The Federal Circuit’s remand in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. requires the Board to reexamine DuPont factors six and one using the correct scope of similarity, and that reconsideration can (and, if the existing record is inadequate, should) include additional third‑party and marketplace evidence. The opinion itself makes clear the Board misapplied the scope of “similar” marks and remanded for reconsideration “in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar,” so the Board must consider a broader universe of third‑party uses and the effect that consideration has on the mark’s commercial strength and overall commercial impression.

Why — legal standard and what the opinion says
- DuPont factor six looks to “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” to show relative weakness or narrow scope of protection. Apex relies on that principle (citing Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin, etc.).
- The Federal Circuit held that the Board “restricted the universe of marks it considered to only those relating to credit card services and excluded marks related to other banking and financing services,” and that was error because the Board had already found the parties’ services “highly similar” (Part III.B). The court therefore vacated factor six and remanded “for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.”
- The court also vacated factor one because “reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression,” so the Board must reassess the marks’ appearance/sound/connotation/ commercial impression in light of any revised analysis of weakness (Part III.C).

What that means for the record on remand
- The remand’s substance (vacatur and instruction to reconsider with a broader scope) necessarily contemplates that the Board consider third‑party uses beyond the narrow credit‑card‑only subset it relied on. If marks or uses already in the original record (for example, Apex’s asserted 42 Aspire‑formative uses) were excluded by the Board’s erroneous scope, the Board must now consider them.
- If the existing administrative record does not contain adequate evidence to apply the correct scope or to assess commercial strength and consumer perception for that broader universe, the Board can and should allow supplementation. Reconsideration under the correct legal standard may require fact‑finding (e.g., additional third‑party use examples, evidence of marketplace ubiquity, consumer surveys, sales/advertising figures) to reach a supported factual conclusion on factor six and any knock‑on effect on factor one.
- Conversely, if the record already contains sufficient, probative evidence for the broader scope, the Board need only reweigh and explain its conclusions under the correct standard.

Does the type of legal error affect whether new evidence is allowed or required?
- Yes. Where the error is purely legal and the record already contains adequate evidence for the correct legal standard, the agency may simply reanalyze the existing record (no new evidence required). The court’s affirmance of factor two illustrates that the Board’s factual findings can stand even when other factors must be reassessed under the correct law.
- Where the error involves an improperly narrowed scope that excluded material categories of evidence (as here), remand ordinarily requires the Board to consider that broader category — and if the record is deficient for that broader inquiry, the Board should accept new evidence. The Federal Circuit’s instruction that the Board consider “the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration” and its linkage of factor six to factor one makes clear that the Board must address whatever evidence is necessary to perform that broadened analysis.

Practical points for the parties and the Board
- Parties should re‑submit or identify any third‑party registrations/uses related to banking and financing (not just credit card services) and provide marketplace context or evidence of commercial strength/weakness they want the Board to consider on remand.
- If the Board believes the current record is sufficient, it should explain why; if it believes further evidence is needed, it should reopen the record (or request supplemental briefing/evidence) and permit the parties to submit it so that its factual findings on factors six and one rest on substantial evidence under the correct standard set out on remand.

Bottom line: Apex’s remand was based on a legal error in defining the universe of “similar” marks. The Board must reconsider factors six and one using the broader scope dictated by its finding that services are highly similar. That reconsideration may — and where the record is incomplete for the broader inquiry likely will — include new third‑party and marketplace evidence.

### Why were costs awarded to the appellant despite only a partial victory (affirmed in part, vacated in part)?
Question:
The judgment states 'Costs to appellant' even though the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board on DuPont factor two and only vacated and remanded on factors six and one. Under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 39 and the Federal Circuit’s local rules, how are costs allocated in a mixed outcome like this? Does obtaining a vacatur and remand on key issues generally make the appellant the 'prevailing party' for cost purposes, or is this a discretionary call the panel makes case by case?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Federal Rules (Rule 39) let the court award taxable costs to the “prevailing party,” but who is the prevailing party on a mixed outcome is a matter for the court to decide.  
- A vacatur and remand on important issues often suffices to make an appellant the prevailing party for cost purposes, but it is not automatic — it is a discretionary, case‑by‑case determination.  
- Apex Bank v. CC Serve illustrates that discretion: the court affirmed one DuPont factor, vacated two others and remanded, and then (in the same judgment) directed “Costs to appellant.”

Why — legal doctrine and practice
- FRAP 39 governs taxation of costs and contemplates awarding costs to the prevailing party; the appellate court or clerk implements that rule consistent with the court’s view of who prevailed. The Federal Circuit’s local rules implement the mechanics (what to file, what items are taxable, deadlines), but they do not change that the court determines who is the prevailing party for purposes of awarding costs.  
- “Prevailing party” is a pragmatic concept. Courts routinely treat as prevailing the party that obtained the relief sought on appeal — including partial relief that materially alters the parties’ legal positions (for example, vacatur of agency findings, remand for further proceedings, reversal of dispositive rulings). Whether a partial win counts as prevailing depends on the substance of the relief (did it change the legal outcome or secure meaningful relief?) and is resolved by the court in the exercise of its discretion.

How the opinion applies
- In Apex Bank v. CC Serve the panel: affirmed the Board on DuPont factor two, but “vacate[d] the Board’s findings with respect to DuPont factors six and one and remand[ed]” for reconsideration (see opinion, Sections III.B–C). The judgment then states “AFFIRMED‑IN‑PART, VACATED‑IN‑PART, AND REMANDED” followed by “COSTS / Costs to appellant.”  
- That disposition shows the court treated Apex’s partial success (vacatur and remand on two DuPont factors, including the sixth factor whose reconsideration the panel linked to commercial strength and might affect the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion result) as sufficient relief to designate Apex the prevailing party for cost purposes. In short: the panel used its discretion to award costs to the appellant after granting meaningful appellate relief.

Practical points for advocates
- On a mixed result, emphasize in any bill of costs or motion for costs why the vacatur/remand was material (it changed the Board’s analysis or will likely affect the ultimate disposition).  
- Be prepared for the court to weigh the degree and importance of success rather than applying a strict all‑or‑nothing rule. The Federal Circuit may award costs to a partially successful appellant when the appellate remedy is meaningful (as it did here), but it could also deny costs or split them in other circumstances.

Conclusion
Apex Bank demonstrates that vacatur and remand on important issues can make an appellant the prevailing party for FRAP 39 purposes, but the decision to award costs is discretionary and assessed case by case.

### Why did the Board treat Apex’s broad 'banking and financing services' as encompassing credit card issuance even though Apex doesn’t currently issue cards?
Question:
Apex argued the parties’ services aren’t directly competitive, emphasizing it doesn’t offer credit cards. The Board still found the services 'legally identical, in part' based on dictionary definitions of 'banking' and 'finance' that include extending credit via credit cards. In Section 2(d) oppositions, is the similarity of services assessed strictly by the identifications in the application/registration rather than actual marketplace activities? How do tribunals handle broad service identifications that logically subsume more specific services the applicant does not (yet) offer?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — in Section 2(d) oppositions tribunals begin with and often give dispositive weight to the identifications of services in the application/registration. If a claimant’s identification is broad enough that its plain meaning (as shown by definitions, the Trademark ID Manual, or other record evidence) encompasses a registrant’s narrower services, the Board may treat those services as legally identical even if the applicant does not yet offer the narrower service in the marketplace. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is an example: the Board (and the Federal Circuit, as to the second DuPont factor) treated “banking and financing services” as encompassing credit‑card issuance because dictionary definitions showed that “banking” and “finance” include extending credit by cards. J.A.25, 27–28; opinion § III.A.

Why and how tribunals do this (doctrinal explanation tied to the opinion)

- Identification-driven inquiry: The second DuPont factor asks whether the parties’ goods/services are similar or related. As the court explained, the services need not be literally identical — the question is whether they are related or marketed in such a way that consumers could think they come from the same source. Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC is cited for that principle and the opinion applies it to the parties’ identified services. Op. § III.A (citing Coach Servs.); see also M2 Software/M2 Communications standard for review. J.A.24–28.

- Plain‑meaning/record evidence controls when given: Where the record shows the plain meaning of the identifications (here via dictionary definitions and the Trademark ID Manual entries), the Board relied on those definitions to conclude that “banking and financing services” encompass credit‑card services (issuing, processing, managing cards). J.A.25, 27–28. The Federal Circuit affirmed that factual finding as supported by substantial evidence, and therefore affirmed the Board’s conclusion on the second DuPont factor. Op. § III.A.

- Marketplace non‑use is not necessarily dispositive: Apex argued it doesn’t issue cards and thus the services aren’t directly competitive. The court rejected that argument because the Board’s analysis focused on the scope and plain meaning of the identified services and on third‑party registrations showing overlap; substantial evidence supported the Board’s conclusion despite Apex’s current non‑use. Op. § III.A (Apex’s argument “unavailing”).

- Third‑party evidence and scope: Tribunals will also consider third‑party registrations/uses to show that particular services are commonly provided together (and to assess commercial strength under the sixth DuPont factor). The Board used third‑party registrations as corroboration that credit‑card services and broader banking/finance services can emanate from the same source. J.A.33–34. The Federal Circuit did not fault that step; it only faulted the Board for then narrowing the universe of third‑party marks it considered under the sixth factor (the court held the Board should have included marks for similar—not necessarily identical—services once it had found the services highly similar). Op. §§ III.B, III.C.

Practical takeaway from the opinion (what tribunals will do)

- When an applicant uses a broad goods/services identification, the Board will interpret that identification according to its ordinary meaning as shown in the record (dictionaries, ID Manual, registrations, evidence of market practice). If that ordinary meaning subsumes the opponent’s narrower services, the services can be treated as legally identical for DuPont factor two even if the applicant is not yet offering the narrower service. See J.A.25, 27–28; Op. § III.A.

- The Board may and should then use a consistent scope when considering other DuPont factors (e.g., third‑party registrations under factor six and commercial impression under factor one). The Federal Circuit remanded here because the Board limited its sixth‑factor analysis to credit‑card marks only, despite its finding that the parties’ services were highly similar; the court required a broader universe of comparable marks consistent with that finding. Op. §§ III.B–C.

That explains why the Board treated Apex’s “banking and financing services” as encompassing credit‑card issuance even though Apex didn’t currently issue cards: the legal analysis turns on the identifications’ plain meaning and the record evidence that those terms cover card issuance, not solely on the applicant’s present marketplace offerings. The Federal Circuit affirmed that aspect of the Board’s DuPont‑two finding (but vacated and remanded parts of the analysis tied to factors one and six because those were applied inconsistently with the Board’s finding of high similarity). Op. §§ III.A–C.
