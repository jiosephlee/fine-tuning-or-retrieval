Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Does a § 2(d) refusal raise any First Amendment concerns after Tam and Brunetti?
Question:
The opinion doesn’t mention constitutional issues, but I’m wondering whether denying registration for likelihood of confusion implicates speech. After Tam/Brunetti struck viewpoint-based bars, is § 2(d) considered a content-neutral, source-identification regulation tied to preventing consumer confusion, and thus constitutional? Are there cases explicitly holding that § 2(d) refusals do not offend the First Amendment because they regulate registration (not use) and serve substantial/compelling interests?
Answer:
Short answer: No — Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. does not address First Amendment issues. The opinion treats §2(d) as a straightforward likelihood-of-confusion refusal under the DuPont framework and contains no constitutional analysis or holding that §2(d) is (or is not) subject to First Amendment scrutiny after Tam and Brunetti.

Why: what this opinion actually does
- The panel frames the dispute purely as a §2(d) likelihood-of-confusion case and reviews the Board’s factual findings under the DuPont factors. See slip op. §§II–III (citing DuPont, M2 Software, Bose). The Court affirms the Board on the second DuPont factor, vacates and remands as to the first and sixth factors, and otherwise confines its decision to trademark-law doctrine and substantial-evidence review. See slip op. pp. 4–12 (affirmed-in-part, vacated-in-part, remanded).
- Nothing in the opinion engages First Amendment doctrine, Tam v. Matal, or Iancu v. Brunetti. The Court does not treat registration refusals as government speech, nor does it analyze whether §2(d) is content- or viewpoint-based, nor does it consider any compelled-speech or prior-restraint problems.

What follows from that (legal takeaways for your question)
- Apex leaves open the constitutional question. Because the opinion never addresses Tam/Brunetti or any free‑speech challenge, it provides no precedent that §2(d) refusals do or do not raise First Amendment problems.
- Substantively, the opinion anchors its analysis to the traditional source‑identification/likelihood‑of‑confusion regime (DuPont factors). If you are thinking doctrinally, the opinion treats §2(d) as a classical trademark refusal doctrine aimed at preventing consumer confusion (see slip op. §II and discussion of the DuPont factors in §III). But the Court does not say whether that regulatory aim renders §2(d) constitutionally permissible post‑Tam/Brunetti.

Practical consequence for litigants/researchers
- You cannot cite Apex for the proposition that §2(d) is immune from First Amendment attack. Apex is useful only for its application of DuPont (e.g., the Court’s treatment of the second, sixth, and first factors and the limits on the Board’s scope of third‑party evidence). See slip op. §§III.A–C.
- If you want binding authority on whether §2(d) raises First Amendment concerns after Tam/Brunetti, you will need to look to other decisions that expressly analyze constitutional issues (Apex does not do so).
