Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### How can the court call each DuPont factor a question of fact but still identify a 'legal error' in the sixth-factor analysis?
Question:
The opinion says each DuPont factor is a factual question reviewed for substantial evidence, and the overall likelihood of confusion is a question of law. Yet the court vacates the Board’s sixth-factor finding for applying 'too narrow' a similarity scope—a legal flaw. How does the court distinguish between (a) a factual finding about third-party use/registrations and (b) a legal error in defining the scope of 'similar goods' considered under factor six? Is this an application of APA/Chenery principles requiring the agency to apply the correct legal framework to its factfinding?
Answer:
Short answer: The court treated the underlying DuPont factor findings as factual but found a legal error where the Board applied the wrong legal scope for which third‑party marks to consider under factor six. The Board’s decision about what counts as “similar” marks was a legal choice about the proper framework for weighing the third‑party evidence (not a dispute about the existence or weight of particular third‑party uses), so the court vacated and remanded that part of the analysis while leaving factual findings subject to substantial‑evidence review intact.

Why that makes sense (with citations to the opinion)

- Familiar allocation of responsibilities. The opinion starts from the settled division of review: “We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382; then it reiterates that “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact” but that “likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts” (Opinion, §II (quoting M2 and Bose)). That framing preserves two levels: (1) factfinding on each factor (substantial‑evidence review), and (2) legal conclusions about the correct legal standards and the court’s ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion determination (de novo).

- What the Board did as fact vs. what the court called a legal error. The Board made factual findings about third‑party uses — e.g., Apex submitted exhibits showing third‑party uses of “Aspire” (J.A. 40–46), and the Board counted nine Aspire‑formative marks for credit‑card services (J.A. 47). Those are factfinding matters the court would test for substantial evidence. But the court identified a legal error in the Board’s choice of which third‑party marks to consider: the Board “restricted the universe of marks it considered to only those relating to credit card services and excluded marks related to other banking and financing services” (Opinion §III.B, J.A. 6–7). The court characterized that as a legal flaw because it rested on the Board’s narrowing legal definition of “similar” goods for purposes of factor six rather than on a contested factual determination about particular third‑party uses.

- Why narrowing scope is legal (not factual) for purposes of review. The court explains that the Board had already found—factually under factor two—that the parties’ services are “highly similar” and even “partially legally identical” (J.A. 27–28). Given that factual finding, the Board’s decision to treat only credit‑card marks as relevant under factor six was a misapplication of the legal standard that factor six requires consideration of “similar” (not identical) goods. The opinion relies on precedent stating that factor six “does not require identical goods—only similar ones” (citing Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation) and concludes that “the Board’s definition of similarity [was] too narrow” (J.A. 7). That is a legal determination about the proper analytic scope, so the court vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding and remanded for reconsideration consistent with the Board’s own factor‑two factual finding.

- The chaining of fact and law. Put differently: the facts found under factor two (services highly similar) change the applicable legal frame for factor six (what counts as “similar goods” to trigger consideration of third‑party marks). The court remanded so the Board can apply the correct legal framework (broader universe of potentially similar third‑party marks) when re‑weighing the third‑party evidence it already has or may collect. That is why the court left the substantial‑evidence finding on factor two undisturbed but vacated factor six as a legal error.

- Relation to agency‑law principles. Although the opinion does not cite Chenery or the APA by name, the court’s action is consistent with the same basic agency‑law principle: an administrative decision must apply the correct legal standard to the facts. The opinion explicitly treats the Board’s restriction as a “legal flaw” and remands for reconsideration in light of its factual finding (J.A. 7). In practice, that is the same corrective mechanism the courts use when an agency’s legal framework is incorrect even though the evidentiary record otherwise contains factual findings.

Bottom line: The court distinguished the Board’s factual findings about specific third‑party uses (reviewed for substantial evidence) from the Board’s legal choice about how narrowly to define “similar” goods when applying factor six. The latter was a legal error because it misapplied the legal standard (especially in light of the Board’s own factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar), so the court vacated and remanded that part of the analysis while leaving the factual findings intact.
