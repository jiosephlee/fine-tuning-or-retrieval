Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### How does the Board properly interpret an identification like 'credit card services' when the ID Manual entry has been deleted since registration?
Question:
The Board used dictionaries to elaborate 'credit card services' after noting the Trademark ID Manual entry was deleted post-registration, then treated various credit-card-related activities as within that identification. Is it proper to rely on current dictionaries or the now-deleted Manual entry to interpret the scope of a decades-old registration? Do TTAB principles require construing identifications as of the registration date, and if so, how is that reconciled with evolving ID Manual entries and terminology?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Board is required to determine what the wording of the earlier registration actually covers and may rely on ordinary‑meaning sources (dictionaries, common usage, the registration file and evidence of how the mark is used) to do so. Where an ID Manual entry that existed at or after registration has been deleted post‑registration, the Board may look to dictionary definitions and other evidence to define the services in the registration. The Federal Circuit accepted that approach here (the Board defined “credit card services” by reference to dictionaries and evidence and the court affirmed the Board’s substantive finding on the second DuPont factor).  
- One caveat the court emphasized: once the Board fixes the scope of services for purposes of the second DuPont factor, it must remain consistent when analyzing other factors (the court vacated the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis because the Board then narrowed the universe of third‑party marks inconsistently).

What the opinion says (specifics)
- The Board recognized that the Trademark ID Manual entry for “credit card services” had been deleted after CC Serve’s registration, so it “first determined the meaning of the identified services.” The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes issuing credit cards, counseling about credit card debt, processing payments/transactions, authorization, monitoring and alerts, managing accounts, and providing access to credit scores. (Opinion cites J.A.25.)
- The Board then relied on dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” showing those words can encompass extending credit or providing funds through issuance of credit cards, and concluded Apex’s “banking and financing services” overlap with CC Serve’s services and are “legally identical, in part.” (J.A.27–28.) The Federal Circuit affirmed that factual finding and the Board’s conclusion on the second DuPont factor (J.A.35).

Legal doctrine and how it applies here
- The central task is to determine what the earlier registration’s identification actually covers. The Board must interpret the plain meaning of the words of the identification in the registration record and may use external, ordinary‑meaning sources (dictionaries, industry usage, evidence of actual services offered under the mark, and third‑party registrations/usages) to do so. That is what the Board did here after noting the ID Manual entry had been deleted.
- The opinion approves that approach in practice: the Board defined the deleted/manually‑changed identification by consulting dictionaries and the record and the Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supported the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar (affirming the second DuPont factor). (See J.A.25, J.A.27–28, J.A.35.)
- However, the Board must be consistent in its chosen scope. The court criticized the Board for using one scope when deciding the second DuPont factor (finding services highly similar / partially legally identical) and then constricting the universe of third‑party uses when analyzing the sixth DuPont factor. The Federal Circuit held the sixth‑factor inquiry should encompass marks used on similar goods/services as the Board already found—i.e., the Board should not apply a narrower definition under later factors than it applied in the second‑factor analysis (see J.A.27–28, J.A.47–48).

Practical takeaways for the Board and practitioners
- If the ID Manual entry has been deleted since registration, expect the Board to define the identification by reference to the ordinary meaning of the words and supporting evidence (dictionaries, industry practice, the registrant’s actual usage and third‑party evidence). That method was upheld here for the second DuPont factor. (J.A.25–28, J.A.35.)
- Preserve and produce contemporaneous evidence of meaning/use at or near the registration/priority date if you want the Board to construe the identification narrowly or as it was understood historically; absent that, the Board will rely on ordinary meanings and present evidence. (The opinion shows the Board filled the gap left by the deleted Manual entry with dictionaries and usage evidence.)
- Be consistent: once the Board fixes the scope of the registration’s services for one DuPont factor, it should apply the same scope in evaluating other factors (the court remanded because the Board narrowed the relevant universe when considering third‑party uses). (J.A.27–28, J.A.47–51.)

Bottom line
The Board acted within acceptable practice by interpreting a registration whose ID Manual entry had been deleted through dictionaries and record evidence and the Federal Circuit affirmed that interpretation for the second DuPont factor. But the Board must stick to the scope it adopts across the DuPont analysis—an inconsistent narrowing (as occurred here in the sixth‑factor analysis) will be vacated and remanded.
