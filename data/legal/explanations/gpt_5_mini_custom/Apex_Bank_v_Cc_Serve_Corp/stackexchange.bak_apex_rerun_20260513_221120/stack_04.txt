Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why must the similarity scope used for factor two carry over to factor six?
Question:
The Board found the parties’ services 'highly similar' and 'legally identical, in part' under factor two, but then limited the relevant public and third-party marks to 'credit card services' only under factor six. The Federal Circuit says that was error and the Board should 'retain the same scope' when evaluating similarity under other factors. What doctrinal principle requires this consistency across factors? Is it that 'similar goods' has one legal meaning across DuPont factors, or that inconsistent scoping is arbitrary under the APA?
Answer:
Short answer: The Federal Circuit requires consistency because the DuPont factors rest on the same underlying factual finding about what goods/services are “similar,” so once the Board defines the scope of similarity for factor two it must use that same scope when asking whether third‑party marks are used on “similar” goods for factor six. That is a doctrinal consistency rule rooted in (1) the way the DuPont framework works—factual findings about similarity supply the universe for other factors—and (2) the Court’s review obligations (an inconsistent narrowing is legally flawed and effectively arbitrary on the record).

Why: what the opinion actually says
- The Court affirms the Board’s factual finding under factor two that Apex’s “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s “credit card services” are “highly similar” and even “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28, 33–34.
- The Court then faults the Board for, in the factor six analysis, restricting the relevant third‑party marks to just those tied to “credit card services” while excluding marks covering other banking and financing services. The Court calls that “an error” and explains: “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar . . . the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.” J.A. 6–7 (¶ beginning “That was an error.”).

Doctrinal content (concise)
- The DuPont analysis is a multi‑factor legal test built on common underlying facts (the similarity of goods/services). Once the Board defines what the services encompass for factor two, that factual definition supplies the proper universe of “similar goods/services” for other DuPont inquiries—e.g., whether third‑party uses show weakness under factor six.
- The Court cites precedent that factor six requires consideration of similar (not identical) goods, so narrowing the universe below the Board’s own finding of similarity is improper (Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation). See J.A. 7 (citing those cases).
- Practically, the error is both a substantive misapplication of the DuPont framework and a legal/arbitrary error subject to reversal: the Board applied a different (more stringent) standard to factor six than it applied to factor two, which is legally inconsistent and therefore vacated. The opinion frames the problem as legal error in the Board’s analysis (and remands for reconsideration), which is the same sort of inconsistency the APA review standard will correct on appeal.

So: it is not a metaphysical claim that “similar goods” has one rigid statutory definition that must mean the same thing in every DuPont factor. Rather, the Court requires that the Board consistently apply its established factual definition of the parties’ goods/services across the DuPont analysis. If the Board departs from that scope without a reasoned basis, that departure is legal error (and effectively arbitrary) under appellate review. The opinion ties both points together: the DuPont framework depends on consistent factual findings, and inconsistent scoping here was a legal/arbritrariness flaw that warranted vacatur and remand (J.A. 6–7).
