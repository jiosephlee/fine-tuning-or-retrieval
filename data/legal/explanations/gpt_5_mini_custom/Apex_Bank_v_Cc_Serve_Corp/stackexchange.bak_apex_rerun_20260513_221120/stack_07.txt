Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Is defining the 'relevant public' for factor six a legal determination or a factual finding?
Question:
The Board narrowed the relevant public to 'consumers of credit card services' and excluded marks used for other banking/financing services; the Federal Circuit called this 'legally flawed.' When the Board identifies the relevant public for assessing third-party use, is that treated as a question of fact, a mixed question, or a pure legal issue about the proper scope of comparison under DuPont? What guidance governs that scoping decision?
Answer:
Short answer: It’s primarily a factual determination (reviewed for substantial evidence) but governed and constrained by legal standards — i.e., a classic mixed-question situation: the Board must find the “relevant public” as a factual matter, but it must apply the legal rule that the sixth DuPont factor looks to similar (not necessarily identical) goods/services and must be consistent with its other DuPont findings. The Federal Circuit reversed here because the Board applied the legal standard too narrowly and inconsistently with its own factual finding on factor two.

Why — explained with the opinion

- Standard of review the court states: “We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382 (quoted at J.A. 3). The court also reminds that “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact,” (Bose, quoted at J.A. 3). That framework controls how the scoping decision is treated on appeal.

- The Board’s task is factual: the Board here defined the “relevant public” to be “consumers of ‘credit card services’” and limited its sixth-factor inquiry to third‑party uses in that narrow universe (J.A. 47). The Federal Circuit treated that as a factual scope choice that must be supported by the record (i.e., substantial evidence), because the DuPont factors depend on underlying factual findings.

- The court held the Board’s scope was legally flawed because of the legal rule governing the sixth factor: the sixth factor “does not require identical goods—only similar ones” (citing Olde Tyme and Juice Generation; holding summarized at J.A. 6–7). Because the Board had already found (as a factual matter under factor two) that the parties’ services are “highly similar, in fact, partially legally identical” (J.A. 27–28), the Board could not then constrict factor six to a more stringent, narrower definition of similarity. The court said: “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar . . . the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.” (J.A. 7.)

What this means as a rule of decision

- The Board must make a factual determination of the “relevant public”/scope of goods or services it will consider under factor six, and that finding is tested on appeal for substantial evidence.

- But the Board’s factual scope determination is constrained by legal doctrines:
  - Sixth DuPont factor requires consideration of marks used on similar (not strictly identical) goods/services (Olde Tyme; Juice Generation — cited and applied at J.A. 6–7).
  - The Board must be consistent: it cannot adopt one scope for factor two (finding high similarity) and then adopt a narrower legal standard for factor six. The scope used under different DuPont factors should be coherent with the Board’s factual findings (J.A. 7).

- On appeal courts will:
  - Review the legal standard applied (e.g., whether the Board required identical goods when law requires similarity) de novo.
  - Review the Board’s factual scope decision for substantial evidence (i.e., whether the record supports the Board’s chosen “relevant public”).

Practical takeaways for the Board (and litigants)

- When the Board defines the “relevant public” for factor six, it should (1) articulate the factual basis for what customers/services are “relevant,” (2) apply the settled legal rule that similarity, not identity, suffices, and (3) ensure the scope is consistent with any prior factual findings about similarity under other DuPont factors (exactly the inconsistency the court reversed here—compare J.A. 27–28 with the Board’s narrow scope at J.A. 47).

In short: scoping is a factual finding subject to substantial-evidence review, but it must apply the correct legal standard (similarity, not identity) and be consistent with the Board’s other DuPont findings; failure to do so is reversible as happened in this opinion (see J.A. 6–7).
