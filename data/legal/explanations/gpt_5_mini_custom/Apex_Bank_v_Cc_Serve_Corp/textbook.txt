Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and marks
This dispute arises between CC Serve Corp. and Apex Bank over the use and registration of marks containing the word “ASPIRE.” CC Serve owns U.S. Registration No. 2,126,948 for the standard-character mark ASPIRE for “credit card services.” That registration issued in 1998 and carries a priority date of October 17, 1996. Apex Bank sought federal registration of two intent-to-use (ITU) marks—an ASPIRE BANK word mark and an ASPIRE BANK word-and-design mark—intended to identify banking and financing services offered under the ASPIRE BANK brand.

## Nature of the services
The parties’ businesses and the services identified in the applications differ in form but overlap in the financial-services sphere. CC Serve does not itself issue credit cards as a bank; rather, it partners with banks to operate branded credit-card programs. Under CC Serve’s model, a bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts, and CC Serve (and its affiliates) provides account servicing, transaction processing, monitoring, and related services in connection with those cards and accounts. Apex Bank is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank with 18 branches that offers deposit accounts, mortgages, and consumer and business loans. Apex did not offer credit cards at the time of the proceedings but planned to launch an internet banking service under the ASPIRE BANK brand.

## Prosecution history at the USPTO
Apex filed its ITU applications in August 2019 to register the ASPIRE BANK marks for “banking and financing services.” During prosecution CC Serve submitted a letter of protest asserting likely confusion with its prior ASPIRE registration. Despite that protest, the examining attorney approved Apex’s ASPIRE BANK applications for publication, and the marks published in the Official Gazette on December 17, 2019. Publication started the statutory window during which third parties could file oppositions.

## TTAB opposition and Board decision
CC Serve filed an opposition at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, alleging that Apex’s ASPIRE BANK applications were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s existing ASPIRE registration. The Board sustained the opposition and refused Apex’s registrations, concluding that consumer confusion was likely. In reaching that conclusion the Board applied the familiar DuPont factors to evaluate likelihood of confusion and made key factual findings about the similarity of the services and the marks’ competitive landscape.

## Appeal to the Federal Circuit and jurisdiction
Apex appealed the TTAB’s decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit has appellate jurisdiction over TTAB decisions refusing trademark registration under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B). On appeal, Apex challenged the Board’s likelihood-of-confusion analysis—particularly the Board’s treatment of certain DuPont factors—and the Federal Circuit reviewed the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and its factual findings for substantial evidence. The court’s judgment ultimately affirmed the Board in part, vacated parts of its analysis, and remanded for further proceedings.

# Governing Law and Standards of Review

This case turns on the familiar Section 2(d) likelihood-of-confusion framework applied by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) and reviewed by the Federal Circuit. Understanding the governing law and the standards the Federal Circuit applies on appeal is essential to reading the opinion: Section 2(d) sets the legal rule refusing registration when concurrent use will likely cause confusion; the DuPont factors supply a multi‑factor, fact‑centered framework for deciding confusion; and the Court’s appellate standards determine how TTAB findings and legal conclusions are reviewed.

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: refusal when concurrent use would likely cause confusion

Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act prohibits registration of a mark if the mark so resembles a previously registered mark that its use on or in connection with the applicant’s goods or services is likely to cause confusion, mistake, or deception. The governing principle—articulated in DuPont and repeatedly applied here—is straightforward in form: registration must be refused when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” That statutory standard does not demand literal identity of goods or marks; it demands a determination whether ordinary consumers are likely to be confused about source.

In practice the TTAB applies Section 2(d) by making factual findings about the parties’ marks and goods/services and then deciding, as a matter of law, whether those facts show a likelihood of confusion. The Apex Bank opinion follows that pattern: the Board made factual findings about the parties’ services and the third‑party landscape, then applied the DuPont factors to reach a legal conclusion denying registration.

## The DuPont factors: thirteen, with the Board considering only those relevant and of record

The DuPont decision supplies the multi‑factor framework used by the TTAB and courts. There are thirteen DuPont factors (e.g., similarity of the marks, similarity of the goods/services, strength of the prior mark, evidence of actual confusion, channels of trade, etc.). The DuPont framework is not a mechanical checklist; rather, it is a flexible set of factual inquiries that guide whether confusion is likely in the marketplace.

Two procedural points about DuPont are important and are emphasized in the opinion. First, each DuPont factor presents a question of fact (as the Federal Circuit explains in Bose), so the Board’s findings on those factors are factual findings reviewed for substantial evidence on appeal. Second, the Board need not address all thirteen factors; it need only consider those factors that are relevant and supported by the record. The Apex Bank opinion follows both points: the Court treated the Board’s factor findings as factual findings subject to substantial‑evidence review, and it acknowledged that the Board had focused on a subset of DuPont factors that were relevant in the record.

A useful concrete illustration from this case: the Board focused on (among others) the second DuPont factor (similarity of services) and the sixth DuPont factor (number and nature of similar third‑party marks). Because those inquiries overlap (they both ask whether goods/services are similar), the Court scrutinized whether the Board applied consistent scopes when evaluating each factor.

## Standard of review: legal conclusions de novo; factual findings for substantial evidence

The Federal Circuit’s standard of review is twofold and shapes how the Court handles TTAB decisions. Legal conclusions—such as the ultimate determination whether confusion is likely—are reviewed de novo. Factual findings—such as whether two services are “highly similar” or how many third‑party uses exist—are reviewed for substantial evidence.

Substantial evidence means “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” That standard is deferential: if the Board’s factual findings are supported by substantial evidence in the record, the Court will accept them even if reasonable minds could draw different conclusions. But legal errors—misstatements of the governing legal standard or the application of an incorrect legal rule—are reviewed without deference and will be corrected.

Apex Bank illustrates the interplay: the Court accepted (under substantial‑evidence review) the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services were highly similar, and thus affirmed the Board’s conclusion on the second DuPont factor. By contrast, the Court found a legal error in the Board’s approach to the sixth factor—specifically, the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party uses it would consider despite having found the services highly similar—and vacated that part of the decision as a legal error requiring remand.

## Nature of the inquiry: likelihood of confusion is a legal question based on underlying factual findings on each factor

The ultimate question—whether confusion is likely—is a legal determination that the Court reaches by weighing the DuPont factual findings. That means appellate review proceeds in two steps. First, the TTAB compiles and weighs record evidence and makes findings under the relevant DuPont factors (questions of fact). Second, the TTAB applies legal principles to those factual findings to answer the likelihood‑of‑confusion question. On appeal, the Court accepts factual findings supported by substantial evidence and reviews the Board’s legal application of those facts de novo.

The Apex Bank decision demonstrates this structure. The Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services were “highly similar” was treated as a factual predicate. Because that finding affects other DuPont inquiries (for example, what third‑party uses are “similar” for purposes of the sixth factor, and how commercially strong the mark appears), the Court required the Board to apply consistent legal reasoning across factors. Where the Board’s legal treatment of the sixth factor was inconsistent with its factual finding on the second factor, the Court vacated and remanded for the Board to reapply the legal standards to the established facts.

## Key precedents cited and their roles in the opinion

A few precedents cited in the opinion frame the governing rules and the standard of review and are worth noting for how the Court relied on them here:

- DuPont (Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.): Provides the thirteen‑factor framework the TTAB uses to assess likelihood of confusion and anchors the whole Section 2(d) analysis.

- M2 Software v. M2 Commc’ns: Articulates the Federal Circuit’s review regime for TTAB decisions—legal conclusions de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence—and is the source for the Court’s emphasis on the separation between facts and legal conclusions in this appeal.

- Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Products: Explains that each DuPont factor is a question of fact; the Apex Bank opinion quotes this principle to justify substantial‑evidence review of the Board’s DuPont findings.

- Coach Services Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC: Provides guidance on the second DuPont factor by explaining that goods/services need not be identical to be related—only related in such a way that consumers could believe they come from a common source. The Board relied on that standard in finding the parties’ services highly similar, a finding the Court affirmed under substantial‑evidence review.

Taken together, these precedents establish the analytical architecture the Court used: DuPont as the substantive test; M2 Software and Bose to frame how the Court reviews the Board’s work; and Coach Services to define the standard for relatedness of goods/services under the second DuPont factor.

Conclusion

When reading the remainder of the Apex Bank opinion, keep this governance structure in mind: the TTAB makes factual findings under selected DuPont factors (only those relevant and of record); the TTAB then reaches a legal conclusion on likelihood of confusion; and the Federal Circuit accepts factual findings supported by substantial evidence but corrects legal errors de novo. The Court applied those rules here by affirming the Board’s supported factual finding on similarity of services, but vacating and remanding portions of the decision where the Board applied a legally inconsistent standard to other DuPont factors in light of that factual finding.

# Factor Two: Similarity of the Services—Affirmance

## Board’s task: compare CC Serve’s “credit card services” to Apex’s “banking and financing services”

Under the DuPont framework, the second factor requires the Board to assess whether the parties’ identified goods or services are related in such a way that confusion about source is likely. The Board’s task is factual: compare the specific services recited in the respective registrations/applications and decide whether they are related or, in the extremes, identical. The Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s factual findings on this point for substantial evidence and its legal conclusions de novo. In this case the Board compared CC Serve’s registered “credit card services” to Apex’s applied-for “banking and financing services” and concluded that the services are highly similar and legally identical in part.

## Defining the identified services: interpreting “credit card services” after ID Manual deletion

A threshold issue the Board faced was the meaning of the term “credit card services,” because that entry had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration issued. The Board nonetheless construed the phrase based on the application/registration language and the record, defining “credit card services” to encompass issuing credit cards to finance purchases, counseling regarding credit card debt, processing payments and transactions, authorizing transactions, monitoring and alert services, managing accounts, and providing access to credit scores. That textual and record-based construction of the recited services framed the scope of comparison with Apex’s broader “banking and financing services” identification.

## Dictionary definitions: “bank,” “banking,” and “finance” include credit extension via cards

To determine whether banking and financing services reach the same territory as credit card services, the Board relied on ordinary meanings from dictionary definitions. Those definitions showed that “bank,” “banking,” and “finance” commonly include the extension of credit and provision of funds—activities that can be carried out by issuing credit cards or otherwise facilitating credit-based transactions. Because credit card issuance and account management are means of extending consumer credit, the Board found that the core activities in CC Serve’s recitation fall within the ordinary scope of “banking and financing” services as recited by Apex.

## Third-party registrations: showing single-source offerings that include both service types

The Board also considered third-party registrations that list both credit-card-related services and broader banking/finance services under single marks. Such evidence serves a familiar purpose in DuPont analyses: it shows that services of the listed types may emanate from a common source under a single mark. The Board found that the presence of registrations covering both credit-card services and banking/finance services supported the conclusion that the services are of a type that may be offered together and thus are related for likelihood-of-confusion purposes.

## Board’s conclusion: high similarity and partial legal identity; factor two weighs heavily for CC Serve

Weighing the defined scope of “credit card services,” the ordinary meanings of “banking/finance,” CC Serve’s demonstrated use, and the third-party registrations, the Board concluded that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services are highly similar and legally identical in part. Because similarity can be established even when services are not identical so long as they are related in a way that could cause source confusion, the Board ruled that the second DuPont factor strongly favors CC Serve. The Board therefore gave the factor substantial weight in its overall likelihood-of-confusion determination.

## Apex’s arguments and the Federal Circuit’s rejection: why substantial evidence supports affirmance

Apex argued on appeal that the services are not the same and are not directly competitive because CC Serve is not itself a bank but instead partners with banks to provide credit card programs. The court rejected that argument as unavailing. The governing principle is that services need not be offered by the same kind of commercial actor to be related; what matters is whether the services themselves overlap or are commonly offered together in the marketplace. The Board’s definition of “credit card services,” the dictionary evidence showing “banking/finance” includes credit extension, CC Serve’s operational description (issuing and servicing cards through bank partners), and the third-party registrations together provided “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept” to support the Board’s finding. Because substantial evidence supports the factual determinations underlying the second DuPont factor, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar and that factor two weighs heavily in favor of the registrant, CC Serve.

# Factor Six: Number and Nature of Similar Marks—Error and Remand

This chapter explains the Federal Circuit’s treatment of DuPont factor six—the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”—in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., why the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (Board’s) analysis was legally flawed, and what the Board must do on remand. The court’s ruling is narrowly focused: factor six is intended to measure the degree to which a field is “crowded” with similar marks (which reduces a mark’s commercial strength and narrows its scope of protection), and the Board erred by artificially narrowing the universe of third‑party marks it would consider after having already found the parties’ services highly similar under factor two.

## Purpose of factor six: what the factor measures and why it matters

DuPont factor six asks the decisionmaker to consider how many similar marks are used on similar goods or services and the nature of those uses. The evidence can come from third‑party registrations, marketplace use, advertising, or other materials showing that others in the field use similar marks. The legal significance is twofold. First, widespread third‑party use of similar marks shows that a term or element is relatively weak in the marketplace and therefore entitled only to a narrow scope of protection. Second, a crowded field suggests that consumers are more practiced at discriminating among similar marks and are thus less likely to be confused by them. The Federal Circuit summarized these purposes by citing cases such as Palm Bay, Juice Generation, and Jack Wolfskin, which treat third‑party use as demonstrating relative weakness and showing that consumers have been “educated” to distinguish similar marks.

The inquiry under factor six does not require identical goods or services; it requires similarity. Thus, marks used on goods or services that are similar enough to the plaintiff’s and applicant’s offerings may be relevant to showing crowding or weakness in the field.

## Apex’s evidence: third‑party Aspire uses across credit‑card and financial services

Apex submitted evidence that multiple third parties used marks containing “Aspire” or Aspire‑formative elements in connection with credit card services and more broadly in banking and financial services. The exhibits included registrations and examples of commercial use showing Aspire marks appearing in contexts that, collectively, span credit card programs and other banking/finance offerings. Apex relied on that broader set of third‑party uses to argue that CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark was commercially weak and therefore should receive a narrower scope of protection.

## The Board’s approach and its error

In evaluating factor six the Board restricted the universe of relevant third‑party marks to uses tied to “credit card services,” excluding Aspire uses that related to other banking and financing services. The Board explained that because CC Serve’s registration identified “credit card services,” the relevant public consisted of consumers of credit card services, and therefore Apex’s evidence of Aspire uses outside that narrowly defined category was “essentially irrelevant.” The Board counted nine third‑party Aspire uses for credit card services and concluded that number was insufficiently “considerable” to demonstrate weakness; it therefore treated CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark as entitled to the normal scope of protection afforded an inherently distinctive mark.

The Federal Circuit held this approach legally flawed. The Board’s limitation was inconsistent with its earlier, independent factual finding under factor two that the parties’ services are highly similar—indeed, partly legally identical—because the Board had already determined that Apex’s “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s “credit card services” overlap. Once the Board concluded the services are highly similar, it could not then adopt a stricter or narrower definition of similarity for factor six and ignore third‑party Aspire uses that appear in the broader category of banking and financing services. In short, factor six looks to similar—not identical—goods and services; when factor two establishes that banking/finance services and credit card services are similar, marks used in the broader banking/finance field are properly within factor six’s scope.

## Precedents the court relied on and what they mean here

The opinion cites Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation for the proposition that factor six does not require identical goods—only similar ones. The court also explained how cases like Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation treated substantial numbers of third‑party marks (the opinions counted dozens or more examples) as persuasive evidence of crowding and weakness. Those precedents establish two principles applied here: (1) the similarity threshold for third‑party evidence is not strict identity, and (2) the quantity and nature of third‑party uses are evaluated in context—comparatively few uses may be unpersuasive, while widespread use across a relevant field can show commercial weakness and narrow the senior user’s scope of protection.

## What the Federal Circuit held about factor six—and what the Board must do on remand

The Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s finding on DuPont factor six and remanded for reconsideration. On remand the Board must (1) employ a scope of similarity for third‑party marks that is consistent with its own factual finding under factor two that the parties’ services are highly similar (i.e., include marks used in banking and financing services that are similar to credit card services), (2) consider the full set of third‑party evidence relevant under that scope, and (3) reassess whether that evidence demonstrates that ASPIRE is relatively weak in the marketplace and therefore entitled only to a narrow scope of protection.

Practically, the Board must revisit its counting and evaluation of the “number and nature” of Aspire uses by including third‑party marks used in the broader banking/finance sphere (not just those labeled as credit card services). The Board must then weigh whether the aggregated evidence shows a crowded field such that consumers are likely practiced in distinguishing Aspire‑formative marks—i.e., whether the field’s crowding reduces the likelihood of confusion and narrows CC Serve’s mark protection.

Because factor six bears directly on the mark’s commercial strength, the Federal Circuit also directed reconsideration of DuPont factor one (similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression) on remand, since a revised assessment of commercial strength may affect the marks’ overall commercial impressions.

## Procedural consequence

The Federal Circuit’s disposition as to factor six is vacatur and remand: the Board’s factor‑six finding was legally erroneous for having used a narrower similarity standard than it applied elsewhere, and the Board must reassess factor six consistently with its prior finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. The court left intact its affirmance of the Board’s factor‑two finding and remanded only for reconsideration of factor six (and, because of its interrelationship, factor one).

# Factor One: Similarity of the Marks, Disposition, and Significance

## Factor one framework: appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression (I.AM.Symbolic)

The first DuPont factor asks whether the marks are similar “in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” That is the practical inquiry in this case. The court here relied on the familiar teaching of In re I.AM.Symbolic: the focus is the overall commercial impression that a prospective consumer would take away, not a dissection of isolated letters or syllables. In practice that means the Board must compare the marks as a whole (visual look and stylization where relevant), how they sound when spoken, what ideas or associations they convey, and the marketplace snapshot a consumer forms on seeing the marks together with the parties’ services. The first factor thus synthesizes perceptual elements into a single, context-dependent judgment about the likelihood of source confusion.

## Interdependence with factor six: how commercial strength/weakness shapes commercial impression

The opinion emphasizes a key structural point: factor one does not stand alone. The Board’s determination of the marks’ commercial impression can be affected materially by the commercial strength or weakness of the senior mark, which is what factor six (third‑party uses of similar marks on similar goods/services) helps to measure. If many third parties use the same or similar word elements in the same or closely related fields, consumers may be accustomed to distinguishing between those uses; the senior mark’s scope of protection narrows and its commercial impression is correspondingly diluted. Conversely, if third‑party uses are sparse, the senior mark’s commercial impression is stronger.

That interdependence is why the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s factor‑one analysis: the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (and in part legally identical) when deciding factor two, but then limited its factor‑six inquiry only to third‑party marks expressly tied to “credit card services,” excluding marks used in other banking and financial services. The court explained that, given the Board’s finding of high similarity across financial services, it was inconsistent to narrow the universe of third‑party marks for factor six. Because a reassessment of factor six could change the determination of the senior mark’s commercial strength, that reassessment could also change the commercial impression analysis under factor one. In short, a corrected, broader consideration of third‑party evidence may alter the weight and content of the marks’ overall commercial impressions, so factor one must be redone in light of any revised factor‑six findings.

## Disposition in this case

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor (the parties’ services are highly similar) because that finding was supported by substantial evidence and sound legal analysis. However, the court vacated the Board’s conclusions as to the sixth and first DuPont factors and remanded both for reconsideration. The reason for vacatur was procedural and doctrinal: the Board applied an unduly narrow scope when assessing third‑party uses under factor six despite having found the parties’ services to be highly similar, and that error required reexamination of factor six and then factor one (commercial impression) in a manner consistent with the high‑similarity finding. The court left the rest of the Board’s analysis intact, and it awarded costs to Apex (the appellant).

## Practical significance for trademark practice

There are four practical takeaways from the court’s decision:

1. Maintain a consistent scope of “similarity” across DuPont factors. If the Board (or a party) establishes that goods or services are highly similar or overlapping for one factor, that finding should inform which third‑party uses are relevant under other factors. It is inconsistent to treat the universe of “similar” goods narrowly for factor six while treating them broadly for factor two.

2. Factor six does not require identical goods. The decision reiterates that third‑party evidence of marks used on similar (not identical) goods or services is relevant to commercial strength and scope of protection. In many technical or adjacent fields—here, across banking, financing, and credit card services—third‑party use in related services can show that a mark is less commercially strong.

3. Robust third‑party use in related financial services can narrow protection and affect commercial impression. If many third parties use the same word element (e.g., “Aspire”) across banking and financial services, that pattern may teach consumers to distinguish between similar marks and reduce the senior mark’s exclusivity. That, in turn, may make the first‑factor comparison less likely to favor a finding of confusion.

4. Litigating third‑party evidence is strategic and consequential. Applicants and registrants should present third‑party use evidence across all services that a Board has found similar, and should argue for a consistent relevant public and scope of similarity. Conversely, parties seeking to strengthen a mark’s protection should explain why third‑party uses are not sufficiently similar in nature, channels, or consumer perception to dilute the mark.

In short, factor one is an overall, contextual judgment about how consumers perceive marks, but that judgment depends on the marketplace context revealed by factor six. When the Board’s definitions of similarity diverge across factors, the Federal Circuit will require reconciliation and, if necessary, remand for a consistent, integrated analysis.
