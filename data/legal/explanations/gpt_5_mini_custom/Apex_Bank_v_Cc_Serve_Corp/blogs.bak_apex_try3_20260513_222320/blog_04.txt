Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# ASPIRE vs. ASPIRE BANK: Strength as a Lens on Commercial Impression

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. turns on a lesson trademark practitioners have long known but sometimes forget: commercial strength shapes commercial impression. The Federal Circuit’s September 25, 2025 decision does not finally resolve whether ASPIRE BANK may register, but it makes clear that the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board must reassess the first DuPont factor (the marks’ overall commercial impression) after properly evaluating the sixth DuPont factor (the number and nature of similar marks in use). In practical terms, evidence that “ASPIRE” is common across the financial-services landscape could dilute CC Serve’s standard-character ASPIRE mark and change the likelihood-of-confusion calculus against Apex’s ASPIRE BANK filing. The remand therefore promises a careful, and potentially different, look at how market crowding influences whether consumers will mistake ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK as coming from the same source.

## The Federal Circuit’s reasoning, in brief

The Board initially found the parties’ services highly similar—indeed, “legally identical, in part”—and held the second DuPont factor strongly in CC Serve’s favor. But when the Board turned to the sixth factor, it limited its inquiry to third-party uses of ASPIRE in connection with credit-card services only, excluding ASPIRE uses in broader banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit rejected that narrowing. Because the Board had already concluded the services were highly similar, the panel said, it must use that same scope when assessing crowdedness under the sixth factor: similar goods/services, not identical labels, govern what third-party marks are “similar.” Having confined the sixth-factor universe too narrowly, the Board’s conclusion that ASPIRE deserved the “normal” scope of protection could not stand. And because commercial impression under the first DuPont factor can be materially affected by how strong or weak a mark is in the marketplace, the court vacated the Board’s first-factor analysis as well and remanded both issues for reconsideration.

The court affirmed the Board only on the second DuPont factor—i.e., the services’ similarity—finding substantial evidence supported the Board’s determination.

## Strength as the pivot of commercial impression

This opinion is a tidy application of established principles: third-party usage that is “voluminous” or otherwise shows a crowded field can demonstrate conceptual or commercial weakness and narrow a mark’s scope of protection; a weaker mark will generate a different commercial impression than an inherently strong one. The Federal Circuit’s remand underscores two doctrinal points worth underscoring.

First, consistency in scope matters. Once the Board defines the relevant goods or services for the second DuPont factor, it cannot pick a different, narrower scope for the sixth factor without explaining why. The same marketplace that makes credit-card and banking/financing services “similar” for confusion purposes determines which competitors’ marks are relevant for assessing strength.

Second, the evaluation of strength is inherently comparative and factual. The Board cited only nine third‑party ASPIRE-formative marks in the credit-card space and concluded that was not “considerable” enough to show weakness. Apex claimed, and submitted evidence of, many more ASPIRE-formative uses across financial services; the Federal Circuit’s remand requires the Board to address that broader record. The upshot: a handful of third-party uses won’t suffice when the record suggests many more; conversely, even many registrations may be less probative than proof of real marketplace use and consumer recognition.

## How “BANK” and design elements will be treated on remand

The opinion itself does not rule on whether “BANK” will be treated as descriptive or whether Apex’s design elements will change the commercial impression. But familiar doctrinal rules give a preview of the issues the Board will confront.

“Bank” is a generic or highly descriptive term for banking services. In cases where an applicant’s mark pairs a potentially distinctive word with a generic or descriptive term (e.g., ASPIRE + BANK), tribunals often view the generic/descriptive element as weak or incapable of serving as the dominant source-identifier. That means the consumer is likelier to focus on “ASPIRE” as the distinctive portion of Apex’s mark. For a standard-character registration like CC Serve’s ASPIRE, protection extends across typefaces and stylizations, so word-only rights can be potent even where an applicant has a word-plus-design application. On the other hand, if the Board finds ASPIRE to be conceptually or commercially weak because of widespread third-party use across banking/financial services, the added, descriptive “BANK” and any modest design flourishes will do little to persuade a finding of likely confusion.

Thus on remand the Board must do at least three things: (1) consider the full universe of ASPIRE-formative marks used in banking and broader financial services consistent with its second-factor finding; (2) assess not just registrations but the nature and extent of actual marketplace use—how the public encounters the marks, length and geographic scope of use, and evidence of consumer perception or marketplace recognition; and (3) integrate that strength assessment into a fresh analysis of the overall commercial impression of ASPIRE versus ASPIRE BANK (and any design), keeping in mind that descriptive elements like “BANK” typically get little weight.

## What to watch for on remand

Practitioners should watch several discrete issues when the Board revisits the record. First, will the Board broaden the set of third-party marks it considers to include banking and financing uses (not just credit-card program marks)? The Federal Circuit expressly signaled that it should. Second, how will the Board treat third-party registrations versus documented uses? Registrations are informative but not conclusive; evidence that customers actually encounter many ASPIRE-brand financial services is far more persuasive of weakness. Third, will the Board give controlling weight to evidence showing a small number of uses (as it initially did) or to Apex’s contention of more pervasive use across financial subfields? And finally, will the Board explicitly analyze whether “BANK” functions as a merely descriptive element that fails to distinguish Apex’s mark from CC Serve’s word-only ASPIRE?

Each of these factual findings—how many relevant third-party uses truly exist, how consumers experience the marks, and the communicative force of “BANK” and any design—can tip the balance. If ASPIRE is shown to be commonplace in the financial services field, CC Serve’s mark will be entitled to only a narrow scope of protection and Apex’s ASPIRE BANK mark may survive. If ASPIRE remains commercially strong, or the Board finds that the third‑party uses are not sufficiently similar or pervasive, the refusal to register could be sustained.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

A few broader lessons follow from the Federal Circuit’s remand. Boards and practitioners must maintain doctrinal coherence across DuPont factors: findings about the similarity of services constrain the universe of relevant third‑party marks. Equally, applicants should be mindful of choosing marks built around common formative words in their industry; a crowded field invites an argument for a narrowed scope of protection. Registrants relying on long-standing standard-character registrations should document not only priority but also marketplace recognition and exclusivity of use where possible. Finally, the decision reiterates the practical importance of submitting real-world evidence—screen shots, advertising, sales, or consumer surveys—when arguing commercial strength or weakness.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is not a dramatic departure from settled DuPont doctrine, but it is an important reminder that strength is the lens through which commercial impression must be viewed. By insisting that the Board keep its scope consistent across factors, the Federal Circuit has ensured the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry will meaningfully account for market crowdedness. The remand will be the place to watch whether ASPIRE is a household financial-services signpost or one of many similar beacons in the banking and credit-card firmament—and that factual determination may well decide whether ASPIRE BANK can enter the register.
