Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# What Counts as “Credit Card Services” When the ID Manual Moves On?

## Why this case matters
Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful reminder that legacy identifications of services can have powerful—and sometimes surprising—reach long after the Trademark ID Manual removes an entry. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s finding that “credit card services” overlapped significantly with “banking and financing services,” but it also corrected the Board for an inconsistent, and too-narrow, treatment of third‑party uses when measuring mark strength. The decision underscores two enduring points for trademark practitioners: tribunals will interpret legacy IDs using ordinary‑tool evidence (dictionaries, industry usage, third‑party registrations), and those interpretations can make services “legally identical, in part” even where the parties perform different roles in the payments ecosystem. How parties draft applications, prosecute registrations, and litigate oppositions should follow.

## What the court did
The Board had sustained CC Serve’s opposition to Apex’s intent‑to‑use registration for ASPIRE BANK, finding the marks likely to cause confusion. The Board read an older ID—“credit card services”—broadly to include issuance, processing, authorization, account management, monitoring and alerts, and counseling about card debt. Because Apex’s claimed “banking and financing services” overlap with those functions, the Board concluded the services were highly similar (indeed, “legally identical, in part”), and the second DuPont factor therefore weighed heavily in favor of confusion.

On appeal the Federal Circuit affirmed that holding: substantial evidence supported the Board’s conclusion that the services were highly similar. But the court vacated the Board’s analysis of the sixth DuPont factor (number and nature of similar marks in use) and, because the first‑factor (similarity of the marks) analysis depends on the mark’s commercial strength, vacated that too. The problem was procedural and conceptual: having found the services highly similar for DuPont two, the Board then restricted the universe of third‑party “Aspire” uses it considered to only those expressly labeled “credit card services,” excluding uses tied to broader banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit held that was inconsistent—once the Board adopts a scope of similarity for the services, it must keep that scope when addressing other relevant DuPont factors. The case was remanded for reconsideration of factors one and six in light of the Board’s findings on factor two.

## How tribunals interpret legacy identifications
A few practical lessons about method:

- Tribunals do not treat deletion from the ID Manual as an eraser. When a registration identifies services using an entry that the Office later deleted, the Board will still construe the words of the registration using ordinary tools: dictionary definitions, industry practice, and third‑party registrations and marketplace evidence.
- Dictionaries and definitional materials remain a common starting point. The Board here relied in part on conventional definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” to show overlap with functions typically thought of as “credit card services.”
- Third‑party registrations and real‑world evidence (how businesses actually brand and market services) matter for showing that different services can emanate from a single source and for evaluating commercial strength.
- The DuPont factors must be applied coherently. A Board finding that two services are highly similar should inform, not contradict, the analysis of third‑party use and mark strength under DuPont six and the overall commercial impression under DuPont one.

## The risk of expansive readings
Reading an older, short identification expansively can be decisive. A registrant with a relatively old or broadly worded registration can defeat an ITU applicant who intends to offer different—but functionally related—services. That can be especially consequential in the financial services and payments space, where banks, card issuers, processors, program managers, and servicers occupy overlapping but distinct roles. The Board’s definition here turned a business that partners with banks to run branded card programs into a competitor, for likelihood‑of‑confusion purposes, of a bank that does not issue cards—simply because the generic functions (issuing, processing, account servicing) sit within the same conceptual sphere.

But expansive readings carry risks too. Overbroad constructions can improperly magnify a registrant’s protection absent strong evidence of marketplace use or consumer perception; they can also invite appellate correction if the Board applies inconsistent scopes across DuPont factors, as happened here.

## Practical takeaways for applicants and registrants

For applicants (and their counsel):
- Don’t file broad ITU identifications unless you mean them. Where possible, tailor identifications to the specific services you will offer and to the channels and customers you will target (e.g., “retail banking services provided through an online platform” rather than just “banking services”).
- If you need broader coverage, build an evidentiary record to support non‑overlap: declarations about intended channels, sample marketing plans, and (for later proceedings) evidence showing distinct purchasers and trade channels.
- Consider limiting language by amendment or accepting a more specific identification during prosecution to reduce the risk of an opposition grounded on legacy definitions.
- If you cannot narrow at the PTO stage, be prepared to litigate on the factual record: show differences in function, purchasers, or ordinary trade channels; employ expert testimony or consumer surveys if appropriate.

For registrants (and their counsel):
- Preserve and develop evidence of actual use and licensing, particularly where the registration predates the ID Manual change. Specimens, partner agreements, and examples of how the mark is presented in the marketplace will support a broader reading.
- Use third‑party registrations and evidence to show the ordinary trade practice of bundling similar financial functions under single brands.
- When asserting likelihood of confusion, be consistent: if you rely on a broad reading of your registration to show overlap for DuPont two, make sure your inventory of third‑party uses for DuPont six reflects that same scope.

For both sides in litigation:
- Don’t treat the ID Manual as determinative. The Board and courts will interpret the words of the registration in light of contextual evidence.
- Be mindful that the Board must be consistent across DuPont factors. If a party gets a favorable factual finding on similarity, it should not be disadvantaged when the same factual scope is relevant to third‑party use and mark strength.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
This decision reinforces that: (1) legacy identifications can be broadly consequential; (2) the Federal Circuit will police internal consistency in the Board’s DuPont analyses; and (3) proof of marketplace realities—how services actually operate and are presented—can be decisive. The remand on factors one and six opens the door for the Board to revisit the mark‑strength analysis: if the Board on remand considers the broader set of third‑party “Aspire” uses (including those tied to banking and financing services), it might find the mark weaker and narrow CC Serve’s protection, which could alter the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion calculus.

More broadly, expect practitioners in banking, payments, and adjacent sectors to pay greater attention to legacy IDs. Oppositions to ITU filings for digital or internet‑only banks (a growing trend) may increasingly invoke older registrations that courts and the Board will construe according to ordinary meaning, not the present contents of the ID Manual.

## Bottom line
Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a cautionary tale: words in a registration—old or new—can travel far. Deleting an entry from the ID Manual does not eliminate the need to define and, where necessary, defend or narrow the scope of services in a registration. The decision also fortifies a simple procedural rule with substantive bite: once the Board adopts a factual scope for service similarity, that scope must govern related DuPont inquiries. Practitioners should treat identifications as strategic drafting choices and should build evidentiary records on marketplace use and customer perception from the outset.
