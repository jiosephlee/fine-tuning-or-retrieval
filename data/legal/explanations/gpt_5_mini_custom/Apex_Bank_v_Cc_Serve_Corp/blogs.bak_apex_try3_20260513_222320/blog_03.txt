Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# Juice Generation, Recharged: Third-Party Use Evidence in a Crowded Financial Field

## Why Apex Bank v. CC Serve matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a timely corrective to a recurring practical and doctrinal problem at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board: when the Board finds two services to be highly similar (or legally identical in part), it cannot then narrow the universe of third‑party marks considered under DuPont factor six to a tighter slice of the market. In doing so, the court reaffirmed that evidence of third‑party registrations and use must be evaluated across the range of similar goods or services—not artificially constrained to a single subcategory—when gauging a mark’s commercial strength. For parties in the financial‑services space (and for TTAB practice generally), Apex Bank signals that a “crowded field” argument must be built and rebutted on the full marketplace the parties occupy.

## The basics of the case

CC Serve owns a long‑registered ASPIRE mark used in connection with credit‑card services; Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services (it does not itself issue credit cards). The TTAB sustained CC Serve’s opposition under Section 2(d), finding a high degree of overlap between “credit card services” and “banking and financing services” and concluding the ASPIRE mark was entitled to the normal scope of protection. Apex appealed, arguing among other things that the Board improperly narrowed its sixth DuPont analysis to marks used only in connection with credit‑card services, excluding “Aspire” uses across broader banking and financing services.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar, but it vacated and remanded the Board’s treatment of DuPont factor six (number and nature of similar marks in use) because the Board had limited its inquiry to credit‑card marks even after concluding the services were highly similar more broadly. Because factor one (the marks’ overall commercial impression) is linked to commercial strength, the court also vacated and remanded that factor for reconsideration in light of the expanded sixth‑factor analysis.

## How this fits with Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin

Apex Bank rides squarely on the reasoning in cases like Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin: third‑party use and registration evidence can show that a field is “crowded,” reducing a mark’s commercial strength and narrowing the scope of protection. Juice Generation treated 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number” indicating crowding, while Jack Wolfskin referred to “voluminous evidence” of similar third‑party uses. But the Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex reinforces an important corollary: the relevant universe of third‑party marks to assess for crowding must be defined consistently with the Board’s finding about the similarity of the parties’ services. If the Board finds services overlap substantially, it cannot then cabin the third‑party inquiry to a narrower subdomain on the premise that only that slice is “relevant.”

That principle prevents the Board from effectively nullifying the sixth factor by narrowing the comparator set after using a broader definition to find similarity on factor two. The court’s instruction avoids circular or cherry‑picked analyses: once you conclude the services are similar, you must consider third‑party uses across that same similarity scope.

## What third‑party evidence does (and doesn’t) prove

Two frequently conflated concepts arise in factor six disputes: third‑party registrations and third‑party use in commerce. Both can be relevant, but they carry different weight.

- Third‑party registrations can suggest that other applicants consider the term available and that the term serves a common or weak source‑identifying function. Registrations alone, however, are not proof that consumers have encountered the marks in the marketplace or been trained to distinguish among them.

- Actual marketplace use (websites, advertising, product or service offerings, merchant materials, press coverage, licensing or partnership agreements, consumer exposure metrics, sales and longevity data) is stronger evidence that consumers are familiar with the term in a competitive sense and are equipped to differentiate similar marks.

The Federal Circuit’s cited precedents treated both registration and use evidence as relevant, but they focused on the degree of marketplace appearance: courts have found 26 active third‑party marks persuasive, and Jack Wolfskin emphasized “voluminous” use. Apex emphasizes that the Board must look beyond a trimmed set of sub‑category uses when services overlap.

## Practical takeaways for practitioners in financial services

For counsel preparing opposition or defense records in the financial sector, Apex Bank suggests a few specific, practical moves.

For challengers seeking to show crowding (opposers or registrants attacking a mark’s strength):
- Do not limit searches or evidence to a single transactional label (e.g., “credit cards”) if the opponent’s services are alleged to overlap with broader banking or financing services. Gather examples across the full range of related financial services the Board has found similar.
- Prioritize documented marketplace use over bare registrations. Capture website screenshots, advertising, merchant program pages, product descriptions, press releases, and partnership announcements, and use the Wayback Machine or dated captures to establish longevity.
- Quantify exposure where possible: geographic reach, dates of first use, estimated user or account counts, number of issuing banks or partners, and (if available) media impressions or ad spend.
- Authenticate evidence with declarants who can attest to the sourcing and accuracy of copies, and where appropriate, obtain declarations from industry participants about common practice and consumer channels (e.g., how consumers access banking vs. card services).

For applicants/registrants seeking to defend strength:
- If the field is crowded, show why the particular mark has distinctiveness or secondary meaning: consumer surveys, brand recognition evidence, unique stylization, or substantial and exclusive use in a particular channel can help.
- Distinguish the relevant consumer: demonstrate differences in channels of trade, customer sophistication, or purchasing process—for instance, if a bank’s deposit‑account customers differ materially from credit‑card program customers, make that record explicit.
- Attack the quality of third‑party evidence: show many registrations lack proof of use, are geographically limited, brand names are descriptive/weak, or that the third parties operate in separate channels not likely to be encountered by the same consumer.

Do not rely on registrations alone. The Board and court will look to whether consumers actually confront the marks in commerce—especially where the contested mark is common or descriptive.

## Doctrinal and practical downstream effects

Apex Bank should nudge TTAB practice toward more disciplined, consistent DuPont analyses. Practically, expect:

- Broader third‑party searches and more voluminous records in oppositions and applications involving general terms in financial services. Parties will marshal broader evidence earlier in proceedings.
- More contested facts about the scope of similarity between services at the outset, since that finding materially shapes the scope of what third‑party evidence the Board must weigh.
- Increased reliance on authenticated marketplace evidence (screenshots, dated captures, declarations) and on metrics that show consumer exposure.
- More remands where the Board narrows the sixth factor after adopting a broader scope on the second factor; the Federal Circuit has now made that an error to watch for.

Finally, the decision reiterates a basic substantive point: trademark adjudicators must be consistent in defining the relevant market across DuPont factors. Narrowing the third‑party comparator set after acknowledging broader service overlap is legally untenable and strategically risky.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is a useful reminder that proof of a crowded field must be considered against the full backdrop of similarity the Board has already found. In financial services—where product lines and distribution channels often bleed into one another—counsel should think expansively when compiling third‑party evidence, prioritize demonstrable marketplace use, and be ready to defend or attack commercial‑strength arguments with concrete metrics that show whether consumers have in fact been trained to distinguish among similar marks. The Federal Circuit’s remand clears the way for the TTAB to take that broader view.
