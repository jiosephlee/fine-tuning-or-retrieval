Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does Minn. Stat. § 181.101 authorize employees to recover "average daily wage penalties," or are such penalties enforceable only by the Commissioner?
Question:
I'm confused about the interaction between MPWA §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 says an employer who violates certain MPWA sections is liable for "compensatory damages and other appropriate relief," and also for "the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated." Cohen argued § 181.101's plain language lets employees obtain average daily wage penalties, but the panel held only the Commissioner can collect penalties under § 181.101(a) after serving a demand for payment. How does the court reconcile § 181.171's broad remedial language with § 181.101's Commissioner-centric penalty mechanism? Is the key that § 181.101 "provides for" civil penalties only for the Commissioner, whereas other MPWA sections (like §§ 181.13 and 181.14) expressly grant employees penalty rights? If so, is "other appropriate relief" under § 181.171 limited to non-penalty relief unless the violated section expressly authorizes private penalties?
Answer:
Short answer: The Eighth Circuit held that Minn. Stat. § 181.101 does not authorize a private plaintiff to recover the statute’s “average daily wage” penalty; § 181.101’s penalty mechanism is enforced by the Commissioner (who, if she collects, pays the money to the employee). Section 181.171’s broad remedial language does not convert § 181.101’s Commissioner‑centered penalty into a private cause of action.

Why: statutory text and structure
- § 181.171 allows an employee to sue for violations of certain MPWA provisions and provides that an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” The Eighth Circuit read that cross‑reference literally: an employee can recover the penalties only if the particular MPWA provision “provides for” private enforcement of that penalty.
- § 181.101(a), however, assigns the penalty process to the Commissioner: the Commissioner must first serve a demand for payment, and only “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty” if wages are not paid within ten days. The statute expressly reserves to the employee only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” The court emphasized that the statute “only references the Commissioner regarding penalties” and that “[p]enalties are only available if the Commissioner first ‘serve[s] a demand for payment . . . on behalf of an employee.’” (Op. at A. (quoting § 181.101(a)).)

Why that resolves the tension between §§ 181.171 and 181.101
- The court reconciled the provisions by applying § 181.171’s qualifier “provided for in the section violated.” That means § 181.171 does not give employees a freestanding right to any and all penalties; it makes available only those statutory penalties that the particular MPWA section itself authorizes a private plaintiff to recover.
- The opinion pointed out that the Legislature knows how to give employees a private penalty remedy when it wants to (e.g., Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13 and 181.14 explicitly allow an employee to “charge and collect a penalty” on demand). Because § 181.101 instead vests the charging/collecting power with the Commissioner, the court concluded the penalty there is not one the employee may enforce privately. (Op. at A; citing Lagasse and the “knows how” principle.)

Practical consequence (as the court put it)
- Employees can still pursue compensatory wages and other non‑penalty relief under § 181.171, and § 181.101 preserves an employee’s right to prosecute a claim for unpaid wages. But the specific “average daily wage” penalty in § 181.101 is recoverable through the Commissioner’s statutory enforcement route only; a private plaintiff may not independently recover that penalty under § 181.171. (Op. at A.)

So: yes — the key is that § 181.101 “provides for” penalties enforceable by the Commissioner, not by private plaintiffs, while other MPWA sections expressly authorize private penalty collection. “Other appropriate relief” under § 181.171 therefore does not expand a private right to recover penalties unless the violated MPWA section itself supplies that private‑enforcement mechanism.

### Can a private MFLSA plaintiff seek statutory penalties that are payable only to the Commissioner, and does Laidlaw provide Article III standing in that scenario?
Question:
The opinion says the parties agree MFLSA statutory penalties (unlike wages and liquidated damages) are payable to the Commissioner, not Cohen. The court remands to determine jurisdiction, citing Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw for the idea that civil penalties can deter and thus redress ongoing harms. But the concurrence questions whether Laidlaw fits the MFLSA context. Is there a Minnesota-law basis for a private plaintiff to ask a federal court to impose MFLSA penalties that are then paid to the Commissioner? If yes, does Laidlaw's deterrence rationale supply Article III redressability when the plaintiff doesn't receive the penalties? What differences between Laidlaw's citizen-suit environmental penalties and MFLSA penalties might defeat standing here (e.g., statutory design, who may sue, state vs. federal enforcement scheme)?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under the Eighth Circuit opinion, Minnesota law treats MFLSA statutory penalties as payable to the Commissioner (the parties so concede), but the court did not decide whether a private plaintiff may obtain an order imposing those penalties in a private suit. The court vacated and remanded on jurisdictional grounds rather than resolving that question.
- The court relied on Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw to explain that civil penalties payable to the government can, by deterring ongoing violations, afford Article III redressability — so Laidlaw supplies a plausible path to standing where the plaintiff can show ongoing or likely-to-recur unlawful conduct. But the concurrence warns that Laidlaw may not map neatly onto the MFLSA context because of differences in statutory design and enforcement; the panel left these matters for the district court on remand.

What the opinion actually establishes (doctrine and controlling points)
- Standing and redressability basics: the court reiterates the settled Article III standards — a plaintiff must have standing for each claim and each form of relief, and that standing must continue throughout the litigation (citing Town of Chester and Sisney). The relevant elements include injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability (opinion ¶ II.B).
- Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale: the panel quotes Laidlaw for the proposition that “civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may ‘afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct’ because such penalties may ‘encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones’” (opinion ¶ II.B (quoting Laidlaw, 528 U.S. at 186)). The court used that principle to explain why statutory penalties payable to the state do not automatically doom a plaintiff’s Article III claim.
- Mootness/voluntary cessation: the opinion applies Laidlaw’s related principle that a claim may become moot if it is “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur,” while also noting that voluntary cessation does not automatically deprive courts of jurisdiction (opinion ¶ II.B (quoting Laidlaw and City of Mesquite)).
- MPWA contrast: the court conclusively held that under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties and that money collected by the Commissioner is to be paid to the employee (opinion ¶ II.A). That statutory allocation informed the court’s disposition on the MPWA claim; it did not resolve the MFLSA penalty question (opinion ¶ II.A–B).

How these points affect a private MFLSA plaintiff seeking Commissioner-payable penalties
- Minnesota-law basis: the opinion shows the MFLSA penalties at issue are payable to the Commissioner (the parties agreed). But the Eighth Circuit did not decide whether Minnesota law permits a private plaintiff, in a private suit, to ask the federal court to impose those penalties (the panel expressly remanded to resolve jurisdiction/mootness rather than rule on entitlement to penalties) (opinion ¶ II.B). By contrast, for the MPWA the court did hold only the Commissioner may seek certain penalties (opinion ¶ II.A).
- Laidlaw as redress: the court signals that Laidlaw provides a workable theory of redressability — even penalties that go to the government can redress an injured plaintiff by deterring ongoing violations — and thus could, in an appropriate factual posture, supply the Article III link for a plaintiff who otherwise faces only penalties payable to the Commissioner (opinion ¶ II.B).
- But the court did not adopt Laidlaw as a per se rule in the MFLSA context. The special concurrence voices skepticism that Laidlaw necessarily fits here (Kobes, J., concurring specially), and the panel remanded so the district court can address whether the case is moot or whether standing exists under the particular facts and statutory scheme.

Practical differences that may defeat standing on remand (drawn from the opinion’s reasoning)
- Statutory design/enforcement scheme: Laidlaw involved environmental enforcement where deterrent penalties were an accepted means of addressing ongoing harms. The concurrence suggests differences in how the MFLSA penalties are structured (payable to the Commissioner, with enforcement by the Commissioner) could weaken the argument that a court-imposed penalty that goes to the state meaningfully redresses a private plaintiff’s injury. The district court should examine whether the MFLSA’s statutory design contemplates (or permits) private suits that effectively use penalties as deterrence-based relief.
- Who may sue / who collects the penalty: the MPWA analysis shows Minnesota statutes can, and sometimes do, restrict the ability to obtain penalties to the Commissioner (opinion ¶ II.A). If the MFLSA likewise reserves practical enforcement and penalty collection to the Commissioner, a private plaintiff’s prayer for penalties might be more properly characterized as a request to order relief that benefits the public rather than redresses a private injury — which affects redressability.
- Ongoing or likely-to-recur injury: the remand instruction focuses on whether Consilio’s overtime policy remains in effect. Laidlaw-based redress depends on a plaintiff showing ongoing or threatened injury such that a deterrent penalty is likely to abate the harm. If the policy has been rescinded and recurrence is not reasonably expected, the claim may be moot (opinion ¶ II.B).
- Causation and likelihood of redress: even if a penalty might deter future violations, the plaintiff must show it is not merely speculative that a penalty paid to the Commissioner will likely prevent recurrence of the particular harm to the plaintiff (Town of Chester and the court’s standing/mootness framework, quoted in opinion ¶ II.B).

Takeaway for the remand
- The Eighth Circuit left the key questions to the district court: (1) Is there a live controversy (i.e., is Consilio’s overtime policy still in force or is recurrence likely)? (2) If so, can Cohen establish Article III standing to seek penalties payable to the Commissioner — including by showing that imposing such penalties would likely deter the defendant and redress his ongoing injury under Laidlaw? (opinion ¶ II.B).
- Expect the district court to parse the MFLSA statutory text and enforcement scheme (as the panel did for the MPWA) and to apply Article III redressability rigorously in light of Laidlaw’s deterrence theory and the concurrence’s caution about differences between environmental citizen-suits and MFLSA enforcement.

Bottom line
- The opinion does not hold that a private MFLSA plaintiff may or may not obtain Commissioner-payable penalties; it leaves that open. It confirms, however, that Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale is a plausible route to Article III redressability when penalties go to the government — but whether that route survives careful scrutiny under Minnesota’s MFLSA (given who may sue, who collects penalties, and the statutory enforcement structure, plus whether the defendant’s conduct is ongoing) is a question the district court must decide on remand.

### What must Consilio show on remand to establish mootness via voluntary cessation of its overtime policy, and who bears the burden?
Question:
The panel vacates and remands the MFLSA penalty issue because the record is unclear whether Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy is still in place. If the policy is gone and won't recur, the claim may be moot; if not, Laidlaw suggests penalties could still deter. On remand, what evidence must Consilio present to make it "absolutely clear" the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur? Does Consilio bear a "heavy burden" under the voluntary cessation doctrine? Practically, would declarations of a permanent policy change suffice, or does the court look for written rescission, compliance audits, or other objective indicia? And does the fact that wages and liquidated damages were paid already affect the mootness analysis for penalties?
Answer:
Short answer
- On remand Consilio must show — by evidence sufficient to make “absolutely clear” that the allegedly unlawful overtime policy has been abandoned and cannot reasonably be expected to recur — that the MFLSA penalty claim is moot. The panel framed the standard by quoting Friends of the Earth: mootness requires it be “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur. Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. at II.B (quoting Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 189).
- The burden to make that showing falls on the party asserting mootness (here, Consilio). The opinion treats mootness as an affirmative jurisdictional question to be decided on remand and relies on the Laidlaw standard that the defendant must show the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur.
- Practically, the court will look for objective, verifiable indicia (not just a conclusory declaration) that the policy is gone and will not be repeated — e.g., formal written rescission or replacement of the policy, company-wide communications showing the change, changes to payroll practice or personnel rules, and demonstrable compliance measures or audits. The panel remanded because the record is simply “unclear whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy is still in place,” so objective evidence addressing that gap is what the district court needs. Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. at II.B.
- The fact that wages and liquidated damages have already been paid does not automatically moot the penalties claim. The panel explicitly noted that penalties (payable to the Commissioner) can still provide redress by deterring ongoing or future violations, so payment of wages/liquidated damages to the plaintiff does not by itself defeat a penalties claim; whether the policy remains in place controls the mootness question. Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. at II.B (citing Laidlaw, 528 U.S. at 186–89).

Explanation and how the district court should apply it
1. The controlling legal standard
- The opinion relies on Friends of the Earth / Laidlaw: a claim may be moot if “subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. at II.B (quoting Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 189). The court also reminds that mere voluntary cessation ordinarily does not divest the court of power to decide the legality of the practice (quoting City of Mesquite), so a defendant’s unilateral cessation must satisfy the “absolutely clear” standard. Id. (citing City of Mesquite, 455 U.S. at 289).
2. Who bears the burden
- The party invoking mootness bears the burden of proving it. The panel framed mootness as a jurisdictional question that the district court must decide on remand; consistent with Laidlaw, that means Consilio — the party asserting that the case is moot because the policy has ended — must make the showing that recurrence is impossible or wholly unlikely. See Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. at II.B (discussing Laidlaw standard and remanding for the district court to determine jurisdiction).
3. What the showing should look like in practice
- The panel vacated and remanded expressly because the record was “unclear whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy is still in place.” Id. That tells you what the district court needs: objective proof that the policy has been rescinded and that nothing in the employer’s current practices or incentives makes recurrence reasonably likely.
- While the opinion does not lay out an exhaustive evidentiary checklist, courts applying the “absolutely clear” standard look for more than a lawyer’s or executive’s conclusory statement. On remand, useful, persuasive, and verifiable evidence would include (but is not limited to):
  - A formal, written rescission or replacement of the 2019 policy (board or HR policy documents showing the old policy revoked).
  - Company-wide communications announcing the rescission and instructions to payroll/HR to comply with overtime rules.
  - Payroll records, timekeeping, or wage payments showing compliance with overtime law after the alleged change.
  - Evidence of compliance programs, audits, or monitoring showing the company is taking steps to prevent recurrence (and remedial steps already implemented).
  - Any binding corporate-level commitments or changes to incentive structures that would remove the motive to revert to the old policy.
- Declarations can help, but under the “absolutely clear” standard they are unlikely to be dispositive if unsupported by documentary or objective proof. The panel’s remand—based on the absence of a clear record—signals that objective indicia will be required. Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. at II.B.
4. Effect of paying wages and liquidated damages
- The panel notes the parties stipulated that Cohen was paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, and it separately explains that civil penalties can still be meaningful because they may deter future violations even though penalties go to the Commissioner. Thus payment of wages/liquidated damages to the plaintiff does not automatically moot a penalties claim; whether the employer’s wrongful conduct still continues (or is likely to recur) remains the critical question. Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. at II.B (discussing Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale).

Bottom line
Consilio must carry the burden of showing, with objective and verifiable proof, that the offending overtime policy has been abandoned and that recurrence is not reasonably foreseeable — in other words, it must make the showing the Supreme Court described as making it “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur. Conclusory declarations alone are unlikely to satisfy that standard on the sparse record the panel described; documentary policy rescissions, communications, payroll compliance, audits, and other objective indicia are the kind of evidence the district court will expect to see. Payments of wages and liquidated damages do not by themselves moot the penalties claim because penalties serve a deterrent public purpose.

### Is there a private right or remedy for MWTA notice violations under § 181.032, and did Cohen waive any such claim on appeal?
Question:
Cohen alleged Consilio failed to provide the written notice of a change in overtime policy required by the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f), (d)(4), and sought penalties. The court affirmed dismissal, noting Cohen stipulated he received all pay due and pointed to no statutory authority allowing civil penalties under § 181.032, and further found he offered no meaningful appellate argument (citing waiver). Does § 181.032 create any private cause of action or remedy for notice failures independent of unpaid wages (e.g., statutory penalties, injunctive relief)? If remedies exist, are they enforceable only by the Commissioner, or can employees sue directly? Finally, was the court's waiver holding based on inadequate briefing alone, or also on the absence of a statutory penalty provision in § 181.032?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Eighth Circuit did not announce a new, independent private right to statutory penalties or injunctive relief under Minn. Stat. § 181.032. Instead, it affirmed dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA notice claim because Cohen (1) “point[ed] to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute” and (2) failed to make a “meaningful argument” on appeal — i.e., the issue was waived. (Opinion § II.C.)
- The court therefore did not hold, on the merits, that § 181.032 definitively authorizes penalties only through the Commissioner or that no private remedy can ever be available; it dismissed the claim on the basis described above and declined to reach detailed statutory construction. (See § II.C and II.D.)

Explanation and legal standards from the opinion
- Waiver on appeal for inadequate briefing. The court applied the familiar appellate rule that issues unsupported by argument or legal authority are waived. It cited Milligan and stated it “will not disturb the district court’s order” where the appellant “points to no statutory authority” and offers “no meaningful argument.” (Op. § II.C (citing Milligan v. City of Red Oak).) So one independent ground for affirmance was appellate-waiver for failing to brief the point.
- Lack of cited statutory authority. The opinion explicitly notes Cohen “points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute” (§ 181.032). That factual/legal premise (no authority pointed to in the briefs) was the other reason the panel declined to reverse the district court as to the MWTA notice claim. The court therefore rejected Cohen’s request for penalties on the practical ground that he did not identify a statutory basis for them in his appellate briefing. (Op. § II.C.)
- Injunctive relief not decided. The panel did not resolve whether § 181.032 supports an injunction. The court explained Cohen had altered his requested injunctive relief between his complaint and his summary-judgment briefing; because the new requests were not properly pleaded, the district court did not consider them and the appellate court declined to address them. The court pointed to the requirement that new claims be raised by amended complaint, not in opposition briefs (citing InfoDeli and Fed. R. Civ. P. 15). (Op. § II.D.)

What this means for your questions
- Does § 181.032 create a private cause of action or statutory penalties enforceable by an employee? The panel did not hold on the statute’s merits. It affirmed dismissal because Cohen failed to identify or argue any statutory authority for civil penalties under § 181.032 and thus waived the issue. The opinion does not itself establish that § 181.032 never supplies a private statutory penalty; it simply confirms that Cohen did not make the statutory-authority argument and that the court would not invent one for him.
- Are penalties under § 181.032 enforceable only by the Commissioner? The court’s discussion of Commissioner-only enforcement was applied to the MPWA (Minn. Stat. § 181.101) where the statute expressly gives the Commissioner the role in collecting penalties. The MWTA § 181.032 question was not resolved on the merits here. So you cannot treat this opinion as a definitive ruling that enforcement under § 181.032 is Commissioner-only.
- Was the waiver holding based only on inadequate briefing? No — the court’s reasoning invoked both points: Cohen “points to no statutory authority” for civil penalties under § 181.032, and he made “no meaningful argument” on appeal. Because of those twin deficiencies, the panel declined to disturb dismissal. (Op. § II.C.) In short, the dismissal rested on the combination of (a) absence of cited statutory basis in the briefs and (b) waiver for inadequate briefing, not on a full merits interpretation of § 181.032.

Practical takeaways for litigants
- If you want to press an MWTA notice-based penalty or an injunction on appeal, you must (a) identify the statutory text or authoritative sources that create the remedy and (b) develop the argument in the district court and on appeal — or risk waiver.
- If the statutory scheme explicitly assigns penalty enforcement to the Commissioner (as the court discussed for the MPWA), courts are likely to treat that allocation seriously; but whether § 181.032 itself does that here was not decided in Cohen.

### Did Cohen forfeit his request for injunctive relief by changing it at summary judgment, and could the court have treated it as a Rule 15 amendment?
Question:
Cohen's complaint sought certain injunctive relief, but at summary judgment he pivoted and asked for an order requiring Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The panel says the district court "understandably" didn't consider this new request because new claims must be raised by amended complaint (citing InfoDeli and Rule 15(a)). Could the court have construed Cohen's briefing as an implicit motion to amend, or used Rule 15(b)'s "amendment to conform to the evidence"? Does Rule 15(b) even apply at summary judgment (as opposed to trial)? If Cohen amends on remand to seek rescission specifically, does that help with standing/mootness, or would any injunctive request still be moot if the policy is already rescinded?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes — by raising a new form of relief for the first time in his summary‑judgment briefing (asking the court to order rescission of the 2019 policy), Cohen effectively forfeited that specific injunction for purposes of this appeal. The panel squarely relied on InfoDeli and Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a) to say new claims/relief must be pleaded, not sprung in opposition briefs (Opinion § II.D).
- The district court could have, in its discretion, treated Cohen’s briefing as seeking leave to amend under Rule 15(a), but it was not required to do so; the panel found it “understandable” the court did not consider an unpleaded remedy. The proper procedural route is an amended complaint (Opinion § II.D).
- Rule 15(b) (amendment to conform the pleadings to the evidence) is a trial‑stage device and does not ordinarily rescue a new claim or remedy first asserted in summary‑judgment papers; the panel’s reliance on Rule 15(a)/InfoDeli shows this was treated as a pleading‑stage problem (Opinion § II.D; see also the court’s separate mootness/standing discussion in § II.B).
- If Cohen amends on remand to expressly seek rescission, that only helps if the injunction would cure an ongoing injury — i.e., if Consilio’s policy is still in effect. If the policy is already rescinded such an injunction is likely moot (Opinion § II.B & § II.D).

Explanation and the controlling logic in the opinion
1) Why the panel treated the rescission request as forfeited
- The panel notes that Cohen “in his complaint … sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief, only to later change course and request different relief—withdrawing the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing.” It then held that “the district court—understandably—did not consider it,” citing InfoDeli and Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a). In plain terms: the court viewed the rescission request as a new form of relief/claim that should have been presented in an amended complaint, not shoehorned into opposition briefing (Opinion § II.D).

2) Could the district court have treated the briefing as an implicit motion to amend?
- Yes, as a matter of discretion the district court could have construed the briefing as asking for leave to amend and then applied Rule 15(a) standards (leave freely given unless there is prejudice, futility, undue delay, etc.). The panel’s citation of Rule 15(a) and InfoDeli indicates that the proper, orthodox procedure is an explicit amended complaint. The panel did not fault the district court for refusing to consider the new remedy; it merely treated the issue as not properly before the court on appeal (Opinion § II.D). So treating the brief as an implicit Rule 15(a) motion was available but not required, and doing so would have been within the district court’s discretion.

3) What about Rule 15(b) – “amendment to conform to the evidence”?
- Rule 15(b) is aimed at trial situations where the evidence adduced warrants treating the case as if a different claim or theory were pleaded; it permits amendment to conform pleadings to what was tried. The panel’s reasoning (requiring claims to be raised by amended complaint and citing InfoDeli/Rule 15(a)) treats this as a pleading problem that arose before trial—summary judgment—so Rule 15(b) is not the appropriate mechanism to revive an unpleaded, new remedy first argued in summary‑judgment papers. The opinion’s disposition (refusing to consider the new injunction because it was raised only in briefing) reflects that posture (Opinion § II.D).

4) Practical consequence on remand — standing and mootness
- The opinion’s Part II.B discusses standing/mootness for monetary penalties and makes the same principle applicable to injunctive relief: relief that would prevent ongoing harm is only meaningful if the allegedly wrongful policy remains in force. The court explains that a plaintiff must have a continuing personal interest and that claims may be moot if “it is absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur” (quoting Friends of the Earth), while also noting voluntary cessation does not automatically defeat jurisdiction (Opinion § II.B).
- Applied here: if Cohen files an amended complaint on remand expressly seeking rescission, that amendment only preserves a live injunctive claim if there is a live controversy — i.e., the 2019 policy (or a functionally equivalent practice) remains and he can show a concrete risk of future harm. If Consilio has already rescinded the policy and it is “absolutely clear” the conduct won’t recur, an injunction ordering rescission would be moot. Conversely, if the policy remains, an amended pleading seeking rescission would be the correct procedural vehicle to present that remedy and to allow the court to consider whether injunctive relief is warranted and whether Cohen has standing to seek it (Opinion §§ II.B, II.D).

Bottom line
- The panel treated the rescission request as forfeited on appeal because it was raised for the first time in summary‑judgment briefing rather than by amended complaint. The district court could have, but was not required to, treat the briefing as a Rule 15(a) motion to amend; Rule 15(b) (conforming to the evidence at trial) is not the cure for an unpleaded remedy first raised at summary judgment. On remand, Cohen’s proper route is to amend his complaint to seek rescission; whether that amendment preserves a live injunctive claim depends on whether Consilio’s policy (or the risk of its recurrence) still exists — if the policy has already been rescinded and there is no reasonable expectation it will recur, an injunction ordering rescission will likely be moot (Opinion §§ II.B, II.D).

### Should the district court have resolved Article III standing/mootness before granting summary judgment, and does the parties’ stipulation affect that sequencing?
Question:
The parties stipulated to a procedure where the court would decide summary judgment on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims, acknowledging a full grant would end the case. The panel ultimately vacates the MFLSA ruling for a jurisdiction check. Under Steel Co.'s prohibition on hypothetical jurisdiction, should the district court have addressed standing/mootness on the MFLSA penalties issue before reaching the merits? Can a parties’ stipulation about an "expedient path" ever justify deciding the merits first, or must the court always confirm Article III jurisdiction sua sponte? If jurisdiction was uncertain only as to one claim, was it proper to reach the merits of the other claims while remanding the jurisdictional question for the MFLSA claim?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes — where Article III jurisdiction (standing/mootness) is genuinely in doubt for a particular form of relief, the court should resolve that threshold issue before deciding the merits of that relief. The Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s MFLSA penalty ruling for that reason.  
- A private stipulation about an “expedient” litigation path cannot manufacture Article III standing; it cannot substitute for the court’s obligation to be satisfied that a live case or controversy exists for each claim and each form of relief.  
- If jurisdictional doubt attaches only to one claim or one remedy, the court may properly decide the other claims whose jurisdiction is not in question and remand the doubtful claim for a jurisdictional determination — which is exactly what the panel did here.

Why (doctrine and how the opinion applies)
- Article III standing and mootness are threshold requirements that must exist for “each claim” and “for each form of relief,” and the “requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness).” The panel quotes Town of Chester and Sisney for this point and emphasizes the continuing nature of the personal interest requirement. (Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. § II.B.)
- The panel identified a concrete jurisdictional concern: Cohen stipulated that he had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, and the parties agree that any statutory penalties available under the MFLSA would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. Because penalties payable to the government may or may not confer a continuing personal stake (they can sometimes provide redress by deterring future violations, but only if the wrongful conduct can reasonably be expected to recur), the court could not be sure the MFLSA penalty claim survived as a live controversy. The panel stated it “ha[d] a serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue” and therefore vacated the MFLSA summary-judgment ruling and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction. (Id. § II.B.)
- The panel’s actions reflect the settled sequencing rule: you cannot reliably adjudicate a substantive claim when it is unclear whether Article III permits the court to do so. The parties’ joint request to take an “expedient and efficient path” and their stipulation that payment resolved wage/liquidated-damages claims did not resolve the distinct Article III question whether Cohen retained a personal stake in penalties payable to the Commissioner.

On the parties’ stipulation and the court’s duty
- Parties can agree to procedures and to which issues to prioritize, but they cannot by stipulation confer Article III jurisdiction where it does not exist. The opinion shows that the Eighth Circuit will not let a procedural stipulation substitute for the court’s independent obligation to satisfy itself that a live case or controversy remains for the relief sought. The district court may heed the parties’ agreed sequencing when jurisdictional facts are clear, but when jurisdiction is genuinely doubtful as to a claim or remedy, the court must address that doubt before the merits of that claim.

On deciding some claims but not others
- It was proper here for the court of appeals to affirm resolution of the MPWA and MWTA claims while vacating the MFLSA penalties ruling and remanding that single issue. The panel affirmed other rulings whose jurisdictional posture was not in doubt and remanded only the MFLSA claim because the jurisdictional question was unique to that claim and remedy. (See id. §§ II.A, II.C, III.)

Practical takeaway
- If a claim’s viability turns on facts bearing on standing or mootness (e.g., whether a challenged policy remains in effect; who would receive any penalty), the district court should — and the appellate courts expect it to — resolve jurisdictional questions for that claim before deciding its merits. A stipulation about case management or “efficiency” does not relieve the court of that Article III duty.

### What is the effect on putative class claims when the named plaintiff receives full wages and liquidated damages before class certification?
Question:
Cohen filed on behalf of a putative class, but then stipulated he was paid all overtime wages and MFLSA liquidated damages he sought. The opinion focuses on his individual claims and does not discuss class certification status. In the Eighth Circuit, if a named plaintiff's individual monetary claims are satisfied before class certification, are putative Rule 23 class claims mooted (distinguishing Genesis Healthcare and Campbell-Ewald)? Does it matter that penalties (if any) would go to the Commissioner rather than the plaintiff? Are there any anti-"pick off" doctrines applicable under Minnesota wage statutes that would preserve a live class controversy despite full payment to the named plaintiff?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under the Eighth Circuit’s reasoning in Cohen, if the named plaintiff’s individual monetary claims are fully satisfied before class certification, those individual claims ordinarily lose the plaintiff’s personal stake and may be moot — and that can doom any putative Rule 23 class claims unless the named plaintiff still has a personal, live interest (for example, an ongoing injury, a viable non‑monetary claim, or some other form of relief that would redress his injury).
- Cohen does not adopt any special Minnesota “anti‑pick‑off” rule that preserves class claims after full payment to the named plaintiff. Instead the court analyzed the case under ordinary Article III standing/mootness principles (Friends of the Earth/voluntary‑cessation line), remanding to determine whether a continuing violation exists that would keep the controversy alive.
- The fact that statutory penalties would be paid to the Commissioner (not the plaintiff) matters: the opinion treats that as an important jurisdictional wrinkle. For MPWA §181.101 the court held the statute gives the Commissioner (not an employee) the power to obtain the statutory daily‑wage penalties; and for MFLSA penalties the court explained that penalties payable to the government may still preserve a plaintiff’s standing only if they can be shown to redress the plaintiff’s injury (deterrent effect), so the claim may survive only if the alleged wrongful conduct is ongoing.

What Cohen actually held and why it matters for class claims
- Cohen stipulated that he “had been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he had been paid any liquidated damages to which he was entitled under the MFLSA. The court viewed that stipulation as removing the plaintiff’s individual monetary stake in wages/liquidated damages and therefore raised a jurisdictional/mootness question as to penalties and injunctive relief (slip op. §§ II.A–B).
- The court relied on ordinary Article III standing and mootness rules: a plaintiff must have a personal interest throughout the litigation, and a claim may become moot “if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” (Slip op. § II.B (quoting Friends of the Earth).) So the controlling inquiry is whether the named plaintiff still has a personal, live injury — e.g., the employer’s violative policy remains in force so the plaintiff faces future injury, or the plaintiff seeks relief (like injunctive relief) that would redress a continuing injury.
- Because Cohen had been paid the overtime wages and liquidated damages, the court remanded the MFLSA penalty question for the district court to determine jurisdiction: if Consilio’s policy remains in place, the penalty claim might be viable; if the policy is gone and recurrence is not reasonably expected, the claim may be moot. (Slip op. § II.B.)

How the Commissioner‑payment rule affects things (Minnesota statutes and MFLSA)
- MPWA §181.101: the opinion reads the statute to channel the ability to obtain “average daily wage penalties” to the Commissioner — the Commissioner must serve a demand, collect unpaid wages and penalties, and any money collected by the Commissioner is paid to the employee; the employee’s private right under §181.101 is limited to prosecuting a claim for wages. The court held that only the Commissioner may seek those daily penalty remedies under §181.101. (Slip op. § II.A.)
- MFLSA penalties payable to the Commissioner: the court recognized the doctrinal complication that civil penalties payable to the government can nonetheless “afford redress” to a citizen plaintiff through deterrence (Friends of the Earth), but stressed that standing/mootness analysis still applies. Because the MFLSA penalties would go to the Commissioner, the Eighth Circuit considered whether the penalties would still redress Cohen’s injury (deterrence of future violations) and remanded for the district court to decide whether the controversy survives. (Slip op. § II.B.)
- Practical consequence from the opinion: when statutory penalties are exclusively channeled to the state/Commissioner, a plaintiff’s receipt of the individual wages (and liquidated damages) weakens his Article III interest in pursuing penalty claims — unless he can show an ongoing injury or that the penalties would provide a cognizable form of redress (deterrence) because the violation is continuing.

Anti‑“pick‑off” doctrines and class preservation under the Cohen opinion
- Cohen does not announce or apply a special Minnesota anti‑pick‑off rule that preserves putative Rule 23 claims after full payment to the named plaintiff. The opinion frames the problem as a straight standing/mootness question: voluntary payment of the plaintiff’s monetary relief can eliminate his personal stake, but it will not necessarily moot the case if the challenged practice continues or is capable of repetition (slip op. § II.B).
- The court’s reasoning implies two practical ways a putative class might survive a pre‑certification payment to the named plaintiff:
  1) show a continuing violation (e.g., the employer’s unlawful policy remains in effect), which keeps the controversy live; or
  2) assert non‑monetary relief (injunctive relief) or some form of relief that would redress the named plaintiff’s ongoing injury.
- Absent those circumstances, and given the MPWA statutory scheme (penalties enforced by the Commissioner), the opinion suggests the Eighth Circuit will be skeptical that a putative class can survive merely because the plaintiff seeks penalties that will go to the Commissioner. The court remanded to let the district court resolve whether the conduct is ongoing and thus whether the penalty claim (and any continuing jurisdiction) survives. (Slip op. § II.B.)

Bottom line for a practitioner or a class‑plaintiff student
- In the Eighth Circuit as reflected in Cohen, a pre‑certification payment that fully satisfies the named plaintiff’s individual monetary demands tends to eliminate his personal stake and can moot the putative class claims unless the plaintiff can show a separate, continuing interest (ongoing policy, injunctive relief, or some other redress). Cohen did not create an exception that preserves class claims simply because penalties would be paid to the state; instead it treats that allocation as a jurisdictional wrinkle and remanded to see whether the conduct can reasonably be expected to recur.
- If you want to preserve a class where defendants may “pick off” the named plaintiff by paying his individual relief, Cohen illustrates the safer path: move for class certification early and, if possible, allege or seek relief that addresses an ongoing policy (injunctive relief) or otherwise show that defendant’s voluntary payment does not remove all of plaintiff’s concrete stake.

### What is the posture of attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest after the panel’s partial vacatur, given the concurrence’s note?
Question:
Judge Kobes's concurrence says he "also vacate[s] the district court’s ruling as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest," but the majority opinion doesn't expressly address fees, costs, or prejudgment interest. What is the operative effect—are those collateral issues automatically reopened because the MFLSA ruling was vacated, or only to the extent they depend on the MFLSA claim? In wage cases under Minnesota law, can a plaintiff recover attorney’s fees or prejudgment interest independent of obtaining penalties (e.g., based solely on recovered wages/liquidated damages already paid pre-judgment), and does the timing of payment affect entitlement?
Answer:
Short answer
- The panel’s judgment formally vacated and remanded only the MFLSA claim (the majority opinion: “We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings.”). The majority did not expressly speak to attorney’s fees, costs, or prejudgment interest.
- Judge Kobes’s separate concurrence expressly says he “also vacate[s] the district court’s ruling as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest.” That is his view, but it is a concurrence—not the majority holding. Practically, the district court on remand should treat fees, costs, and prejudgment interest as open for reconsideration to the extent they depend on the vacated MFLSA ruling; awards that rest solely on claims the panel affirmed need not be disturbed by the majority’s mandate.

What the opinion itself shows (and what you can rely on)
- Majority: MFLSA claim vacated and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction/mootness. The panel’s reasoning focuses on standing/mootness because Cohen had been paid overtime and liquidated damages and because the continuance of Consilio’s overtime policy is disputed. (“We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings.”; discussion at II.B.)
- Concurrence: Judge Kobes separately states he would also vacate the district court’s rulings as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest so that the district court can decide the mootness question on remand. (“I also vacate the district court’s ruling as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. … On remand, the district court may decide the mootness question first.”)

How that plays out on remand (practical/legal posture)
- Collateral matters are reopened only to the extent they depend on the vacated MFLSA ruling. If a fee/cost/interest award was premised on (or inseparable from) the MFLSA judgment the majority vacated, then the logical and necessary effect of vacatur is that the district court should revisit that award on remand. The panel’s remand expressly puts the MFLSA claim and any jurisdictional/mootness questions back before the district court.
- By contrast, if the district court entered fees, costs, or prejudgment interest under a claim or statutory basis that the panel affirmed (for example, an award tied exclusively to MPWA or MWTA rulings that the court left intact), those parts of the judgment were affirmed and are not undone by the MFLSA vacatur in the majority opinion.
- Judge Kobes’s concurrence signals that at least one member of the panel thought vacatur of the MFLSA claim should carry with it vacatur of the fee/cost/interest rulings to avoid leaving collateral relief in place while the underlying claim is remanded for possible mootness disposition. That is persuasive guidance for the district court on remand, but it is not an additional circuit holding beyond the majority’s remand of the MFLSA claim.

Recovery of attorney’s fees and prejudgment interest under the opinion’s facts
- The opinion does not decide whether Cohen can recover attorney’s fees or prejudgment interest independent of statutory penalties. The majority’s analysis focused on (1) who may recover particular penalties under the MPWA and (2) whether the MFLSA penalty claim is moot or justiciable given the stipulation that wages and liquidated damages were paid. It does not resolve fee‑shifting or prejudgment‑interest entitlements. (See II.A (MPWA penalties belong to Commissioner); II.B (MFLSA penalties and mootness).)
- What the opinion does make clear for this case:
  - Cohen stipulated he “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” That factual posture is the predicate for the panel’s concern about standing/mootness and for the remand. Those payments bear on whether the MFLSA claim—and any relief tied to it, potentially including fees/interest—remains justiciable.
  - Under the MPWA the panel concluded that average‑daily‑wage penalties are enforceable only by the Commissioner; the opinion therefore rejects a theory of employee‑recovered MPWA penalties in this posture. That portion of the judgment was affirmed. (II.A.)
- Because the opinion does not adjudicate fee‑shifting or prejudgment‑interest rules, a plaintiff’s entitlement to fees or prejudgment interest here must be decided on remand by the district court (1) after it determines whether the MFLSA claim is moot/justiciable and (2) after it examines the statutory bases or contractual grounds for fees/interest and the temporal facts (e.g., when payment was made, whether payment was voluntary and complete, whether any fee statutes apply to the remaining claims).

Practical checklist for the district court on remand (derived from the opinion)
1. Decide the jurisdiction/mootness question for the MFLSA claim first (the panel invited that approach). II.B; Kobes concurrence.
2. If the MFLSA claim is adjudicated or deemed live, determine whether any prior award of attorney’s fees, costs, or prejudgment interest was dependent on the vacated MFLSA ruling; if so, revisit those awards. (Majority remand + Kobes concurrence.)
3. If fees/costs/interest were awarded under claims the panel affirmed, those awards are not disturbed by the majority’s MFLSA vacatur and should stand unless independently invalidated on the merits.
4. Apply governing fee‑shifting and prejudgment‑interest law (not addressed in the opinion) to the facts on remand, including the parties’ stipulation that wages and liquidated damages were paid and the timing of those payments.

Bottom line: The majority vacated only the MFLSA claim; Judge Kobes’s concurrence expressly vacated fees, costs, and prejudgment interest as well, but that concurrence does not expand the majority’s holding. Practically, the district court should treat fees, costs, and prejudgment interest as open on remand to the extent they are tied to the MFLSA ruling (and the court should decide jurisdiction/mootness first, as the opinion suggests). The opinion does not resolve whether fees or prejudgment interest may be recovered independently of penalties; that question is for the district court on remand.
