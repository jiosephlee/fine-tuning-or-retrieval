Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does Minn. Stat. § 181.101 authorize employees to recover "average daily wage penalties," or are such penalties enforceable only by the Commissioner?
Question:
I'm confused about the interaction between MPWA §§ 181.171 and 181.101. Section 181.171 says an employer who violates certain MPWA sections is liable for "compensatory damages and other appropriate relief," and also for "the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated." Cohen argued § 181.101's plain language lets employees obtain average daily wage penalties, but the panel held only the Commissioner can collect penalties under § 181.101(a) after serving a demand for payment. How does the court reconcile § 181.171's broad remedial language with § 181.101's Commissioner-centric penalty mechanism? Is the key that § 181.101 "provides for" civil penalties only for the Commissioner, whereas other MPWA sections (like §§ 181.13 and 181.14) expressly grant employees penalty rights? If so, is "other appropriate relief" under § 181.171 limited to non-penalty relief unless the violated section expressly authorizes private penalties?
Answer:
Short answer: The Eighth Circuit held that Minn. Stat. § 181.101 does not authorize a private plaintiff to recover the statute’s “average daily wage” penalty; § 181.101’s penalty mechanism is enforced by the Commissioner (who, if she collects, pays the money to the employee). Section 181.171’s broad remedial language does not convert § 181.101’s Commissioner‑centered penalty into a private cause of action.

Why: statutory text and structure
- § 181.171 allows an employee to sue for violations of certain MPWA provisions and provides that an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” The Eighth Circuit read that cross‑reference literally: an employee can recover the penalties only if the particular MPWA provision “provides for” private enforcement of that penalty.
- § 181.101(a), however, assigns the penalty process to the Commissioner: the Commissioner must first serve a demand for payment, and only “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty” if wages are not paid within ten days. The statute expressly reserves to the employee only the right to “prosecut[e] a claim for wages.” The court emphasized that the statute “only references the Commissioner regarding penalties” and that “[p]enalties are only available if the Commissioner first ‘serve[s] a demand for payment . . . on behalf of an employee.’” (Op. at A. (quoting § 181.101(a)).)

Why that resolves the tension between §§ 181.171 and 181.101
- The court reconciled the provisions by applying § 181.171’s qualifier “provided for in the section violated.” That means § 181.171 does not give employees a freestanding right to any and all penalties; it makes available only those statutory penalties that the particular MPWA section itself authorizes a private plaintiff to recover.
- The opinion pointed out that the Legislature knows how to give employees a private penalty remedy when it wants to (e.g., Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13 and 181.14 explicitly allow an employee to “charge and collect a penalty” on demand). Because § 181.101 instead vests the charging/collecting power with the Commissioner, the court concluded the penalty there is not one the employee may enforce privately. (Op. at A; citing Lagasse and the “knows how” principle.)

Practical consequence (as the court put it)
- Employees can still pursue compensatory wages and other non‑penalty relief under § 181.171, and § 181.101 preserves an employee’s right to prosecute a claim for unpaid wages. But the specific “average daily wage” penalty in § 181.101 is recoverable through the Commissioner’s statutory enforcement route only; a private plaintiff may not independently recover that penalty under § 181.171. (Op. at A.)

So: yes — the key is that § 181.101 “provides for” penalties enforceable by the Commissioner, not by private plaintiffs, while other MPWA sections expressly authorize private penalty collection. “Other appropriate relief” under § 181.171 therefore does not expand a private right to recover penalties unless the violated MPWA section itself supplies that private‑enforcement mechanism.
