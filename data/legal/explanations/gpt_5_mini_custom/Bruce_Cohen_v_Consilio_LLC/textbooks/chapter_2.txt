Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # MPWA Claim: Who Can Obtain Average Daily Wage Penalties

This chapter explains the Eighth Circuit’s analysis of Bruce Cohen’s claim under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) that he was entitled to “average daily wage” penalties for unpaid overtime. The court’s reasoning turns on close statutory reading—what the MPWA’s text actually authorizes—and on Minnesota interpretive canons about how to infer legislative intent from differences in statutory design. The outcome: because Cohen’s claim rested on Minn. Stat. § 181.101, which authorizes the Commissioner (not private employees) to demand payment and collect penalties, an employee suing under § 181.171 may recover wages but not the average daily wage penalties created by § 181.101; the district court’s grant of summary judgment on that point was affirmed.

## Statutory framework: § 181.171 and § 181.101

Understanding the opinion requires keeping two provisions in view and seeing how they fit together. Section 181.171 supplies a private cause of action: it says a person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations of certain MPWA provisions (including § 181.101), and that an employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” when a violation is found. But § 181.171 also limits recovery of “civil penalties or damages” to those penalties “provided for in the section violated.” In other words, § 181.171 creates a vehicle for private enforcement but does not itself create every form of relief; the relief available in a private suit depends on what the specific MPWA provision at issue authorizes.

Section 181.101, the provision Cohen invoked, addresses timely payment of wages and sets out the Commissioner of Labor’s enforcement mechanism. Under § 181.101(a), the Commissioner may serve a demand for payment on an employer and, if wages are not paid within ten days of the demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” The statute also expressly provides that money collected by the Commissioner is to be paid to the employee concerned. Finally, § 181.101 includes the caveat that it “does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages,” which preserves an employee’s ability to sue for unpaid wages but does not on its face grant the employee the Commissioner’s enforcement tools.

## Textual focus: the Commissioner’s exclusive enforcement role in § 181.101

The court’s decision rests on a textual point: § 181.101 speaks repeatedly in terms of the Commissioner’s authority to demand, charge, collect, and pay out penalties. It does not say that an aggrieved employee may independently obtain the “penalty” the Commissioner can collect; instead, it contemplates the Commissioner serving a demand and collecting the penalty if the employer does not comply. Because § 181.171 allows private plaintiffs to recover only those civil penalties “provided for in the section violated,” the question is whether § 181.101 “provides for” an employee to recover the average daily wage penalty directly. The court found that it does not—the statutory text unambiguously confines the penalty-collection mechanism to the Commissioner.

The opinion applies Minnesota’s plain-meaning rule: when statutory language is plain and unambiguous, courts must give effect to that language. Reading § 181.101’s references to the Commissioner and the specific process (service of demand, ten-day cure period, and collection by the Commissioner) leads to the conclusion that the legislature intended the Commissioner to be the entity who enforces and collects the penalty the statute creates.

## Comparative statutory design: the legislature “knows how” to give employees penalty rights

The court bolsters its textual reading by pointing to other MPWA provisions that do expressly give employees statutory access to penalties when the legislature intended it. For example, §§ 181.13 and 181.14 (addressing unpaid wages when an employee is discharged or quits) explicitly provide that, upon an employee’s demand, the employee “may charge and collect a penalty.” That express authorization demonstrates that when the Minnesota Legislature intends to empower employees to collect a penalty directly, it knows how to do so and will say so. The absence of similar language in § 181.101 is therefore probative: the legislature’s deliberate choice to assign enforcement of § 181.101’s penalty to the Commissioner indicates that private plaintiffs like Cohen were not meant to obtain that penalty in a civil suit.

This comparative-design argument relies on a familiar interpretive canon (used by Minnesota courts): difference in statutory language across provisions can signal different legislative intent. The Eighth Circuit cites Lagasse for that principle and applies it to conclude that § 181.101’s penalty belongs to the Commissioner’s enforcement toolbox, not to a private plaintiff’s remedies.

## Canons and cases the court relied upon

The opinion invokes Minnesota precedent to support its plain-meaning approach and its treatment of money collected by the Commissioner. Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co. is cited for the proposition that plain and unambiguous statutory language must be given effect. Milner v. Farmers Insurance Exchange is cited for the point that money collected by the Commissioner is to be paid to the employee, underscoring the statutory design: the Commissioner acts, collects, and then the collected funds go to the employee, but the authority to collect is placed in the Commissioner.

The decision also notes prior district-court authority reaching the same conclusion—Hull v. ConvergeOne—where a Minnesota district court had explained that § 181.101 limits the ability to enforce the penalty to the Commissioner, with any collected funds going to the employee. The Eighth Circuit adopted the same reading at the appellate level.

## Holding applied to the facts and the district court’s disposition

Cohen’s claim alleged a violation of § 181.101 and sought average daily wage penalties. The court concluded that § 181.101, by its plain language, reserves the power to demand and collect those penalties to the Commissioner, and that § 181.171 only allows private plaintiffs to recover penalties that the violated section itself authorizes for private enforcement. Because § 181.101 does not authorize employees to obtain the average daily wage penalty—only the Commissioner—the district court correctly granted summary judgment to Consilio on Cohen’s MPWA penalties claim.

In practical terms, the court affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim. Cohen was not left without remedy for unpaid wages—§ 181.101 preserves an employee’s right to “prosecute a claim for wages,” and the parties in this case had stipulated that Consilio had paid the wages and liquidated damages at issue—but if an employee wants the particular penalty available under § 181.101, the statute assigns the Commissioner, not the private plaintiff, the authority to seek it.

## Teaching takeaways for reading opinions

When a court interprets statutory remedies in a private enforcement context, pay attention to (1) the text of the specific provision creating the penalty, (2) any cross-references to enforcement mechanisms, and (3) whether a general private-right provision (like § 181.171) makes relief available only as “provided for in the section violated.” Comparative statutory language is a powerful clue: express employee-penalty rights elsewhere in the statutory scheme suggest that the absence of such language in the provision at issue is meaningful. Finally, recognize how the court applies the plain-meaning rule: if the operative language unambiguously describes a government-enforcement mechanism, the court will generally enforce that distribution of authority rather than read a private right into the statute by implication.
