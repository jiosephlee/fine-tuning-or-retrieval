Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Case Background, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## Parties and roles
This case arises from an employment dispute between Bruce Cohen, an hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer, and his employer, Consilio, LLC, a nationwide legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. Cohen sued on his own behalf and as a putative class representative for other Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney reviewers. The parties’ identities and roles are important because the asserted violations concern Minnesota wage statutes as applied to employees working for a company that operates nationwide but maintains Minnesota offices.

## 2019 policy change
In July 2019 Consilio sent an email to Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney reviewers announcing a new overtime policy to take effect in August 2019. The email eliminated the company’s overtime premium pay and stated that affected employees would be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked. That corporate communication is the operative factual touchstone for Cohen’s claims: he alleges Consilio’s change deprived him and similarly situated employees of overtime pay to which they were entitled under Minnesota law.

## Claims filed in August 2020
Cohen filed suit in August 2020, asserting three state-law claims based on the 2019 policy change. His complaint invoked (1) the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. §§ 181.101 and 181.171; (2) the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27; and (3) the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. § 181.032. His pleaded remedies sought unpaid overtime wages, MFLSA liquidated damages (a statutory double-recovery measure), statutory penalties under the various statutes, and injunctive relief to address the ongoing application of the policy. The complaint therefore sought both make-whole monetary relief for alleged underpayment and statutory penalties and injunctions intended to deter or prevent future violations.

## Consilio’s post-suit payments
After the suit was filed, Consilio paid $256,010.01 to the group of Minnesota licensed-attorney reviewers to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen’s individual share of that payment was $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. Those payments materially narrowed the live controversy: they resolved the particular wage and liquidated-damages amounts Cohen alleged were owed under the MFLSA.

## Key stipulations and narrowed dispute
Consistent with the payments, the parties stipulated that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” The parties could not agree about statutory penalties, however. Cohen sought substantial penalties under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA; Consilio argued the statutes do not authorize such penalties in these circumstances. Because the core wage and liquidated-damages claims were acknowledged as satisfied, the litigation was narrowed to the question whether Cohen may obtain statutory penalties and certain forms of injunctive relief.

## Summary judgment posture in the district court
The parties jointly proposed that the district court resolve Consilio’s summary judgment motion on the statutory claims (MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA) before additional discovery or further litigation, stipulating that if the motion were granted in full the case would be over. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all three statutory claims. Because the parties had elected to present the statutory-interpretation issues at summary judgment, the district court’s order disposed of the entire case in the district court, prompting Cohen’s appeal.

## Appellate standard of review
On appeal the court reviews the grant of summary judgment de novo. Consistent with the district-court standard explained in the opinion, the appellate court must “view[] the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn from the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.” Summary judgment is appropriate only if there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Because Cohen is the nonmoving party on appeal, the court construes the record and permissible inferences favorably to him while assessing whether the statutory claims were correctly resolved as a matter of law.

# MPWA Claim: Who Can Obtain Average Daily Wage Penalties

This chapter explains the Eighth Circuit’s analysis of Bruce Cohen’s claim under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) that he was entitled to “average daily wage” penalties for unpaid overtime. The court’s reasoning turns on close statutory reading—what the MPWA’s text actually authorizes—and on Minnesota interpretive canons about how to infer legislative intent from differences in statutory design. The outcome: because Cohen’s claim rested on Minn. Stat. § 181.101, which authorizes the Commissioner (not private employees) to demand payment and collect penalties, an employee suing under § 181.171 may recover wages but not the average daily wage penalties created by § 181.101; the district court’s grant of summary judgment on that point was affirmed.

## Statutory framework: § 181.171 and § 181.101

Understanding the opinion requires keeping two provisions in view and seeing how they fit together. Section 181.171 supplies a private cause of action: it says a person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations of certain MPWA provisions (including § 181.101), and that an employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” when a violation is found. But § 181.171 also limits recovery of “civil penalties or damages” to those penalties “provided for in the section violated.” In other words, § 181.171 creates a vehicle for private enforcement but does not itself create every form of relief; the relief available in a private suit depends on what the specific MPWA provision at issue authorizes.

Section 181.101, the provision Cohen invoked, addresses timely payment of wages and sets out the Commissioner of Labor’s enforcement mechanism. Under § 181.101(a), the Commissioner may serve a demand for payment on an employer and, if wages are not paid within ten days of the demand, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” The statute also expressly provides that money collected by the Commissioner is to be paid to the employee concerned. Finally, § 181.101 includes the caveat that it “does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages,” which preserves an employee’s ability to sue for unpaid wages but does not on its face grant the employee the Commissioner’s enforcement tools.

## Textual focus: the Commissioner’s exclusive enforcement role in § 181.101

The court’s decision rests on a textual point: § 181.101 speaks repeatedly in terms of the Commissioner’s authority to demand, charge, collect, and pay out penalties. It does not say that an aggrieved employee may independently obtain the “penalty” the Commissioner can collect; instead, it contemplates the Commissioner serving a demand and collecting the penalty if the employer does not comply. Because § 181.171 allows private plaintiffs to recover only those civil penalties “provided for in the section violated,” the question is whether § 181.101 “provides for” an employee to recover the average daily wage penalty directly. The court found that it does not—the statutory text unambiguously confines the penalty-collection mechanism to the Commissioner.

The opinion applies Minnesota’s plain-meaning rule: when statutory language is plain and unambiguous, courts must give effect to that language. Reading § 181.101’s references to the Commissioner and the specific process (service of demand, ten-day cure period, and collection by the Commissioner) leads to the conclusion that the legislature intended the Commissioner to be the entity who enforces and collects the penalty the statute creates.

## Comparative statutory design: the legislature “knows how” to give employees penalty rights

The court bolsters its textual reading by pointing to other MPWA provisions that do expressly give employees statutory access to penalties when the legislature intended it. For example, §§ 181.13 and 181.14 (addressing unpaid wages when an employee is discharged or quits) explicitly provide that, upon an employee’s demand, the employee “may charge and collect a penalty.” That express authorization demonstrates that when the Minnesota Legislature intends to empower employees to collect a penalty directly, it knows how to do so and will say so. The absence of similar language in § 181.101 is therefore probative: the legislature’s deliberate choice to assign enforcement of § 181.101’s penalty to the Commissioner indicates that private plaintiffs like Cohen were not meant to obtain that penalty in a civil suit.

This comparative-design argument relies on a familiar interpretive canon (used by Minnesota courts): difference in statutory language across provisions can signal different legislative intent. The Eighth Circuit cites Lagasse for that principle and applies it to conclude that § 181.101’s penalty belongs to the Commissioner’s enforcement toolbox, not to a private plaintiff’s remedies.

## Canons and cases the court relied upon

The opinion invokes Minnesota precedent to support its plain-meaning approach and its treatment of money collected by the Commissioner. Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co. is cited for the proposition that plain and unambiguous statutory language must be given effect. Milner v. Farmers Insurance Exchange is cited for the point that money collected by the Commissioner is to be paid to the employee, underscoring the statutory design: the Commissioner acts, collects, and then the collected funds go to the employee, but the authority to collect is placed in the Commissioner.

The decision also notes prior district-court authority reaching the same conclusion—Hull v. ConvergeOne—where a Minnesota district court had explained that § 181.101 limits the ability to enforce the penalty to the Commissioner, with any collected funds going to the employee. The Eighth Circuit adopted the same reading at the appellate level.

## Holding applied to the facts and the district court’s disposition

Cohen’s claim alleged a violation of § 181.101 and sought average daily wage penalties. The court concluded that § 181.101, by its plain language, reserves the power to demand and collect those penalties to the Commissioner, and that § 181.171 only allows private plaintiffs to recover penalties that the violated section itself authorizes for private enforcement. Because § 181.101 does not authorize employees to obtain the average daily wage penalty—only the Commissioner—the district court correctly granted summary judgment to Consilio on Cohen’s MPWA penalties claim.

In practical terms, the court affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim. Cohen was not left without remedy for unpaid wages—§ 181.101 preserves an employee’s right to “prosecute a claim for wages,” and the parties in this case had stipulated that Consilio had paid the wages and liquidated damages at issue—but if an employee wants the particular penalty available under § 181.101, the statute assigns the Commissioner, not the private plaintiff, the authority to seek it.

## Teaching takeaways for reading opinions

When a court interprets statutory remedies in a private enforcement context, pay attention to (1) the text of the specific provision creating the penalty, (2) any cross-references to enforcement mechanisms, and (3) whether a general private-right provision (like § 181.171) makes relief available only as “provided for in the section violated.” Comparative statutory language is a powerful clue: express employee-penalty rights elsewhere in the statutory scheme suggest that the absence of such language in the provision at issue is meaningful. Finally, recognize how the court applies the plain-meaning rule: if the operative language unambiguously describes a government-enforcement mechanism, the court will generally enforce that distribution of authority rather than read a private right into the statute by implication.

# MFLSA Claim: Statutory Penalties, Article III, and Mootness

This chapter explains why the Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s summary-judgment ruling on Cohen’s Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim and remanded for a jurisdictional determination. The court’s reasoning turns on three linked ideas: the narrow postures of the parties’ dispute, who would receive any statutory penalties, and the Article III requirement that a plaintiff retain a concrete, continuing interest in each claim and each form of relief throughout the litigation. Whether the court can decide Cohen’s claim depends on a fact-intensive mootness inquiry about Consilio’s current overtime policy.

## Scope of the remaining dispute

At the time of summary judgment the parties had resolved the central money damages Cohen initially sought under the MFLSA: Consilio paid him the overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed, and the parties stipulated Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled.” That stipulation narrows the live controversy: the only relief Cohen continues to pursue under the MFLSA is statutory penalties. Because the stipulation removes any personal monetary shortfall, the court flagged the distinct legal issues that arise when a plaintiff seeks only penalties rather than payment owed directly to her.

## Penalty payee: penalties go to the Commissioner, not the employee

Both parties agreed the particular statutory penalties at issue are payable to the state Commissioner (i.e., the government), not to Cohen himself. That allocation matters for Article III purposes: relief that returns money to the plaintiff plainly redresses a past monetary injury, but relief that is paid to the government may nonetheless be constitutionally cognizable only if the plaintiff can show some other redressable injury (for example, an ongoing or threatened future harm that prospective relief and deterrence would remedy).

## Article III requirements: standing must persist throughout the case

The panel underscored the settled Article III rule that a plaintiff must establish standing for each claim and for each form of relief sought, and that the personal interest giving rise to standing at the outset of the suit must continue throughout the litigation. The opinion cites Town of Chester for the proposition that standing must be shown for each form of relief, and Sisney for the principle that standing and mootness are continuity requirements that persist while the case proceeds. Those precedents frame the jurisdictional question here: Cohen must have a continuing, concrete interest in obtaining penalties before a federal court may adjudicate whether he is entitled to them.

## Deterrent effect and civil penalties under Laidlaw

The court recognized Laidlaw’s teaching that civil penalties—although payable to the government—can sometimes provide redress to plaintiffs because penalties deter ongoing unlawful conduct and thereby remedy threatened future harms. In Laidlaw, the Supreme Court allowed citizens to sue for civil penalties where the penalties would encourage the defendant to stop harmful conduct that injured or threatened to injure plaintiffs. The Eighth Circuit applied that logic here: even where penalties go to the state, a plaintiff may have standing if the sought penalties would deter ongoing violations that threaten future injury to the plaintiff.

## Mootness and voluntary cessation: when penalties cannot sustain jurisdiction

Laidlaw’s rationale does not permit federal jurisdiction in perpetuity. The panel emphasized that a claim for penalties may become moot if “subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” The voluntary-cessation doctrine counsels that a defendant’s mere cessation of the challenged practice does not automatically moot a case; the burden remains on the defendant to show that the behavior will not recur. Only when recurrence is truly implausible—i.e., it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur—will courts dismiss as moot. Thus the availability of penalties to provide Article III redress depends on whether deterrence remains necessary to prevent future harm.

## Factual uncertainty controls the legal outcome

The record did not resolve whether Consilio’s overtime policy that allegedly eliminated premium overtime pay was still in place. That factual uncertainty is dispositive. If the policy remains—or if there is a reasonable expectation it could be reinstated—Cohen can plausibly claim that awarding statutory penalties would deter continued or renewed violations and would therefore redress a threatened injury, preserving Article III jurisdiction consistent with Laidlaw. But if the policy has been abandoned in a way that makes recurrence implausible, then Cohen’s sole remaining claim for penalties may be moot because there is no ongoing or threatened injury that penalties would redress. Given that factual dispute, the appellate court concluded it could not definitively decide the MFLSA penalties claim on the present record.

## Disposition: vacatur and remand for the jurisdictional inquiry

Because of the jurisdictional doubt created by the parties’ stipulation about back pay and the unresolved factual question about Consilio’s current policy, the court vacated the district court’s summary-judgment ruling on the MFLSA penalties claim and remanded. On remand the district court must determine whether Article III jurisdiction exists—i.e., whether Cohen still has a concrete, continuing interest in the penalties—by resolving whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in place, whether cessation (if any) is truly permanent, and whether deterrent penalties would provide redress for a threatened injury. The panel indicated that if the district court finds the policy persists or that recurrence is reasonably likely, jurisdiction may be proper; if it finds the opposite, the claim may be moot.

## Concurring perspective: unresolved questions about Laidlaw’s fit

The concurring judge signaled skepticism about whether Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale comfortably supports standing for MFLSA penalties. The concurrence observed that Laidlaw involved a particular statutory penalty scheme and fact pattern, and noted differences in how MFLSA penalties are structured and administered. Neither party had fully briefed the standing question in that doctrinal depth, so the concurring judge accepted the majority’s practical approach of vacatur and remand but suggested the district court—on remand—could decide the mootness question first. The concurrence thus leaves open a possible further appellate confrontation about whether Laidlaw’s deterrence-based standing analysis maps cleanly onto MFLSA penalties payable to the Commissioner.

Conclusion

The Eighth Circuit’s remand illustrates how Article III standing and mootness doctrines operate when a plaintiff has been paid the direct monetary relief sought and only statutory penalties remain—particularly when those penalties would go to the government. Whether a plaintiff may proceed depends on whether the court can find a continuing, redressable interest in deterrence-based relief; when the record leaves that factual predicate unsettled, the proper remedy is to vacate and return the issue to the district court for a focused jurisdictional determination.

# MWTA Notice Claim and Injunctive Relief: Preservation and Waiver

This chapter explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of Bruce Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) notice claim and his request for injunctive relief in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC. The opinion’s disposition turns on two intertwined themes: (1) the statutory and remedial posture of the MWTA notice theory when the plaintiff has been paid what he sought, and (2) appellate preservation and waiver principles that preclude raising new theories or new forms of relief for the first time in briefing. Understanding these doctrines clarifies why the court affirmed dismissal of the MWTA claim and declined to grant the injunctive relief Cohen later requested.

## MWTA theory: alleged failure to provide required notice of pay policy change (§ 181.032(d)(4), (f))

Cohen alleged that Consilio violated Minnesota statutes requiring notice of a change in pay policy—specifically provisions cited by the court as § 181.032(d)(4) and (f). Read in context, Cohen’s MWTA claim rests on an assertion that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice when it changed its overtime-pay practice in 2019. That is a notice-and-procedure theory: the statutory command creates an obligation to inform employees of certain pay-rule changes, and a failure to give notice can be wrongful independent of whether the employee ultimately suffers uncompensated lost wages.

Two points matter for how the court treated the claim. First, Cohen had stipulated (for purposes of summary judgment) that Consilio had paid him all overtime and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. That concession narrows the practical relief available to him under any wage-theft theory: if all wages and MFLSA liquidated damages have been paid, the MWTA claim is not a straightforward vehicle for recovering additional wage awards tied to the same underlying unpaid overtime. Second, the opinion notes that Cohen did not identify—and the court did not find—any statutory authority within the MWTA provisions he invoked that authorizes civil penalties payable directly to an employee for the specific notice violation he asserted. Put simply, the court treated Cohen’s MWTA theory as limited by (a) his stipulation that no unpaid wages remain and (b) the absence of any cited provision creating employee-enforceable civil penalties for the particular MWTA notice violation alleged.

These two facts led the court to conclude there was no meaningful basis on appeal to disturb the district court’s disposition of the MWTA claim. The MWTA theory, as briefed and preserved, did not supply a live claim for additional money damages to Cohen or point to a statutory mechanism awarding the penalties he sought.

## Relief mismatch: stipulation of payment and absence of statutory basis for civil penalties

When evaluating statutory claims for penalties, courts look to whether the statute provides for the particular remedy a plaintiff seeks and to who is the proper payee for any penalties. The Cohen opinion emphasizes that an employee’s stipulation that he “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” materially affects the remedial landscape. If no unpaid wages remain, a plaintiff must identify some separate statutory right to additional relief (for example, statutory penalties payable to the employee) to avoid mootness or dismissal. Here, Cohen failed to point the court to any MWTA provision that authorizes the civil penalties he requested payable to him on the facts as stipulated.

In short, Cohen’s stipulation eliminated any argument that unpaid wages remained as a basis for relief under the MWTA claim, and the opinion records that Cohen “points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” That failure of statutory hook is decisive: the appellate court will not reverse where the plaintiff both (i) has been made whole on wages and (ii) cannot point to a statutory basis for the additional penalties demanded.

## Appellate waiver: lack of developed argument or legal authority to reverse; Milligan waiver doctrine

A central procedural principle in the opinion is the waiver doctrine: issues not supported by developed argument or legal authority are forfeited on appeal. The court relied on Milligan v. City of Red Oak to state that where an appellant’s brief “does not support [an] assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue,” and the appellate court will not address it. This doctrine enforces a basic appellate-administered rule: identify the specific error, explain why it is error, and back that explanation with legal authority and reasoned argument.

Applied to Cohen’s MWTA claim, the waiver principle operates two ways. First, Cohen’s stipulation and his failure to invoke a statutory source for employee penalties left him with little substantive argument to advance. Second, on appeal he did not meaningfully develop any alternative legal authority that could have supported reversal (for example, by pointing to a MWTA provision authorizing the penalties or explaining why the stipulated payment left a live controversy). The Eighth Circuit therefore treated the MWTA challenge as waived to the extent it was not meaningfully briefed and affirmed the district court on that claim.

For students: when reading opinions, watch carefully for language like “points to no statutory authority” and citations to waiver cases. Those cues often indicate that the court’s ruling rests as much on the appellant’s failures in advocacy and preservation as on the underlying statutory merits.

## Injunctive relief evolution: complaint vs. summary judgment briefing

Cohen’s requested injunctive relief underwent a substantive change during the litigation. The complaint sought certain particular forms of injunctive relief, but Cohen later abandoned those specific requests and, in his summary judgment briefing, sought a different form of injunctive relief—namely, an affirmative order requiring Consilio to rescind its 2019 policy. The court treated this as a change in the relief sought rather than merely a refinement of the request.

The district court did not consider this newly proposed remedy, and the Eighth Circuit held that it was not properly before the courts because Cohen introduced it only in briefing rather than by amending his complaint. The opinion cites InfoDeli, LLC to reiterate the settled rule: new claims or new forms of relief should be raised by amendment (via Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)), not by asserting them for the first time in opposition papers. That rule serves important procedural and fairness objectives: it gives the defendant proper notice of the claim, allows the district court to frame the case, and ensures that discovery and adjudication proceed on a known record.

Put another way, a party cannot change the nature of the lawsuit in its brief and expect the court to treat the new request as if it were part of the complaint. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s refusal to consider Cohen’s late-shifted injunctive demand on that basis.

## Procedural bar: Rule 15(a) and why courts reject new theories raised only in briefing

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) governs amendments to pleadings and provides the mechanism for adding new claims or altering the relief sought. The opinion cites Rule 15(a) to underline that amendment—where appropriate and timely—is the proper procedural path to present materially different claims or remedies to the court. The policy reasons for this rule are pragmatic and equitable: an amendment puts the opposing party on notice, permits targeted discovery and response, and allows the district court to manage the litigation with the full roster of claims and remedies identified.

InfoDeli, LLC (as cited by the Eighth Circuit) encapsulates the court’s refusal to entertain new claims shoehorned into briefing: courts expect adherence to pleading rules and will not reward litigants who bypass them by springing new theories in motion practice. The appellate court’s affirmance on the injunctive-relief point thus rests on familiar procedural grounds rather than a merits judgment about whether rescission of the 2019 policy would, in theory, be appropriate.

For students: when reading an opinion, identify whether the court’s refusal to consider a remedy is based on substantive law or on procedural posture. Here, the district court’s non-consideration—and the appellate affirmation—was procedural: the remedy was not pleaded and so was not properly before the court.

## Bottom line from the opinion

The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA notice claim and his request for injunctive relief for two related reasons grounded in the appellate record and basic procedural rules. First, Cohen had stipulated that he was paid the wages he sought and failed to identify statutory authority for additional civil penalties under the MWTA provisions he invoked; second, he attempted to obtain a materially different form of injunction (rescission of the 2019 policy) only in summary judgment briefing rather than by amending his complaint as Rule 15(a) requires. Because Cohen offered no developed argument or statutory hook to reverse the MWTA ruling and did not properly preserve the modified injunctive demand, the court treated those points as waived or unpreserved and affirmed.

When you read similar opinions, pay attention to three signals: stipulations that narrow the live controversy, whether the plaintiff identifies a statutory source for the relief sought, and whether the claim or remedy was pled (or properly amended) before being argued in briefing. Those procedural and remedial details frequently determine appellate outcomes as much as the underlying merits.

# Outcome and Practical Significance

This opinion resolves several discrete questions that matter in Minnesota wage litigation: who may enforce particular penalties under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), when a federal court retains jurisdiction to award penalties that in practice will be paid to the government, and how tactical choices—stipulations, pleading, and amendment practice—can determine whether a case survives to judgment on penalties or is dismissed as moot. The court affirmed the district court on most issues, but vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalties question so the trial court can decide whether it retains Article III jurisdiction. The practical implications below track the opinion’s holdings and remaining uncertainties and translate them into strategic points for plaintiffs’ and defendants’ counsel as well as compliance takeaways for employers.

## Disposition: Affirmed on MPWA, MWTA, and injunctive relief; vacated and remanded on MFLSA penalties for jurisdictional determination

The panel affirmed the district court’s summary judgment ruling insofar as it held that (1) under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 the authority to seek “average daily wage” penalties lies with the Commissioner of Labor and Industry rather than individual employees, and (2) Cohen’s MWTA claim and the district court’s refusal to consider a belatedly raised injunctive form of relief need not be disturbed. The court vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalties ruling because the record left open a live Article III jurisdictional question: the parties had stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime and liquidated damages, but they disputed whether the employer’s overtime policy was still in effect; if the policy has been discontinued and cannot reasonably be expected to recur, the penalties claim may be moot. The remand instructs the district court to resolve whether an ongoing or reasonably likely recurrence of violations exists before adjudicating statutory penalties payable to the state.

## Remedial architecture matters: under § 181.101 MPWA penalties are enforceable only by the Commissioner; employees may pursue unpaid wages in court but not Commissioner penalties

A central doctrinal point in the opinion is statutory text controlling who may seek particular remedies. The court read § 181.101 to vest the Commissioner with authority to serve a demand and, if payment is not made, to “charge and collect” wages and a penalty; the same subsection expressly contemplates that money collected by the Commissioner is to be paid to the employee. Because § 181.171 authorizes actions “seeking redress” but ties civil penalties to those “provided for in the section violated,” the court concluded that the average-daily-wage penalty mechanism in § 181.101 is not an employee-enforceable remedy in a private civil action. The decision emphasizes the point—repeated in the opinion—that where the legislature knows how to confer a private right to collect a penalty (for example elsewhere in §§ 181.13 and 181.14) its omission from § 181.101 is significant. Practically, plaintiffs can and should continue to litigate unpaid wages and liquidated damages under the MPWA in their own names, but they should not expect to obtain the MPWA average daily wage penalty in a private suit unless the Commissioner has invoked the statutory enforcement mechanism.

## Article III vigilance: where only government-payable penalties remain, plaintiffs must show ongoing or reasonably expected future violations to avoid mootness

The panel’s vacatur of the MFLSA penalties ruling centers on Article III standing and mootness doctrine. Even when statutory penalties are payable to the government (or collected and remitted through the government), plaintiffs can sometimes satisfy Article III by showing an ongoing injury or a realistic prospect that the alleged unlawful conduct will recur—the core of Friends of the Earth. But the pleadings and stipulations here created a posture in which only statutory penalties remained in dispute after the employer paid wages and liquidated damages. When a case effectively becomes a contest over statutory penalties alone, courts must scrutinize whether there remains a live controversy: has the employer discontinued the challenged practice, or could it resume it? The opinion instructs plaintiffs that, to avoid dismissal or remand for mootness, they should preserve and document facts showing the continuing existence of the challenged policy or a credible threat of recurrence (for example through contemporaneous policies, communications, or other evidence), rather than relying on the possibility that penalties would redound to a public official.

## Stipulations narrow cases: agreeing that wages and liquidated damages are fully paid can convert a case into a pure-penalty dispute, raising jurisdictional risks

The parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages narrowed the litigation to whether statutory penalties were available. That narrowing produced the core jurisdictional issue on appeal: once the monetary injury that initially supported the suit is eliminated, plaintiffs may lack a continuing personal stake in penalties payable to the government unless they can show ongoing or reasonably likely violations. The practical lesson is double-sided. Plaintiffs should be cautious when stipulating to full payment of damages without preserving the record on the employer’s policy and without seeking or expressly reserving ancillary relief (such as injunctive or declaratory relief) that would sustain a live controversy. Defendants, by contrast, can reduce federal-court exposure by tendering back wages and liquidated damages, but should be aware that doing so does not automatically eliminate all litigation risk if the plaintiff can credibly show ongoing unlawful practices.

## Preservation and pleading discipline: seek desired injunctive relief in the complaint or via amendment; avoid raising new remedies in summary judgment briefing

The opinion underscores fundamental procedural hygiene. Cohen sought a novel form of injunctive relief—rescission of the 2019 policy—only in his summary judgment briefing, not in the operative complaint. The panel declined to consider that newly raised remedy, reiterating that new claims belong in an amended complaint, not in opposition briefs. For practitioners this is straightforward but crucial: if a plaintiff wants prospective or structural relief (injunctions, declaratory judgments, policy rescission), those remedies should be pleaded and, if later refined, should be added through an amendment complying with Rule 15. Likewise, defendants should press challenges to the sufficiency of pleaded remedies early. On remand, the district court will likely expect a clear record reflecting what relief was pleaded, whether it was preserved, and whether additional discovery is needed to substantiate requests for prospective relief.

## Employer compliance note: changes to overtime policies implicate multiple Minnesota statutes; properly notice changes (MWTA), ensure timely payment (MPWA), and maintain compliant pay practices to avoid MFLSA exposure

Although the panel’s holdings are statutory-interpretive and jurisdictional, the underlying facts illustrate practical compliance traps for employers. An employer’s unilateral change to overtime-pay treatment (here, announcing pay “at [base] pay rate” and eliminating a premium) can trigger distinct statutory regimes. Under the MPWA, the Commissioner has an administrative enforcement route that can impose penalties if a demand is not honored; employers should understand that use of that route by the Commissioner may be outcome-determinative. The MWTA claims here flagged notice requirements tied to wage changes; employers should review the statute’s notice provisions when altering pay practices to avoid independent state-authorized penalty exposure. Finally, the MFLSA claim—federal-law style overtime protection in Minnesota—remains a separate source of liability; even if statutory penalties under state law are limited to Commissioner enforcement, federal/state overtime obligations must still be respected. Risk-minimizing practices include (1) documenting and distributing clear, contemporaneous employee notices of any pay-policy change consistent with statutory notice requirements; (2) preserving records that demonstrate the discontinuation or retention of disputed policies; and (3) promptly responding to Commissioner demands when presented, since § 181.101 prescribes a 10-day window for payment after a demand.

Conclusion

Bruce Cohen v. Consilio illustrates how remedial text, Article III principles, and litigation tactics interact in wage cases. The court’s careful parsing of § 181.101 shows the limits of private enforcement of certain MPWA penalties; its remand on the MFLSA issue reminds litigants that stipulations narrowing the dispute to penalties raise live-mootness questions that must be answered before a merits ruling. Practitioners should heed the opinion when structuring stipulations, pleading injunctive relief, preserving a factual record about ongoing policies, and counseling employers on compliant pay-policy changes.
