Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Waiver by Under‑briefing: The Demise of the MWTA Claim and Lessons for Plaintiffs

## Why this decision matters

The Eighth Circuit’s disposition of Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is a reminder that in wage‑theft litigation, victory on the merits is not the only hurdle: a plaintiff who fails to develop statutory arguments or to link requested relief to a statute’s remedial scheme risks losing claims regardless of factual support. Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) count—premised on an allegedly deficient notice of an overtime policy change—did not fail because the court parsed complicated text about notice obligations; it failed because the plaintiff offered “little to no” briefing and “pointed to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties” under the MWTA, and therefore waived meaningful appellate review. The opinion reinforces two basic lessons for practitioners: preserve issues by briefing them fully at summary judgment and plead and prove entitlement to the specific remedies you seek.

## What the court did

Cohen sued his employer under three Minnesota wage statutes (the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA) alleging unpaid overtime and related penalties and injunctive relief. After the employer paid the overtime and liquidated damages under the MFLSA and the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all wages he claimed, the district court granted summary judgment to the employer on all claims. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s disposition of the MPWA and MWTA claims, vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalties question for the district court to consider mootness/standing issues, and left injunctive relief unaddressed as not properly presented.

On the MWTA claim specifically, the appellate court’s analysis was brief but decisive. Cohen had alleged that Consilio failed to provide the statutorily required notice of a change in its overtime policy under Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). But the Eighth Circuit observed that Cohen “stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him, and he points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” Given Cohen’s scant briefing and lack of statutory grounding for a private civil penalty remedy, the court invoked the well‑worn principle that issues not meaningfully briefed on appeal (and in this instance, not meaningfully developed below) are forfeited. The court cited Milligan v. City of Red Oak and declined to disturb the district court’s dismissal.

## The doctrinal takeaways

There are three doctrinal strands woven through this short passage of the opinion that litigators should take to heart.

First, remedial entitlement matters. The Eighth Circuit did not rule on whether the MWTA’s notice provisions were violated on the facts; it ruled that Cohen had not shown that those provisions create a private civil‑penalty remedy payable to him. When statutes allocate penalties or enforcement authority to a government official (as the court earlier concluded the MPWA does for certain “average daily wage penalties”), private plaintiffs must show that the statutory design permits the specific relief they seek. It is not enough to show an alleged statutory violation; counsel must also show where the statute grants a private remedy.

Second, forfeiture by under‑briefing remains a live procedural weapon. Courts routinely refuse to address under‑developed arguments. The Eighth Circuit’s citation of Milligan underscores that a party cannot salvage a thinly briefed claim on appeal; the district court’s reasoning—if any—may stand unchallenged. Practitioners should treat appellate briefing obligations as jurisdictional in practice: the failure to brief an issue adequately can be outcome‑determinative.

Third, stipulations that eliminate damages can put the remaining claim (penalties payable to government or injunctive relief) at risk of dismissal for lack of a concrete, continuing injury. The opinion’s treatment of the MFLSA penalties (vacated and remanded because of potential mootness and standing issues) contrasts with the MWTA result; in the MWTA context, Cohen neither marshaled remedial authority nor preserved argumentation that a non‑monetary or public‑interest remedy remained viable.

## Practical lessons for plaintiffs and counsel

Successful MWTA litigation depends as much on statutory craftsmanship as on facts. A few concrete practices should reduce the risk of a Cohen‑style loss:

- Identify the remedy in the complaint. Plead not only the statutory violation but also the specific relief authorized by the statute—or explain why equitable relief or a public‑interest theory permits a plaintiff to seek penalties otherwise payable to a government official. If the statute channels penalties to the Commissioner, explain how that channeling does not foreclose private relief (or show alternative grounds for relief).

- Preserve and develop arguments below. Don’t rely on appellate rescue. Fully brief statutory interpretation and remedial issues at summary judgment and offer the district court supporting authorities; an under‑developed theory below is likely waived on appeal.

- Avoid stipulating away core injuries without reservation. If you accept payment of back wages to resolve one discrete remedy (e.g., overtime), consider preserving the right to litigate non‑monetary relief, penalties, or injunctive claims. If you must stipulate, make explicit reservations about remaining claims and the rationale for why those claims remain justiciable.

- Think administratively where the statute does. If a statute expressly empowers the state commissioner to demand wages and assess penalties, coordinate with the agency or at least plead how private enforcement can proceed in parallel or why equitable relief is available.

- When in doubt, plead alternative remedies. Pair MWTA theories with MPWA or MFLSA claims (when appropriate) and with requests for declaratory or injunctive relief that do not depend on a private civil‑penalty theory payable to the Commissioner.

## Broader implications and where this might lead

Cohen is unlikely to be read as a broad attack on MWTA claims; instead, it will be cited as a cautionary tale about the mechanics of statutory enforcement. Practically speaking, plaintiffs’ firms will likely sharpen pleadings and summary‑judgment briefs to spell out remedial bases; courts will continue to hold litigants to ordinary briefing standards. Legislatively, the decision highlights a potential gap: where the Legislature intends a private remedy for notice or other technical violations, it should say so clearly if it wants private enforcement to be the norm rather than the exception.

For defense counsel, the ruling reinforces two attractive case‑management strategies: press for stipulations that resolve wages and liquidated damages early, and exploit careful statutory reading (or lack of plaintiff briefing) to move for summary judgment on remedies. For plaintiffs’ counsel, the lesson is simple and unforgiving: don’t let a strong factual record be undone by weak briefing of statutory remedies.

Cohen demonstrates that in wage litigation, as in so much of litigation generally, the merits are necessary but not sufficient. The ability to identify and defend a path to the relief sought — and to preserve that path through the district court and on appeal — can mean the difference between a recoverable wrong and an enforceable victory.
