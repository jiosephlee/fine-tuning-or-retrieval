Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Pleadings Matter: Why Cohen’s Late-Breaking Injunctive Theory Went Nowhere

## Why this case matters
The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is a reminder that in federal practice, how you plead your case matters as much as the merits. Cohen tried to pivot at summary judgment from a monetary overtime claim to seeking an affirmative injunction ordering his employer to rescind a contested overtime policy. The court refused to entertain that new remedial theory because it was never pleaded—relying on the familiar principle that new claims belong in an amended complaint, not in opposition briefs. That procedural posture proved decisive: plaintiffs in wage-and-hour litigation who hope to preserve forward-looking relief (and thus avoid mootness when defendants tender back pay) take heed.

## What happened (short version)
Cohen sued Consilio for unpaid overtime and related state-law penalties after Consilio announced a change to its overtime premium policy. Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages Cohen sought under the Minnesota FLSA (and sent stipulated payments to the putative class), and the parties asked the district court to resolve remaining statutory-penalty and injunction issues on summary judgment. In briefing, Cohen abandoned some earlier requests and, for the first time, sought an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind the 2019 policy. The district court did not consider that newly pressed remedy, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed that omission: “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs” (citing InfoDeli, LLC). The court left open the MFLSA penalties claim (vacating that portion of the judgment) because tendered back pay can moot monetary claims, but it rejected Cohen’s belated injunctive demand as improperly raised.

## The court’s reasoning
The Eighth Circuit’s rejection of Cohen’s late theory rests on two interconnected principles. First, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15 and the case law embody a norm that parties should not spring entirely new causes of action or remedies on an opponent at the summary-judgment stage. A summary-judgment response is not a vehicle to plead a new claim because the opposing party then lacks fair notice and the district court lacks a developed record tailored to that theory. Second, the panel relied on its own recent precedent (InfoDeli) to emphasize that amendments to the substantive scope of relief are properly effected through an amended complaint, not through argument introduced in opposition briefs. The district court’s omission, therefore, was not an abuse of discretion.

The opinion also signals another procedural and substantive concern that pushed against Cohen’s strategy: the risk of mootness. Consilio’s tender of overtime pay and liquidated damages meant the tangible monetary injury was addressed. To preserve a live federal case in those circumstances, plaintiffs often need prospective or injunctive relief to show continuing or imminent injury; but you cannot rely on a theory of forward-looking relief that the complaint never put at issue.

## Why shifting remedial theories at summary judgment is risky
Beyond the formal Rule 15 wrinkle, there are concrete reasons courts resist remedial pivots at summary judgment. The defendant has not had discovery directed at the new remedy; factual development on whether the policy is ongoing or whether rescission is necessary may be absent; the defendant lacks adequate notice to address the new relief; and allowing such pivots invites gamesmanship—plaintiffs could hold back requests until they see how defendant responds and then seek substantive relief without the normal pleading and amendment procedures.

From a strategic perspective, wage-and-hour plaintiffs who wait to request injunctive relief until after defendants tender back pay often find themselves too late. Tender may remove the plaintiff’s live monetary stake, leaving only forward-looking remedies as the basis for Article III jurisdiction. If those remedies were never pleaded, the court may dismiss or refuse to consider them. The Eighth Circuit’s remand on mootness of statutory penalties underscores the stakes: had Cohen pleaded an injunction at the outset, the court could have evaluated whether the policy posed a real and continuing threat and thus whether declaratory or injunctive relief kept the case alive despite the tender.

## How to preserve injunctive relief—practical lessons
Plaintiffs and counsel who want to avoid forfeiting prospective relief should heed three practical points.

First, plead forward-looking remedies from the outset. Include, in the complaint, clear requests for declaratory and injunctive relief tailored to the conduct you want the court to stop (e.g., rescission of an unlawful policy, a requirement to pay overtime going forward at a particular rate, or modification of notice protocols). If you believe a policy change is at issue, allege the policy, how it is unlawful, and why an injunction is necessary to prevent recurrence.

Second, be precise about the scope of injunctive relief. Vague requests for “all appropriate relief” are less helpful than specific, concrete requests (for example, a request that the employer reclassify certain positions, reinstate an overtime-premium practice, or provide specific employee notice). Specificity gives the defendant fair notice and helps justify expedited discovery on the question of ongoing violation.

Third, if a need to pivot arises mid-litigation, move to amend the complaint under Rule 15(a) promptly—do not merely argue a new theory in an opposition brief. Rule 15(a) directs that leave to amend be “freely given when justice so requires,” but courts may deny amendments that are untimely, prejudicial to the defendant, or futile. To improve the odds of a late amendment: explain the reason for delay, propose only narrow, tailored amendments, and show the amendment will not prejudice the defendant (for example, by proposing targeted expedited discovery or by explaining prior discovery already developed the relevant facts).

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Cohen reinforces a structural lesson: pleading shapes procedure and relief. For wage-and-hour litigation in particular, the case sharpens a familiar dynamic—defendants can neutralize monetary claims by tendering back pay, and plaintiffs must have contemporaneous claims for prospective relief if they wish to preserve a live controversy. The Eighth Circuit’s hesitance to entertain a new injunction theory at summary judgment also dovetails with separation-of-powers and notice principles: courts should adjudicate claims parties actually bring, after appropriate procedural steps, not theories first raised in response papers.

The opinion may also affect class litigation. Class plaintiffs who seek systemic relief to stop an employer’s policy must plead injunctive or declaratory relief clearly in the complaint. If they rely only on monetary damages, a defendant’s tender could scuttle the litigation by mooting the named plaintiff’s claim before class certification—leaving individual class members without a named representative with a live claim.

Finally, the concurrence’s skepticism about the standing consequences of statutory penalties (and the court’s vacatur of MFLSA summary judgment on mootness grounds) highlights another wrinkle: even when penalties are payable to the government rather than a plaintiff, Article III standing and mootness doctrines still govern. Plaintiffs should not assume that statutory penalties alone will preserve a live controversy where monetary relief has been satisfied. Injunctive relief remains the safest path to maintain jurisdiction when defendants attempt to end litigation through tender.

## Conclusion
Cohen v. Consilio is not a dramatic new rule of law, but it is a useful, concrete lesson in federal practice: if you want forward-looking relief, plead it timely and specifically. Don’t try to sneak new claims or remedies into a summary judgment response; courts expect parties to use the amendment process to enlarge the scope of relief. In wage-and-hour suits where defendants can blunt monetary claims by tendering back pay, failing to plead injunctive or declaratory relief can be fatal to the case’s survival. Counsel should plead forward and plead precisely—or be prepared to move early and explain why a late amendment is warranted.
