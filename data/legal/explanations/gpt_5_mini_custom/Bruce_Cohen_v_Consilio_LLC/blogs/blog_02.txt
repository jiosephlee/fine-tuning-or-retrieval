Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Civil Penalties and Article III in Wage-and-Hour Cases: Laidlaw’s Shadow over MFLSA Enforcement

## Why Cohen matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC highlights a recurring fault line in wage-and-hour litigation: what happens to a plaintiff’s Article III standing when an employer pays the wages demanded mid‑case but leaves statutory penalties on the table — penalties that, under Minnesota law as the court reads it, would be paid to the state or its commissioner rather than to the employee. The panel affirmed dismissal of some claims, but it vacated and remanded the district court’s judgment on Cohen’s Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties, instructing the district court to decide whether Cohen’s remaining claim is moot under the framework set out in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc. The opinion — and Judge Kobes’s skeptical concurrence — underscores how Laidlaw and the Supreme Court’s recent Article III jurisprudence (including Town of Chester’s point that standing and relief are claim‑specific) shape the practical ability of individual plaintiffs to pursue public‑facing penalties in private suits.

## How the Eighth Circuit framed the issue

The decision proceeds in familiar stages. Cohen admitted he’d been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. He nonetheless pressed for statutory penalties, which the parties agreed, unlike back pay, would be payable to the Commissioner. The court recognized that civil penalties payable to government actors do not categorically defeat standing: Laidlaw holds that a plaintiff can have a cognizable interest in penalties even when collected by the government because the penalties can redress an injury through deterrence and by encouraging discontinuance of ongoing illegal conduct. But standing is claim‑ and relief‑specific under Town of Chester, and the court correctly flagged that the case may be moot if the allegedly unlawful policy has been abandoned and is not reasonably likely to recur.

Because the record did not establish whether Consilio’s overtime policy remains in place, the panel concluded the jurisdictional question was live and remanded for the district court to determine whether there is a continuing controversy. The court emphasized the well‑settled rule that voluntary cessation does not automatically moot a case; a defendant cannot defeat jurisdiction merely by ending the challenged conduct unless it is “absolutely clear” the behavior will not recur. Judge Kobes’s concurrence adds a helpful caution: although Laidlaw can support a private plaintiff’s interest in public penalties, the doctrinal fit is not automatic — MFLSA penalties differ from the penalties in Laidlaw in ways the court did not fully brief — so the district court should consider those differences on remand.

## What the district court needs to decide (and what record it should develop)

On remand, the district court’s threshold task is factual: determine whether there remains a live controversy over the MFLSA penalties. That inquiry requires more than the stipulation that Cohen received his back pay. The court should develop a record answering whether Consilio’s allegedly violative overtime policy is still in force, and if it is not, whether there is a reasonable expectation that Consilio could or would resume the policy.

Useful facts and documentary proof the district court should seek include contemporaneous written policies and any subsequent revisions; company communications to employees about overtime pay practices (including the 2019 email and any later emails or memos); payroll records showing how overtime has been calculated and paid after the 2019 change; testimony from corporate representatives about whether the policy remains, the business rationale for past or present policies, and whether the employer reserves the right to revert to the old policy; and evidence of systemic practice (for example, whether offices outside Minnesota continue or resumed the challenged practice). The court should also consider whether Consilio has taken binding steps that would make recurrence unlikely — for instance, a court‑ordered injunction, a binding settlement providing for injunctive relief, or widespread, permanent policy changes reflected in formal corporate governance documents.

The district court should then assess whether, under Laidlaw, Cohen can plausibly show that future violations are reasonably likely such that civil penalties would provide the deterrent and prospective redress Laidlaw describes. If the record shows the policy persists or that the employer has a concrete ability and incentive to revert to the policy, the court should find the controversy live. If the record shows the policy has truly been abandoned and the employer’s cessation is both genuine and unlikely to be reversed, the claim as to penalties may be moot.

## The doctrinal tension: Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale vs. public penalties

The Eighth Circuit fairly summarizes the tension that underlies Laidlaw: civil penalties that inure to the public can still redress a private plaintiff’s interest by preventing future harm. But Judge Kobes’s concurrence raises an important question: do penalties payable to a state actor under the MFLSA map neatly onto Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale? The answer is not obvious and may depend on statutory structure and remedy design. Laidlaw involved environmental penalties and a defendant whose ongoing conduct plausibly put the plaintiff at risk; the Supreme Court found that the possibility of future violations — and the prospect that civil penalties would constrain them — meant the plaintiff had a cognizable interest even though it could not pocket penalty proceeds.

MFLSA statutory penalties that go to the Commissioner are similar in that penalties are public inurements. But there are differences that could matter: statutory allocation of recovered penalties, administrative enforcement mechanisms, and the remedial role of the Commissioner (including whether the Commissioner has independent prosecutorial authority) may influence the Article III analysis. The Eighth Circuit correctly refrained from resolving those finer points without a developed record and briefing; Judge Kobes’s concurrence signals that future panels or the Supreme Court may be asked to decide whether Laidlaw’s logic covers every statutory penalty regime the same way.

## Practical implications for litigants and courts

Cohen’s remand is a reminder of several practical lessons for litigants in wage‑and‑hour cases.

For plaintiffs, the decision underscores the importance of preserving a live controversy on the penalty question. If a defendant tenders back pay or liquidated damages, plaintiffs should seek to preserve claims for penalties and injunctive relief — and seek an evidentiary record on whether the offending policy remains. Early steps that help avoid mootness include promptly moving for class certification if appropriate (class claims raise separate mootness issues but can change the landscape), seeking a declaratory judgment or injunction as part of the complaint, and pressing for admissions or stipulated facts about whether the policy has been changed and whether the employer will continue the practice. If an employer pays back wages during litigation, plaintiffs should consider seeking a consent decree resolving both monetary and injunctive claims rather than accepting a unilateral tender that leaves only public penalties and thus raises standing issues.

For defendants, the case highlights a defensible tactic: paying wages demanded may reduce or remove a plaintiff’s personal injury and thereby complicate a penalty claim that is payable to the state. But voluntary payment is not a guaranteed jurisdictional escape hatch: courts will probe for continuing practices and for the reality of cessation. Employers should be prepared to show concrete and binding changes if they are to argue that a case is moot.

For courts, Cohen v. Consilio provides a useful roadmap for handling mid‑case tenders and voluntary cessation claims. The district court should make findings on whether challenged policies remain and whether cessation is genuine and permanent; those factual findings will decide the jurisdictional question and will help avoid piecemeal appeals.

## Broader doctrinal consequences

This remand may portend further doctrinal development. Different circuits have approached Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale and voluntary cessation doctrine with varying emphases. Cohen flags that Article III’s demand for a live case-or-controversy can create substantial practical limits on private enforcement of public penalties. If defendants can routinely moot cases by paying back pay while leaving statutes’ public penalties in play but without clear prospects of enforcement, private enforcement as a practical matter could be weakened — unless plaintiffs are able to secure injunctive relief, class certification, or otherwise obtain binding commitments from employers.

Judge Kobes’s invitation to scrutinize whether Laidlaw’s rationale uniformly supports claims for public penalties is likely to attract further attention. Lower courts will have to decide how much difference it makes that a penalty is payable to a public official rather than to the aggrieved individual. That question sits at the intersection of standing doctrine, remedial law, and statutory design — and it may push litigants to seek more robust injunctive or declaratory claims to avoid Article III problems.

## Bottom line

Cohen v. Consilio is a practical and doctrinally interesting decision: it affirms that paying a plaintiff’s back pay does not automatically render penalty claims moot, but it requires a concrete factual showing that wrongful conduct is likely to recur before a court will allow a private plaintiff to pursue penalties that inure to the state. The Eighth Circuit’s remand is a sensible, case‑specific application of Laidlaw and Town of Chester, and it invites district courts to develop the factual record needed to decide whether Article III permits private suits for public‑facing penalties to proceed after defendants tender full wages. Litigants on both sides should treat mid‑case payments as the start of a jurisdictional inquiry rather than its end.
