Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The Commissioner’s Penalty Monopoly: Eighth Circuit Narrows Private Remedies Under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act

## Why this case matters
In Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC, the Eighth Circuit confronted a deceptively simple question with outsized practical consequences: who may pursue the MPWA’s “average daily wage” penalty for unpaid wages under Minn. Stat. § 181.101? The court answered that question plainly — only the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry’s Commissioner may seek those penalties. The decision, grounded in textual analysis and statutory structure, removes a key enforcement lever from private plaintiffs litigating wage claims in Minnesota. That matters because penalty exposure is often the principal source of leverage in wage-and-hour litigation; its absence changes settlement dynamics, enforcement strategies, and the practical remedies available to aggrieved employees.

## What the court held
The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim. The panel concluded § 181.101’s penalty scheme is commissioner-centric: penalties are collectible only after the Commissioner serves a demand for payment and pursues collection, and any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee. Reading the statute against neighboring provisions such as §§ 181.13 and 181.14 — which expressly authorize employees to recover penalties in certain contexts — the court relied on the familiar canon that legislative silence across provisions is significant. Because the MPWA’s text reserves the penalty enforcement mechanism to the Commissioner, private plaintiffs cannot independently seek the average daily wage penalty under § 181.101 through their civil suits.

## How the court reasoned
The opinion performs a straightforward textualist inquiry. It starts from § 181.171, which authorizes civil actions for MPWA violations and provides that an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” But the panel did not treat that language as a grant of a private mechanism to impose all penalties the statute mentions. Instead, it turned to § 181.101 itself, which ties the availability of penalties to a Commissioner-initiated demand and collection process: the Commissioner serves a demand, waits ten days, and only then “may charge and collect” wages and a penalty, with collected funds distributed to the employee. That provision, the court reasoned, plainly contemplates administrative enforcement by the Commissioner rather than private civil actions for the same penalty.

The court reinforced its reading by comparing § 181.101 to separate MPWA provisions that explicitly authorize employee-initiated penalties when the Legislature intended that result — notably §§ 181.13 and 181.14. Citing Minnesota precedent (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.), the panel invoked the canon of expressio unius: when the Legislature knows how to grant employees a private right to recover penalties, its failure to do so in § 181.101 suggests it did not intend a private penalty remedy there. The opinion also leaned on lower-court authority applying the same textual reading (Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., D. Minn. 2021).

## Analytical perspective
The court’s conclusion is hardly surprising if you accept a plain-text approach to statutory interpretation. The statutory scheme reads like a deliberate design to channel penalty enforcement through the administrative apparatus of the Department of Labor and Industry. That design has two sensible internal logics: it centralizes investigations and penalty determinations in an agency with expertise and statewide perspective, and it lets the agency manage the distribution of recovered funds. But it also produces a more pro-employer litigation posture in private suits: absent an active commissioner-led enforcement action, plaintiffs in private litigation cannot extract the extra-damages penalty Congress or the Minnesota legislature built into the statute.

Two features of the opinion are worth underscoring. First, the court did not deny that employees ultimately receive penalty funds collected by the Commissioner — indeed, it highlighted that any money the Commissioner collects must be paid to the employee. The court’s point is procedural and institutional rather than substantive: the MPWA envisions administrative initiation of penalties even if the economic beneficiary is the private employee. Second, the decision rests heavily on the Legislature’s contrasting treatment of other statutes. That reliance is a double-edged sword: it is persuasive in a state-law context where the bench applies state canons, but it leaves open the policy question whether administrative channeling results in adequate enforcement in practice.

## Practical consequences for litigants and enforcement
For plaintiffs’ counsel, the ruling shifts the calculus in Minnesota wage cases. A private suit for unpaid overtime under § 181.101 now cannot — standing alone — yield the average daily wage penalty absent parallel administrative action by the Commissioner. That reduces the maximum downside exposure for employers in private litigation, weakening plaintiffs’ bargaining position at mediation and during settlement negotiations.

Practically, the decision means plaintiff-side strategies will likely bifurcate. One track is litigation focused on wage recovery and liquidated damages (where available), accepting that § 181.101 penalties are off the table in private court. The other is administrative: counsel must proactively engage the Department of Labor and Industry by filing administrative complaints and urging the Commissioner to serve the statutory demand that unlocks penalties. Because the Commissioner’s resources, priorities, and enforcement cadence may vary, this places significant weight on whether the agency will pursue the case energetically — and it introduces a layer of uncertainty and delay for workers seeking full statutory redress.

For defendants, the ruling offers an avenue to limit exposure: timely payment of wages may forestall commissioner-initiated penalties, and the absence of a private penalty remedy reduces leverage for class-wide treble-like exposure (the “average daily wage” penalty can be significant). That might tilt incentives toward paying what is due rather than litigating liability, but it could also encourage more aggressive policy changes by employers who view the administrative bottleneck as a protective buffer.

## Coordination with MFLSA, MWTA, and mootness concerns
Cohen’s case also touched on other statutes. The Eighth Circuit declined to resolve the parallel MFLSA statutory-penalty issue on the merits, vacating and remanding because the record leaves open whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in effect and thus whether Cohen retains a live interest (the court rightly flagged Article III mootness/standing issues). The MWTA claim was dismissed primarily for lack of developed argument on appeal. Those rulings remind practitioners that statutory choice and procedural posture matter: state-law limitations on private penalties do not necessarily foreclose federal FLSA-based strategies (though the MFLSA remand shows that Article III considerations can complicate recovery of penalties that are payable to the state).

## Strategic options for plaintiffs and counsel
Given Cohen, plaintiffs should consider at least three concurrent steps. First, file administrative complaints with the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry promptly and demand that the Commissioner serve the § 181.101 demand for payment; the statute itself requires commissioner initiation for penalties to become available. Second, structure civil suits to preserve claims that do not depend on the commissioner’s action (for example, wage recovery and liquidated damages under MFLSA where available), but be mindful of mootness — defendants can attempt to moot claims by tendering owed wages. Third, develop an advocacy strategy aimed at the agency: briefs, evidence packages, and media or stakeholder pressure may increase the odds the Commissioner acts.

Class-action counsel will need to adjust expectations about the remedies likely to be secured in state-court MPWA class suits versus federal FLSA collective actions. If penalties are a critical component of class recovery or attorney-fee calculations, plaintiffs may need to emphasize coordination with the Commissioner or seek alternative statutory vehicles that permit private penalties.

## Legislative fixes and broader lessons
If Minnesota’s Legislature thinks the Commissioner-only mechanism creates an enforcement gap — for example, if agency resource constraints leave many meritorious claims unaddressed — there are clear legislative fixes. The Legislature could amend § 181.101 to add an express private right to the average daily wage penalty, or revise § 181.171 to clarify that civil actions may seek both compensatory damages and the statutory penalties set out elsewhere in the MPWA. Alternatively, lawmakers could expand the Commissioner’s authority to delegate or accelerate enforcement, or provide targeted funding to the Department of Labor and Industry to ensure victimized employees receive timely penalty recovery.

The opinion also offers a broader doctrinal reminder: statutory structure and the presence or absence of specific employee-directed language matter. Courts will not lightly infer a private enforcement mechanism where the statute’s text centralizes enforcement authority in an administrative actor and where nearby provisions show the Legislature knows how to empower private suits when it wants to.

## Conclusion
Cohen v. Consilio is a practical decision with predictable doctrinal logic: the MPWA’s penalty machinery belongs to the Commissioner unless the Legislature says otherwise. For employees and their counsel, the ruling means rethinking enforcement strategies in Minnesota wage cases — pairing private suits for wages with administrative complaints and advocacy before the Commissioner will now be the path to seek statutory penalties under § 181.101. For policymakers, the decision highlights the tradeoff inherent in channeling enforcement through an agency: it can bring expertise and uniformity, but it can also limit private enforcement and weaken deterrence if the agency lacks the capacity or will to act.
