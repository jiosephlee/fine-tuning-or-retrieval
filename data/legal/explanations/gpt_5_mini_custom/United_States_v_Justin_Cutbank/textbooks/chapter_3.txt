Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 3: # Applying the Law to the Five Trial Rulings (A–E)

This chapter explains how the Eighth Circuit applied controlling evidentiary rules and standards of review to each of Cutbank’s five trial challenges. For each ruling (A–E) the court followed a familiar sequence: identify the governing legal principle (e.g., what counts as intrinsic evidence, when a limiting instruction is required, the scope of permissible impeachment), state the standard of review (usually abuse of discretion), apply the principle to the record facts, and decide whether any error was harmless. Understanding that sequence will help you read appellate opinions that review trial-court evidentiary decisions.

## A. Prior sightings of the sawed‑off rifle by D.F. and C.F.: intrinsic evidence, not Rule 404(b) propensity

The key legal question in A was whether testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 on prior occasions was forbidden propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) or admissible intrinsic evidence relevant to the charged possession. The court relied on the well‑established distinction between propensity evidence (extrinsic acts offered only to show a defendant’s character to prove action in conformity therewith) and intrinsic evidence (events that are part of the same transaction or are directly probative of an essential element, such as possession, identity, or motive).

The opinion cites United States v. Battle for the proposition that prior possession of a firearm is “directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” In practical terms, if a witness has repeatedly seen the defendant with the same gun before the charged incident, those sightings tend to prove continuous control or ownership and therefore are not being offered simply to show a criminal propensity. The district court made that factual-legal determination at the pretrial stage, and the Eighth Circuit treated it as a discretionary evidentiary ruling. Because the testimony bore directly on an element of the offense (possession/control of the weapon) and fit within the intrinsic evidence doctrine, the appellate court concluded that the district court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the testimony.

When you read this section of the opinion, note the two-step move typical in 404(b) disputes: identify the proffered purpose for the prior act (here, to show continuous possession/control), and evaluate whether the prior act is actually intrinsic to the charged conduct rather than extrinsic propensity evidence. The appellate court accepted the district court’s judgment that the prior‑sighting testimony was intrinsic, which is a deferential outcome in this type of evidentiary dispute.

## B. Denial of a pretrial limiting instruction about D.F.’s statements to police: hearsay and Confrontation Clause considerations

Cutbank asked the court to instruct the jury that D.F.’s statements to police could not be used as substantive evidence until D.F. testified, aiming to avoid hearsay and Confrontation Clause problems. The court applied two controlling ideas.

First, a trial judge need not repeatedly give limiting instructions at the precise moment evidence is introduced so long as an appropriate limiting instruction is provided at the close of trial; courts review refusals to give such piecemeal instructions for abuse of discretion (citing United States v. Bennett). Second, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant appears and testifies in person, so statements D.F. made to police lose their Confrontation‑Clause bite once she testified at trial (citing United States v. Charboneau).

Applying those principles, the Eighth Circuit observed that (1) the record did not show the district court admitted hearsay that required immediate limiting instructions, (2) D.F. later testified at trial, and (3) the Confrontation Clause problem therefore did not exist. The appellate court thus found no abuse of discretion in the district court’s handling of the requested pretrial instruction. Read this as an application of the principle that procedural protections (like limiting instructions or confrontation rights) must be considered in context: a pretrial request for prophylactic language is judged against what actually transpired at trial and whether the defendant was prejudiced by any admitted statements.

## C. Renewed objections to prior‑sighting testimony at trial: consistency with the pretrial ruling

At trial Cutbank renewed his objections to D.F.’s and C.F.’s testimony that they had previously seen Cutbank with the rifle. The district court overruled these renewed objections consistent with its pretrial ruling that the evidence was intrinsic. On appeal, Cutbank offered no new legal authority to undermine that pretrial ruling.

The appellate court’s decision here is an example of deference to a district court’s evidentiary rulings when the trial court’s reasons are consistent and grounded in the record. Because the court had already decided that prior sightings were directly relevant to possession and continuous control (Battle), sustaining that ruling at trial did not constitute an abuse of discretion. When a defendant reasserts an argument already rejected pretrial, the appellate inquiry focuses on whether the trial record or the asserted legal framework shows that the trial court’s decision was unreasonable; here it did not.

## D. Refusal to strike D.F.’s statement that her sister was killed by a gun: explanation for inconsistent statements and Rule 403 balance

During cross‑examination about prior inconsistent statements, D.F. explained some inconsistencies by saying she was “not familiar with guns” and that she did not like guns because her sister had been killed by a gun. Cutbank moved to strike the latter comment. The district court denied the motion.

The court applied two connected evidentiary ideas. First, testimony that tends to explain why a witness made inconsistent prior statements is generally permissible impeachment‑rehabilitation material: it is responsive to cross‑examination and helps the jury evaluate credibility. Second, even if such testimony has some prejudicial tendency, Rule 403 requires excluding only when the probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. The appellate court saw no substantial risk of unfair prejudice here: the statement was a brief explanation offered in response to cross‑examination, and C.F. later made a similar comment without objection, reducing the likelihood that the jury was unfairly swayed by an isolated emotional statement.

Thus the Eighth Circuit concluded the district court did not abuse its broad discretion in admitting the explanation. For students, the lesson is how explanation-for-inconsistency functions: when a witness offers a reason for an earlier inconsistent statement, courts generally allow it unless the statement’s prejudicial effect greatly outweighs its probative value. The appellate court will defer to the trial judge’s resolution of that balancing unless the ruling is unreasonable.

## E. Limits on reading from a police report to impeach C.F.: authentication and permissible impeachment

Cutbank’s defense sought to impeach C.F. by reading portions of a police report into the record that memorialized things C.F. said to an investigator. The district court allowed the defense to question C.F. about what she told the officer, but sustained government objections to counsel’s attempt to read the officer‑authored report into evidence without foundation. On appeal the court treated the rule governing extrinsic impeachment (and document use) as central.

The governing principle is that extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach a witness’s prior inconsistent statement, but such extrinsic evidence must be authenticated and otherwise admissible. The Eighth Circuit cited United States v. Cumbie for the authentication requirement. A report authored by an officer is not automatically the witness’s own statement; to admit the report (or read it into evidence) the proponent must establish that it is authentic and permissible as a prior recorded recollection or a statement of the witness under an applicable hearsay exception—or else obtain the witness’s admission of the report’s contents. The district court’s approach—permitting direct cross‑examination about what C.F. told police while requiring a foundation before admitting the officer’s report—reflects ordinary evidentiary safeguards and was a valid exercise of discretion.

The opinion also notes that Cutbank did not develop this argument on appeal, and that the government’s brief tried to expand the issues; the panel declined to entertain any problem not properly focused by the appellant. This illustrates two practical points for litigators: (1) you must preserve arguments and develop them on appeal, and (2) appellate courts will not overturn reasonable trial‑court document‑admission rulings where proper authentication was lacking.

## Harmlessness and cumulative‑error resolution

Even assuming any of the district court’s evidentiary rulings were erroneous, the Eighth Circuit applied the harmless‑error framework and found no reversible error. The opinion invokes the standard that an appellate court will reverse only if the erroneously admitted evidence may have substantially swayed the jury (citing DeMarce). Here, the record contained substantial independent evidence tying Cutbank to the rifle and the charged conduct: DNA on the gun matched Cutbank, the phone linked to the victim was recovered along the path between the victim’s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself, eyewitness identifications placed the weapon with Cutbank, and Cutbank physically resisted arrest and injured an officer. Given that robust mix of physical and testimonial proof, the appellate court held any evidentiary error either had only slight influence or none at all on the verdict.

The court also rejected a cumulative‑error claim. Even when multiple errors are alleged, reversal is appropriate only if the errors cumulatively result in substantial prejudice. Because the individual rulings were proper or harmless and the government’s evidence was strong, the panel concluded there was no substantial cumulative prejudice.

Conclusion

Across rulings A–E the Eighth Circuit applied familiar, deferential standards: admission of intrinsic evidence and reasonable Rule 403 balances are reviewed for abuse of discretion; limiting‑instruction refusals are reviewed for abuse of discretion and judged in context of what actually happened at trial and whether the declarant testified; extrinsic impeachment requires authentication; and any errors must be shown to have substantially influenced the jury to warrant reversal. In each instance the court concluded the district court acted within its broad trial discretion and, in any event, any error was harmless in light of the strong direct and physical evidence. This sequence—identify the rule, apply deferential review, and assess prejudice—is the template to watch for when reading appellate treatment of trial evidentiary rulings.
