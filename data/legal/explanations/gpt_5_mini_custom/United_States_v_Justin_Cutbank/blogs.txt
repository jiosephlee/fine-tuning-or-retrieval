Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Intrinsic Evidence, Not 404(b): The Eighth Circuit’s Broad Admissibility Rule for Prior Gun Possession

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a useful reminder that in the Eighth Circuit, testimony that a defendant was seen with the very firearm charged in a felon‑in‑possession case will usually be treated as intrinsic evidence rather than as a 404(b) “other‑acts” issue. That classification matters: intrinsic evidence is admissible without the government giving the 404(b) notice and without the trial court performing the usual Rule 404(b) analysis aimed at guarding against propensity inferences. The panel’s decision reaffirms Battle’s continuing force in firearm prosecutions and signals a prosecution‑friendly path for proving dominion and control over the specific weapon at issue.

## What the court held and how it reasoned

Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a sawed‑off Marlin .22 and received an ACCA sentence. On appeal he argued the district court abused its discretion in allowing two witnesses—his girlfriend D.F. and a housemate, C.F.—to testify that they had seen him with the sawed‑off rifle on prior occasions. The district court had denied the defense’s pretrial Rule 404(b) motion, accepting the government’s position that such testimony was intrinsic evidence relevant to “continuous possession” and to proving ownership/control of the exact gun found along the route Cutbank took when fleeing. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, relying principally on United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014): “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

The panel also rejected ancillary challenges about hearsay/Confrontation Clause issues and Rule 403 balancing tied to a witness’s statement that her sister had been killed by a gun. The court held the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because the declarant testified at trial, and found no Rule 403 abuse in allowing responsive testimony about the sister’s death. The court further noted authentication requirements for extrinsic impeachment materials under Cumbie and applied the harmless‑error standard to conclude any subsidiary evidentiary rulings did not substantially influence the verdict.

## Why the Eighth Circuit’s approach matters doctrinally

Two doctrinal points stand out. First, the Eighth Circuit continues to treat prior possession of the identical weapon as intrinsically tied to the charged possession offense. That reasoning is intuitive: if the same gun can be shown to have been seen in the defendant’s hands before the incident, the testimony tends to prove ownership, control, or at least dominion, without the need to posit an improper propensity inference. Battle remains the controlling point of reference for that proposition in this circuit.

Second, by labeling such evidence “intrinsic” the court sidesteps the formal 404(b) framework that would require (1) notice, (2) a finding that the evidence is offered for a non‑propensity purpose, and (3) a Rule 403 weighing of unfair prejudice. Although the court did address Rule 403 and found no abuse on the particular facts (e.g., the sister’s death comment arose in response to cross‑examination), the broader effect is to narrow the occasions in which defense counsel can rely on 404(b) procedural protections in gun cases where the government points to the same weapon.

These doctrinal moves have predictable consequences. They make it easier for prosecutors to place a defendant before jurors as someone who has had the weapon in hand before the charged episode. For the Eighth Circuit, that linkage often will prove dispositive in cases where direct evidence of possession at the precise charged moment is contestable. The decision thus reinforces a prosecutorial strategy of marshaling lay identification of the same gun as a bridge from past conduct to present possession.

## How this fits in the broader circuit landscape

Not all courts treat the “inextricably intertwined” or “same transaction” labels identically. Some circuits have narrowed the intrinsic‑evidence doctrine or cautioned against letting it swallow Rule 404(b)’s protections—insisting on a closer, necessary nexus between the other act and the charged crime before bypassing 404(b). The Eighth Circuit’s application in Cutbank, however, follows its own precedents and adopts a relatively expansive view: prior sightings of the same firearm help prove ownership/control and therefore qualify as intrinsic. Practitioners operating in circuits that are more skeptical of intrinsic evidence should expect a different set of arguments and rulings; in the Eighth Circuit, those arguments will often be uphill.

## Practical takeaways for defense counsel

Cutbank illustrates what defense counsel must do to try to cabin “continuous possession” proofs:

- Preserve the record early. File a specific 404(b) motion and get rulings on whether the testimony is intrinsic and, if so, an explanation on the record. Courts in the Eighth Circuit may still articulate the reasons they treat such evidence as intrinsic, which helps frame any later appeal.

- Attack identity of the weapon. The most direct way to blunt intrinsic‑evidence claims is to show that prior sightings were of a different gun, or that the earlier observations lack sufficient detail to tie them to the weapon recovered (different make/model/marks, different physical characteristics). Where the weapon is not unique, that argument gains traction.

- Emphasize time gaps and context. The farther separated temporally and factually the prior sightings are from the charged incident, the better the chance of persuading a court that the evidence is not properly intrinsic but rather an 404(b) other act subject to stricter scrutiny.

- Use authentication and foundation rules. As the Eighth Circuit noted via Cumbie, impeachment with extrinsic materials requires proper authentication. Where prosecutors rely on police reports or third‑party statements to shore up a witness’s memory of prior possession, insist on foundation and authentication.

- Seek limiting instructions and Rule 403 rulings. Even if a court treats the evidence as intrinsic, ask for a limiting instruction and a contemporaneous Rule 403 balancing on the record. That preserves appellate arguments that the evidence was unduly prejudicial or that the trial court failed to weigh prejudice.

- Cross‑examine vigorously on specifics. If a witness testifies she had seen the defendant with “the gun” a handful of times, elicit details (dates, settings, distinguishing features) to expose uncertainty and undermine the inference that the earlier sightings prove ownership of the recovered weapon.

## Bottom line

Cutbank reaffirms a familiar Eighth Circuit posture: testimony that a defendant had been seen with the same firearm before the charged incident is intrinsic and admissible to prove ownership and control. That ruling makes it easier for prosecutors to connect a defendant to the precise weapon and harder for defense counsel to invoke Rule 404(b)’s procedural protections in typical gun‑possession prosecutions. Defense attorneys in the circuit should plan their evidentiary strategies accordingly—attack identity and foundation early, preserve objections, and push for Rule 403 and limiting instructions—even while recognizing that, in the Eighth Circuit, the judicial inclination will often be to treat prior possession as probative, not improper propensity evidence.



# From Gall to Goodhouse: Stacking Enhancements and the Presumption of Reasonableness in ACCA Sentencing

## Why this case matters
United States v. Cutbank is a compact, instructive example of how multiple guideline enhancements can be layered to produce a very long advisory range in an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) case — and how difficult it is for a defendant to dislodge that result on appeal when a district court adopts an unobjected-to Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and imposes a within‑range sentence. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion affirms a 292‑month term at the bottom of the guideline range, grounded primarily on: (1) application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (a four‑level increase for possession “in connection with another felony offense”), (2) other discrete enhancements (NFA‑type weapon base level, obliterated serial number, reckless endangerment), and (3) the ACCA finding and the Guidelines rule that the greater offense level applies under § 4B1.4(b). Cutbank crystallizes two themes that every practitioner and sentencing judge should heed: the heavy consequence of failing to preserve objections to PSR facts and the appellate deference that attends within‑range ACCA sentences.

## The court’s reasoning in brief
The Eighth Circuit affirmed on both trial and sentencing issues, but the sentencing discussion is the lodestar for future litigation. The key points the panel relied on were straightforward.

First, the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection. That adoption supplied the factual basis to find the firearm was “used in connection with another felony,” triggering the four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. The court cited the guideline comment that the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (USSG §2K2.1, comment n.14(C)) and reviewed the standard of review for contested sentencing facts (citing Dunn for the clear‑error standard).

Second, the panel recognized that, because ACCA applied, the total offense level produced by the ACCA guideline (USSG §4B1.4) would have been 34 but the stacking of enhancements pushed the total to 35; the Guidelines themselves direct that “the greater offense level applies” under §4B1.4(b).

Third, the court treated the within‑range 292‑month sentence as presumptively reasonable on appeal (citing Goodhouse), noting the district court had considered the §3553(a) factors and the defendant’s arguments. That presumption, together with the deference afforded to a district court’s factual findings at sentencing, proved fatal to Cutbank’s challenge that the enhancement and sentence were substantively unreasonable under §3553(a).

## The legal mechanics: how the enhancements stack
Cutbank illustrates the mechanics by which a PSR’s unchallenged facts plus guideline text can transform a firearms case. Several discrete enhancements were applied to reach the offense level used to calculate the advisory range: an elevated base offense level because the weapon qualified as an NFA‑style firearm (sawed‑off rifle), a three‑level adjustment for an obliterated serial number, a two‑level reckless endangerment increase tied to flight from officers, and the contested four‑level §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for possession in connection with another felony.

Two features make this stacking potent. One is the guideline comment explicitly authorizing the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement without requiring a formal charge or conviction for the “other felony,” so courts look to underlying facts rather than to parallel prosecutions. The other is §4B1.4(b)’s instruction that, where the career offender/ACCA guideline produces a lesser total offense level than the adjusted §2K2.1 total, the greater level is used — which runs the enhancements into the ACCA framework and can ratchet the range even higher. The net result: once a district court accepts the PSR’s account, the pathway to a significantly increased advisory range is direct.

## The presumption of reasonableness and the limits of §3553(a) arguments
Cutbank also shows the uphill climb a defendant faces when asking a district court for a below‑guidelines sentence on §3553(a) parsimony grounds, and then trying to overturn a within‑range sentence on appeal. The Eighth Circuit follows the now‑familiar trilogy of precedents: district courts must correctly calculate the Guidelines (Gall), appellate review first ensures no significant procedural error (Rosales‑Mireles), and within‑range sentences are presumptively reasonable on appeal (Goodhouse). The panel emphasized that a district court need not give a “mechanical recitation” of all §3553(a) factors so long as the record shows the court considered the factors and the defendant’s arguments — a record the court found present here.

That presumption of reasonableness is a powerful backstop. Even if a defense lawyer can persuade a sentencing court that an enhancement overstates culpability or double counts conduct, failure to make specific objections and create a developed record reduces the chance of reversal: appellate review defers to factual findings and to the district court’s sentencing exercise, particularly where the sentence is within the calculated range.

## Double‑counting concerns — recurring but unresolved
Although the Eighth Circuit did not expressly decide a “double counting” objection here, the case highlights why such objections are common in firearms sentencing. Defendants frequently argue that the same core conduct — brandishing or using a gun during a single episode of wrongdoing — should not be counted both to qualify them for ACCA and again to support a §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, or be used simultaneously to increase offense level and criminal history consequences. Cutbank’s brief invocation of §3553(a)’s parsimony principle gestures at that tension, but the panel’s decision illustrates the practical challenges of converting doctrinal concerns into reversible error.

Two doctrinal points bear emphasis. First, §2K2.1 comment n.14(C) contemplates enhancement based on uncharged or unconvicted conduct, so defendants cannot rely on the absence of a separate prosecution to defeat the enhancement. Second, when the Guidelines themselves create a mechanism to choose the greater offense level (§4B1.4(b)), the Guidelines’ internal structure can defeat arguments that the enhancements improperly duplicate ACCA’s effect. Those rules do not eliminate the possibility that a district court could (on a developed record) find the enhancements substantively unjustified under §3553(a), but Cutbank shows appellate courts will defer where the Guidelines were properly calculated and the district court gave meaningful consideration to the statutory sentencing factors.

## Practical takeaways for defense counsel
Cutbank furnishes several practical lessons.

First, preservation matters. When a PSR contains facts that, if adopted, will trigger enhancements, timely, specific objections and a hearing are essential. The Eighth Circuit repeatedly treated the district court’s unobjected PSR adoption as dispositive. A defendant should secure findings on the record — contest provenance, challenge relevance and authentication, and demand that the government prove the factual predicates for any enhancement by the appropriate standard (typically preponderance of the evidence).

Second, challenge the factual predicate for a §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement head on. The government must establish that possession or use of the firearm was “in connection with” another felony — i.e., that the firearm facilitated, furthered, or was closely related to another criminal objective. That is a factual inquiry amenable to adversarial testing at sentencing; don’t leave it to the PSR.

Third, develop the §3553(a) record. If you contend the stacking of enhancements produces a punishment greater than necessary, make a detailed §3553(a) argument and elicit the sentencing court’s reasoning on the record. Appellate courts presume the district court considered the factors, but a bald assertion will not substitute for a reasoned, specific plea for lenity supported by the record.

Fourth, anticipate and brief double‑counting and proportionality claims in the district court. Where the same conduct is doing heavy lifting for multiple guideline calculations, show how the Guidelines’ structure and the sentencing factors counsel against compounding punishment.

## Conclusion
United States v. Cutbank is a reminder that in ACCA and serious firearms cases, small procedural lapses at sentencing can have large substantive consequences. The combination of an unobjected‑to PSR, guideline comment language that validates enhancements absent separate charges, and the Guidelines’ own “greater offense level” rule can produce an advisory range that is hard to contest on appeal. For defense counsel, the path forward is clear: litigate the PSR and the enhancement predicates at sentencing, develop a robust §3553(a) record, and press factual and legal objections while there is still time. On the appellate side, Cutbank confirms the circuit’s continued deference to within‑range ACCA sentences where the Guidelines were correctly calculated and the district court gave meaningful consideration to the statutory factors.



# Hearsay, Confrontation, and Cross-Examination Pitfalls: The Eighth Circuit’s Practical Evidentiary Playbook

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank offers a compact clinic in routine — but often decisive — evidentiary maneuvers: when prior sightings of a weapon are intrinsic rather than impermissible character evidence; when and whether a trial court must give a limiting instruction about out‑of‑court statements; how the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant later testifies; and what foundation is required to use police reports as extrinsic impeachment. The opinion is not revolutionary, but it matters because it shows how deferential appellate review (abuse of discretion and harmless‑error standards) interacts with fairly common trial choices. For trial lawyers, the decision is a useful set of practice notes: if you don’t preserve issues carefully and can’t show prejudice, the district court’s discretionary calls will survive.

## The court’s reasoning, in brief

The Eighth Circuit affirmed Cutbank’s conviction and sentence after rejecting several evidentiary objections that the defendant raised on appeal. First, the court held that testimony by the victim and a housemate that they had previously seen the sawed‑off Marlin .22 with Cutbank was intrinsic evidence relevant to the charged possession, not forbidden propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The panel relied on its prior decision in Battle and the familiar proposition that prior possession of the same weapon can help establish ownership or control.

Second, Cutbank sought a pretrial limiting instruction forbidding the jury from treating D.F.’s out‑of‑court statements to police as substantive proof until she testified. The district court declined to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction, admitting officer testimony about events to explain the officers’ conduct and reserving any instruction to the close of trial. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit applied Bennett to uphold that approach: a trial court need not give a limiting instruction at the moment evidence is admitted so long as an appropriate instruction is given (or the issue is otherwise cured), and here the record showed no hearsay abuse and, in any event, D.F. testified at trial. The panel relied on Charboneau for the straightforward Confrontation Clause point: if the declarant appears and testifies, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied.

Third, the court affirmed the district court’s refusal to let the defense read portions of a police report into the record for impeachment without authentication. The panel cited Cumbie: extrinsic evidence used to impeach by prior inconsistent statement must still be authenticated. Allowing counsel to read unattributed report passages into evidence without foundation would be improper.

Fourth, the court rejected an argument that the elicited testimony that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun was unfairly prejudicial under Rule 403. That testimony was responsive to cross‑examination about inconsistent statements and therefore admissible; the defendant pointed to no appreciable prejudice.

Finally, the panel disposed of any cumulative‑error claim under the standard that a conviction will not be overturned unless the errors substantially swayed the jury, noting the government’s substantial physical and testimonial evidence.

## Analytical perspective

Cutbank reinforces familiar lines in the Eighth Circuit, and it does so predictably: where the contested evidence is functionally probative of an element of the crime (here, control/possession of a particular firearm), courts will label it intrinsic and permit it; where hearsay statements are explained by a later in‑court declarant, Confrontation worries evaporate; and where defense counsel wants to use a third‑party police report to impeach, authentication remains an absolute gating requirement.

Two practical subtleties are worth emphasizing. First, the panel’s treatment of the limiting‑instruction request is permissive but not boundless. Bennett permits a court to defer the instruction to the end of trial, but that discretion turns on the character of the challenged testimony and the likelihood of juror confusion or prejudice. In a close case in which officers’ testimony about a complainant’s out‑of‑court statements could be the centerpiece of the government’s case before the declarant ever appears, a contemporaneous limiting instruction might be sensible and defensible. Defense lawyers who want that protection should ask for it expressly and preserve the record.

Second, the court’s approach to police reports for impeachment highlights a recurring trap for the defense. There are reliable ways to place a prior inconsistent statement before the jury without reading an unsigned police report aloud: obtain the witness’s personal adoption of the report, use prior recorded testimony, make the witness confirm the written statement or read the relevant language into the record through the witness, or authenticate the report under an applicable exception (business records, recorded recollection, etc.). Failure to lay that foundation invites a sustained objection and an adverse, appellate‑proof ruling like the one here.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Doctrinally, Cutbank is an incremental but useful confirmation of existing principles rather than a course change. It reiterates the intrinsic‑evidence exception to Rule 404(b) where prior possession helps establish current possession or control, a line with which the lower courts are well familiar. That stability matters: it gives prosecutors confidence to put before juries testimony that ties a particular weapon to a defendant across time, and it gives defense counsel a predictable standard on which to strategize.

On Confrontation Clause doctrine, the opinion simply applies settled precedent: an in‑court witness who testifies cures potential Crawford problems with prior hearsay, provided the testimony is genuine and responsive to cross‑examination. But practitioners should not take the ruling as a license to blithely allow officers to narrate unsworn victim statements whenever convenient. The timing and context matter: where officers’ retelling risks becoming substantive proof in the absence of the declarant, defense counsel should press the point early.

The authentication requirement for police reports used in impeachment counsel caution for the defense and clarity for prosecutors. Defense teams should plan foundational steps well before cross‑examination; prosecutors should be prepared to authenticate or, absent that, to object and keep the competing narrative before the jury.

Finally, the court’s aside about appellees “unnecessarily expand[ing] the issues on appeal” functions as a practical reminder: appellate courts expect litigants (on both sides) to frame clear, discrete issues and to brief them with supporting authority. Overbroad or amorphous appellate briefs risk forfeiture on procedural grounds and irritate courts asked to clean up sprawling litigation.

## Practical takeaways for practitioners

For defense counsel: preserve objections with specificity; if you think officer testimony about a complainant’s out‑of‑court statements will be prejudicial, ask for a contemporaneous limiting instruction and note the request on the record; when impeaching a witness with a prior inconsistent statement, secure authentication or use the witness to place the statement before the jury rather than reading third‑party reports aloud; and where emotionally charged testimony is elicited in response to your cross, be prepared to show actual prejudice if you later seek relief under Rule 403.

For prosecutors: when relying on prior witness statements, consider calling the declarant to cure Crawford concerns; ensure you can authenticate records you intend to use for impeachment; and, when offering evidence of prior sightings of a weapon, be prepared to articulate why that testimony is intrinsic and necessary to show ownership or control.

## Conclusion

Cutbank is a pragmatic opinion that underscores the heavy tilt in appeals toward district‑court discretion on evidentiary questions and the difficulty of prevailing on harmless‑error review when the government’s case is strong. It supplies a tidy playbook for trial counsel: think ahead about foundations, preserve specific objections, and be strategic about when to demand limiting instructions. The opinion won’t reshape doctrine, but it should sharpen trial strategy on several recurring evidentiary battlegrounds.



# ACCA After Erlinger: Jury Findings and the Eighth Circuit’s Streamlined Review in United States v. Cutbank

## Why Cutbank matters

United States v. Cutbank — an October 7, 2025 decision by an Eighth Circuit panel — is not a flashy doctrinal pivot, but it is an instructive snapshot of how lower courts are managing the interplay between jury factfinding for ACCA enhancements and judge-driven guideline calculations in the wake of recent developments (what practitioners here are calling “post‑Erlinger” practice). The case shows two linked realities: first, trial juries increasingly are being asked to resolve the ACCA-triggering questions that matter to statutory punishment (here, the jury found Cutbank had three or more prior violent felonies, which brought the ACCA into play); and second, once a sentencing court properly calculates the Guidelines range — often relying on an adopted Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) — appellate courts will give substantial deference, making it difficult to overturn guideline-driven within-range sentences.

Cutbank therefore highlights the tactical fault lines defense counsel must guard: which ACCA facts to put to the jury, what to preserve (and how) at sentencing for objections to PSR narratives, and how appellate review will treat sentencing challenges when the district court appears to have followed the mechanics the courts demand.

## What the Eighth Circuit did

The facts underlying Cutbank’s conviction are stark: a violent domestic incident, flight and resistance to police, recovery of a sawed-off Marlin .22 with Cutbank’s DNA on it, and eyewitness identification placing the weapon with him before the night in question. The jury convicted him of being a felon in possession under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), and the jury specifically found he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, triggering ACCA enhancements.

On appeal Cutbank raised a mélange of evidentiary objections and attacked a four‑level guidelines enhancement under USSG §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (possession “in connection with another felony offense”). The Eighth Circuit rejected the evidentiary claims as either unsupported or harmless in light of the strong record. As to sentencing, the court emphasized two linked principles. First, the record shows the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements “without objection,” and those adopted facts were sufficient to support the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. Second, because the district court correctly calculated the applicable Guidelines and imposed a within-range sentence while stating it considered the §3553(a) factors, appellate courts will ordinarily presume the sentence reasonable and grant only deferential review.

The opinion reiterates the settled procedural point from Gall and Rosales‑Mireles: a correct Guidelines calculation is a prerequisite to substantive reasonableness review, and the Eighth Circuit will not demand a “mechanical recitation” of §3553(a) factors so long as the record shows they were considered.

## Where Erlinger and Cutbank intersect — and where questions remain

The opinion itself does not redraw ACCA doctrine, but it reflects a practical posture increasingly visible after Erlinger (and the subsequent district and circuit practice that followed): when ACCA is at issue, courts often submit the predicate‑conviction questions — particularly the “different occasions” or multiplicity questions that determine whether prior convictions count separately — to juries. Cutbank’s posture, with the jury having found three or more prior violent felonies, fits that trajectory. That matters because, where jury findings are made, the government has satisfied the Apprendi/Blakely/Alleyne line of concerns about judicial factfinding increasing statutory penalties.

But Cutbank also underscores an open set of questions that practitioners should watch. Erlinger (and the post‑Erlinger practice noted by courts) has not settled every boundary of what must be found by a jury versus what a judge may decide at sentencing. Is the jury required to resolve the character of each prior conviction (for example, categorical versus conduct‑based inquiries), or may some predicate‑related factual nuances be resolved by the judge so long as the jury determines the ultimate ACCA threshold? Lower courts continue to differ on the contours of that division, and Cutbank shows appellate courts will be disposed to accept a district court’s handling of those questions when the procedural boxes are checked — jury findings where parties press for them, and an adopted PSR to supply the factual substrate otherwise.

## PSRs, §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and the practical import of unobjected facts

Cutbank’s unsuccessful attack on the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement is a useful cautionary tale about PSRs. The guideline commentary expressly allows the enhancement “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The PSR in Cutbank’s case recommended multiple enhancements and was adopted by the district court without objection. On appeal, that adoption supplied the factual basis for the four‑level enhancement; the Eighth Circuit applied ordinary (and deferential) review and found no clear error.

Two practical lessons flow from that posture. First, defense counsel must actively contest PSR statements they dispute — timely, specific objections preserve issues for later review. Second, when the government seeks enhancements tied to “connection with another felony,” it will often rely on PSR narratives and non‑conviction conduct; judges commonly accept those narratives absent timely, authenticated dispute. Cutbank reiterates the point that failure to contest PSR facts is frequently fatal to appellate challenges.

The opinion also touches on authentication and impeachment rules in trial evidence disputes (for example, the court permitted defense cross-examination but required foundation for reading portions of a police report into the record). These evidentiary skirmishes matter because the same factual threads that support trial convictions often feed PSR narratives and sentencing enhancements; a robust, preserved record at trial improves the defendant’s posture at sentencing and on appeal.

## Appellate posture: constrained review when the Guidelines are properly calculated

Cutbank demonstrates the Eighth Circuit’s streamlined approach on appeal to sentences within correctly calculated guideline ranges. The court treats a within-Guidelines sentence as presumptively reasonable and will not upset the district court absent clear procedural error or substantive unreasonableness demonstrated in the record. Importantly, the circuit requires only that the record show the court considered §3553(a) factors; it does not demand an itemized or formulaic recitation.

For defense counsel, that means two things: pressing for a lower Guidelines calculation (e.g., by litigating enhancements and base‑level components) is often the most effective route to a more favorable sentence, and preserving objections to PSR facts and to the admission of the underlying evidence becomes doubly important because appellate courts will otherwise decline to second‑guess a within‑range sentence grounded in an adopted PSR.

## Practice pointers and unresolved issues for practitioners

Cutbank’s practical lessons are straightforward. At trial and in the jury charge phase, defense counsel should insist on clear jury findings on any fact that could elevate statutory exposure under ACCA — and, when appropriate, press for specific interrogatories so the record plainly shows what the jury decided. At sentencing, scrutinize the PSR line by line: lodge timely, specific objections, request evidentiary hearings when facts are disputed, and ensure the district court does not adopt contested assertions without addressing objections. Where a guideline enhancement rests on conduct for which there was no separate charge or conviction, demand the government produce admissible, authenticated proof sufficient for the judge to find the necessary facts; do not rely on the PSR as a fallback.

On the doctrinal front, Cutbank highlights that we remain in a transitional posture: courts are accommodating jury resolution of ACCA-triggering issues in many cases, but the exact demarcation between what juries must decide and what judges may find for guideline purposes is still being worked out in lower courts. That uncertainty will likely drive continued litigation over jury interrogatories, the admissibility and sufficiency of PSR materials, and the interplay between Apprendi‑line jury requirements and guideline factfinding.

## Bottom line

Cutbank is not a doctrinal watershed, but it is a practical road map. When a jury finds the ACCA threshold, and a sentencing court adopts a PSR without objection, appellate courts will routinely affirm tough, within‑range sentences. For defense lawyers that means litigating FACTS early and often — in pleadings, at trial, in jury instructions, and at the PSR stage — because once the PSR is adopted and the Guidelines are correctly computed, the path to relief on appeal becomes narrow.
