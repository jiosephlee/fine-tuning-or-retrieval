Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the Eighth Circuit distinguish 'intrinsic' firearm-possession evidence from Rule 404(b) propensity evidence?
Question:
The panel allowed D.F. and C.F. to testify that they had seen the same sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle in Cutbank’s possession on earlier occasions, treating that as intrinsic evidence of the charged § 922(g) possession and citing United States v. Battle. What makes prior sightings of the same gun 'intrinsic' rather than 404(b) propensity evidence? Do courts require some threshold showing that it is in fact the same specific firearm (e.g., serial number, unique features), or will a witness’s identification based on appearance suffice? How does the 'continuous possession' concept work in this context, and does Rule 403 balancing still apply even if the evidence is labeled intrinsic?
Answer:
Short answer
The Eighth Circuit treats prior sightings of the same gun as “intrinsic” (not 404(b) propensity) when those sightings are directly probative of the charged possession — i.e., they help prove identity, ownership, control, or continuous possession of the weapon. United States v. Justin Cutbank applied United States v. Battle for that point. The Cutbank panel did not require proof of a serial number or other forensic uniqueness in that case; witness identification of the distinctive sawed‑off Marlin .22 (corroborated by the physical recovery of the rifle and a DNA match to defendant) was sufficient to support the intrinsic-characterization. And even when evidence is treated as intrinsic, the district court retains its Rule 403 discretion to exclude evidence as unfairly prejudicial; Cutbank simply failed to show such prejudice.

Explanation and authority from the opinion

1) The doctrinal line the court applied
- The court relied on Battle and explained the controlling idea: “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.” Slip op. § II.A (quoting Battle, 774 F.3d at 511).  
- Applied to Cutbank, testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen the same sawed‑off rifle on prior occasions was held to be intrinsic because it bore directly on the element of possession and on “continuous possession of the firearm before the incident.” Slip op. § II.A–C.

2) What courts look for — identity, ownership/control, continuity
- The point is whether the prior sightings are proof of the charged conduct (possession) rather than proof of defendant’s bad character or propensity. If the prior act helps establish that the defendant owned or controlled the very gun charged in the indictment (or that he had it continuously), it is intrinsic. Cutbank treats the witnesses’ prior sightings as relevant to exactly that question. Slip op. § II.A–C.

3) Do courts demand serial numbers or other mechanical proof?
- The Cutbank opinion does not impose a categorical requirement of a serial‑number match or other forensic uniqueness. The court accepted testimony that witnesses had seen “the sawed‑off rifle” a “handful of times” (D.F.) and “once or twice” (C.F.) as intrinsic evidence, in the context of other corroborating facts (police recovered a sawed‑off Marlin .22 along the path between the scenes and DNA on the gun matched Cutbank). Slip op. §§ I, II.A–C.  
- Practically, that means witness identification by appearance can suffice to treat prior sightings as intrinsic when (as here) the identification and surrounding facts make it reasonable to infer the prior sightings were of the same weapon.

4) Continuous‑possession concept
- Cutbank frames the testimony as demonstrating “continuous possession” — prior sightings show the defendant had possession or control of the same weapon before the charged incident, which makes later possession more likely and is therefore directly probative of the offense. Slip op. § II.A.

5) Rule 403 balancing still matters
- The opinion demonstrates that the court reviews district courts’ evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion and that unfair prejudice under Rule 403 is a relevant consideration. Although the court accepted the evidence as intrinsic, it also noted that the defendant pointed to no unfair prejudice and cited Fed. R. Evid. 403 when rejecting other objections. Slip op. §§ II.C–D. In short: labeling evidence “intrinsic” does not eliminate the district court’s Rule 403 gatekeeping; it merely makes exclusion for propensity less apt where the evidence is genuinely probative of an element of the offense. Cutbank found no abuse of discretion in the balancing the district court performed.

Practical takeaways for practitioners (from Cutbank)
- If prior sightings bear directly on identity, ownership, control, or continuity, argue they are intrinsic (Battle/Cutbank).  
- Corroboration (physical recovery of the same sort of gun, forensic links like DNA, temporal proximity) strengthens the intrinsic characterization. Cutbank relied on witnesses’ descriptions plus recovery of a sawed‑off Marlin .22 and a DNA match. Slip op. §§ I, II.A–C.  
- Don’t assume a serial‑number match is required; courts may accept appearance‑based identifications when the totality of the evidence supports that the prior sightings were of the same weapon.  
- Even so, be prepared to litigate Rule 403 prejudice — intrinsic evidence can still be excluded if it is unfairly prejudicial, and the district court’s balancing will be reviewed for abuse of discretion.

References from the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank, No. 24‑1225, slip op. §§ I–II (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) (citing United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014)).

### Was a contemporaneous limiting instruction required when officers relayed D.F.’s out-of-court statements before she testified?
Question:
Before trial, the defense asked the court to instruct the jury not to consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence until D.F. testified, to avoid hearsay/Confrontation issues. The court declined, and the panel later said no hearsay was admitted and the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because D.F. ultimately testified. Are officers’ references to a victim’s statements admissible to show context/why police acted, and if so is a limiting instruction typically required at that time? If the statements were 'testimonial' under Crawford, does the declarant’s later in-court testimony cure any Confrontation problem stemming from earlier admission? What standard of review applies to the refusal to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction, and how much preservation was needed here?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes — under the Eighth Circuit’s reasoning in Cutbank, officers may recount a victim’s out‑of‑court statements to explain the context for police actions (what the officers knew and why they acted). The district court has discretion whether, and when, to give a limiting instruction about how the jury may use those officer‑recounted statements.
- A contemporaneous (immediate) limiting instruction is not categorically required; the court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” United States v. Bennett, quoted in United States v. Cutbank, No. 24‑1225, slip op. § II.B (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).
- If the statements were “testimonial” under Crawford, the Confrontation Clause problem is cured if the declarant later appears and testifies in court: “The Confrontation Clause is satisfied ‘when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.’” (quoting United States v. Charboneau in Cutbank).
- The standard of review for refusing a contemporaneous limiting instruction is abuse of discretion. In Cutbank the court found no abuse because (a) no hearsay was admitted during trial and (b) the declarant (D.F.) testified.

Short explanation and how Cutbank applies
1) Officers’ references as context
- The government argued (and the Court accepted) that officers’ testimony about what D.F. told them was relevant to show the context for police conduct and their knowledge at the time. Cutbank cites that argument and treats it as a permissible use of officer testimony. Cutbank therefore recognizes (at least in this case) that such testimony can be admissible for that non‑hearsay purpose.

2) Is a contemporaneous limiting instruction required?
- Cutbank relies on United States v. Bennett for the proposition that a district court need not give a limiting instruction at the moment each item of evidence is admitted; a timely, appropriate instruction at the close of trial can suffice. The Eighth Circuit reviewed the district court’s refusal to give the requested pretrial contemporaneous instruction for abuse of discretion and found no abuse.

3) Effect of later in‑court testimony (Confrontation Clause)
- The court explicitly held that the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because D.F. subsequently testified at trial. Quoting Charboneau, the panel said the Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Thus, where the (allegedly testimonial) out‑of‑court statement is later matched by live testimony, Cutbank treats that as curing any Crawford problem.

4) Standard of review and preservation
- Standard of review: abuse of discretion for the denial of the requested limiting instruction (Cutbank § II.B, citing Bennett).
- Preservation: Cutbank had made a pretrial request that the jury be instructed not to consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence until she testified; that request was the basis for appellate review. The panel therefore treated the issue as preserved and resolved it on the merits (finding no abuse of discretion because no hearsay was admitted and D.F. testified). Cutbank also suggests the district court could have given a closing limiting instruction if needed; the government had argued a bench instruction at close of trial is “customary and sufficient,” and the court accepted that approach.

Practical takeaways for trial practice (what the opinion implies)
- If you fear officer testimony will convey hearsay that could be used substantively, ask for a contemporaneous limiting instruction pretrial and renew the objection when the testimony occurs — that preserves the issue and frames the record.
- But expect the district court to have discretion to decline an immediate instruction so long as it gives an appropriate limiting instruction later (and especially if the declarant will testify at trial; Cutbank treats that as curing any Confrontation issue).
- If the declarant does testify live, Cutbank squarely holds the Confrontation Clause is satisfied.

References from Cutbank (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025)
- Reliance on Bennett for the “need not instruct . . . at the time” principle and abuse‑of‑discretion review (Cutbank § II.B).
- Reliance on Charboneau for the rule that live testimony by the declarant satisfies the Confrontation Clause (Cutbank § II.B).

### Did the court properly deny the motion to strike D.F.’s remark that her sister was killed by a gun under Rule 403?
Question:
On cross about inconsistencies, D.F. volunteered that she dislikes guns because her sister was killed by one. The court denied the motion to strike, and the panel found no unfair prejudice. How do courts assess the risk of unfair prejudice from emotionally charged background facts like a family member’s death, especially when they could bolster the witness’s credibility or evoke sympathy? Does it matter that the defense elicited the context on cross? Is a sua sponte curative instruction expected in this situation, and under what circumstances would admitting such a remark be reversible error under Rule 403?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — under the facts and law the Eighth Circuit upheld the district court’s denial of the motion to strike. The panel explained why the remark was admissible (and not unfairly prejudicial) and articulated the standard for reversal.

Why the court treated the remark as admissible
- Rule 403 requires balancing probative value against unfair prejudice. The opinion treats the comment as directly probative: D.F.’s statement explaining she “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun” was given in response to cross‑examination about prior inconsistent statements. The panel says that the testimony “responded to cross‑examination regarding inconsistent prior statements,” and thus had legitimate explanatory value (Opinion § II.D).
- The defense elicited the context on cross. That matters: when a witness’s testimony is responsive to defense questioning, courts are less likely to view the statement as unfairly prejudicial because the defendant “opened the door” to the subject matter. The opinion notes both that the remark was responsive and that C.F. later mentioned the same fact without objection, undercutting any argument of unique inflammatory impact.

Curative instruction
- A sua sponte curative instruction is not required as a matter of course. The opinion relies on the general principle that a district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial” (citing United States v. Bennett). The panel did not find fault in the district court’s handling here and observed no prejudice that would have made a curative instruction necessary.

When admitting such remarks becomes reversible error
- The panel applied the usual abuse‑of‑discretion/harmless‑error framework. It emphasized that the appellant pointed to “no unfair prejudice or confusion” and that none was apparent (Opinion § II.D). The court also summarized the standard for reversal: it will reverse only if the jury may have been “substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence,” and will not overturn if the error was harmless given the strength of the other evidence (quoting DeMarce and Keys in the opinion). Thus, admission of an emotional background fact would be reversible only if (1) the district court abused its Rule 403 discretion in admitting highly inflammatory evidence whose probative value was minimal, and (2) the defendant can show the admission likely substantially influenced the verdict.

Practical takeaways for similar situations
- Courts balance probative value (explanation of inconsistency, witness state of mind, context) against the risk of sympathy/charisma/inflammatory effect. Statements that directly explain a witness’s inconsistent statements or mental state are strong on probative value.
- If the defense elicits or opens the subject on cross, exclusion is less likely.
- A curative instruction is available but not automatically required; the court’s discretion is large and appellate reversal requires a showing of substantial prejudice given the whole record.

In this case the panel found no unfair prejudice, noted the defense had elicited the context and that another witness mentioned the same fact without objection, and concluded the district court did not abuse its broad evidentiary discretion.

### Can defense impeach with a police report the witness didn’t author? What foundation is required under Rules 613 and 901?
Question:
The judge allowed questions about C.F.’s prior inconsistent statements to an investigator but barred defense counsel from reading from the police report because C.F. didn’t write it, citing authentication concerns. Under Rule 613(b), can counsel confront the witness by quoting the report’s language if the witness acknowledges speaking to the officer? Must the officer (or report author) testify to authenticate the report under Rule 901, or can the defense use it as impeachment-only without offering it for the truth? Could the report come in as a recorded recollection (803(5)) or public/business record (803(6)/(8)) for impeachment purposes, and how is hearsay within hearsay handled when the goal is only to impeach?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes — under Rule 613(b) the defense may confront a testifying witness about prior inconsistent statements and may quote the language to the witness on cross‑examination (that is exactly what happened here: the court “allowed questions regarding what C.F. said to police”). United States v. Cutbank, No. 24‑1225, slip op. at II.D–E (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).
- But reading a police report written by someone else into evidence is “extrinsic evidence” of the statement and, as the Cutbank panel emphasized, must be authenticated under Rule 901 before it can be used. Cutbank quotes Cumbie: “Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated.” Slip op. at II.E (quoting United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022)). The district court did not abuse its discretion in requiring that foundation.

What the rules require (plain-English)
- Rule 613(b): A party may use extrinsic evidence to impeach by prior inconsistent statement only if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny the statement and the opposing party is given an opportunity to examine the witness about it. That permits asking the witness on cross whether she said X to an officer and confronting her with the wording.
- Rule 901: Any extrinsic document or report offered to prove that the witness made the statement must be properly authenticated — i.e., the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. Cutbank makes clear the point: authentication is required even for impeachment use of a report.

Practical foundation paths (consistent with Cutbank)
- To read aloud or otherwise admit a police report written by an officer, you must lay foundation under Rule 901. Typical ways to do that include:
  - call the officer/author to testify that the report accurately records what the witness said at the time (author testifies to authorship and accuracy); or
  - elicit from the witness on the stand that she in fact said the quoted words to the officer and that she recognizes the words — thereby showing the report’s contents are attributable to her (adoption/acknowledgment); or
  - other circumstantial proof that the report reliably records the witness’s statement (chain of custody, contemporaneous notes, etc.).
The district court in Cutbank barred reading from the report because it was not authored by C.F. and the required authentication/foundation had not been supplied. Slip op. at II.E.

Hearsay / exceptions / “impeachment only”
- Cutbank does not address and does not resolve whether a police report could be admitted under a hearsay exception (e.g., recorded recollection, business/public records) when offered for impeachment rather than for its truth. The opinion’s holding is narrower: extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated; the court sustained objections to reading the report for lack of foundation. Slip op. at II.E. Thus, under Cutbank you cannot avoid laying authentication foundation simply by asserting the document is used only for impeachment — you still must authenticate it before the jury may see/read it.
- Because Cutbank did not analyze hearsay‑within‑hearsay or exceptions, you should preserve and develop those arguments at trial (offer the officer’s testimony, lay foundation for an exception, or obtain a stipulation) if you want the report admitted.

Bottom line
- You may confront C.F. with her prior statements on cross (the court allowed that). If you wish to read a police report containing those statements into the record or introduce it as extrinsic impeachment, Cutbank requires authentication under Rule 901 (e.g., officer/author testimony or clear witness adoption). The panel found the district court acted within its discretion in refusing to let defense read the unauthenticated report. Slip op. at II.E.

### What qualifies as 'another felony offense' for § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) on these facts, and what evidentiary showing is needed?
Question:
The court adopted PSR facts and concluded the gun was used 'in connection with another felony,' but the opinion doesn’t identify which felony (e.g., domestic assault, kidnapping/false imprisonment, burglary/trespass at the garage, assault on an officer). In the Eighth Circuit, does 'in connection with' require that the firearm facilitate the other felony (nexus/facilitation), or is contemporaneous possession during the felony enough? What is the standard of proof at sentencing, and can uncharged conduct described in a PSR—supported by hearsay—alone establish the enhancement if the defendant lodges no factual objection? Would a legal objection (but no factual objection) preserve a challenge to applying the enhancement?
Answer:
Short answer

Under Cutbank the enhancement turns on whether the district court finds the firearm was “used or possessed … in connection with another … felony” — a finding the court may make from the PSR’s factual recitation even as to uncharged offenses. The opinion does not adopt a new gloss (e.g., a rigid “facilitation/nexus” test) or require a separate conviction; instead it affirms the enhancement where the district court adopted PSR facts (which described contemporaneous violent felony conduct — keeping the victim at gunpoint/false imprisonment/kidnaping, assault in the home, and injuring an officer while resisting) without factual objection. Because the court “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements,” the appellate court reviewed those findings only for clear error and affirmed. Cutbank therefore illustrates three practical points:

- What counts as “another felony”: any felony conduct that the district court finds the firearm was used in connection with — here the court accepted a range of violent felonies described in the PSR (domestic assault/false imprisonment/kidnaping; assault in the home; injuring a police officer while resisting arrest; and related trespass/barricade facts) as supplying the “another felony” for §2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The opinion expressly notes those offense descriptions when upholding the enhancement.

- Nexus/facilitation vs. contemporaneity: the opinion does not announce a formal rule distinguishing mere contemporaneous possession from possession that facilitated the offense. Instead the court accepted factual findings showing the gun was used/possessed during violent, coercive acts (pointed at the victim’s face, held her at gunpoint in a closet, threatened and struck her, fled with phone and gun, then resisted police). That factual picture is the kind of temporal/functional connection the court treated as “in connection with” the other felonies; Cutbank therefore shows the Eighth Circuit will accept possession shown to be part of or tied to violent felony conduct without requiring a separate conviction.

- Evidentiary showing and objections at sentencing: Cutbank makes clear two procedural lessons. First, the Guidelines commentary expressly allows the enhancement “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought” (USSG §2K2.1, comment. n.14(C)), and the district court may adopt PSR factual allegations (which often rest on hearsay) and rely on them at sentencing. Second, where the district court adopted the PSR “without objection,” the Eighth Circuit reviewed for clear error and affirmed: “Thus, the court did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.” That means uncharged conduct stated in a PSR — even if supported mainly by hearsay — can support the enhancement if the defendant lodges no factual objection and the court accepts the PSR.

Preservation and appellate review

- If a defendant fails to make factual objections to the PSR at sentencing, Cutbank shows the appellate court will treat the adopted PSR findings as established and review them only for clear error. The opinion rests its affirmance on the district court’s unchallenged adoption of the PSR.

- A legal challenge (arguing the guideline does not apply as a matter of law) may be preserved even if the defendant did not press or win a factual challenge, but Cutbank demonstrates that pressing a legal argument on appeal will not overcome the deferential review of unobjected-to factual findings. The panel reviewed the application of §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) in light of the PSR-adopted facts and rejected the defendant’s legal objections; the opinion does not say that a mere legal objection without a contemporaneous factual objection would automatically preserve a reversal of the factual predicate.

Practical takeaways for preservation (what a defense lawyer should do based on Cutbank)

- Make specific factual objections to any PSR assertions you dispute before or at sentencing, and, if necessary, request a hearing so the district court resolves the disputed facts on the record. Cutbank turned on the PSR having been adopted “without objection.”

- If you also dispute the legal application of §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), state that objection separately so the district court can rule on the law; appellate review treats legal questions differently from factual findings.

- If you expect the PSR’s allegations rest on hearsay, press for live evidence or an opportunity to contest the underlying sources; otherwise Cutbank shows those unobjected-to PSR allegations can be sufficient to support the enhancement.

Relevant quotes from the opinion (illustrative)

- Enhancement applicability: the court quotes the commentary that the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” (USSG §2K2.1, comment. n.14(C)).

- Adoption without objection: “At sentencing, the district court adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements … Thus, the court did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.”

- Factual bases relied on: the opinion describes the offense conduct later summarized at sentencing — “domestic abuse, kidnaping, assault in the victim’s home in the presence of her children, and injuring a police officer while resisting arrest” — as the basis for concluding the enhancement was properly applied.

Conclusion

Cutbank does not announce a new narrow test; it illustrates that in the Eighth Circuit (1) the enhancement can be based on uncharged felony conduct described in the PSR, (2) the court will accept PSR findings (even hearsay-based) if adopted without factual objection, and (3) appellate review of such adopted facts is deferential (clear‑error). To preserve a realistic chance of overturning an enhancement you must lodge timely, specific factual objections at sentencing (and request a hearing) in addition to any legal objections.

### Is there impermissible double counting when both ACCA status and § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) drive the offense level, with § 4B1.4 picking the higher?
Question:
Here, ACCA would set the offense level at 34 under § 4B1.4, but enhancements under § 2K2.1 (including (b)(6)(B)) yielded 35, so the court applied § 4B1.4(b)’s 'greater offense level applies' rule. Does the Guidelines framework expressly authorize using § 2K2.1 enhancements to displace the ACCA-specific guideline, or is there a double-counting concern when prior violent felonies both trigger ACCA and provide predicates for higher base levels/enhancements under § 2K2.1? Is there controlling Eighth Circuit precedent on whether it is proper to apply § 2K2.1 enhancements in ACCA cases when § 4B1.4 also applies?
Answer:
Short answer: No — at least in the Eighth Circuit after United States v. Cutbank, applying a § 2K2.1 enhancement that produces a higher total offense level than the Armed Career Criminal Guideline is not impermissible double counting. The Guidelines themselves resolve the apparent overlap by directing the court to use the greater offense level, and Cutbank affirms that result.

Why: the Guidespeak and Cutbank
- USSG § 4B1.4(b) expressly provides that when the ACCA-based career offender guideline and the underlying guideline both apply, “the greater offense level applies.” Cutbank cites that provision and relies on it to justify using the § 2K2.1-driven level (Opinion § III).
- The court accepted the PSR’s calculation that § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (the 4‑level enhancement for possession in connection with another felony) produced an offense level of 35, while the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced level 34, and therefore affirmed the district court’s choice to use the higher level under § 4B1.4(b). The court held that the district court “did not err” in applying the § 2K2.1 enhancement and in imposing a within‑Guidelines sentence (Opinion § III).

What this means doctrinally
- The potential “double counting” concern (that the same prior violent felonies both trigger ACCA and function as predicates or support enhancements under § 2K2.1) is addressed by the Guidelines’ text: § 4B1.4(b) tells the sentencing court to adopt the higher Guidelines offense level rather than attempt to subtract or disallow the underlying guideline enhancements.
- Cutbank shows that, where the PSR facts support the § 2K2.1 enhancement and the district court correctly calculates the Guidelines range and considers § 3553(a), the Eighth Circuit will affirm the sentence. The court also notes that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (citing the commentary) — a point the court used to uphold the factual finding supporting the enhancement (Opinion § III).

Practical points for a defense objection
- To prevail you must show a procedural error in the Guidelines calculation (not just gripe about double counting in the abstract). Cutbank reaffirms the usual sequence: correct Guidelines calculation first, then § 3553(a) consideration (citing Gall and Rosales‑Mireles). Here the defendant’s challenge was rejected because the PSR facts were adopted and the district court legitimately selected the higher level under § 4B1.4(b).
- If you want to press a double‑counting claim in the Eighth Circuit going forward, you should (a) preserve a timely objection at sentencing, (b) focus on procedural error in calculating the Guidelines (showing the enhancement is impermissibly duplicative of the ACCA predicate rather than merely higher), and (c) be prepared to contest the factual basis for the § 2K2.1 enhancement. Cutbank indicates that absent such a showing, the court will defer to the Guidelines’ “greater offense level” rule.

Bottom line: Cutbank is controlling Eighth Circuit authority that applying § 2K2.1 enhancements which produce a higher offense level than § 4B1.4 is permissible under the Guidelines; the court will affirm where the PSR facts support the enhancement and the district court properly calculated the range and considered § 3553(a).

### What are the preservation and standard-of-review consequences when the defendant adopts the PSR and cites little authority on appeal?
Question:
The panel notes that Cutbank 'provid[ed] no cases and almost no analysis' and that he adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection. Should unpreserved evidentiary objections or sentencing fact disputes be reviewed only for plain error? Can the appellate court affirm for lack of prejudice without first expressly applying plain-error review? By adopting the PSR facts, did Cutbank waive only factual disputes or also his ability to raise purely legal challenges to the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based on those facts?
Answer:
Short answer
- Adopting the PSR and failing to press objections at sentencing largely surrenders factual disputes; the court treated the PSR findings as adopted and said the district court “did not err, much less clearly err” in relying on them. (Op. § III: “At sentencing, the district court adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements … Thus, the court did not err, much less clearly err ….”)
- Unpreserved evidentiary objections or fact disputes are forfeited and reviewed deferentially; the opinion disposes of several such claims either by applying the ordinary abuse-of-discretion standard for preserved rulings or by finding any error harmless. (Op. II.A–E; II.E: “this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless … we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed …”.)
- The panel did not mechanically invoke the “plain‑error” label every time; it affirmed on harmlessness or lack of prejudice where the record supported that disposition. So an appellate court can affirm for lack of prejudice without an express incantation of the plain‑error test, provided the record shows no substantial prejudice.
- Adopting the PSR mostly waives disputes over sentencing facts; it does not forever bar purely legal challenges to the Guidelines or to the legal application of an enhancement, but it makes reversal unlikely because the factual basis will be treated as unchallenged and appellate review of discretionary or factual determinations is deferential. (Op. III: the court considered Cutbank’s § 3553(a) / reasonableness argument on the legal side and affirmed.)

Explanation and doctrinal footing (grounded in the opinion)
1) Preservation and review of evidentiary rulings
- When the district court’s evidentiary rulings are preserved, the court reviews for abuse of discretion (see Op. II.B: “We review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.”).  
- For rulings not pressed on appeal or not preserved, the panel treated the points as forfeited and disposed of them by showing lack of prejudice or harmlessness (Op. II.E: “this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless … we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed …”). In some places the opinion simply says a claim “whether preserved or not, … is without merit” (Op. II.E regarding the cross‑examination/authentication issue).

2) Sentencing facts when the PSR is adopted
- The panel emphasized that Cutbank “adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutback’s prior felony convictions.” That adoption meant the district court’s factual finding that supported the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement stood: “Thus, the court did not err, much less clearly err, in finding that the facts in the PSR establish that the gun was used in connection with another felony.” (Op. III.) The opinion cites Dunn for the applicable standard on review of such sentencing findings, indicating deferential review of adopted PSR facts.

3) Affirmance without a formal plain‑error recital
- The panel did not rigidly announce a plain‑error framework in every instance. For evidentiary claims it disposed of them on harmlessness/no prejudice grounds. For sentencing, because the PSR was adopted and no objection was lodged, the court concluded no (clear) error and then addressed the legal § 3553(a) argument. Thus, as this opinion shows, an appellate court can (and did) affirm by finding lack of prejudice or no clear error without first reciting a formal plain‑error rubric, so long as the record supports the conclusion that any error was not outcome‑determinative.

4) Waiver of factual disputes vs. ability to raise legal arguments
- By adopting the PSR without objection, Cutbank effectively waived contesting the PSR’s factual statements on appeal (the opinion treats those facts as established and not clearly erroneous).  
- That waiver of facts does not automatically extinguish purely legal challenges (e.g., whether the Guidelines provision should apply as a matter of law or whether the sentence was substantively unreasonable). The panel did consider Cutbank’s legal argument under § 3553(a) and substantive‑reasonableness principles and rejected it on the merits, noting the guideline range was calculated and the sentence was within range and thus presumed reasonable. (Op. III: “we presume that the district court considered and rejected [the § 3553(a) arguments] … [a within‑range sentence] we presume is reasonable …”.)

Practical takeaway for litigants
- If you accept the PSR or fail to object at sentencing, you should expect appellate courts to treat the PSR facts as binding unless there is clear error. That makes challenging enhancements that depend on those facts difficult.  
- Preserve evidentiary and factual objections at trial and at sentencing if you want full appellate review; if you don’t, an appeal will be met with deferential review and likely disposed of on harmless‑error or lack‑of‑prejudice grounds.  
- You can still press legal arguments about the correct application of a Guideline or statutory sentencing principle even if you adopted the PSR facts, but the court will give the district court’s factual findings weight and will address legal arguments deferentially where the sentence is within the Guidelines.

References to the opinion
- Adoption of PSR facts and review: Op. § III (“At sentencing, the district court adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements … Thus, the court did not err, much less clearly err ….”).  
- Harmless‑error treatment of evidentiary rulings: Op. II.E (“this court will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. … we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed …”).  
- Preservation/abuse‑of‑discretion standard for instructions and evidentiary rulings: Op. II.B (citing Bennett).  
- Consideration of § 3553(a) legal arguments and presumption of reasonableness for within‑range sentences: Op. III (“Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.”; “we presume [a within‑range sentence] is reasonable.”).

Answer in one sentence: The Cutbank opinion shows that adopting the PSR waives factual challenges (the court treated the PSR facts as not clearly erroneous), unpreserved evidentiary or sentencing‑fact claims are dealt with deferentially (often resolved on harmlessness/no‑prejudice) and an appellate court may affirm on that basis without an express plain‑error incantation, while pure legal challenges to the application of a guideline remain open but face deferential treatment when the underlying facts have been adopted.

### How does harmless-error and cumulative-error analysis interact with potential Confrontation Clause violations?
Question:
The court concludes there was no hearsay admitted and D.F. testified, then says any evidentiary errors were harmless in light of strong evidence and rejects cumulative-error claims. If there had been a Confrontation Clause error, would the Eighth Circuit apply the Chapman 'harmless beyond a reasonable doubt' standard rather than the usual evidentiary harmlessness test? When multiple alleged errors include both constitutional and non-constitutional claims, how does the cumulative-error doctrine assess prejudice—does it aggregate under a single constitutional harmlessness standard, or treat each type of error under its respective standard?
Answer:
Short answer
- United States v. Cutbank did not apply Chapman because the panel found no Confrontation Clause violation — it concluded D.F. testified and the Clause was satisfied (citing Charboneau), and so the court evaluated the evidentiary rulings under the circuit’s ordinary harmless‑error test for nonconstitutional errors (DeMarce) and rejected a cumulative‑error claim unless there was “substantial prejudice” (Keys).
- The opinion therefore does not decide the hypothetical you pose. Generally, a Confrontation Clause error is a constitutional error and is reviewed under the constitutional harmless‑error standard; mixed (constitutional + nonconstitutional) errors are typically analyzed under their respective standards and then assessed for cumulative prejudice. Cutbank itself, however, never reaches or articulates that framework because it found no constitutional error.

Why — what the opinion actually does
- Confrontation Clause: The court held the Clause was satisfied because the hearsay declarant (D.F.) “actually appear[ed] in court and testify[ed] in person,” citing United States v. Charboneau. So Cutbank does not treat the admission as a Confrontation problem. (Cutbank, Part II.B.)
- Nonconstitutional harmlessness: For the evidentiary rulings actually litigated, the panel relied on the ordinary evidentiary harmless‑error rule: “we will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless…we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” The opinion cites DeMarce for that proposition and applies it given the “substantial” government evidence. (Cutbank, Part II, final paragraph.)
- Cumulative error: The panel applied the circuit’s cumulative‑error formulation: it “will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant,” quoting Keys. Because the panel found any individual errors harmless and the evidence strong, it rejected a cumulative‑error reversal. (Cutbank, Part II, final paragraph.)

How that maps onto your hypothetical
- Cutbank does not say what it would do if there had been a Confrontation Clause violation. Because the opinion resolves the case on a finding that D.F. testified and on nonconstitutional harmlessness and cumulative‑prejudice rules, it does not articulate whether the panel would have applied Chapman’s “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt” formulation or how it would have folded a constitutional error into a mixed‑error cumulative analysis.
- Practical takeaway (what courts do outside Cutbank): when a Confrontation Clause violation is established, courts treat it as a constitutional error and apply the constitutional harmlessness standard; nonconstitutional evidentiary errors are judged under the ordinary harmlessness test. If errors are mixed, courts (including the Eighth Circuit in other precedents) evaluate each error under its proper standard and then consider whether the aggregate effect was prejudicial — if a constitutional error cannot be found harmless under the constitutional standard, that generally is dispositive. Cutbank, though, does not reach or adopt that precise sorting because it found no Confrontation problem and thus confined its analysis to DeMarce/Keys.

Bottom line
Cutbank itself applied the ordinary evidentiary harmless‑error rule (DeMarce) and the circuit’s cumulative‑prejudice standard (Keys) because it found no Confrontation Clause violation (Charboneau). It does not decide whether Chapman would govern a Confrontation Clause error or how the court would reconcile mixed errors; those questions would require a case in which the panel found a constitutional violation and expressly addressed harmlessness and aggregation.
