Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# From Gall to Goodhouse: Stacking Enhancements and the Presumption of Reasonableness in ACCA Sentencing

## Why this case matters
United States v. Cutbank is a compact, instructive example of how multiple guideline enhancements can be layered to produce a very long advisory range in an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) case — and how difficult it is for a defendant to dislodge that result on appeal when a district court adopts an unobjected-to Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and imposes a within‑range sentence. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion affirms a 292‑month term at the bottom of the guideline range, grounded primarily on: (1) application of USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (a four‑level increase for possession “in connection with another felony offense”), (2) other discrete enhancements (NFA‑type weapon base level, obliterated serial number, reckless endangerment), and (3) the ACCA finding and the Guidelines rule that the greater offense level applies under § 4B1.4(b). Cutbank crystallizes two themes that every practitioner and sentencing judge should heed: the heavy consequence of failing to preserve objections to PSR facts and the appellate deference that attends within‑range ACCA sentences.

## The court’s reasoning in brief
The Eighth Circuit affirmed on both trial and sentencing issues, but the sentencing discussion is the lodestar for future litigation. The key points the panel relied on were straightforward.

First, the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection. That adoption supplied the factual basis to find the firearm was “used in connection with another felony,” triggering the four‑level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. The court cited the guideline comment that the enhancement applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (USSG §2K2.1, comment n.14(C)) and reviewed the standard of review for contested sentencing facts (citing Dunn for the clear‑error standard).

Second, the panel recognized that, because ACCA applied, the total offense level produced by the ACCA guideline (USSG §4B1.4) would have been 34 but the stacking of enhancements pushed the total to 35; the Guidelines themselves direct that “the greater offense level applies” under §4B1.4(b).

Third, the court treated the within‑range 292‑month sentence as presumptively reasonable on appeal (citing Goodhouse), noting the district court had considered the §3553(a) factors and the defendant’s arguments. That presumption, together with the deference afforded to a district court’s factual findings at sentencing, proved fatal to Cutbank’s challenge that the enhancement and sentence were substantively unreasonable under §3553(a).

## The legal mechanics: how the enhancements stack
Cutbank illustrates the mechanics by which a PSR’s unchallenged facts plus guideline text can transform a firearms case. Several discrete enhancements were applied to reach the offense level used to calculate the advisory range: an elevated base offense level because the weapon qualified as an NFA‑style firearm (sawed‑off rifle), a three‑level adjustment for an obliterated serial number, a two‑level reckless endangerment increase tied to flight from officers, and the contested four‑level §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for possession in connection with another felony.

Two features make this stacking potent. One is the guideline comment explicitly authorizing the §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement without requiring a formal charge or conviction for the “other felony,” so courts look to underlying facts rather than to parallel prosecutions. The other is §4B1.4(b)’s instruction that, where the career offender/ACCA guideline produces a lesser total offense level than the adjusted §2K2.1 total, the greater level is used — which runs the enhancements into the ACCA framework and can ratchet the range even higher. The net result: once a district court accepts the PSR’s account, the pathway to a significantly increased advisory range is direct.

## The presumption of reasonableness and the limits of §3553(a) arguments
Cutbank also shows the uphill climb a defendant faces when asking a district court for a below‑guidelines sentence on §3553(a) parsimony grounds, and then trying to overturn a within‑range sentence on appeal. The Eighth Circuit follows the now‑familiar trilogy of precedents: district courts must correctly calculate the Guidelines (Gall), appellate review first ensures no significant procedural error (Rosales‑Mireles), and within‑range sentences are presumptively reasonable on appeal (Goodhouse). The panel emphasized that a district court need not give a “mechanical recitation” of all §3553(a) factors so long as the record shows the court considered the factors and the defendant’s arguments — a record the court found present here.

That presumption of reasonableness is a powerful backstop. Even if a defense lawyer can persuade a sentencing court that an enhancement overstates culpability or double counts conduct, failure to make specific objections and create a developed record reduces the chance of reversal: appellate review defers to factual findings and to the district court’s sentencing exercise, particularly where the sentence is within the calculated range.

## Double‑counting concerns — recurring but unresolved
Although the Eighth Circuit did not expressly decide a “double counting” objection here, the case highlights why such objections are common in firearms sentencing. Defendants frequently argue that the same core conduct — brandishing or using a gun during a single episode of wrongdoing — should not be counted both to qualify them for ACCA and again to support a §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement, or be used simultaneously to increase offense level and criminal history consequences. Cutbank’s brief invocation of §3553(a)’s parsimony principle gestures at that tension, but the panel’s decision illustrates the practical challenges of converting doctrinal concerns into reversible error.

Two doctrinal points bear emphasis. First, §2K2.1 comment n.14(C) contemplates enhancement based on uncharged or unconvicted conduct, so defendants cannot rely on the absence of a separate prosecution to defeat the enhancement. Second, when the Guidelines themselves create a mechanism to choose the greater offense level (§4B1.4(b)), the Guidelines’ internal structure can defeat arguments that the enhancements improperly duplicate ACCA’s effect. Those rules do not eliminate the possibility that a district court could (on a developed record) find the enhancements substantively unjustified under §3553(a), but Cutbank shows appellate courts will defer where the Guidelines were properly calculated and the district court gave meaningful consideration to the statutory sentencing factors.

## Practical takeaways for defense counsel
Cutbank furnishes several practical lessons.

First, preservation matters. When a PSR contains facts that, if adopted, will trigger enhancements, timely, specific objections and a hearing are essential. The Eighth Circuit repeatedly treated the district court’s unobjected PSR adoption as dispositive. A defendant should secure findings on the record — contest provenance, challenge relevance and authentication, and demand that the government prove the factual predicates for any enhancement by the appropriate standard (typically preponderance of the evidence).

Second, challenge the factual predicate for a §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement head on. The government must establish that possession or use of the firearm was “in connection with” another felony — i.e., that the firearm facilitated, furthered, or was closely related to another criminal objective. That is a factual inquiry amenable to adversarial testing at sentencing; don’t leave it to the PSR.

Third, develop the §3553(a) record. If you contend the stacking of enhancements produces a punishment greater than necessary, make a detailed §3553(a) argument and elicit the sentencing court’s reasoning on the record. Appellate courts presume the district court considered the factors, but a bald assertion will not substitute for a reasoned, specific plea for lenity supported by the record.

Fourth, anticipate and brief double‑counting and proportionality claims in the district court. Where the same conduct is doing heavy lifting for multiple guideline calculations, show how the Guidelines’ structure and the sentencing factors counsel against compounding punishment.

## Conclusion
United States v. Cutbank is a reminder that in ACCA and serious firearms cases, small procedural lapses at sentencing can have large substantive consequences. The combination of an unobjected‑to PSR, guideline comment language that validates enhancements absent separate charges, and the Guidelines’ own “greater offense level” rule can produce an advisory range that is hard to contest on appeal. For defense counsel, the path forward is clear: litigate the PSR and the enhancement predicates at sentencing, develop a robust §3553(a) record, and press factual and legal objections while there is still time. On the appellate side, Cutbank confirms the circuit’s continued deference to within‑range ACCA sentences where the Guidelines were correctly calculated and the district court gave meaningful consideration to the statutory factors.
