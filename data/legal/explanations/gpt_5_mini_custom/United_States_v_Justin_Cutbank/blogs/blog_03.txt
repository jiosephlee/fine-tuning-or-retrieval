Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Hearsay, Confrontation, and Cross-Examination Pitfalls: The Eighth Circuit’s Practical Evidentiary Playbook

## Why this case matters

United States v. Cutbank offers a compact clinic in routine — but often decisive — evidentiary maneuvers: when prior sightings of a weapon are intrinsic rather than impermissible character evidence; when and whether a trial court must give a limiting instruction about out‑of‑court statements; how the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant later testifies; and what foundation is required to use police reports as extrinsic impeachment. The opinion is not revolutionary, but it matters because it shows how deferential appellate review (abuse of discretion and harmless‑error standards) interacts with fairly common trial choices. For trial lawyers, the decision is a useful set of practice notes: if you don’t preserve issues carefully and can’t show prejudice, the district court’s discretionary calls will survive.

## The court’s reasoning, in brief

The Eighth Circuit affirmed Cutbank’s conviction and sentence after rejecting several evidentiary objections that the defendant raised on appeal. First, the court held that testimony by the victim and a housemate that they had previously seen the sawed‑off Marlin .22 with Cutbank was intrinsic evidence relevant to the charged possession, not forbidden propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The panel relied on its prior decision in Battle and the familiar proposition that prior possession of the same weapon can help establish ownership or control.

Second, Cutbank sought a pretrial limiting instruction forbidding the jury from treating D.F.’s out‑of‑court statements to police as substantive proof until she testified. The district court declined to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction, admitting officer testimony about events to explain the officers’ conduct and reserving any instruction to the close of trial. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit applied Bennett to uphold that approach: a trial court need not give a limiting instruction at the moment evidence is admitted so long as an appropriate instruction is given (or the issue is otherwise cured), and here the record showed no hearsay abuse and, in any event, D.F. testified at trial. The panel relied on Charboneau for the straightforward Confrontation Clause point: if the declarant appears and testifies, the Confrontation Clause is satisfied.

Third, the court affirmed the district court’s refusal to let the defense read portions of a police report into the record for impeachment without authentication. The panel cited Cumbie: extrinsic evidence used to impeach by prior inconsistent statement must still be authenticated. Allowing counsel to read unattributed report passages into evidence without foundation would be improper.

Fourth, the court rejected an argument that the elicited testimony that D.F.’s sister had been killed by a gun was unfairly prejudicial under Rule 403. That testimony was responsive to cross‑examination about inconsistent statements and therefore admissible; the defendant pointed to no appreciable prejudice.

Finally, the panel disposed of any cumulative‑error claim under the standard that a conviction will not be overturned unless the errors substantially swayed the jury, noting the government’s substantial physical and testimonial evidence.

## Analytical perspective

Cutbank reinforces familiar lines in the Eighth Circuit, and it does so predictably: where the contested evidence is functionally probative of an element of the crime (here, control/possession of a particular firearm), courts will label it intrinsic and permit it; where hearsay statements are explained by a later in‑court declarant, Confrontation worries evaporate; and where defense counsel wants to use a third‑party police report to impeach, authentication remains an absolute gating requirement.

Two practical subtleties are worth emphasizing. First, the panel’s treatment of the limiting‑instruction request is permissive but not boundless. Bennett permits a court to defer the instruction to the end of trial, but that discretion turns on the character of the challenged testimony and the likelihood of juror confusion or prejudice. In a close case in which officers’ testimony about a complainant’s out‑of‑court statements could be the centerpiece of the government’s case before the declarant ever appears, a contemporaneous limiting instruction might be sensible and defensible. Defense lawyers who want that protection should ask for it expressly and preserve the record.

Second, the court’s approach to police reports for impeachment highlights a recurring trap for the defense. There are reliable ways to place a prior inconsistent statement before the jury without reading an unsigned police report aloud: obtain the witness’s personal adoption of the report, use prior recorded testimony, make the witness confirm the written statement or read the relevant language into the record through the witness, or authenticate the report under an applicable exception (business records, recorded recollection, etc.). Failure to lay that foundation invites a sustained objection and an adverse, appellate‑proof ruling like the one here.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Doctrinally, Cutbank is an incremental but useful confirmation of existing principles rather than a course change. It reiterates the intrinsic‑evidence exception to Rule 404(b) where prior possession helps establish current possession or control, a line with which the lower courts are well familiar. That stability matters: it gives prosecutors confidence to put before juries testimony that ties a particular weapon to a defendant across time, and it gives defense counsel a predictable standard on which to strategize.

On Confrontation Clause doctrine, the opinion simply applies settled precedent: an in‑court witness who testifies cures potential Crawford problems with prior hearsay, provided the testimony is genuine and responsive to cross‑examination. But practitioners should not take the ruling as a license to blithely allow officers to narrate unsworn victim statements whenever convenient. The timing and context matter: where officers’ retelling risks becoming substantive proof in the absence of the declarant, defense counsel should press the point early.

The authentication requirement for police reports used in impeachment counsel caution for the defense and clarity for prosecutors. Defense teams should plan foundational steps well before cross‑examination; prosecutors should be prepared to authenticate or, absent that, to object and keep the competing narrative before the jury.

Finally, the court’s aside about appellees “unnecessarily expand[ing] the issues on appeal” functions as a practical reminder: appellate courts expect litigants (on both sides) to frame clear, discrete issues and to brief them with supporting authority. Overbroad or amorphous appellate briefs risk forfeiture on procedural grounds and irritate courts asked to clean up sprawling litigation.

## Practical takeaways for practitioners

For defense counsel: preserve objections with specificity; if you think officer testimony about a complainant’s out‑of‑court statements will be prejudicial, ask for a contemporaneous limiting instruction and note the request on the record; when impeaching a witness with a prior inconsistent statement, secure authentication or use the witness to place the statement before the jury rather than reading third‑party reports aloud; and where emotionally charged testimony is elicited in response to your cross, be prepared to show actual prejudice if you later seek relief under Rule 403.

For prosecutors: when relying on prior witness statements, consider calling the declarant to cure Crawford concerns; ensure you can authenticate records you intend to use for impeachment; and, when offering evidence of prior sightings of a weapon, be prepared to articulate why that testimony is intrinsic and necessary to show ownership or control.

## Conclusion

Cutbank is a pragmatic opinion that underscores the heavy tilt in appeals toward district‑court discretion on evidentiary questions and the difficulty of prevailing on harmless‑error review when the government’s case is strong. It supplies a tidy playbook for trial counsel: think ahead about foundations, preserve specific objections, and be strategic about when to demand limiting instructions. The opinion won’t reshape doctrine, but it should sharpen trial strategy on several recurring evidentiary battlegrounds.
