Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Red Lines and Red Flags: The Federal Circuit Tosses the ’134 Verdict over an Expert’s Muddled Mapping

## Why this decision matters
The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC is a sobering reminder of how fragile infringement verdicts can be when the plaintiff’s case relies principally on an expert’s mapping of claim elements onto a defendant’s system. The court reversed the jury verdict of literal infringement on the ’134 patent because Finesse’s expert repeatedly tied the claim term “interference generating signals” to a “modeled PIM path” that, crucially, is generated downstream of the accused receiver’s analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC). Because a receiver cannot have sampled signals that did not yet exist at the time of sampling, the expert’s mapping—at least as explicitly offered at trial—could not support the jury’s verdict. The Federal Circuit’s ruling underscores that element‑by‑element proof matters, and ambiguous or self‑contradictory expert testimony can doom a plaintiff’s case on appeal.

## The factual and evidentiary hinge
The asserted claim in the ’134 patent requires, at step 1[a], “over‑sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, pointed the jury to a Nokia technical diagram and repeatedly testified that the RF ADC (the receiver) samples the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” But the same diagram’s legend labels the red path as “DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path,” and Dr. Wells also testified (more explicitly elsewhere) that the modeled PIM path is generated in the GROOT FPGA—i.e., after sampling.

AT&T and Nokia seized on that inconsistency. If the “interference generating signals” are the modeled PIM path, they cannot have been sampled by the RF ADC because they are generated later in the processing chain. At trial Dr. Wells attempted to pivot, asserting that the red path really reflected two inputs—x1 and x2—and that those are the sampled signals underlying the claim. The district court, trying to sustain the verdict, accepted an inference that Dr. Wells must have meant x1 and x2 when he referred to the red path. The Federal Circuit rejected that rescue.

## The Federal Circuit’s reasoning
The panel focused on the actual trial record and the explicit mappings the expert offered. It found that Dr. Wells “consistently and repeatedly” mapped the claimed “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and that his later suggestion that “x1 and x2 are the two inputs here” never amounted to a clear, contemporaneous testimony that x1 and x2 correspond to the claimed elements. The court stressed the point that the modeled PIM path is created after the RF ADC, and so could not satisfy the claim’s sampling requirement. Because the plaintiff bore the burden of proving that the receiver sampled both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals, the court concluded the evidence did not permit a reasonable jury to find infringement.

The opinion cites Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope to support the proposition that a verdict cannot rest on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony. In that precedent, the court reversed a verdict where the expert’s conflicting descriptions of how an accused device worked made it impossible for a reasonable jury to find the required claim elements satisfied. The same logic carried the day here: where the expert offered conflicting and unclear mappings, the appellate court could not uphold the verdict as supported by substantial evidence.

## Doctrinal implications
Two doctrinal points stand out. First, this decision reaffirms that element‑by‑element proof is not a mere trial‑technique nicety but a substantive requirement for infringement. It is not enough to point to a diagram and assert that something “must” be what the claims require; plaintiffs must tie each claim limitation to evidence showing the accused product or process actually performs that limitation.

Second, the ruling clarifies the limits of a district court’s power to salvage a verdict by drawing inferences from ambiguous testimony. Under the Fifth Circuit’s (and thus the Federal Circuit’s) JMOL standard, appellate review asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, nonetheless points so strongly in favor of the moving party that no reasonable jury could have ruled otherwise. That standard does not allow fuzzy, after‑the‑fact reinterpretations of expert testimony to stand when the expert’s explicit testimony mapped a claim element to a part of the system that could not fulfill the limitation.

Finally, the decision underscores that expert testimony cannot be saved on appeal by arguments developed post‑trial. The Federal Circuit criticized Finesse for trying to clarify or reframe the mapping on appeal in ways the jury never heard.

## Practical lessons for practitioners
Plaintiffs: If your infringement case depends on expert testimony, make sure the expert offers a clear, consistent, and contemporaneous element‑by‑element mapping at trial. Avoid reliance on ambiguous demonstratives or diagram legends without a solid, corroborating chain of testimony showing how the diagram entries correspond to actual signals or components in the accused system. Anticipate cross‑examination aiming to expose any timing or causation gaps (for example, “were these signals present at the sampling stage?”).

Defendants: Cross‑examination that pinpoints temporal or causal impossibilities (e.g., “how could the ADC have sampled a signal that’s generated downstream?”) can create an appellate record favorable to JMOL/X‑overturn. When you can show that an expert’s mapping places a claimed element downstream of a required event (sampling, emission, etc.), you put the plaintiff to the hard burden of reconciling the timing.

Judges and experts: Demonstratives and block diagrams are indispensable, but they cannot substitute for precise testimony. Experts should be prepared to explain what each label means, where each signal originates, and—critically—when each signal exists relative to the claim steps. Judges should be wary of saving jury verdicts by inferring an unspoken clarification that the expert never actually provided on the stand.

## Bottom line
Finesse lost not because the underlying science was hopelessly complex, but because the proof—an expert’s repeated mapping of a claim term to a modeled signal that by the expert’s own testimony did not exist at the time of sampling—was logically inconsistent with the claim language. The Federal Circuit’s reversal is a crisp reminder: in patent trials, clarity wins. Ambiguous expert mappings, especially those that rely on diagram legends or post‑hoc reinterpretations, risk being stripped of probative force on appeal.
