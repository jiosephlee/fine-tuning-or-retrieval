Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# After the Static: Implications for PIM‑Cancellation Patents and Proving DSP Infringement

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a reminder that in patents whose claims turn on discrete digital‑signal‑processing steps, the proof must be element‑by‑element and technically precise. The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of two PIM‑cancellation patents and vacated a $166 million lump‑sum damages award. The reversal turned on two familiar but increasingly important themes in modern patent litigation: (1) for sampling‑related limitations, the accused device must actually have the claimed signals present at the claimed sampling point; and (2) for multiplication‑based claims, the patentee must show the accused product performs each required mathematical operation as claimed, not merely something that looks similar at a high level (e.g., modulus or conjugate operations). For practitioners, the case highlights how expert testimony and technical documents can make or break a jury verdict in DSP‑heavy suits.

## What the court held and why

The opinion addressed two patents with different technical hooks. The ’134 patent required, among other things, “over‑sampling … a passband of received signals” that “include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert relied on a Nokia block diagram and testimony that pointed to two signals on a red path in the diagram—what he variously called the “DL(TX) reference” and the “modeled PIM path,” and sometimes referred more abstractly to x1 and x2. The problem, as the Federal Circuit framed it, was that the modeled PIM path in the Nokia materials was generated downstream of the RF analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC). In other words, the thing the expert identified as the “interference generating signal” did not exist at the claimed sampling point; it was created later in the receiver chain inside the FPGA. The expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent and unclear about whether x1/x2 or the labeled DL/ modeled PIM paths were the mapped signals. Because Finesse bore the burden to prove the presence of both kinds of signals at the sampling point, and its expert’s testimony could not supply that element with substantial evidence, the court concluded no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the ’134 claims.

The ’775 patent claimed generating IMP cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 in seven specific third‑order products. The district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique,” and the Federal Circuit agreed that prosecution history did not amount to a clear and unambiguous surrender of two‑signal implementations. That said, the central problem at trial was evidentiary: Finesse’s expert pointed to a Nokia document that listed three distinct operations—one involving a signal multiplied by its modulus twice, others involving a conjugate—and then asserted those map onto the seven claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit found the Nokia material showed only three distinct multiplications and did not demonstrate that the seven claimed multiplications were actually performed (nor did the expert explain how to map the defendant’s modulus/conjugate operations onto the claimed products). Because the patentee failed to show the seven claimed multiplications were performed, the infringement verdict for the ’775 claims could not stand.

Because both infringement findings failed for lack of substantial evidence, vacating damages followed naturally.

## Analytical perspective

Two features of the opinion are worth emphasizing. First, this is an archetypal expert‑driven case. The jurors do not read FPGA source code or parse analog front‑end timing diagrams themselves; they rely on experts to translate block diagrams into “meets limitation X” statements. The Federal Circuit’s willingness to treat internally inconsistent or equivocal expert testimony as insufficient evidence is straightforward law, but it has renewed force in DSP contexts: when claims involve timing, sampling order, or signal identity, the expert must tie the asserted claim language to a concrete point in the signal chain with clarity.

Second, the court’s technical strictness about mathematical operations matters. Modulus operations and complex conjugation are not ipso facto equivalent to multiplying literal copies of time‑domain signals S1, S2, S3 in the precise combinations a claim recites. The Federal Circuit demanded a mapping from what the device actually computes to the claim’s algebraic products. In short, similarity of purpose (the device is trying to cancel PIM) does not substitute for identity of operations when the claims specify particular mathematical steps.

The appellate court was careful not to re‑write claim construction. It rejected the argument that amendment to recite “three signals” constituted a clear, disallowing surrender of two‑signal implementations. But that concession did not save the verdict because Finesse still could not produce evidence that the accused radios performed the seven listed multiplications.

## Doctrinal implications

For patent law doctrine, the decision reinforces several settled propositions while sharpening their application in high‑tech cases:

- Proof requirement and JMOL/substantial evidence: The decision is a textbook application of the standard that a jury verdict must rest on substantial, non‑contradicted evidence. In DSP cases, that means the patentee’s expert must provide a consistent, element‑by‑element narrative mapping claim language to device behavior.

- Claim construction and prosecution history: The Federal Circuit continued to adhere to the “clear and unambiguous” rule for prosecution disclaimer. Amending to recite “three signals” did not necessarily exclude implementations with repeated or copied signals unless the record shows an unmistakable surrender. But claim drafting that leaves ambiguous whether signals must be distinct invites dispute and makes infringement harder to prove.

- Mathematical claim elements: The court’s insistence on mapping actual operations—e.g., real multiplications of signal copies—onto claimed algebraic forms highlights that courts will look to the exact mathematics underlying alleged infringement. Extrinsic materials like block diagrams, modulus/computational descriptions, and source/FPGA code will be parsed with algebraic precision.

## Practical takeaways for litigants and drafters

For patentees and litigators: prepare to prove the math. When claims hinge on when a signal exists (before or after an ADC, pre‑ or post‑FPGA) or on performing precise algebraic operations, the record must show that identity and timing clearly. That often means obtaining and exposing low‑level artifacts: FPGA logic, firmware, timing diagrams tied to physical sampling points, signal traces, and deterministic mappings from implemented operations to the claimed mathematical products. Expert narratives should avoid equivocation; demonstratives should show the one‑to‑one correspondence between accused operations and claim elements. If an expert introduces multiple, inconsistent characterizations of the same signal path (as Dr. Wells did here), the jury verdict will be vulnerable to JMOL.

For defendants and vendors: internal block diagrams and marketing materials can be turned into decisive noninfringement evidence—if you can show that the claimed signal simply does not exist at the claimed sampling point or that the implemented math differs from the claimed math (e.g., modulus and conjugation rather than literal multiplicative combinations). Cross‑examination that exposes ambiguity in an opposing expert’s mapping is a proven tactic.

For drafters: be explicit about signal identity, sampling points, and the algebraic nature of claimed operations. If a claim requires “three signals” consider defining whether those must be distinct or may be copies, and whether modulus or conjugate operations count as the same “multiplication” as multiplying distinct time‑domain copies. Dependent claims that specify signal generation location (e.g., pre‑ADC versus post‑ADC), number of unique inputs, or exact mathematical forms will give patentees fallback positions and reduce the risk that an ambiguous claim construction dooms a case.

For both sides, the decision suggests an increased role for early technical discovery and, potentially, early dispositive motions. When the accused device’s implementation details can be shown (or excluded) with technical artifacts, summary judgment or JMOL may be more attractive and more successful.

## Conclusion

Finesse v. AT&T is not a doctrinal revolution, but it is a crisp lesson about the evidentiary realities of modern patent litigation where signal processing and algebra are the contested terrain. Plaintiffs must prove, with clear, noncontradictory expert testimony and documentary mapping, that the accused device has the claimed signals at the claimed point and performs the claimed mathematical operations. Defendants should watch their internal diagrams and implementation details—those same materials that help them innovate can, if unaddressed, undercut infringement theories. The long‑term signal to industry is straightforward: in DSP‑heavy patents, prove the math, or risk having the verdict swept away with the static.
