Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Substantial Evidence, Fifth‑Circuit Style: Undoing a $166 Million E.D. Tex. Verdict

## Why this case matters
Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is a reminder that large jury verdicts cannot survive on fuzzy expert testimony or appellate reconstructions. The Federal Circuit reversed the Eastern District of Texas’ denial of JMOL and vacated a $166 million damages award after concluding that, viewed under the Fifth Circuit’s JMOL framework and the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence standard, no reasonable jury could have found infringement of either asserted patent. The decision is important not because it rewrites patent law, but because it underscores how closely appellate courts will scrutinize the evidentiary glue — particularly expert mappings of accused systems to claim elements — that supports jury verdicts.

## What the court held and how it reasoned
The appeal involved two patents aimed at canceling passive intermodulation (PIM) products in radios. For the ’134 patent, the claim required sampling a passband that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s case depended entirely on its expert, Dr. Wells, who tethered the claimed terms to elements shown in Nokia technical documents. But the Nokia documentation placed the “modeled PIM path” downstream of the radio’s analog‑to‑digital converter (ADC), meaning it was generated after sampling and therefore could not be a sampled “interference generating signal.” The expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent: at times he described the red path in the diagram as two signals (x1 and x2), but he repeatedly and unambiguously mapped the claim terms to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. The Federal Circuit concluded that this contradictory testimony — and the absence of a clear, unequivocal mapping of x1/x2 to the claim elements — failed to provide substantial evidence of infringement.

For the ’775 patent, the claim required generating intermodulation cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, S3 in seven specified third‑order products. The district court construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily unique. On the evidence, however, the Nokia documents showed only three distinct multiplications (and involved moduli and complex conjugates rather than plain signal multiplications). Dr. Wells did not explain how those three operations corresponded to the seven claimed products, nor did he present a mapping that the jury could rely on. The Federal Circuit held that, absent an evidentiary showing that the seven claimed multiplications actually occur, the jury verdict lacked substantial evidentiary support.

In both instances the panel emphasized that post‑hoc clarifications on appeal cannot substitute for the missing or contradictory trial record, and it cited precedent (e.g., Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope) for the proposition that courts may find expert testimony self‑defeating when it contains irresolvable inconsistencies.

## The procedural architecture: Rule 50, regional standards, and substantial evidence
A technical but pivotal feature of this decision is the interplay between regional‑circuit JMOL standards and the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence review of infringement findings. The Federal Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit (here, the Fifth Circuit), while separately reviewing factual findings of infringement for substantial evidence. That procedural architecture produces two overlapping constraints on the patentee: first, the need to preserve discrete Rule 50(a) (and (b)) grounds at trial; and second, the need to produce a trial record that, even viewed most favorably to the verdict, contains enough coherent evidence to meet the substantial‑evidence bar on appeal.

Finesse’s loss illustrates both constraints. The court did not adopt a new theory of preservation; it reversed because the record — not the standard of review — was inadequate. But because Federal Circuit review must be tethered to the regional standard for the initial JMOL denial, district‑court practice (including preservation of issues at Rule 50(a)) remains crucial to shaping appellate review.

## Analytical perspective: why the Federal Circuit pushed back
Two themes animate the opinion. First, appellate courts will not accept creative appellate reconstructions when the trial record is internally contradictory or silent. Finesse tried to stitch together testimony and later appellate argument to salvage its case, but the panel would not let those patchwork inferences substitute for a clear, admissible mapping presented to the jury. That principle protects the Seventh Amendment jury role while also ensuring that verdicts rest on evidence, not post‑verdict spin.

Second, the opinion reflects skepticism toward experts who rely on diagrams, legends, or manufacturer documents without a contemporaneous, explicit explanation tying each claimed limitation to an identified physical or digital element. Diagrams that label a path “DL(TX) reference & modeled PIM path” do not do the work for an expert if the expert cannot say — without contradiction — which signals on that path satisfy the claim limitations, and whether those signals are even present at the relevant point in the device’s processing chain (e.g., before vs. after an ADC).

Finally, on prosecution history and claim scope, the court reiterated a familiar point: narrowing amendments do not automatically amount to clear and unmistakable disavowal. Finesse’s reduction to “three signals” during prosecution did not, in the panel’s view, incontrovertibly disclaim claims over implementations with fewer unique inputs where the claimed language was satisfied in another way. But lack of disclaimer did not save Finesse, because the evidentiary showing on the seven multiplications was still missing.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
This decision will be cited by defendants and appeals courts as authority for vigorous scrutiny of expert testimony and documentary mappings. Expect more JMOL motions that focus not just on legal standards but on the internal coherence of expert narratives. Plaintiffs will find it harder to rely on diagrams and legends without contemporaneous, precise testimony linking each claim limitation to specific signals or operations in the accused product.

The ruling also reinforces appellate reluctance to credit new theories or mappings introduced for the first time on appeal. That constraint tightens the spotlight on trial counsel to adduce a complete record. For claim‑construction and prosecution strategy, the case underlines that amendments can be risky, but that courts will still require clear disavowal to find prosecution disclaimer; plaintiffs should be cautious about over‑reliance on ambiguous claim amendments to fend off § 102/103 rejections while later arguing broad scope at trial.

## Practice pointers for patent trials in E.D. Tex. (and elsewhere)
Trial teams should take away several practical lessons. First, when your case turns on mapping technical diagrams to claimed elements, lock down those mappings in expert reports, deposition testimony, and demonstratives. Don’t rely on the implication of a legend or on a passing sentence of testimony; require your expert to state plainly which internal signal is S1, S2, or the “interference generating signal,” and to explain timing issues (e.g., whether a signal exists pre‑ or post‑ADC).

Second, watch for internal inconsistencies in expert testimony. If an expert offers two descriptions of the same path, reconcile them before trial or eliminate the inconsistency on cross‑examination and redirect. Jurors and judges alike are more comfortable with a straightforward, replicable mapping than with elaborate technical finesse that cannot be pinned down.

Third, preserve Rule 50(a) objections with specificity. If you think an essential element lacks evidence, make that argument expressly at the close of evidence; that both gives the district court a chance to rule and preserves the point for appeal.

Fourth, avoid leaning on post‑verdict affidavits, argument, or appeal‑only mappings. If you need to respond to a perceived evidentiary gap, do it at trial — supplementing the record later will likely fail.

## Conclusion
Finesse Wireless is not a turn in patent doctrine so much as a cautionary tale: a seven‑figure jury verdict will not survive if the evidentiary thread tying accused systems to claim limitations is ambiguous or internally inconsistent. The Federal Circuit’s opinion is a clear exhortation to patentees to present crisp, unequivocal expert testimony and to defendants to seize on any uncertainty at the JMOL stage. For trial counsel in E.D. Tex. and elsewhere, the safe course is obvious — make your mappings plain, your expert testimony coherent, and your Rule 50 preservation precise.
