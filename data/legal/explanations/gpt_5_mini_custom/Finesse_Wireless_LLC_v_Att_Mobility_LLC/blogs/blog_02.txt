Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Seven Means Seven: Why the ’775 Patent Failed on Proof, Not Construction

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a useful corrective for litigants in the signal‑processing and software patent world: when a claim spells out a specific mathematical recipe, courts will demand a jury‑ready, element‑by‑element showing that the accused product actually carries out that recipe. The Federal Circuit declined to import a narrow prosecution‑history estoppel to limit “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to three distinct, unique inputs, but nonetheless reversed the jury’s infringement verdict because Finesse’s proof simply did not show that the accused Nokia radios performed the seven enumerated third‑order multiplications recited in the ’775 patent. In short, claim scope survived challenge, but the patentee’s evidence did not.

## What the court did

The ’775 patent claims an interference‑cancellation method that, for third‑order terms, requires generating seven specific products from three signals S1, S2, S3: in the patent’s words, S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; and S2×S3×S3. At summary judgment the district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique.” On appeal the Federal Circuit agreed that the prosecution history did not clearly and unambiguously disclaim two‑signal embodiments when the patentee added “three signals” to distinguish Filipovic; the amendment did not amount to the sort of unequivocal surrender that would bar coverage of an accused product with only two unique inputs.

That was the doctrinal win for Finesse. It was, however, a Pyrrhic one. The jury verdict could not survive scrutiny because the only documentary evidence Finesse offered—a Nokia technical document relied on by its expert—listed only three distinct multiplication terms and used operations like complex conjugation and modulus, not literal third‑order multiplications of three separate S‑signals. The expert did not show how those three listed operations mapped to the seven claimed products, nor did he walk the jury through any repetition or algebraic equivalence that would yield the full set of seven terms. Finesse’s later attempts (on appeal) to point to a ten‑term polynomial in Nokia materials and retroactively assign which of those terms corresponded to the seven claimed products came too late; the mapping was never presented to the jury in a way that would permit a reasonable juror to find infringement. The Federal Circuit therefore held the evidence was not substantial and reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the ’775 claims.

## Analytical perspective: claim drafting survives, proof must follow

There are two lessons layered here. First, the Federal Circuit continues to police prosecution disclaimer with a high bar: an amendment or statement during prosecution must be “clear and unambiguous” to shrink claim scope. Adding “three signals” to fend off Filipovic did not, by itself, equate to a clear disavowal of embodiments that use fewer unique physical signals (for example, reusing a signal or employing a copy). That is consistent with recent Federal Circuit authority that refuses to read unambiguous disclaimers into ordinary narrowing amendments.

Second—and more practically decisive for litigants—the court reiterates that where claims recite precise mathematical operations, patentees must provide equally precise proof. The words of the claim mattered: the claim sets out seven enumerated third‑order products, and the patentee’s burden at trial was to show that the accused system performs those products (or equivalents) in the claimed manner. A vendor document that lists only three distinct terms, and which uses modulus and conjugate notation, is not a substitute for an expert’s element‑by‑element demonstration that those operations are the functional or algebraic equivalents of each claimed multiplication. The Federal Circuit refused to allow appellant‑side re‑labeling or post hoc mappings on appeal to bootstrap the inadequate trial record.

The court’s invocation of Johns Hopkins—where the patentee’s contradictory expert testimony doomed the infringement case—underscores the evidentiary point: inconsistent or conclusory expert testimony cannot carry the patentee’s burden. And the court’s reliance on Continental Circuits for the prosecution history point shows the two ideas are separable: a patentee can avoid an estoppel finding but still lose if it fails to prove infringement.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

Doctrinally, the decision draws a clean line between claim‑scope fights and sufficiency of proof. The Federal Circuit avoided creating a new rule of prosecution disclaimer while reaffirming that precise claim language imposes an exacting proof obligation. That distinction matters: patentees may breathe easier about routine claim amendments during prosecution, but they should not assume that relaxed claim construction will rescue a weak evidentiary record.

Practically, the ruling will be cited by defendants in cases involving algorithmic, mathematical, or signal‑processing claims. When a claim explicitly enumerates algebraic terms, defendants should focus on forcing patentees to prove the mapping from the accused product’s implementation to each recited term. Vendor or standards documents that use different notation (conjugates, moduli, filters) are fertile grounds for cross‑examination: those differences can be fatal unless the patentee’s expert supplies a clear algebraic equivalence and ties the operations to the accused product’s executed code or hardware flow.

For patentees and drafters, the case suggests two sensible precautions. First, consider drafting claims (or dependent claims) that capture alternative representations of the same mathematical operations—e.g., allow for conjugate or modulus‑based implementations, or include functional language about generating specific frequency components—if multiple mathematical formulations are foreseeable. Second, build the trial record: if the infringement theory relies on algebraic equivalence, have the expert show step‑by‑step how the accused system’s computations produce each claimed term, and introduce low‑level technical evidence (code excerpts, FPGA block diagrams, test output) that corroborates the mapping.

For defense counsel, the decision proves the value of a narrow, forensic attack: isolate a single claim element that is amenable to documentary refutation (here, “seven specific multiplications”), and force the patentee to produce a jury‑ready mapping. If the patentee’s proof is inconsistent, equivocal, or dependent on undocumented inferences, ask for JMOL and press the appellate record.

## Downstream effects

We should expect two immediate litigational effects. First, patentees will sharpen expert presentations in trials involving mathematical claims—more algebra on the board, more demonstrable linkage between source code/firmware and claimed operations, and, where possible, demonstrable test vectors or outputs. Second, defendants will more aggressively pursue JMOLs (and Rule 50 motions) when the patentee’s case rests on inferential leaps from high‑level vendor documents. The Federal Circuit’s decision incentivizes early, element‑specific attacks and makes it harder for juries to speculate their way to infringement on technical claims that require precise arithmetic operations.

Finally, the decision is a reminder that “seven means seven” in patent proof. Claim construction can be generous; proof cannot be. Where a patentee chooses to claim a particular numerical combination of operations, it must prove that combination was performed (or that the accused product practices the claimed mathematical equivalence) with a clarity that matches the claim’s precision.
