Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 1: # Case Background, Technology, and Procedural Posture

## Parties and accused products: AT&T and Nokia’s PIM‑C feature in Nokia radios
The dispute pits Finesse Wireless LLC, the patent holder, against AT&T Mobility LLC and intervening Nokia of America Corporation. Finesse sued AT&T for allegedly using radios that incorporate Nokia’s PIM cancellation feature (referred to in the opinion as “PIM‑C”), asserting that the accused equipment practices methods claimed in two patents owned by Finesse: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the ’134 patent) and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the ’775 patent). Nokia intervened in the litigation and defended the operation of its radios. At trial Finesse relied primarily on testimony from its technical expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, and on internal Nokia technical documents to explain how the accused radios operate and why they allegedly satisfy the claim limitations.

## Technical backdrop: IMPs and PIM, internal vs. external PIM, and why radios care
The patents and the disputed technology address intermodulation products (IMPs), which are new frequencies that can be created when two or more radio-frequency signals mix. In practical wireless systems, transmit signals can combine—often when they interact with imperfect or non-linear elements such as metal structures, corroded connectors, or other discontinuities—to produce IMPs that may fall into the same frequency band as desired receive signals, causing interference.

Passive intermodulation (PIM) is a subset of IMPs caused by passive (non-powered) defects or mechanical issues in the signal path—examples include loose connections, rusted contacts, or foreign objects near antennas. The opinion also distinguishes internal versus external PIM: internal PIM arises from mechanical problems inside the radio itself, while external PIM has its source outside the radio (for example, nearby metallic structures). PIM matters because it can create in‑band interference that degrades receiver performance; the accused PIM‑C feature purports to mitigate such in‑band PIM by generating cancellation signals computed from transmitter signals and subtracting estimated IMPs from the received signal.

## Asserted claims: the ’134 patent (isolating signals and canceling IMPs)
The ’134 patent asserts a multi-step method for reducing IMP interference. Representative claim 1 requires, among other steps, that a receiver (i) over‑sample a passband of received signals to create a bit stream that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” (ii) isolate the signals of interest and the interference generating signals using decimating filters, (iii) compute an estimate of the intermodulation products generated by the interference generating signals, (iv) cancel the in‑band intermodulation products using that estimate, and (v) perform closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment on the estimations—explicitly using sub‑sample phase shifts. A key factual and legal dispute about these claims centered on whether the accused radios’ receiver actually samples a passband that includes both the claimed “signals of interest” and the claimed “interference generating signals,” or whether portions of the signal path (the “modeled PIM path” in Nokia documents) are generated after sampling and thus cannot be samples of received signals as the claim requires.

## Asserted claims: the ’775 patent (generating cancellation signals via specified multiplications)
The ’775 patent claims a method for generating intermodulation product cancellation signals (ICSs) in a receiver that is co‑located with its transmitter. The representative claim requires the ICSs to be generated continuously and near real time using copies of transmitter signals, and it specifies that passive IMPs addressed by the claim are generated in passive transmitter and receiver components after the high‑power amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter. Critically, claim 1 requires that generating an n‑th order ICS be done by digitally multiplying three signals—labelled S1, S2, and S3—in a particular set of seven third‑order multiplications (for the third order example in the claim). Central disputes at trial were whether (1) the claim language “three signals S1, S2 and S3” requires three distinct (unique) input signals or simply three separately identifiable signals (which could include copies), and (2) whether the accused radios actually perform the seven specified multiplications (or the needed subset of operations) as claimed. Finesse’s infringement theory again relied on Nokia technical documents and expert testimony to show the accused radios perform the claimed multiplicative operations to generate cancellation signals.

## District court proceedings: jury verdict of validity and infringement; damages award
The case was tried in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. A jury found all asserted claims of both the ’134 and the ’775 patents to be valid and infringed by the accused Nokia radios used by AT&T. The jury awarded a lump‑sum damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the asserted patents. Those findings—validity, infringement, and the damages award—formed the basis for post‑trial motions from the defendants.

## Post‑trial motions: JMOLs and new‑trial motions denied
After the verdict, AT&T and Nokia moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement and for JMOL or a new trial on damages. The district court denied these motions. The defendants appealed the denial of JMOL on noninfringement for both patents and the denial of a new trial (and JMOL) on damages. The Federal Circuit’s opinion considers whether the jury’s infringement findings were supported by substantial evidence given the expert testimony and the Nokia documents, and whether any of the post‑trial denials were properly decided.

## Appeal posture and jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)
The appeal was brought to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The court exercised jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), which grants the Federal Circuit jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of district courts in patent cases. The Federal Circuit reviewed the denial of JMOL under the regional (Fifth Circuit) law applicable to JMOL denials and reviewed the sufficiency of the evidence for infringement under the substantial‑evidence standard, setting up the opinion’s legal analysis that follows from these factual and procedural foundations.
