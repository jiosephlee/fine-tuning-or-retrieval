Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the Federal Circuit reconcile Fifth Circuit JMOL review with its own "substantial evidence" standard for infringement findings?
Question:
The opinion says denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo under Fifth Circuit law and will be reversed only if the evidence points so overwhelmingly to one side that no reasonable jury could find otherwise. Yet it also says a jury's infringement finding is reviewed for substantial evidence. Practically, which lens controls the outcome here? Are these just two ways of stating the same inquiry on appeal, or is there a distinct two-step analysis (regional-circuit JMOL standard layered over Federal Circuit patent-specific substantial evidence review)?
Answer:
Short answer
They’re not two separate, sequential tests that pull in opposite directions. Finesse applies the Fifth Circuit’s JMOL procedural rule (because denials of JMOL are reviewed under the regional circuit’s law) but decides the ultimate question by asking whether the jury’s infringement finding is supported by substantial evidence. In practice the Federal Circuit treats the regional JMOL framework and the patent “substantial evidence” formulation as the same, outcome-determinative inquiry: could a reasonable jury — viewing the record in the light most favorable to the verdict — have found infringement? If not, reversal follows.

Why and how the opinion says this
- Procedural locus: the court begins by stating the governing procedural rule: “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit.” (Opinion, Discussion I.) It then quotes the Fifth Circuit formulation: denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo and are reversible “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” (Id.)
- Substantive framing: immediately after, the opinion states the patent-specific substantive standard it will apply to the jury’s infringement finding: “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence,” and defines substantial evidence as meaning “a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” (Id.)
- How the standards operate together: the opinion applies both formulations in the same breath and then performs the same inquiry — asking whether the record supports a reasonable jury verdict. The court’s reversals of JMOL denials are expressly premised on the conclusion that “the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence.” (See holdings on the ’134 and ’775 patents.)

Practical conclusion
The regional-circuit JMOL rule governs the procedural posture (we review the denial), but the Federal Circuit resolves the appeal by applying the substantial-evidence question the opinion articulates: could a reasonable jury, viewing the evidence favorably to the verdict, have found infringement? Because the two formulations ask essentially the same thing (whether reasonable jurors could have reached the verdict), the court treats them as a single, collapsed inquiry rather than a distinct two-step test. Finesse demonstrates that approach by invoking the Fifth Circuit’s JMOL language and then reversing because the infringement findings lack substantial evidence.
