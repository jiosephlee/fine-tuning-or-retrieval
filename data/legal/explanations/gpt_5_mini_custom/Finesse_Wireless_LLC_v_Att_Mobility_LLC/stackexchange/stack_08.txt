Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Was it proper for the district court to rely on inventor testimony to construe S1, S2, S3 as 'separately identifiable' rather than 'unique'?
Question:
The district court's construction leaned on intrinsic evidence and also credited inventor testimony that the three signals 'can be the same signal or different signals.' Under Phillips, extrinsic evidence has limited weight. Was it appropriate to use inventor testimony here, and did the Federal Circuit implicitly endorse that approach by not disturbing the construction? What would have been needed in the intrinsic record to require 'unique' instead?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — the district court acted within established claim‑construction practice in crediting inventor testimony to resolve an ambiguity once the intrinsic record did not clearly require “unique” signals. Under Phillips, extrinsic evidence (including inventor testimony) may be consulted to resolve genuine ambiguity in the intrinsic record, but it cannot be used to override a clear, unambiguous intrinsic definition or prosecution disclaimer. The Federal Circuit did not disturb the district court’s construction that S1, S2, S3 are “separately identifiable” (and not necessarily “unique”); instead it relied on that construction when explaining why there was no clear and unmistakable surrender of two‑signal embodiments and then reversed JMOL for lack of evidence on other infringement elements. To have required “unique,” the intrinsic record would have had to contain a clear and unmistakable definition or prosecution disclaimer to that effect.

Why that is the correct reading of the opinion
- The opinion reports that the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to be “separately identifiable,” but not “unique,” and that the court “credited inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence, that ‘S1, S2 and S3’ represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals.” J.A. 109–15, 115. That is the factual posture on which the Federal Circuit reviewed the later JMOL issues.
- Legal standard the opinion applies: the court explicitly analyzed whether Finesse had “clearly and unambiguously” disavowed claim scope over two‑signal products during prosecution, citing Continental Circuits and the well‑known requirement that a patentee’s amendment or statements must be clear and unambiguous to amount to disclaimer. The Federal Circuit concluded the amendment and prosecution history did not rise to that level, and at most required “three separately identifiable signals” without mandating a specific number of unique inputs. J.A. 110–11 (citing Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019)).
- How Phillips and extrinsic evidence fit in (as applied by the court): Phillips teaches that intrinsic evidence (claims, specification, prosecution history) controls and extrinsic evidence is of limited value and cannot be used to contradict the intrinsic record. Where the intrinsic record is not decisive, however, courts may consider extrinsic evidence to resolve ambiguity. That is exactly what happened here: the district court found the intrinsic evidence did not mandate “unique,” and so it relied in part on inventor testimony to clarify that S1–S3 can be the same or different signals. The Federal Circuit did not reverse that construction; it treated the absence of an intrinsic, clear disavowal as dispositive on surrender and proceeded to JMOL review on other grounds. See J.A. 109–15, 110–11.
- What the Federal Circuit did (and did not) do: the court did not overturn the district court’s construction nor say the district court erred in considering inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence; rather, it concluded the prosecution history/amendment did not amount to the “clear and unambiguous” surrender that would force a reading of “unique,” and then reversed JMOL because the proof at trial was insufficient to show the accused devices performed all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 110–11, 38–40.

What would have been necessary in the intrinsic record to require “unique”
To construe S1, S2, S3 as necessarily “unique,” the intrinsic record would have had to contain a clear, unambiguous statement or definition showing the patentee limited the claim to distinct signals. Concretely, that could have been:
- Specification language that defines S1, S2, S3 as distinct or different signals (e.g., repeatedly and consistently describing S1, S2, S3 as coming from distinct transmitters, separate sources, or always “different”); or
- Prosecution statements or claim amendments that unmistakably disclaim two‑signal embodiments — for example, an explicit response to a rejection that says the inventor limited the claims to three distinct/unique signals (not merely added “three signals” without clarifying that they must be distinct), or an amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals” that is accompanied by a clear argument that the three must be different and that two‑signal variants are excluded; or
- Intrinsic structural/functional limitations that make the claimed result achievable only if the three inputs are distinct (so the specification and claims inevitably imply uniqueness).

Absent that kind of “clear and unmistakable” intrinsic language or prosecution history, the court properly may (and here did) rely on extrinsic inventor testimony to resolve ambiguity and adopt “separately identifiable” rather than “unique.” The Federal Circuit’s treatment — accepting that the intrinsic record did not compel “unique” and evaluating the prosecution history against the high bar for disclaimer — is consistent with Phillips and Continental Circuits as applied in the opinion.
