Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### When does contradictory expert testimony require JMOL rather than letting a jury weigh credibility?
Question:
For the '134 patent, the panel held Dr. Wells’s testimony was too confusing/contradictory to support the verdict, citing Johns Hopkins v. Datascope. The district court inferred he actually meant x1/x2 were the claimed signals despite repeated references to the modeled PIM path. What's the line between permissible inference-building by the factfinder and an impermissible attempt to salvage a case with self-contradictory testimony? Is there a doctrinal test or set of factors (e.g., frequency of contradiction, lack of reconciliation, centrality to an element) that triggers reversal?
Answer:
Short answer
There is no single bright‑line rule, but Finesse (following Johns Hopkins) shows the court will take the case from the jury and grant JMOL only when the plaintiff’s proof of an element rests on an expert’s testimony that is internally contradictory, irreconcilable, and central to the claim so that no reasonable juror could accept it. Put differently: when the expert is the only source of proof for a claim element, the expert’s testimony must be coherent and explain how the accused device meets the element; repeated, unreconciled contradictions (especially ones that create a physical impossibility) will not suffice to support the verdict.

Why (the doctrine and standard)
- JMOL review (Fifth Circuit standard applied in Finesse) asks whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” (Finesse, citing Wi‑LAN). Finesse also reviews infringement findings for substantial evidence — i.e., whether a reasonable jury could have found for the patentee in light of the trial record.  
- Courts have held that an expert’s testimony can be the only evidence on a point, but that testimony must be sufficiently clear and non‑contradictory to support a verdict. Johns Hopkins v. Datascope is the principal precursor cited by Finesse: there the Federal Circuit held that an expert’s opinion that an accused device “contacts the vessel in three dimensions” could not support a literal‑infringement finding when the same expert elsewhere said the device contacts the vessel in only two places — the contradictions left no reasonable basis for the jury’s literal‑infringement finding. Finesse applies that principle to reverse JMOL where the patentee’s case depended on Dr. Wells’ shifting testimony. (Finesse, pp. quoted at J.A. 15010–19; opinion at pp. 10–12).

What the Finesse opinion treated as decisive (factors the court relied on)
The opinion identifies several interrelated features that made the expert’s testimony insufficient as a matter of law:

1. Centrality to a claim element: Dr. Wells’ mappings were the plaintiff’s proof that the accused radios sampled both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” — a required claim limitation. Because that mapping was essential, contradictions were dispositive. (Finesse at pp. 7–12).

2. Internal contradiction and confusion: Dr. Wells repeatedly and consistently mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, but the modeled PIM path is generated after sampling (i.e., it could not have been sampled). When confronted, Dr. Wells began saying there are “two signals” (x1 and x2) but never clearly or directly mapped x1/x2 to the claimed terms; his testimony therefore remained internally inconsistent and confused. The court characterized this as a “confusing change of course” and “nothing clear about” his testimony. (Finesse, citing J.A. 15010–19 and district court record).

3. Lack of reconciliation or detailed explanation: the expert did not give a clear, detailed reconciliation of the conflicting statements — no straightforward mapping, no explanation of how the modeled PIM path could be both generated post‑ADC and be the “interference generating signals,” nor an explicit statement that x1/x2 were meant in place of the modeled PIM path. The panel emphasized that piecing together snippets ninety pages apart is not an adequate substitute for coherent testimony. (Finesse at pp. 10–12).

4. Physical impossibility or contradiction with the device operation: the expert’s mapping, if taken at face value (modeled PIM path = interference generating signal), produced a logical impossibility (the receiver allegedly samples a signal generated only after sampling). Contradictions that create such impossibilities are especially fatal. (Finesse at pp. 21–22).

5. Exclusive reliance on the expert: the opinion stresses that the patentee “rest[ed] its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony.” When the party with the burden rests on such testimony, reversal may follow. (Finesse quoting Johns Hopkins).

Practical rule of thumb (how courts will apply these considerations)
- If the expert is the sole or principal evidentiary source for a claim element, make sure the expert: (a) expressly maps each claim limitation to specific structures/operations of the accused device, (b) gives a consistent account, and (c) if there is apparent tension with device operation or documentary evidence, provides a clear reconciliation.  
- If an opponent can show the expert repeatedly made contradictory identifications, failed to reconcile them, and those contradictions go to an essential claim element (or create a physical impossibility), JMOL is likely appropriate because no reasonable juror could find for the patentee on that element.  
- By contrast, if there is other non‑contradicted evidence supporting the mapping, or the expert’s testimony can reasonably be harmonized, then credibility is for the jury.

Applying it to Finesse (concise)
Finesse reversed because (1) infringement turned on whether the RF ADC sampled the claimed interference signals; (2) Dr. Wells’ testimony was the patentee’s proof of that mapping; (3) his testimony repeatedly identified the modeled PIM path as the interference signal even though that path is generated after sampling (a clear operational contradiction); and (4) he never actually or clearly said x1/x2 were the claimed signals nor reconciled the inconsistency — the patentee asked the courts to stitch together scattered transcript fragments instead of offering coherent testimony. Under those circumstances the panel concluded no reasonable juror could rely on the expert’s testimony to find infringement.

Bottom line
There is no fixed counting formula (e.g., “three contradictions = JMOL”), but courts will grant JMOL when an expert’s testimony — which is necessary to prove a claim element — is internally contradictory, unreconciled, and produces an outcome that no reasonable juror could accept (particularly where the contradiction conflicts with the device’s actual operation). Finesse and Johns Hopkins set the governing examples of that doctrine.
