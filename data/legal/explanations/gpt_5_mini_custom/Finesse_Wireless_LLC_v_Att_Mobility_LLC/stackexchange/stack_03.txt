Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Must the '134 patent's "interference generating signals" be present in the received passband pre-ADC, or can a modeled/derived internal signal qualify?
Question:
The court found no infringement because the accused radios' RF ADC could not have sampled the modeled PIM path—it was generated downstream after sampling. Does the claim language ("over-sampling ... a passband of received signals") categorically require that the 'interference generating signals' be actual received RF content pre-ADC? Could a digitally generated model of PIM ever count as an 'interference generating signal' under this claim, and if not, is that due to claim construction, written description, or just the evidence presented?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit treated the ’134 claim’s “over‑sampling … a passband of received signals” limitation as requiring that the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” be among the received signals actually sampled by the RF ADC. A PIM signal that is only produced later inside the radio (i.e., generated downstream of the ADC as a modeled/derived signal) will not satisfy that limitation unless the patentee proves the modeled signal corresponds to something that was in the passband at the time of sampling. In this case the court held the evidence did not do that and therefore the verdict of infringement was not supported.

Why (what the opinion says)
- The court framed limitation 1[a] of claim 1 expressly: “over‑sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals … wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals” (opinion reproducing claim language). The patentee (Finesse) bore the burden of proving the accused radios “sample a passband of signals that includes both the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’” (Op. § I.A.)
- The court relied on the undisputed operational fact in the record that the Nokia radios generate the “modeled PIM path” inside the GROOT FPGA after the RF ADC sampling. The court therefore concluded that “if the modeled PIM path is the claimed ‘interference generating signals,’ then limitation 1[a] cannot be met because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband of received signals that includes interference generating signals since those signals are not generated until after sampling occurs.” (Op. pp. 21–22 (citing trial transcript).)
- The court’s decision was grounded in insufficiency of the evidence: the plaintiff’s expert repeatedly and inconsistently mapped the claim terms to the Nokia diagram (at times pointing to a modeled PIM path that is generated post‑ADC), and the court found that conflicting, confusing testimony inadequate to carry Finesse’s burden. The court reversed JMOL of noninfringement because “the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence.” (Op. pp. 21–22.)

What that means for modeled/derived signals
- A modeled or computed signal will not automatically qualify as an “interference generating signal” under limitation 1[a] if that modeled signal is produced only after the ADC has sampled the passband. The claim text (and the court’s analysis) ties the limitation to “received signals” that are being over‑sampled.
- That does not foreclose every possibility that a digital/model‑derived quantity could be treated as the claimed “interference generating signal.” The opinion focuses on the evidentiary reality: if the model is simply a downstream construct with no showing that it corresponds to (or was derived from) actual components of the received passband as sampled, it cannot meet the claim. If, conversely, a patentee can show the modeled signal is nothing more than a faithful representation or extraction of components that were actually present in the received passband at the time of sampling, then the modeled signal could—factually—be shown to satisfy the limitation. The court’s holding turns on the record evidence and the expert’s mapping, not on a broader claim construction exclusion of every internally generated signal.

Is this claim construction, written‑description, or evidence?
- The opinion treated the issue as an evidentiary one: Finesse had the burden to prove the ADC sampled both kinds of signals, and the court found the expert testimony and documentary record did not substantiate that mapping. The court did not adopt a new or narrow claim construction that generically forbids any modeled signals; instead it applied the claim language (“passband of received signals”) to the facts and rejected the proof offered. (See Op. § I.A.)
- The opinion does not rest on a written‑description or enablement ground. Its reversal was for lack of substantial evidence supporting the jury’s finding that the accused devices met limitation 1[a].

Practical takeaway for litigation/claim drafting
- If your claim requires sampling a passband that “includes” interference‑generating signals, you must be able to show (with clear expert testimony and documentary support) that the accused ADC actually sampled those signals — or that any model used post‑ADC is demonstrably derived from sampled passband components so that it is functionally the same signal for purposes of the claim.
- Absent that showing, a post‑ADC modeled/derived signal will be vulnerable to a noninfringement JMOL or directed verdict challenge, as happened here.

References to the opinion
- Claim limitation and plaintiff’s burden described at Op. § I.A (reproducing claim 1[a]).  
- Court’s finding that the “modeled PIM path is generated in the GROOT FPGA” and therefore cannot have been sampled by the RF ADC, and its conclusion that if the modeled path is the claimed interference‑generating signal limitation 1[a] cannot be met (Op. pp. 21–22).  
- Court’s reversal based on insufficiency of the expert evidence and lack of substantial evidence for the jury verdict (Op. pp. 21–22).
