Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Case Background and Statutory Framework

## Parties and objectives: AFL’s FOIA request and purpose
America First Legal Foundation (AFL) is a private litigant that sought internal Department of Justice (DOJ) communications as part of an inquiry into judicial recusals. AFL submitted a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request to DOJ’s Civil Division asking for emails between an outside attorney and Civil Division personnel. The request targeted records AFL thought would shed light on whether certain judges had been recused and how those recusals were handled.

## Alleged DOJ policy: not searching employee emails without consent
AFL’s FOIA request was denied, in part, on the asserted ground that the Civil Division would not search employees’ personal or employee email accounts for responsive records without the employees’ consent. AFL characterized that practice as a policy of categorically refusing to search employee emails and contended that the policy produced arbitrary or capricious withholdings of FOIA-responsive material.

## Parallel FOIA litigation against DOJ
Concurrently with its administrative complaints, AFL sued DOJ under FOIA to compel production of the same records. DOJ produced, and continued to produce, records responsive to AFL’s FOIA request; the parties and courts have not litigated the sufficiency of that production as part of the appeal considered here. In short, the FOIA suit against DOJ proceeded in parallel, but the sufficiency of DOJ’s production was not the central issue on appeal to the D.C. Circuit.

## Request to OSC under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)
AFL also asked the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy. OSC is the independent agency created to promote “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service.” Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, OSC is statutorily directed to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings that are arbitrary or capricious. AFL invoked § 1216(a)(3) to compel OSC to investigate the Civil Division’s email-search practices.

## OSC’s initial denial and reliance on FOIA’s enforcement-trigger provision
OSC declined AFL’s request to investigate. Its stated reason was that FOIA contains a separate enforcement-trigger provision—5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—that, in OSC’s view, circumscribed OSC’s duty to act. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides that OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted” only when three conditions are met in a FOIA enforcement action: (1) a court orders production of records improperly withheld, (2) the court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) the court specifically questions whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.” OSC took the position that these FOIA-specific predicates must be satisfied before OSC must initiate disciplinary proceedings or otherwise act under FOIA-related authorities.

## OSC authority under the Civil Service Reform Act: §§ 1214, 1215, 1216
To understand the dispute over OSC’s duties, it is essential to see OSC’s role under the Civil Service Reform Act. Section 1214 provides OSC the procedures for investigating “prohibited personnel practices” and for seeking corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB); § 1214 also contains procedural protections, including provisions for notifying and updating a complaining party on the status of an investigation. Section 1215 authorizes OSC to seek disciplinary action where appropriate. Section 1216 is the statutory bridge to FOIA enforcement: it authorizes OSC to investigate and seek corrective or disciplinary action for other kinds of misconduct, expressly including “arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” See 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) and § 1216(c) (authorizing OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved”). The tension in this case arises from whether § 1216’s investigatory mandate operates independently of, or is qualified by, FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) referral provision.

## FOIA’s separate OSC referral provision: § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)
FOIA itself contains the narrower provision addressing OSC involvement in FOIA litigation. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides a tripartite trigger: when (1) a court orders production of records improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) the court awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) the court specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously regarding the withholding, then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the employee primarily responsible. That provision is aimed at channeling OSC disciplinary reviews to follow judicial findings in FOIA actions, but it is distinct from § 1216’s broader investigatory authorization.

## District court ruling: interplay of § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) and remand
AFL sued OSC in district court to compel the investigation. The district court held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC’s authority under § 1216—that is, FOIA’s referral provision did not displace § 1216’s independent investigatory command. The court therefore concluded OSC had erred as a matter of law in disclaiming any authority to investigate. Nonetheless, the district court stopped short of directing OSC to take enforcement action. It concluded that § 1216(c) left OSC discretionary as to whether to seek corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215, so the court remanded for OSC to reconsider in light of the correct legal view. After OSC again declined to investigate on remand, the district court entered final judgment dismissing AFL’s remaining claims for failure to state a claim.

## Appeal posture: de novo review and supplemental standing briefing
AFL appealed the district court’s dismissal to the D.C. Circuit. The court stated that its review of the legal questions would be de novo. Because neither party initially addressed Article III standing, the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing on that jurisdictional issue before deciding the merits. The panel ultimately addressed standing as the threshold question of subject-matter jurisdiction, but the procedural path to the court involved an initial district-court ruling that construed the statutory interplay and remanded to OSC before final dismissal, followed by AFL’s appeal and the appellate court’s request for additional briefing on standing.

# Article III Standing and the Procedural-Injury Framework

This chapter explains how courts analyze Article III standing when a plaintiff alleges that a defendant violated a statutory procedural right. It uses America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer to illustrate the doctrinal tests for injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability, and the limited circumstances in which courts “relax” some standing requirements for procedural injuries.

## Constitutional baseline: injury in fact, causation, and redressability (Lujan)

Article III limits federal courts to resolving “Cases” and “Controversies,” and standing doctrine flows from that limitation. To have standing, a plaintiff must show three elements: an injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability. An injury-in-fact must be a concrete and particularized invasion of a legally protected interest that is actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical. The alleged injury must be fairly traceable to the challenged action of the defendant (causation), and it must be likely — not merely speculative — that the injury will be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. These principles, articulated in Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife and repeated in later cases, function as jurisdictional prerequisites; if a plaintiff cannot establish them, the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction and must dismiss.

In practice, courts test each element against the facts the plaintiff pleads. At the motion-to-dismiss stage the plaintiff must allege facts plausibly showing each requirement, not merely legal conclusions. The D.C. Circuit treated that standard as binding in Food & Water Watch v. Vilsack and applied it here: when neither party initially addressed standing, the court ordered supplemental briefing and evaluated whether AFL had plausibly alleged injury, causation, and redressability.

## Procedural rights cannot stand alone: no standing for a procedural right in vacuo (Summers)

A recurring limitation on procedural-right claims is that a procedural right by itself does not create Article III standing. In Summers v. Earth Island Institute the Supreme Court held that the “deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation — a procedural right in vacuo — is insufficient” to establish standing. That principle prevents plaintiffs from obtaining judicial review solely to vindicate procedural violations when no concrete interest is at stake.

Applied to AFL, this means that alleging OSC violated a statutory duty to investigate (a procedural entitlement, if such exists) is not enough. AFL had to show that OSC’s alleged failure to investigate harmed a concrete, particularized interest of AFL’s own. The court framed AFL’s attempt to avoid Summers by claiming two informational harms — nondisclosure of FOIA records and lack of investigatory notices — and then asked whether those injuries satisfied the concrete-interest requirement Summers demands.

## Relaxed standing in procedural-injury cases: what is relaxed, and what is not

Although Summers restricts “procedural rights in vacuo,” courts recognize a narrower doctrine that sometimes relaxes certain standing requirements when a statutory procedural right is intended to protect a concrete interest. In the D.C. Circuit this is described as a relaxation of imminence and redressability, but not a wholesale elimination of the need to show injury-in-fact or causation. Cases such as Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education and Florida Audubon illustrate this calibrated approach: when a statute creates a procedural protection designed to safeguard a concrete interest, plaintiffs need not show the same level of immediacy or certainty of redressability that would be required in other contexts. But they still must show that the procedural violation resulted in an injury to a concrete, particularized interest and that the injury is fairly traceable to the defendant.

The D.C. Circuit reiterated that distinction in AFL. The court explained that while courts can relax imminence and redressability in appropriate procedural-right cases, they do not relax the core requirements that there be a concrete injury and a plausible causal link between defendant’s conduct and the injury. Thus, AFL could not succeed merely by invoking a procedural right to an OSC investigation; it had to tie that procedural claim to a concrete harm fairly traceable to OSC’s refusal to act.

## Pleading standard at motion to dismiss: plausibly alleging each standing element (Food & Water Watch)

At the pleading stage, the plaintiff must allege facts that plausibly support each element of standing. The D.C. Circuit follows the general plausibility standard: allegations must be more than conclusory labels; they must contain factual matter that, if true, would make each element reasonable. Food & Water Watch v. Vilsack and related authority require a factual showing sufficient to permit a reasonable inference that the injury was caused by the defendant and that a favorable decision would likely redress it.

In AFL, the court applied that standard to the two informational injuries AFL asserted. The question was not whether the procedural statute (section 1216) might be violated, but whether AFL had pleaded facts plausibly establishing a concrete injury and that OSC’s refusal to investigate was the likely cause of, and remedy for, that injury.

## Application in America First Legal Foundation: two asserted informational injuries

AFL framed OSC’s refusal to investigate as a procedural violation and identified two concrete informational injuries it claimed flowed from that violation. The court analyzed each theory and rejected both.

First, AFL argued that because OSC declined to investigate, DOJ has not produced the FOIA records AFL sought. The court found fatal problems with causation and redressability. The alleged causal chain depended on multiple contingent and independent steps: OSC would investigate and then initiate corrective or disciplinary proceedings; the Merit Systems Protection Board might impose sanctions; DOJ might then change its FOIA response and disclose the records. That sequence required speculation about the decisions of independent actors not before the court and about how OSC would exercise its enforcement discretion — matters courts typically cannot assume. The court relied on the principle that causation cannot rest on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors, and on the doctrine that judicial compulsion of prosecutorial discretion is limited (see Heckler v. Chaney). Relatedly, redressability focuses on whether a judgment would bind the defendant to take steps that would likely remedy the injury; here, even a court order that OSC must investigate would not directly bind DOJ or the MSPB, nor would it make it likely that they would take the necessary follow-up steps to produce the records. The court also emphasized that redressability looks to whether the judgment would bind the parties, not whether it might persuade third parties to act (citing Haaland). Because the causal chain was too speculative and the remedy too attenuated, AFL failed to plausibly allege that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused the nondisclosure injury or that a judicial order against OSC would be likely to redress it.

Second, AFL claimed an informational injury in being denied the notices and investigatory information that would accompany an OSC investigation. To show informational standing, a plaintiff must ordinarily be denied information to which a statute entitles it. The court observed that even if section 1216 requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it does not itself create an entitlement to receive investigatory notices. AFL’s theory rested on the premise that if OSC were investigating under section 1216 it would be required to follow section 1214’s notice rules; but AFL conceded, and precedent supports, that OSC’s choice to apply section 1214 procedures in a section 1216 investigation is discretionary (Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel). Because AFL had no statutory entitlement to receive section 1214 notices in the section 1216 context, it lacked the concrete informational right necessary for informational standing. Moreover, even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, AFL had not plausibly shown that such an order would make it materially more likely to receive the contested information.

The combined result was dispositive: because AFL could not satisfy the core requirements of injury-in-fact and causation for either asserted informational injury, it lacked Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit therefore vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instruction to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

## Teaching points and practical guidance

When you analyze procedural-injury standing in a contested case, follow the sequence the court used in AFL. First, identify the concrete interest the statute is meant to protect; a bare procedural entitlement is not enough. Second, test whether the plaintiff has alleged a concrete, particularized injury connected to that interest. Third, scrutinize causation: would the defendant’s violation be a direct and non-speculative cause of the injury, or does the causal chain depend on independent actors or discretionary steps? Fourth, consider redressability: would a favorable ruling bind the defendant in a way that would likely remedy the injury, or would relief at most persuade third parties to act? Finally, remember the pleading-stage constraint: the plaintiff must allege facts plausibly showing each element, not just legal conclusions.

AFL exemplifies how those principles operate in practice: courts will relax imminence and redress concerns in narrow circumstances, but they will not tolerate speculation that a judicial victory will set off a chain of discretionary decisions by other agencies sufficient to remedy the plaintiff’s asserted informational injury.

# First Standing Theory: FOIA Records Deprivation Traceable to DOJ

This chapter explains the court’s rejection of America First Legal Foundation’s (AFL) first standing theory: that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 caused AFL’s informational injury—its failure to obtain records from the Department of Justice (DOJ) under FOIA. The opinion treats this claim under the familiar Article III standing requirements of causation and redressability, and concludes that AFL’s causal theory is too attenuated and speculative to sustain a federal case or controversy.

AFL’s theory in plain terms was straightforward. AFL alleged that DOJ refused to search and produce certain employee emails in response to its FOIA request. AFL contended that if OSC had been required to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings under § 1216, such an investigation could have led OSC to seek corrective or disciplinary proceedings under § 1214 or § 1215. Those proceedings, in turn, might have caused the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to sanction responsible DOJ employees, and those sanctions might have prompted DOJ to produce the requested records. AFL relied on this chain to show that OSC’s refusal to investigate was a but-for cause of AFL’s lack of access to the records.

Traceability: the injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC
A central defect identified by the court is traceability: the injury AFL asserts—the nonproduction of records—flows directly from DOJ’s choice to withhold, not from OSC’s inaction. Article III requires that the injury be “fairly traceable” to the defendant’s challenged conduct, not to the independent action of a third party. Because DOJ, not OSC, is the actor whose withholding immediately produced AFL’s lack of records, the claim as pleaded attributes the primary causal responsibility to DOJ. That distinction matters at the standing stage: plaintiffs cannot sustain standing by pointing to some remote governmental inaction when the immediate source of the injury is another actor’s refusal to act.

The speculative causal chain: too many contingent steps
The opinion next dissects the causal chain AFL posits and explains why each link makes the overall theory speculative. To get from OSC’s non-investigation to AFL’s receipt of records, multiple contingent events would have to happen: OSC would have to initiate an investigation; OSC would have to decide to pursue corrective or disciplinary proceedings under § 1214 or § 1215; the MSPB would have to adjudicate and impose sanctions or order corrective relief; and DOJ would have to respond by producing the records. Each link is a discretionary decision by an independent actor. When standing turns on a sequence of hypothetical choices by agencies or adjudicative bodies not before the court, causation becomes too attenuated to support Article III jurisdiction.

Independent actors and precedent on attenuation
The court anchored this attenuation analysis in controlling precedent. It cited FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for the proposition that causation “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” That line of authority bars plaintiffs from invoking hypothetical reactions by third parties as the chain that connects the defendant’s conduct to the plaintiff’s injury. The OSC→MSPB→DOJ sequence is precisely the sort of multi-step reliance on independent actors the court found insufficient.

Enforcement discretion and Heckler v. Chaney
A related limitation arises from prosecutorial and enforcement discretion. Even if OSC could be ordered to investigate, how it would exercise any enforcement authority (for example, whether to seek corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215) is discretionary. Heckler v. Chaney teaches that courts are generally reluctant to compel an agency’s exercise of enforcement discretion; plaintiffs cannot establish causation by assuming a particular prosecutorial path that the agency might have taken had it investigated. Because a favorable ruling against OSC would not control or command the discretionary enforcement choices that might have followed, the causal link on which AFL relies is further weakened.

Redressability: a favorable judgment would not very likely produce the records
Closely related to traceability is redressability. Even accepting AFL’s chain of events, the court asked whether a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would be likely to redress AFL’s FOIA injury. The court relied on the formulation used in Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC: a plaintiff must show that relief would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury. Here, the court concluded it would not. A judicial order against OSC could at best bind OSC to undertake an investigation; it would not bind the MSPB to impose sanctions or DOJ to alter its FOIA practices. Because the critical remedial decisions would remain in the hands of independent actors whose responses cannot be predicted or compelled, the requested relief would not be likely to produce the records AFL seeks.

Binding-parties principle: redressability focuses on what the court can order
The court also emphasized an important structural rule about redressability: it generally depends on whether the court can issue an order that binds the defendant and thereby leads directly to relief, not on whether the court’s rationale might persuade nonparties to act. Haaland v. Brackeen underscores that redressability looks to the effect of relief on the parties before the court. AFL’s theory depends largely on persuading or prompting the MSPB and DOJ—neither of which would be bound by an OSC investigatory order—to change course. That reliance on persuasive, nonbinding effects is insufficient.

Conclusion: causation and redressability fail even under relaxed procedural standards
AFL attempted to frame its claim as a procedural-right injury—OSC’s failure to investigate under § 1216—but the court applied the relaxed standard for procedural injuries only to the questions of imminence and redressability, not to causation. Even under that more permissive approach, AFL could not establish the necessary causal and redressable connection between OSC’s refusal to investigate and AFL’s ongoing lack of FOIA records. The injury is traceable to DOJ’s withholding, and the remedial pathway from an OSC investigatory order to production of records is too contingent on the discretionary actions of independent actors to satisfy Article III. Consequently, AFL’s first standing theory fails.

# Second Standing Theory: Informational Injury From OSC Investigation Notices

This section analyzes AFL’s second theory of Article III standing: that OSC’s refusal to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 deprived AFL of information that the statute entitles it to receive—principally the notices and updates that accompany an OSC investigation under § 1214—and that this informational deprivation is a concrete injury sufficient to confer standing. The court rejected that theory for two linked reasons: (1) AFL identified no statutory entitlement to receive investigation-related information from OSC in the §1216 context, and (2) even if OSC were ordered to investigate, any judicial relief would not be likely to require OSC to provide the §1214-style notices that AFL says it lost.

## AFL’s claim: entitlement to investigation-related information

AFL’s argument rests on a factual and statutory linkage. It points to §1214, which governs OSC’s corrective-action procedures and includes certain notice and progress-reporting obligations to a complaining party, and to §1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” AFL’s theory is straightforward: because §1216 mandates OSC investigations of alleged FOIA-withholdings, OSC’s refusal to investigate under §1216 deprived AFL of the notices and information that OSC would have provided if it had been conducting a §1214-style investigation. In other words, AFL claims an informational injury arising from not receiving investigation-related notices and updates.

## Informational-standing law: entitlement to information under a statute

The court framed this claim under established informational‑standing principles. A plaintiff can have a concrete informational injury when a statute has created a right to receive particular information and the defendant has denied that information. See, e.g., FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998); Public Citizen v. Department of Justice, 491 U.S. 440 (1989); Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13 (D.C. Cir. 2011). The key point from these precedents is that denial of information is actionable only when the statute actually entitles the plaintiff to that information; a bare procedural entitlement unconnected to any concrete interest—or a denial of a procedure “in vacuo”—does not satisfy the injury‑in‑fact requirement. See Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009) (procedural right without a concrete interest is insufficient).

## No statutory entitlement under §1216 to receive §1214-style notices

Applying that rule, the court asked whether §1216 (even assuming it mandated an investigation) created an entitlement to receive the §1214-style notices and updates AFL seeks. The court concluded that it did not. Section 1216(a)(3) directs OSC to “conduct an investigation” of alleged FOIA withholdings, but the statute does not itself promise complainants the §1214 notice protections; and AFL did not argue that §1216 independently contains affirmative disclosure or notice obligations of the type recognized in Akins or Public Citizen. AFL’s attempt to bootstrap a notice entitlement rested on the possibility that, if OSC conducted a §1216 investigation, it might choose to follow §1214’s procedures and thereby provide AFL the notices it seeks. But that is precisely speculation about what the agency might do, not a demonstration that a statute entitles AFL to receive particular information.

## Discretionary nature of applying §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations (Carson)

The court reinforced this statutory point by relying on precedent recognizing OSC’s discretion in applying §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations. The Sixth Circuit in Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), held that OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214’s procedural requirements when investigating under §1216 is discretionary. AFL itself acknowledged that line of cases. That acknowledgment is important: it undercuts AFL’s central premise that a §1216 investigation would automatically carry with it a statutory entitlement to §1214-style notices. If the decision to apply §1214 procedures in a §1216 investigation is discretionary, there is no statutory command creating a right in complainants to receive those procedures.

## No concrete injury: absence of a legal right defeats informational standing

Because §1216 does not itself create a right to receive the §1214 notice regime, the court found no statutory entitlement to the information AFL claims it was denied. Under the informational‑standing precedents, that absence of a legal entitlement means AFL suffered no cognizable informational injury from OSC’s refusal to investigate. The injury AFL describes is the loss of information that might have been provided if OSC had chosen—as a matter of discretion—to follow §1214 procedures; such an injury is not the denial of a statute‑created informational right, but the denial of a discretionary benefit that the statute does not compel.

## Redressability failure: an order to investigate would not guarantee §1214-style notices

The court also tied the lack of entitlement to a redressability problem. Even assuming a court could order OSC to investigate under §1216, such an order would not force OSC to adopt §1214’s notice provisions in that §1216 investigation because the application of §1214 procedures in §1216 matters is discretionary. Thus a favorable judicial ruling mandating investigation would not be “reasonably likely” to produce the notification and information AFL claims it lost. Under settled standing doctrine, a plaintiff must show that a favorable decision would likely redress the alleged injury; where the remedy would leave the agency free to withhold the very information the plaintiff seeks, redressability is lacking.

## Conclusion

The court therefore rejected AFL’s second standing theory: informational standing requires that the plaintiff be entitled by statute to the information allegedly withheld. AFL identified no such entitlement in §1216, and it acknowledged that §1214 notice procedures are discretionary in §1216 investigations. Because there was no legal right to the notices AFL claims it was denied—and because an order compelling an investigation would not reliably produce those notices—the court concluded AFL suffered no concrete, redressable informational injury and thus lacked Article III standing on this theory.

# Disposition and Significance

## Judgment: vacatur and remand with instructions to dismiss
The D.C. Circuit disposed of America First Legal Foundation’s (AFL’s) appeal on threshold jurisdictional grounds. Because AFL lacked Article III standing, the court vacated the district court’s merits decision and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. In practical effect, the panel erased the district court’s substantive conclusions about OSC’s legal obligations and instructed the lower court to close the case for want of jurisdiction rather than resolve the statutory questions AFL pressed.

This disposition matters procedurally: vacatur removes any precedential force the district court’s merits rulings might have borne, and remand with instructions terminates the litigation rather than sending the merits back for further agency consideration. The panel’s remedy follows the familiar rule that a court without Article III jurisdiction cannot enter a binding merits judgment and therefore must dismiss.

## Unreached merits: the court did not resolve statutory interplay
Because standing was lacking, the panel expressly declined to decide the contested statutory questions that animated the litigation. The opinion does not resolve whether 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) independently requires OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings or whether FOIA’s enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), limits OSC’s investigatory duty. Although the district court had held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin § 1216, the appellate panel left that statutory interpretation question unanswered. Likewise, the court did not decide whether § 1216 in itself mandates particular investigatory steps or procedures.

Students should note the practical consequence: a later case presenting properly pleaded standing could still obtain a judicial ruling on the statutory interplay or on whether § 1216 imposes a mandatory investigatory duty.

## Key takeaway on standing: link procedural rights to concrete, redressable interests
The opinion is a compact lesson in contemporary Article III standing doctrine, especially where plaintiffs assert a procedural-right injury. The panel applied the familiar three-part test: injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability (Lujan). It reiterated that mere denial of a procedural entitlement—“a procedural right in vacuo”—is insufficient; the plaintiff must show that the lack of the procedure has harmed a concrete, particularized interest. When alleging a procedural injury, courts relax—but do not eliminate—requirements of imminence and redressability; plaintiffs still must show that the procedural violation resulted in an injury to a concrete interest that a favorable decision would likely redress.

In AFL’s case the court concluded that the asserted procedural right to an OSC investigation did not translate into a concrete, redressable injury. AFL could not show that a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would be likely to lead to the FOIA relief it sought, nor that it had a statutory entitlement to the investigative fruits that would give rise to informational standing.

## Limits on leveraging third‑party enforcement: speculative causal chains fail
A central reason AFL lacked standing was the speculative chain of causation necessary to connect OSC’s non-investigation to AFL’s alleged informational injury. The panel reasoned that any informational injury from DOJ’s non-production of records is directly traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the supposed causal path—from an OSC investigation to MSPB or disciplinary proceedings, then to a change in DOJ’s FOIA response—rests on multiple independent actors and discretionary choices. Courts will not attribute causation to a defendant based on a speculative series of decisions by other agencies or actors not before the court. The opinion emphasized that redressability normally depends on how a judgment binds the defendant, not on the hope that a binding opinion will persuade independent actors to change course.

The practical doctrinal point is twofold: first, attempts to secure relief indirectly by compelling an enforcement agency to act will often founder if the success of that enforcement action depends on independent agencies or discretionary prosecutorial choices; second, the courts will treat prosecutorial or enforcement discretion (and choices by nonparty agencies) as obstacles to showing a non-speculative causal and redressable link.

## Informational standing caution: need a statutory entitlement to information
The court reiterated the rule that a plaintiff can have an informational injury only when a statute entitles the plaintiff to particular information. Even where a statute creates investigatory duties, that does not automatically create a right to receive the investigatory materials or notices—unless the statute clearly provides such an entitlement. AFL’s attempt to claim informational standing based on an asserted right to receive notices and information from an OSC investigation failed because AFL did not identify a statutory entitlement to those communications under § 1216 itself, and AFL conceded that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations is discretionary under existing precedent.

Put simply: to ground informational standing, identify a clear statutory provision that gives the plaintiff a right to the information sought; mere expectations that an agency might (or might not) share information in the course of an investigation are insufficient.

## Strategic implications for litigants
The opinion suggests clear tactical lessons. If a litigant’s primary goal is to obtain agency records, the reliable route is to pursue FOIA’s own remedies—file and litigate the FOIA request, seek a court order for production, and, if appropriate, obtain attorneys’ fees and a judicial finding of arbitrary withholding. Those FOIA remedies are direct and bind the agency whose disclosure practices are at issue. By contrast, trying to obtain records indirectly by compelling OSC to investigate is a fraught strategy: a judicial order against OSC will not necessarily bind DOJ or the MSPB, and any chain of causation depending on discretionary enforcement choices or nonparty actors is likely to be treated as speculative.

A litigant who nonetheless wants to press an OSC-based theory should (1) plead and develop facts showing a non-speculative, direct connection between OSC’s action or inaction and a specific, concrete right to information or other relief; or (2) identify statutory language giving a cognizable entitlement to notices or investigative products that the plaintiff seeks. Absent that, the court’s reasoning counsels that courts will dismiss for lack of Article III standing.

Conclusion
The panel’s disposition—vacatur and dismissal for lack of jurisdiction—resolves AFL’s case without reaching the contested statutory questions. Its analysis emphasizes the practical limits of using an enforcement agency’s investigatory obligations to secure FOIA relief and reinforces the standing principles that require plaintiffs to tie alleged procedural deprivations to concrete, redressable interests that a favorable judicial order will directly remedy.
