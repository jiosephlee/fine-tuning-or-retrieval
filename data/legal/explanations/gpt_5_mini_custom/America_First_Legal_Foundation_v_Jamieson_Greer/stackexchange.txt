Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” language create a judicially enforceable procedural right for complainants?
Question:
The opinion suggests AFL framed its claim as deprivation of a procedural right to an OSC investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), but the court says a bare procedural right in vacuo doesn’t confer standing (citing Summers). I’m confused: when a statute says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, why isn’t that enough to be a concrete procedural entitlement for the complainant? Is the problem that § 1216 directs OSC’s duties without creating any corresponding right in requesters, or that even mandatory procedures still require a separate concrete interest tied to the procedure?
Answer:
Short answer: No — under this opinion §1216(a)(3)’s “shall … conduct an investigation” imposes a statutory duty on OSC but does not, by itself, create a judicially enforceable procedural right for FOIA requesters unless the plaintiff can show a concrete, particularized injury that is caused by OSC’s refusal and likely to be redressed by a court order. The court’s ruling rests on standing doctrine, not on a textual refusal to read “shall” as mandatory.

Why: the governing standing principles
- A plaintiff asserting a procedural right must still plead a concrete and particularized injury traceable to the defendant and redressable by the court. Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009) (a “procedural right in vacuo” is not enough).
- Informational-standing rules require a statute to create an entitlement to information; otherwise denial of information is not a concrete injury. See Zivotofsky/Akins line, quoted in the opinion.

How the court applied those principles here
- AFL framed its claim as deprivation of a procedural right to a §1216 investigation. The court accepted that §1216 “shall … conduct an investigation” imposes a duty on OSC, but rejected AFL’s standing for two independent reasons the plaintiff proffered as concrete injuries:
  1. Injury from DOJ’s nondisclosure of records: the court held the alleged informational injury was traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the causal chain connecting an OSC investigation to DOJ producing records was too speculative. That chain would depend on discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ—precisely the sort of independent-actor speculation courts cannot base redressability on (citing Heckler v. Chaney and FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.). Thus a judicial order requiring OSC to investigate would not be likely to “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s FOIA injury.
  2. Injury from deprivation of investigatory notices/information: the court explained that §1216 does not itself entitle a requester to receive the notices that §1214 provides; OSC’s use of §1214 procedures in §1216 investigations has been treated as discretionary (Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)). Because AFL could not show a statutory entitlement to those investigatory materials, there was no concrete informational injury of the sort recognized in Akins/Zivotofsky/Pub. Citizen.

Bottom line
- The problem is twofold in the court’s reasoning: (1) §1216 is framed as a duty owed by OSC (a public-law duty), not as an express statutory entitlement to particularized information or process in favor of FOIA requesters; and (2) even where a statute imposes mandatory procedures, a plaintiff must still show a concrete interest tied to that procedure (Summers). The opinion concludes §1216’s “shall” alone does not satisfy Article III standing requirements when the alleged injuries are speculative or no statutory entitlement to information/process has been shown.

### Why doesn’t informational standing attach to the notices and updates AFL hoped to receive if OSC investigated under § 1216?
Question:
The court says informational standing requires a statute that entitles the plaintiff to receive information, and concludes § 1216 creates no such entitlement. AFL argued that if OSC investigated under § 1216, it might choose to provide the notifications that § 1214 requires in prohibited personnel practice cases. Given § 1216(c) says OSC may investigate and seek corrective action “under § 1214 … in the same way,” why doesn’t that incorporate § 1214’s notice requirements? Is Carson v. OSC controlling nationwide that § 1214’s procedures are discretionary in § 1216 investigations, and if so, what statutory text makes those notices optional rather than mandatory?
Answer:
Short answer
Informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the information. The D.C. Circuit held that §1216 does not create any such entitlement to the notices and updates that §1214 provides. That is why AFL’s claimed “informational” injury fails: AFL conceded and the court treated as correct that OSC’s decision whether to apply §1214’s procedural rules in a §1216 investigation is discretionary (see Carson), and §1216’s text does not convert those §1214 procedures into a statutory right to receive notice.

Why (doctrinally) informational standing fails here
The court rests its ruling on the settled principle that a plaintiff can obtain standing based on denial of information only when a statute “entitle[s] him to receive” that information (Zivotofsky/Akins/Pub. Citizen line of cases). The opinion repeats that rule (citing American Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., and other authorities) and then asks whether the statutes at issue actually create an entitlement to receive the §1214-style notices AFL wanted. It concludes they do not. Without a statutory entitlement to the information, there is no concrete informational injury for Article III purposes.

What the opinion says about §1214 and §1216
- §1214 contains procedural protections for complaining parties in forbidden-personnel-practice cases, including specific notice provisions (see 5 U.S.C. §1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)). The opinion acknowledges those notice provisions exist in §1214.
- §1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings; §1216(c) states OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The court treated that language as permissive in effect for purposes of creating an entitlement to information.
- Crucially, the court observed that AFL did not claim §1216 itself requires OSC to “engraft” §1214’s notice regime onto every §1216 investigation; AFL instead accepted the line of cases that treats OSC’s choice whether to apply §1214’s procedures in a §1216 matter as discretionary (the court cites Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), as an example). Given that concession and the statutory text, the court concluded §1216 creates no entitlement to receive §1214-style notices. Because no entitlement exists, AFL lacks informational standing.

Is Carson “controlling nationwide”?
No—Carson is a Sixth Circuit decision, so it is not binding outside that circuit. The D.C. Circuit opinion does not treat Carson as binding nationwide either; it cites Carson as one of the cases recognizing that OSC’s application of §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations has been treated as discretionary, and it notes AFL’s concession that such cases exist. The D.C. Circuit’s own holding here reaches the same practical result (no entitlement, no informational standing), but that is the D.C. Circuit’s holding, not Sixth Circuit precedent imposed nationwide.

What in the statute makes the notices optional rather than mandatory?
Two textual points the opinion relies on (either expressly or by reasonable implication) support the conclusion that §1214 notice procedures are not an automatic entitlement in §1216 investigations:
1. §1216(c) uses permissive language (“may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way …”), which authorizes OSC to use §1214 procedures but does not itself command that OSC must do so in every §1216 investigation. The permissive “may” is the primary textual hook for optionality.
2. Congress elsewhere imposed a clear, mandatory OSC duty when it intended one: FOIA’s separate enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. §552(a)(4)(F)(i), says OSC “shall promptly initiate” a proceeding if a court orders production, awards fees, and questions arbitrary withholding. The opinion highlights that Congress knows how to make OSC’s duties mandatory when it wants to; the absence of a parallel mandatory command in §1216 supports the view that §1214 procedures are not automatically entitling outsiders to notices in §1216 investigations.

Bottom line
Informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the information. The D.C. Circuit concluded §1216 does not create such an entitlement to §1214-style notices: §1216(c) authorizes OSC to apply §1214 procedures but does not compel OSC to do so, AFL acknowledged precedent treating that choice as discretionary (Carson), and FOIA contains other, explicit mandatory commands where Congress intended them. Because there was no statutory right to the notices, AFL lacked the concrete informational injury needed for Article III standing.

### How exactly do causation and redressability fail when the requested relief is just an order that OSC investigate?
Question:
The opinion describes a speculative causal chain: if OSC investigated, it might pursue corrective/disciplinary action; the MSPB might sanction; DOJ might change course; and thus AFL might get records. In procedural-injury cases courts relax imminence and redressability somewhat. Why didn’t that relaxed standard suffice here? Are there examples where a court found standing to compel a preliminary procedural step even though ultimate relief depended on independent actors? What distinguishes those cases from this one?
Answer:
Short answer: Because AFL’s injury depended on a highly speculative chain of discretionary acts by multiple independent actors, the relaxed standing standard for procedural injuries could not bridge the fatal causation and redressability gaps the court identified. The opinion rests on three linked points: (1) the alleged informational harm is traceable to DOJ (not OSC), (2) any pathway from an OSC investigation to receipt of records requires multiple discretionary choices by other agencies (OSC’s enforcement choices, the MSPB’s sanctions, DOJ’s production), and (3) Congress did not create an entitlement to the investigative “fruits” that would give AFL an informational right enforceable against OSC.

Why the relaxed “procedural-injury” rule didn’t save AFL
- Governing law. Article III requires injury-in-fact that is caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable judgment. Lujan. Courts do “relax” imminence and redressability in some procedural-right cases—but not the requirements of injury-in-fact or causation. The opinion cites Center for Law & Educ. and Fla. Audubon for that limited relaxation, and Summers for the principle that a procedural right alone (a “procedural right in vacuo”) is not enough.
- Traceability. The court held AFL’s informational injury from DOJ’s withholding is “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.” A plaintiff cannot bootstrap standing against one agency by pointing to a separate agency’s alleged wrongdoing unless the complaint shows a direct causal link.
- Speculative causal chain and independent actors. The opinion lays out the chain AFL would need: OSC investigates → OSC pursues corrective/disciplinary action → MSPB sanctions employees → DOJ produces records. The court rejected that as “too speculative and attenuated,” invoking the general rule that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (citing All. for Hippocratic Med.) and Heckler v. Chaney for the principle that courts generally may not compel discretionary enforcement decisions.
- Informational-entitlement failure. For an “informational” injury to support standing, the plaintiff must be denied information to which a statute entitles it (Akins, Pub. Citizen, ASPCA). The court found no statutory entitlement here: even if section 1216 requires OSC to investigate, it does not itself create a right to receive the investigatory notices or reports; OSC’s obligation to follow section 1214’s procedural-notice regime in a section 1216 probe is discretionary (Carson), so AFL cannot claim a statutory informational entitlement that a judicial order would vindicate.

When courts do find standing to compel a preliminary step (and why those cases differ)
- The opinion points to the class of procedural-right decisions where courts relax imminence/redressability because the statute’s procedure protects a concrete interest. In those cases (see Center for Law & Educ., Fla. Audubon), the procedural right either: (a) is closely tailored to protect a concrete, particularized interest the plaintiff alleges has been harmed, or (b) creates a clear entitlement to information or process such that ordering the preliminary step would itself redress the injury.  
- By contrast, AFL’s claim lacks those features: there is no statutory entitlement to the investigatory information it wants, the injury is traceable to another agency (DOJ), and any remedy would rely on discretionary actions by OSC and independent agencies (MSPB, DOJ). Those distinctions are precisely why the court rejected standing here.

Bottom line
The relaxed standard for procedural injuries only helps where the procedural violation is tied to a concrete, particularized interest or where the statute creates an entitlement whose enforcement would itself redress the injury. Here, the causal path from an OSC investigatory order to AFL’s receipt of records is too speculative (dependent on discretionary acts by multiple independent actors), and there is no statutory entitlement to the investigatory outputs AFL seeks—so causation and redressability fail.

### How are § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) (FOIA’s OSC referral provision) and § 1216 (general OSC authority) supposed to interact?
Question:
The district court held that FOIA’s three triggers in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) do not limit OSC’s authority under § 1216 to investigate arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. The D.C. Circuit didn’t reach merits but recites that holding. Statutorily, is § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) best read as an additional mandatory-investigation pathway when its predicates are met, without precluding § 1216 investigations otherwise? If so, what is the practical difference between a § 1216-initiated investigation and a § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)-triggered one, especially regarding what information, if any, must be shared with the complainant?
Answer:
Short answer
Read together (and as the district court read them in the opinion), § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is a separate, FOIA‑specific command that creates an additional, mandatory referral path to OSC when its three predicates occur. It does not, by its text and as the district court concluded, strip OSC of the independent authority to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. The practical difference is mostly procedural and remedial: the FOIA trigger compels a prompt proceeding directed at whether the employee “primarily responsible” should be disciplined, while §1216 authorizes OSC to investigate allegations more broadly but leaves OSC discretion about how (and whether) to apply §1214’s procedural/notice rules and what information to share with a complainant. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion recites these points and rests the case on standing; it does not alter that statutory interaction.

Why (and where the opinion says this)
- §1216(a)(3) says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The opinion therefore treats §1216 as a broad statutory grant of investigatory authority over alleged FOIA withholding abuses. (Op. I.A.)
- FOIA’s separate provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), provides that if a court (1) orders production, (2) awards attorneys’ fees, and (3) “specifically question[s] whether the agency ‘acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding,’” then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” The opinion sets this up as a distinct, mandatory referral mechanism in FOIA. (Op. I.A.)
- The district court — and the opinion recounts this ruling — concluded that the FOIA referral provision does not limit OSC’s independent §1216 power to investigate alleged FOIA violations. The opinion therefore frames the two provisions as complementary: §552(a)(4)(F)(i) supplies a mandatory, case‑specific referral when its predicates are met, but it does not nullify §1216’s separate investigatory grant. (Op. I.B.)

Practical differences (especially about information sharing)
- Character of the duty. A §552(a)(4)(F)(i) event creates a clear, FOIA‑based duty to “promptly initiate” a proceeding to examine disciplinary liability of the employee “primarily responsible.” By contrast §1216 is a general investigatory command (“shall … conduct an investigation”) covering allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but the practical contours of how OSC handles such §1216 investigations (e.g., whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary remedies) are implemented through the §1214/§1215 machinery and involve OSC exercise of enforcement discretion. (Op. I.A–B; II.)
- Who is the focus. The FOIA referral is targeted at whether the employee primarily responsible should face discipline; §1216 authorizes OSC to investigate allegations about agency practice or policy as well as individual misconduct. (Op. I.A–B.)
- Notice and complainant information. Section 1214 contains procedural provisions “including provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of an investigation.” The opinion also observes that §1216(c) authorizes OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” But the opinion also recounts that OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214’s procedural requirements in a §1216 investigation has been treated as discretionary (the opinion cites that point and the district court’s reliance on it). Thus:
  - If OSC conducts a §1216 investigation and elects to proceed under §1214’s procedures, the complaining party may receive the notices §1214 provides.
  - If OSC conducts a §1216 investigation but does not invoke §1214 procedures, there is no statutory entitlement to receive investigative materials or progress notices merely from §1216 itself.
  - The FOIA §552(a)(4)(F)(i) referral requires OSC to “promptly initiate” a proceeding but does not, on its face, create a separate statutory right for the FOIA requester to obtain OSC’s investigatory materials or compel OSC to apply §1214 notice rules. The opinion relied on this point to deny AFL an informational‑injury basis for standing: even if OSC were required to investigate under §1216, AFL had no statutory entitlement to the investigatory information it sought. (Op. I.A; II.)

Takeaway for litigation strategy and expectations
- Treat §552(a)(4)(F)(i) as an additional, mandatory route to compel OSC action when a FOIA litigant can establish the three FOIA predicates; it does not extinguish §1216.
- Don’t assume that a successful §1216 investigation (or an OSC proceeding under the FOIA referral) creates a statutory right for the FOIA requester to receive OSC’s investigative files or guaranteed status updates unless OSC applies §1214 procedures (and the decision to do so in §1216 investigations has been viewed as discretionary in the cases the opinion recounts).
- The opinion itself did not decide the full merits of competing constructions; it recited the district court’s view that §552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit §1216 and then disposed of the case on standing grounds. (Op. II–III.)

References to the opinion
- §1216(a)(3) requirement to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings; §1216(c) authorization to use §1214/§1215 procedures (Op. I.A).
- §552(a)(4)(F)(i) three predicates that trigger OSC’s duty to “promptly initiate” a proceeding (Op. I.A).
- District court’s conclusion that §552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit §1216 authority; district court’s view that §1216(c) commits procedural choices to OSC’s discretion (Op. I.B).
- The court’s discussion of §1214’s notice provisions and the consequence for standing (Op. I.A; II).

### Could the district court properly remand to OSC for reconsideration if Article III standing was lacking from the start?
Question:
The district court said OSC committed a legal error in reading § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to limit § 1216, but then declined to order an investigation and instead remanded to OSC. The D.C. Circuit vacated for lack of standing. Under Steel Co., courts must assure jurisdiction before reaching the merits. Was the district court’s remand itself a merits-based action taken without jurisdiction? More generally, can a court remand to an agency for further consideration if the plaintiff lacks standing, or must it dismiss outright without any substantive guidance?
Answer:
Short answer: No. Under Steel Co. and the D.C. Circuit’s decision in this case, a court must resolve Article III standing before reaching the merits, and it may not enter a merits‑based remand while lacking jurisdiction. Because the district court reached a legal conclusion on OSC’s authority and remanded for reconsideration without first ensuring AFL had Article III standing, the appellate court vacated and instructed dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

Why: the governing rule and how the opinion applies it
- Article III standing is a threshold, jurisdictional requirement: courts must assure a “case or controversy” before resolving the merits. The panel expressly invoked Steel Co. at the start of Part II: “Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject‑matter jurisdiction. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998).”
- The district court here concluded OSC had committed a legal error in reading the statutes and then remanded to OSC for further consideration. That was a merits determination (it resolved the statutory question and directed further agency action founded on that resolution).
- The appellate opinion holds that AFL lacked Article III standing and therefore that the district court lacked subject‑matter jurisdiction to enter its merits‑based judgment/remand. The court’s ultimate remedy was to “vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.”

Bottom line for practice
- If a plaintiff lacks Article III standing, the district court has no power to grant merits relief or to remand on the basis of a merits ruling; the proper course is dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction. The D.C. Circuit’s disposition in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer embodies that rule: resolve standing first; if it fails, vacate any merits disposition and dismiss.

### Is there any scenario where a FOIA requester would have standing to compel OSC action under § 1216?
Question:
The panel emphasizes problems of traceability and redressability and the lack of a statutory entitlement to information from OSC. Hypothetically, could a requester ever show standing to force a § 1216 investigation—for example, if OSC’s own regulations promised complainant updates, or if the requester could allege facts making MSPB or agency action sufficiently likely? Or is Chaney’s enforcement-discretion presumption and the lack of a complainant-facing entitlement under § 1216 a categorical bar?
Answer:
Short answer: Not categorically impossible, but extremely unlikely under the legal rules the panel applied. America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer makes clear that a FOIA requester lacks standing to force OSC to investigate unless the plaintiff can show (1) a concrete informational injury traceable to OSC’s refusal and (2) that a favorable judgment would very likely redress that injury—facts the court found lacking in AFL’s case. The opinion therefore leaves only narrow, fact‑specific pathways to standing.

Why AFL lost (doctrinal roadmap from the opinion)
- Article III standing requires injury in fact, causation, and redressability (Lujan). The court applied the relaxed framework for procedural‑right suits but still required a concrete, particularized interest affected by the procedural deprivation (summarized at II).  
- Informational standing exists only when the plaintiff is denied information to which a statute entitles him (Feld Ent., Akins, Pub. Citizen). The opinion rejects an “informational injury in vacuo.”  
- Causation/redressability failed because any FOIA non‑production was traceable to DOJ (not OSC) and relief would depend on a speculative chain: OSC might investigate → OSC might seek corrective/disciplinary action → MSPB or DOJ might act → DOJ might produce documents. The court stressed that causation cannot rest on speculation about independent actors and that courts generally may not compel an agency’s enforcement discretion (citing Heckler v. Chaney). The opinion also emphasized redressability turns on whether the judgment would bind the defendant, not merely persuade third parties (II).

Practical scenarios where standing might exist (consistent with the opinion)
1) Statutory or regulatory complainant entitlement to information or a non‑discretionary OSC duty. The opinion notes FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which imposes an OSC duty when three predicates are met (court orders production, fees awarded, and a court questions arbitrariness). When statutory language creates a mandatory OSC duty and the statutory predicates are satisfied, denial of the mandated OSC action would be a concrete deprivation of a statutory entitlement and would be more plausibly redressable. In short: standing is more likely where the statute itself creates a mandatory, complainant‑facing right that a court order would enforce.  
2) OSC regulation creating a complainant‑facing entitlement. The court relied on the principle that an informational injury requires a statutory entitlement; that same logic applies to binding agency regulations. If OSC adopted (and legally had) a regulation that affirmatively and non‑discretionarily guaranteed complainants specific notices or investigation outcomes in § 1216 matters, a denial of those promised notices could supply the required concrete injury and the refusal to comply would be directly redressable against OSC. The opinion references § 1214’s notice provisions and the discretionary question whether § 1214’s procedures apply to § 1216 investigations (citing Carson), which suggests the importance of whether any procedural entitlement is mandatory rather than discretionary.  
3) A case where judicial relief would bind OSC (not merely persuade third parties) and produce the records without speculative links. The opinion rejects standing when redress depends on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” By contrast, if a plaintiff could show that a declaratory or injunctive judgment would directly require OSC to take a non‑discretionary step that itself produced the requested information (or required an agency party the court can bind to produce the records), redressability would be stronger.

Why these windows are narrow
- The court explicitly relied on Heckler/Chaney’s teaching that enforcement‑discretion decisions are presumptively non‑reviewable and that courts generally may not compel an agency to exercise enforcement powers. When relief requires forcing OSC to exercise enforcement discretion (e.g., to initiate a disciplinary or MSPB action that might in turn prompt document production), standing will be hard to establish.  
- The opinion finds AFL’s theories failed because they required multiple speculative intervening choices by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ—exactly the kind of attenuated causal chain the court disapproved.

Bottom line
A plaintiff can sometimes have standing to compel OSC under § 1216, but only in narrow situations the panel recognized as different from AFL’s facts: where there is a clear statutory or regulatory entitlement to information or a non‑discretionary OSC duty (or where a court’s ruling would directly bind the defendant and “very likely spur” redress), rather than only a hope that OSC, MSPB, or the producing agency will act. Absent such a complainant‑facing entitlement or a judgment that directly binds the relevant actor, Chaney’s presumption of enforcement discretion plus the causation/redressability principles the court applied will foreclose standing.

### What counts as the requisite “concrete, particularized interest” tied to the alleged procedural violation in this setting?
Question:
The court says even under the relaxed procedural-injury framework, AFL must show the violation of a procedural right affected a concrete, particularized interest. If the interest is access to DOJ records, the court says that’s traceable to DOJ, not OSC. If the interest is the investigation’s information, the court says there’s no entitlement. What kind of concrete interest could satisfy this requirement in an OSC-investigation context—e.g., would a federal employee whistleblower alleging a prohibited personnel practice have a different result because § 1214 gives them specific notification rights?
Answer:
Short answer
The court applied the familiar “procedural injury” rule: a plaintiff asserting only a procedural right must also show that the procedural deprivation harmed a concrete, particularized interest that the statute was designed to protect. Under America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer, that means the plaintiff needs either (a) a concrete informational entitlement to receive particular information from OSC itself, or (b) some other concrete, particularized interest that OSC’s procedural failure directly injures and that a court order against OSC would likely redress. AFL’s FOIA-requester interest failed both tests; a federal employee who is explicitly entitled by § 1214 to investigatory notices (a “complaining party”) would be a much closer case and could satisfy the requirement if the defendant OSC actually withheld the statutorily mandated notices.

Relevant legal standard (as the opinion states it)
- Article III requires a concrete and particularized injury that is caused by the defendant and likely redressable by the court. Lujan, Summers, etc., quoted in the opinion.  
- The “relaxed” procedural-injury framework only loosens imminence/redressability; it does not eliminate the need for a concrete injury caused by the defendant. (Apln. Br.; see opinion II, citing Summers 555 U.S. at 496 and Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157.)  
- Informational standing exists only when the plaintiff is denied information the statute entitles him to receive. The court cites Feld Ent. and Akins for that point (opinion II).

How the court applied the standard to AFL’s theories
1. Interest in the DOJ records (AFL’s FOIA claim)
- The court treated any injury from DOJ’s nondisclosure as traceable to DOJ, not OSC, so causation fails. OSC’s refusal to investigate would at best create a speculative, multi-step causal chain (OSC → possible OSC enforcement → MSPB action → DOJ production) that depends on the discretionary choices of other, independent actors. The court relied on the principle that standing cannot rest on such speculation about unfettered choices of third parties (citing All. for Hippocratic Med. and Haaland v. Brackeen). Thus AFL’s FOIA-related informational injury is not fairly traceable to OSC and is not redressable by a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate. (See opinion II, paras 2–4.)

2. Interest in getting investigatory notices/information from OSC itself
- Informational standing requires an entitlement to the information. The court observed that § 1214 contains procedures (including notice provisions) for corrective actions and that § 1216 permits OSC to investigate FOIA-withholdings “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” But the court also concluded § 1216 does not itself create an entitlement to receive the § 1214 notices in every § 1216 investigation, and AFL conceded that OSC’s choice to apply § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 investigations has been treated as discretionary (citing Carson). Because AFL had no statutory entitlement to the § 1214 notices in the § 1216 context, it lacked the concrete informational injury necessary for standing. (See opinion II, paras 5–8.)

What kind of “concrete, particularized interest” would satisfy the requirement here?
- A statutory entitlement to particular investigatory information or notices from OSC, coupled with an allegation that OSC (the defendant) actually withheld that information, is the paradigmatic example. The opinion points to § 1214 as an instance of statutory procedures that include notification rights (the opinion notes § 1214 “sets forth procedures … including provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of an investigation”). If the plaintiff is a person whom § 1214 actually entitles to those notices (e.g., a “complaining party” in a prohibited personnel practice inquiry), then being denied those statutorily required notices would be a concrete informational injury under the Feld Ent./Akins line and not merely a procedural right in vacuo.  
- By contrast, a remote interest in records held by another agency (DOJ here) that can be remedied only by a lengthy, speculative chain of enforcement steps (OSC → MSPB → DOJ) will not suffice because of traceability and redressability limits.

Would a federal employee whistleblower fare differently?
- Yes — plausibly. The opinion recognizes that § 1214 contains specific notice and progress-reporting provisions for corrective actions and appears to treat those as the very kind of statutory entitlements that can support informational standing. If a federal employee (or other person) is the sort of “complaining party” to whom § 1214 expressly promises notices and OSC refused to give those notices, then the plaintiff would be asserting denial of information to which the statute entitles them. That situation is much closer to the paradigmatic informational injury the court describes and would avoid the “traceable to DOJ/not OSC” problem that doomed AFL’s FOIA-based theory. But the plaintiff would still have to show that a favorable judicial decree against OSC would redress the injury (i.e., that a court order compelling OSC to provide the statutorily required notices would actually remedy the denial).

Bottom line
- The requisite “concrete, particularized interest” is (most straightforwardly) a statutory entitlement to information or notices from OSC itself — not an attenuated expectation that OSC’s investigation might indirectly produce records from another agency. A whistleblower who is statutorily entitled to § 1214 notice rights and who alleges OSC withheld those notices would present the classic kind of informational injury the opinion says could meet the relaxed procedural-injury test; AFL’s FOIA-requester interest did not.

### Does Heckler v. Chaney effectively swallow § 1216’s mandatory ‘shall investigate’ language for reviewability purposes?
Question:
The opinion leans on Chaney to highlight the judiciary’s inability to compel agency enforcement choices and uses that to show redressability is speculative. But § 1216(a)(3) says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of allegations about arbitrary FOIA withholdings. When, if ever, is a ‘shall investigate’ command judicially enforceable post-Chaney? Is the idea that the initial decision to open an investigation might be reviewable if truly mandatory, but choices about seeking corrective/disciplinary action are not—or does Chaney’s presumption extend even to the threshold investigation decision when the statute lacks complainant-facing rights and standards?
Answer:
Short answer
No—Heckler v. Chaney does not automatically nullify every statutory “shall … conduct an investigation” command. But America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer illustrates how Chaney’s presumption against judicially compelling enforcement powers will defeat many challenges unless the statute (1) creates a concrete, complainant‑facing entitlement or (2) imposes a nondiscretionary, judicially manageable duty whose performance would itself redress the plaintiff’s injury. In AFLF the court relied on Chaney plus redressability principles to reject review because §1216 did not create an informational entitlement and any relief would depend on speculative choices by multiple independent actors.

Why the D.C. Circuit resolved AFLF the way it did
- Chaney’s baseline: The opinion reiterates Chaney’s core point—that courts generally may not compel agency enforcement decisions and that speculation about how an agency might exercise enforcement discretion is an insufficient basis for Article III relief (citing Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985)). The panel used that rule to evaluate whether a court order compelling OSC to investigate would actually redress AFL’s claimed injuries.

- Standing/redressability was fatal here: Rather than decide whether §1216 is on its face reviewable, the court held AFL lacked Article III standing because any causal chain from an OSC investigation to production of the requested FOIA records required multiple discretionary steps by independent actors (OSC choosing corrective/disciplinary proceedings, the MSPB imposing sanctions, DOJ deciding to produce records). Because redressability “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors,” the court found a favorable judgment unlikely to “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s asserted informational injury (citing FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Haaland v. Brackeen).

- Informational‑entitlement problem: The court also emphasized the separate doctrine that an informational injury can support standing only when a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive information (citing Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Akins, Zivotofsky). §1216(a)(3) commands OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but it does not—according to the panel—create a complainant’s entitlement to receive information about or flowing from such an investigation. And OSC need not apply §1214’s notice rules when it acts under §1216 (the opinion cites Carson on that point). That lack of a statutory entitlement further undermined AFL’s ability to show a redressable informational injury.

What this means for “shall investigate” post‑Chaney
- Where “shall investigate” will be enforceable: The opinion points to (and rests its analysis upon) two essential features a plaintiff must show to overcome Chaney’s presumption and redressability hurdles: (1) the statute must create a concrete, plaintiff‑facing right or entitlement to information or relief such that an agency’s adherence to the “shall” would by itself redress the plaintiff’s injury; or (2) the statutory command must be sufficiently mandatory and legally manageable that a court can order the agency to act without relying on further discretionary enforcement steps by other actors. If those features are present, Chaney does not automatically preclude judicial enforcement.

- Where Chaney will block enforcement: When the “shall” is not tied to a complainant‑facing entitlement or when compliance would merely set in motion discretionary enforcement and adjudicative processes (as here, involving OSC’s exercise of prosecutorial tools, MSPB action, and then agency choices), Chaney’s presumption and redressability doctrine will normally prevent a court from ordering relief. That is exactly the posture in AFLF: the panel did not hold §1216 never requires OSC to investigate, but it concluded AFL could not show that a judicial order compelling investigation would likely redress its claimed informational harms.

A final caution from the opinion
The court disposed on standing and did not definitively decide whether §1216 is judicially reviewable as a matter of administrative‑law doctrine. Its reasoning, however, makes clear the practical point: a bare “shall investigate” will be hard to enforce when (a) the statute does not create an individualized entitlement to the information or procedures the plaintiff seeks and (b) meaningful relief would depend on later discretionary acts by independent actors. In such circumstances, Chaney’s presumption—combined with redressability rules—will usually be dispositive.
