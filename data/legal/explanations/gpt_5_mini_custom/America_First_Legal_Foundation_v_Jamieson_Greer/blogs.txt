Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# When “Shall” Meets Standing: D.C. Circuit Says You Can’t Force OSC to Investigate FOIA Withholdings

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a tidy lesson in the limits of Article III standing when plaintiffs try to use courts to compel investigatory agencies to act. AFL asked the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate an asserted DOJ policy of not searching employee emails for FOIA requests. When OSC declined, AFL sued to force an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. The district court found OSC had erred as a matter of law but declined to order a particular remedial regime and remanded. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit did not reach those statutory questions; instead it disposed of the case on Article III grounds, holding AFL lacked standing. The decision tightens the gate for litigants who try to convert a statutory “shall” into judicially enforceable relief via a suit alleging only procedural injuries.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The court framed the dispute as a classic standing inquiry: injury in fact, causation, and redressability. AFL characterized its injury in two ways. First, it said OSC’s refusal to investigate prevented DOJ from producing the FOIA records AFL sought. Second, it said OSC’s refusal denied AFL the informational benefits that an OSC investigation (and related notices under § 1214) would yield.

The panel rejected both theories. On the first, the court emphasized that any informational injury from nondisclosure is traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the causal chain needed to connect an OSC investigation to the production of records was too speculative. That chain would require OSC to initiate an investigation, choose to seek corrective or disciplinary action, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to impose sanctions, and DOJ to respond to those sanctions by producing records. The court invoked the familiar rule that causation cannot rest on speculation about the independent choices of multiple actors not before the court and underscored Heckler v. Chaney’s protection of agency enforcement discretion. Because a favorable judicial decree compelling OSC to investigate would not make those intervening steps “very likely,” redressability failed.

On the second theory—informational standing—the court held that a plaintiff has an informational injury only when statute grants a right to receive specific information. Section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings, but it does not itself create an entitlement to the notices and updates that § 1214 provides in the context of prohibited personnel-practice investigations. AFL conceded that whether § 1214’s procedural protections apply in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary under controlling authority (the court cited Carson and similar precedents). Without a statutory entitlement to receive OSC-produced information, AFL could not show the kind of concrete informational injury necessary to establish Article III standing.

Because AFL failed to allege a cognizable, redressable injury, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

## A close look at causation and redressability

The panel’s causation and redressability analysis is methodical and squarely rooted in recent Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit precedents. Two points deserve emphasis.

First, the decision applies the Court’s skepticism toward attenuated causal chains involving multiple independent decisionmakers. The speculative sequence—OSC investigates; OSC seeks disciplinary or corrective action; the MSPB imposes sanctions; DOJ alters its FOIA posture and produces records—puts too many “ifs” between a judicial order and the plaintiff’s requested relief. The court cited FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Haaland v. Brackeen to stress that redressability hinges on the likely effect of a judgment on the parties before the court, not on imagined persuasion of other actors.

Second, Heckler v. Chaney remains a powerful barrier to judicially forcing agency enforcement. Even if § 1216 contains the “shall” language, the panel treated enforcement choices by OSC and remedial choices by the MSPB as involving classic executive discretion that courts generally cannot compel through mandamus or declaratory relief unless a plaintiff shows a more direct and concrete injury.

Together these themes explain why procedural statutes that say “shall” do not automatically give plaintiffs Article III access when the practical effect of compelling agency action would still depend on discretionary choices by nonlitigants.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

This decision fits within a broader trend in the post-Lujan era of tightening standing doctrine for procedural injuries. The panel relied on Summers and related D.C. Circuit authority that a “procedural right in vacuo” is not enough; a plaintiff must connect the procedural lapse to a concrete, particularized interest. That approach will make it harder for litigants to pursue cases meant primarily to police statutory or regulatory compliance by investigatory agencies where the plaintiff cannot show a direct, legally cognizable harm that the court can fairly redress.

Practically speaking, the ruling narrows a path some FOIA litigants had envisioned: if an agency fails to search certain sources, compel OSC to investigate, thereby producing pressure on the agency to disclose. The court’s holding closes that indirect route unless a plaintiff can show a much tighter causal connection—for example, a situation where an OSC investigation is constitutionally or statutorily guaranteed to trigger particular remedial steps that directly produce the records sought, or where the plaintiff is independently entitled to specific information from OSC as a statutory matter.

The decision also preserves a space for meaningful OSC enforcement in the political and administrative arenas while limiting judicial intrusion into prosecutorial choices. If Congress wants OSC’s investigatory duties to be directly enforceable in federal court and to carry predictable remedial consequences, it may need to be explicit not only that OSC must investigate but also that certain notifications or corrective outcomes are owed to affected parties and that those obligations are judicially enforceable.

## What this decision does not decide

The panel intentionally confined itself to Article III jurisdiction and did not resolve competing statutory interpretations—most notably whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)’s three predicates limit OSC’s authority under § 1216(a)(3), or whether § 1214’s procedural protections must accompany a § 1216 investigation. The district court had thought § 1216 was not cabined by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) but that § 1216(c) nevertheless left remedial choice to OSC. The D.C. Circuit’s dismissal on standing leaves those statutory questions open for another day—assuming a plaintiff with standing presses them.

## Bottom line

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a reminder that statutory “shall”s cannot overcome Article III limits when the path from a court’s decree to the plaintiff’s real-world relief runs through the discretionary choices of other agencies and adjudicatory bodies. For FOIA plaintiffs and their counsel, the decision signals that the traditional vehicle to challenge nondisclosure remains a FOIA suit against the withholding agency itself; efforts to compel third-party investigatory agencies to act will confront significant causation and redressability hurdles unless Congress has fashioned a clearer, enforceable entitlement to information or more direct remedial mechanisms.



# Informational Standing Without an Entitlement: Why § 1216 Doesn’t Grant Access to OSC Investigation Outputs

## Why this case matters
The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is an important reminder of the limits of so‑called “informational standing.” Public‑interest and watchdog groups often try to ground Article III jurisdiction on the loss of information they say statutes entitle them to receive. But in a string of cases the court has made clear that not every statutory duty to act creates a cognizable informational injury; the plaintiff must show either a concrete, legally protected informational entitlement or a plausible chain of causation from the defendant’s conduct to the requested relief. America First Legal reinforces both points and narrows the routes by which challengers can sue to force investigatory agencies like the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to produce investigatory outputs or notices.

## The posture and the court’s reasoning
AFL sued OSC after OSC declined to investigate whether the Department of Justice arbitrarily withheld FOIA‑responsive emails, invoking 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). AFL alleged two informational injuries: (1) DOJ still had not produced all the records AFL had sought under FOIA, and (2) OSC had not provided the notices and information AFL would receive if OSC were investigating under the § 1214 notice regime. The district court found OSC legally wrong to decline investigation but nevertheless remanded for OSC to reconsider. On appeal the D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits; it held AFL lacked Article III standing and ordered dismissal for lack of jurisdiction.

The court applied the settled framework for procedural and informational injuries. It began from Summers and Lujan: a procedural right alone, “in vacuo,” does not supply Article III standing unless the procedural right is designed to protect a threatened concrete interest and the violation of the procedure resulted in a concrete harm. The court rejected AFL’s first theory because causation and redressability were too attenuated. Even if OSC had been compelled to investigate, AFL’s theory required a speculative sequence of events—OSC choosing enforcement, the Merit Systems Protection Board imposing sanctions, DOJ then deciding to produce records—reliant on independent actors’ discretionary choices. As the court emphasized, courts cannot base standing on speculation about the unfettered acts of non‑parties (citing FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Haaland v. Brackeen).

The court rejected AFL’s second theory for a different reason: informational standing depends on an entitlement to information created by statute or regulation. The Supreme Court’s decisions in FEC v. Akins and Public Citizen v. DOJ illustrate the point: those plaintiffs had been denied information to which Congress had given them a right, and that statutory entitlement created a concrete injury. By contrast, § 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings but does not create any entitlement for private parties to receive notices, reports, or other investigatory outputs. And, crucially, binding authority (Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 6th Cir.) recognizes that OSC’s application of § 1214’s procedural notice regime to § 1216 investigations is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to receive OSC materials, and with § 1214 procedures not automatically grafted onto § 1216, AFL could not show the kind of concrete informational injury that Akins and Public Citizen require.

Because AFL failed to plead a concrete, traceable, and redressable informational injury, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

## How to read the decision against Akins and Public Citizen
Akins and Public Citizen are not overruled; rather, America First Legal illustrates the boundary lines those cases set. Akins found standing where Congress created an informational right that enabled judicial review of the substance of agency action—the absence of statutorily required information deprived the plaintiffs of their ability to participate meaningfully in the democratic process. Public Citizen held that when Congress gives a private party a statutory benefit—here, access to litigation records and the ability to challenge alleged FOIA misconduct—denial of that benefit can produce a judicially cognizable injury.

By contrast, § 1216 does two things: it instructs OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings and authorizes OSC to pursue corrective or disciplinary action under the frameworks of §§ 1214–1215. But it does not itself create a right in private requesters to receive OSC investigatory disclosures or to be apprised of OSC’s decisionmaking in any particular way. Without such a statutory entitlement, informational standing doctrine requires something more: either a direct informational entitlement or a non‑speculative chain of causation showing that the defendant’s refusal to act is likely to lead to the very relief the plaintiff seeks.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Practically, the decision narrows one path for watchdog groups to sue for investigatory outputs. Two implications stand out.

First, plaintiffs cannot rely on the mere existence of a statutory duty to investigate—absent an express statutory promise of disclosure—to confer informational standing. Courts will scrutinize whether Congress intended private parties to receive the information allegedly withheld. If not, the “procedural rights” line of cases offers only a limited relaxation of the imminence and redressability requirements; it does not free plaintiffs from proving a concrete, traceable injury.

Second, the decision reinforces courts’ reluctance to resolve disputes where redress would depend on discretionary decisions by multiple agencies or independent actors. Where the causal chain to relief requires the enforcement choices of agencies not before the court (the MSPB, DOJ’s litigation choices, OSC’s exercise of prosecutorial discretion), redressability is often too speculative. That reasoning dovetails with established limits on judicially compelling agency enforcement (Heckler v. Chaney) and recent cases emphasizing that standing cannot rest on speculative downstream acts by third parties (All. for Hippocratic Med., Haaland).

Institutionally, the ruling may reduce the practical leverage of compelled‑investigation claims against agency watchdogs. If litigants cannot show either a statutory entitlement to investigatory outputs or a direct injury that would be redressed by a judicial order, such suits will likely be dismissed at the pleading stage. OSC’s discretion in applying § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations (per Carson) remains important; absent a statutory change or contrary controlling precedent, OSC will have room to decide how transparent an investigation will be and whether to provide requester‑oriented notices.

## Practical takeaways for watchdog groups and litigators
For advocates seeking to base standing on informational injuries, the decision suggests several strategic lines:

- Seek or identify statutory entitlements. Informational standing is strongest when a statute expressly gives the plaintiff (or a defined class that includes the plaintiff) a right to receive particular information. When drafting legislation or rule‑making petitions, be precise about requester rights to notices, reports, or investigatory findings.

- Tie the injury to the defendant’s conduct, not to the speculative acts of third parties. If your claim for relief depends on enforcement steps by non‑party agencies (MSPB, independent disciplinary bodies) or discretionary decisions by other actors, emphasize how a judicial order would directly bind the defendant in a way likely to produce the requested information.

- Use FOIA’s own enforcement triggers where possible. FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates a clearer path to OSC engagement when a court orders production, awards fees, and specifically questions arbitrary withholding. A final judicial ruling on FOIA—especially one that satisfies those three predicates—can produce a non‑speculative duty for OSC to act.

- Preserve concrete harms in the complaint. If you can plead an immediate and particularized informational harm (for instance, an agency’s express refusal to provide information the statute promises), that is far more likely to survive a standing challenge than a generalized interest in agency transparency.

- Consider alternative remedies. Where mandating an OSC investigation is unlikely to be redressive, litigants may seek other relief—declaratory rulings binding only the defendant agency, structural injunctions, or litigation designed to trigger the FOIA enforcement mechanism set out in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

## Conclusion
America First Legal is a useful doctrinal signpost: absent a statutory entitlement to receive investigatory outputs, plaintiffs cannot create Article III standing simply by invoking a statutory duty to investigate or by inferring that an investigation might eventually produce the records they want. Akins and Public Citizen still govern the other end of the spectrum—where Congress has plainly conferred an information right—but this decision clarifies that informational standing requires either a statutory entitlement or a non‑speculative causal pathway to relief. For watchdog groups, the case counsels care in pleading concrete, traceable injuries and in pursuing statutory or litigative paths that more directly compel disclosure.



# The FOIA–OSC Two‑Track Enforcement Puzzle the D.C. Circuit Didn’t Decide

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s unpublished decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer resolves nothing about a knotty statutory question that matters to FOIA litigants, agency officials, and accountability advocates: does the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) have an independent, mandatory duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) to investigate any allegation that an agency has “arbitrarily or capriciously” withheld records under FOIA, or is OSC’s investigatory and disciplinary role tied to the separate, FOIA‑specific trigger in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—a judicial finding that an agency withheld records improperly plus an award of attorney’s fees and an explicit question about arbitrariness? The district court said § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin § 1216; the D.C. Circuit sidestepped that dispute on standing grounds. That avoids an important interpretive choice about how two overlapping statutory regimes interact, leaving the law murky for litigants who hope to use OSC as a backstop for FOIA compliance.

The case therefore matters because it exposes the practical and doctrinal uncertainty about the breadth of OSC’s role in policing FOIA compliance. Depending on how courts answer the question, OSC could be available as an investigatory engine for any credible allegation of arbitrary withholding, or its role could be limited to situations in which a court has already made specific findings in a FOIA case and triggered OSC’s FOIA‑specific referral duty.

## What the D.C. Circuit actually decided

Rather than take the statutory question head on, the D.C. Circuit dismissed AFL’s suit for want of Article III standing. The panel accepted (as the district court had) that AFL asked OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent. But the court concluded that AFL’s asserted injuries were too speculative or too attenuated to support standing.

AFL advanced two theories of informational injury: (1) that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused DOJ to withhold responsive records, and (2) that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of the notices and information it would receive in an OSC probe. The court rejected both. With respect to (1), the causal chain—OSC investigates, OSC seeks corrective or disciplinary action, MSPB or DOJ imposes sanctions, DOJ produces records—was too contingent on independent actors’ choices to support Article III causation and redressability. With respect to (2), the court said that § 1216 does not itself create an enforceable right to receive investigatory information; any notice obligations that might flow from § 1214 procedures do not automatically attach to § 1216 investigations, and district‑court precedent recognizing such an overlap (the district court had remanded once on the merits before dismissing) is not binding on the circuit. Because AFL could not show a concrete, redressable informational injury, the appeals court vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

That procedural holding leaves the substantive interpretive dispute unresolved: the court did not decide whether § 1216(a)(3) authorizes (or requires) OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholding independently of the FOIA provision that mandates OSC disciplinary proceedings after certain judicial findings.

## How the two provisions read in tension

At first glance the two provisions point in different directions. Section 1216(a)(3) is broad: in the statute that creates OSC’s investigative authority, Congress says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The language looks categorical and seems to empower OSC to investigate FOIA complaints. By contrast, FOIA’s own enforcement provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), imposes a discrete, court‑triggered duty: if a court orders production, awards attorneys’ fees, and “specifically question[s]” whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding, then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action against the employee responsible is warranted.

There are plausible, competing interpretive readings.

One reading treats § 1216(a)(3) as an independent, affirmative investigatory mandate: Congress meant OSC to investigate FOIA withholding allegations whenever such an allegation is made, and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) supplies an additional, focused obligation to begin disciplinary proceedings (and thus to refer matters) when a court has already made a finding. Under this view the provisions are complementary: § 1216 authorizes (indeed commands) OSC to investigate allegations; § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) requires a particular response—prompt initiation of a proceeding to determine disciplinary action—once a court reaches certain conclusions in a FOIA adjudication.

An alternative reading sees § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as the primary vehicle for FOIA‑related enforcement and interprets § 1216(a)(3) more narrowly—either as implicitly conditioned on the FOIA provision’s trigger, or as granting OSC discretion to investigate in the FOIA context subject to FOIA’s separate disciplinary mechanism. On this view, § 1216 supplies a general investigatory hook but does not displace FOIA’s detailed, court‑based referral mechanism; Congress’s creation of a special, mandatory disciplinary referral following a judicial finding signals that FOIA enforcement relies on that particular, adjudication‑dependent pathway.

Statutory canons and structure can be marshaled for both sides. The express FOIA trigger’s detailed, mandatory language supports the view that Congress intended a specific post‑judgment disciplinary referral process. But that express provision does not say OSC may not investigate under § 1216; conversely, § 1216’s plain text gives OSC a duty to investigate allegations of arbitrary withholding without referencing the § 552 trigger. Harmonizing the text—reading the provisions as complementary—has doctrinal appeal but still leaves practical questions about what investigative procedures and notice obligations apply in a § 1216 investigation that arises outside the FOIA‑specific, court‑triggered context.

## Why the question matters in practice

The answer has concrete consequences for accountability, agency behavior, and OSC’s workload.

If § 1216 is truly an independent, enforceable investigatory duty, then FOIA requesters (and watchdogs) could potentially compel OSC to probe agency FOIA policies and practices even when no court has yet found a FOIA violation. That would create a route for public scrutiny earlier in the enforcement process. It could encourage agencies to adopt more careful FOIA search practices lest they face OSC scrutiny. But it would also raise administrative‑law concerns: OSC would face potentially many more investigations, possibly straining resources and transforming a prosecutorial office into a first‑responder inspectorate for FOIA disputes. It could also provoke clashes between OSC and agencies over investigatory reach and procedure.

If, by contrast, OSC’s FOIA role is effectively tied to the FOIA‑specific trigger, then most FOIA enforcement via OSC will be reactive and judicially channeled: first win in court, obtain a judicial reluctance or finding that the withholding was arbitrary, secure fees, and then compel OSC to initiate discipline referrals. That route provides a clearer, less speculative chain of causation for litigants seeking to hold OSC accountable and ensures that OSC’s disciplinary process is deployed only after a court has made substantive findings. But it also means that many allegations of systematic or policy‑level FOIA problems—where plaintiffs lack a favorable judicial decree—may never be investigated by OSC.

The D.C. Circuit’s standing opinion reinforces an important practical point: litigants trying to force OSC’s hand via § 1216 face a difficult causation and redressability hurdle. As the court said, ordering OSC to investigate under § 1216 is unlikely in itself to compel DOJ (or the MSPB) to produce records or to impose sanctions; that chain depends on independent actors’ choices. So even if § 1216 is broad on paper, litigants who have not secured the judicial findings specified in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) may struggle to show Article III injury traceable to OSC’s refusal.

## Doctrinal lines to watch and litigation strategies

Given the D.C. Circuit’s jurisdictional exit, the next time this issue reaches a merits decision it will matter which plaintiff and which posture present the case.

The cleanest path for a merits ruling would come from a FOIA plaintiff who has obtained the very judicial findings that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) requires—production ordered, attorney’s fees awarded, and an explicit judicial question about arbitrariness—and then sues OSC for failing to “promptly initiate a proceeding.” That scenario eliminates the speculative causation problem highlighted here: the FOIA statute itself creates a clear, nondiscretionary duty that the plaintiff can seek to vindicate against OSC. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion strongly suggests that plaintiffs are more likely to have standing in that posture because the injury and redressability are tightly connected to OSC’s own obligation under FOIA.

Another promising litigant would be someone who can point to a statutory entitlement to information that OSC would have to disclose as part of an investigation—if such an entitlement can be tied unambiguously to the statutory scheme. The panel emphasized that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive information. If a plaintiff can allege and later prove that § 1216 or the cross‑references to § 1214 create enforceable notice or information rights in the FOIA context, standing might follow. But that is a hard case to make given the precedent the court cited (and district courts that have treated application of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 investigations as discretionary).

Litigants seeking to force OSC investigations based on systemic FOIA complaints without first securing a favorable FOIA judgment should be prepared to litigate standing vigorously and to offer evidence that OSC’s investigatory decision causes a concrete, particularized informational harm that a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate will likely cure. The D.C. Circuit’s causation analysis highlights that generalized or attenuated chains of causation through other agencies will not do.

## Institutional and policy tradeoffs

Beyond any single case, the unresolved tension reflects a policy tradeoff Congress left unresolved: whether to routinize a robust, pre‑judicial investigatory role for OSC in FOIA enforcement, or to reserve OSC‑driven discipline for post‑judgment findings by courts. Each approach reflects distinct priorities—earlier administrative policing of potential misconduct versus reliance on the courts to marshal the evidentiary predicate and limit OSC’s investigatory burden.

If future courts interpret § 1216 broadly, Congress or the Executive Branch may need to reckon with resource allocation and procedural protections (what process should an affected agency employee receive in a § 1216 investigation that concerns FOIA?). If courts instead construe § 1216 narrowly or tether it to FOIA’s trigger, FOIA complainants’ path to OSC involvement becomes longer and more litigation‑intensive, but OSC’s role remains clearly bounded by judicial findings.

## Bottom line and likely next steps

The D.C. Circuit’s dismissal on standing grounds leaves open the central statutory question. For now, the practical rule is simple: plaintiffs seeking to force OSC action are better positioned when they arrive with a judicial ruling that satisfies § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). That posture avoids the speculative causal chain that doomed AFL’s standing here.

Expect the issue to resurface. A future plaintiff who secures a district‑court finding of arbitrary withholding (with fees and the court’s explicit questioning of the agency’s conduct) and then sues OSC for refusing to “promptly initiate” a proceeding will present the cleanest vehicle for a merits decision about the interplay of § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). Alternatively, Congress could step in to clarify whether OSC’s investigatory duty under § 1216 is independent of FOIA’s court‑triggered disciplinary referral. Until then, the “two‑track” enforcement regime remains unsettled, and FOIA litigants and the agencies that respond to them will operate with that uncertainty in their strategic and administrative calculus.



# Independent Actors and the Standing Trap: Lessons from AFL v. Greer for Multi-Agency Litigation

## Why this case matters
The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a useful lesson in the modern limits of Article III standing when a plaintiff’s path to relief runs through multiple, independent government actors. Public‑interest litigants often try to compel an enforcement agency to act because that agency, in turn, might prod or punish the withholding agency into producing records or changing behavior. The court here tightened the causal and redressability requirements for such multi‑actor theories, leaning on recent Supreme Court guidance to treat chains of unfettered decisions as too speculative to carry Article III jurisdiction. That has immediate practical consequences for FOIA strategy and for suits that seek structural remedies by corralling separate enforcement bodies.

## What the court held (and what it left undecided)
AFL sued the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) seeking to force an investigation into an alleged DOJ FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent. OSC declined, and AFL brought suit. The district court thought section 1216’s language required OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings and remanded, but ultimately dismissed AFL’s claims on the merits. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit did not reach those statutory questions. Instead, it concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing and therefore vacated and remanded for dismissal for lack of jurisdiction.

The panel’s central standing rulings: AFL’s theory that OSC’s failure to investigate led to DOJ’s continuing refusal to produce records failed both causation and redressability. That causal chain would have depended on (at a minimum) OSC deciding to prosecute or seek corrective action, the Merit Systems Protection Board imposing sanctions or remedies, and then DOJ changing its FOIA response. The court found such a sequence too speculative to support Article III standing, relying on the proposition that courts cannot rest jurisdiction on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the court.” The panel likewise rejected AFL’s attempt to claim an independent informational injury in the fruits of an OSC investigation: even if section 1216 commanded an OSC investigation, AFL lacked a statutory entitlement to the particular notices or investigatory disclosures it sought, so it had no concrete informational right on which to ground standing.

## The court’s doctrinal touchstones — and why they matter
The opinion reads as an application of Summers and Lujan’s procedural‑injury limits together with the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications about redressability in cases involving independent actors. The panel cited Summers to reiterate that a procedural right without a concrete interest is "procedural right in vacuo" and insufficient for standing. It then leaned on Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Haaland v. Brackeen for the proposition that redressability cannot depend on persuading or predicting the discretionary choices of non‑parties or independent agencies. Heckler v. Chaney likewise loomed in the background: prosecutorial and enforcement discretion means courts are often ill‑suited to compel agency enforcement decisions, and plaintiffs relying on such enforcement as the proximate source of relief face an uphill Article III climb.

On informational standing, the court applied the well‑trod rule from Akins, Public Citizen, and related D.C. Circuit authorities: only where a statute creates a substantive entitlement to receive information will the denial of that information support standing. Here, even assuming section 1216 required OSC to investigate, AFL could not point to a statutory right to receive specific investigatory notices or disclosures—especially because OSC’s use of section 1214’s procedural notice provisions in a section 1216 investigation is, per precedent the plaintiff conceded, discretionary.

## Analytical perspective: a sensible (if restrictive) tightening
The panel’s approach is defensible and coherent with recent high‑court trends emphasizing the practical limits of federal courts’ power to remedy harms that depend on multiple independent discretionary decisions. It avoids turning federal courts into triers of probabilities about how an enforcement agency might wield discretionary authority in hypothetical future proceedings before another independent tribunal. That restraint protects separation of powers values and prevents plaintiffs from bootstrapping standing simply by pointing to a possible chain of enforcement outcomes.

At the same time, the ruling highlights a tension. Congress can write statutes that create direct entitlements or trigger mechanisms (as FOIA does in 552(a)(4)(F)(i) in certain circumstances), but where Congress leaves enforcement to an entity with prosecutorial discretion, private parties will struggle to show that a judicial order compelling investigation will “very likely” lead to the remedial act they seek. The decision thus places a heavier evidentiary and pleading burden on plaintiffs: establish an injury directly traceable to the defendant or show that the relief you seek would bind the actor who controls the remedial lever.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Practically, the opinion will make it harder for public‑interest groups to pursue systemic redress by suing enforcement agencies that are one step removed from the source of the concrete injury. Lower courts will likely cite AFL v. Greer when rejecting standing theories premised on multistep causation through independent actors. The decision reinforces that courts will scrutinize redressability and causation, not merely the presence of a statutory “shall.”

This may steer litigants toward two paths. First, litigants will double down on targeting the withholding agency directly in FOIA suits, giving judges an opportunity to order production (and thereby, if the statutory triggers are present, to activate OSC’s FOIA‑specific enforcement duty). Second, plaintiffs may craft finer factual allegations demonstrating closer causation—e.g., showing that the enforcement agency’s refusal to investigate itself constituted a particularized, concrete injury (although Heckler and Summers still constrain such arguments).

Congress may react as well. If Congress wants to facilitate third‑party enforcement triggers—e.g., to empower citizens to enforce noncompliance through standing—it can do so by creating clearer statutory entitlements to information or making certain investigatory steps nondiscretionary and tightly tied to identifiable private interests. Absent such changes, plaintiffs must navigate Article III’s tightening boundaries.

## Practical takeaways for litigants and counsel
If your goal is to obtain documents or specific relief against a withholding agency, the safest route is to press the FOIA claim directly against the agency that controls the records and, where feasible, obtain a judicial ruling that can produce immediate, binding relief. Avoid construing a statutory “shall” directed at an independent enforcer as a shortcut past Article III unless you can show a concrete, particularized injury caused by that enforcer and a remedy that would bind the party causing the harm.

If you must involve an enforcement agency, allege facts that reduce the speculative hops: show that the enforcement agency’s action (or inaction) itself caused a discrete, cognizable injury, or that a judicial order would change a legal obligation that directly controls the injurious behavior. Otherwise, expect courts to treat multi‑actor causal stories as jurisdictionally inadequate.

## Bottom line
AFL v. Greer underscores a central lesson of contemporary standing doctrine: pleading a chain of hopeful consequences through separate, independent public actors is a poor substitute for pleading a concrete injury traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by a judicial order. For FOIA practitioners and public‑interest litigators seeking systemic enforcement, the case presses a practical question: if the outcome you want depends on persuading other bodies to act, make sure either the defendant is the right target or Congress has supplied an entitlement that gives you a concrete, legally cognizable stake.
