Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 4: # Second Standing Theory: Informational Injury From OSC Investigation Notices

This section analyzes AFL’s second theory of Article III standing: that OSC’s refusal to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 deprived AFL of information that the statute entitles it to receive—principally the notices and updates that accompany an OSC investigation under § 1214—and that this informational deprivation is a concrete injury sufficient to confer standing. The court rejected that theory for two linked reasons: (1) AFL identified no statutory entitlement to receive investigation-related information from OSC in the §1216 context, and (2) even if OSC were ordered to investigate, any judicial relief would not be likely to require OSC to provide the §1214-style notices that AFL says it lost.

## AFL’s claim: entitlement to investigation-related information

AFL’s argument rests on a factual and statutory linkage. It points to §1214, which governs OSC’s corrective-action procedures and includes certain notice and progress-reporting obligations to a complaining party, and to §1216(a)(3), which requires OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” AFL’s theory is straightforward: because §1216 mandates OSC investigations of alleged FOIA-withholdings, OSC’s refusal to investigate under §1216 deprived AFL of the notices and information that OSC would have provided if it had been conducting a §1214-style investigation. In other words, AFL claims an informational injury arising from not receiving investigation-related notices and updates.

## Informational-standing law: entitlement to information under a statute

The court framed this claim under established informational‑standing principles. A plaintiff can have a concrete informational injury when a statute has created a right to receive particular information and the defendant has denied that information. See, e.g., FEC v. Akins, 524 U.S. 11 (1998); Public Citizen v. Department of Justice, 491 U.S. 440 (1989); Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., 659 F.3d 13 (D.C. Cir. 2011). The key point from these precedents is that denial of information is actionable only when the statute actually entitles the plaintiff to that information; a bare procedural entitlement unconnected to any concrete interest—or a denial of a procedure “in vacuo”—does not satisfy the injury‑in‑fact requirement. See Summers v. Earth Island Inst., 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009) (procedural right without a concrete interest is insufficient).

## No statutory entitlement under §1216 to receive §1214-style notices

Applying that rule, the court asked whether §1216 (even assuming it mandated an investigation) created an entitlement to receive the §1214-style notices and updates AFL seeks. The court concluded that it did not. Section 1216(a)(3) directs OSC to “conduct an investigation” of alleged FOIA withholdings, but the statute does not itself promise complainants the §1214 notice protections; and AFL did not argue that §1216 independently contains affirmative disclosure or notice obligations of the type recognized in Akins or Public Citizen. AFL’s attempt to bootstrap a notice entitlement rested on the possibility that, if OSC conducted a §1216 investigation, it might choose to follow §1214’s procedures and thereby provide AFL the notices it seeks. But that is precisely speculation about what the agency might do, not a demonstration that a statute entitles AFL to receive particular information.

## Discretionary nature of applying §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations (Carson)

The court reinforced this statutory point by relying on precedent recognizing OSC’s discretion in applying §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations. The Sixth Circuit in Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), held that OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214’s procedural requirements when investigating under §1216 is discretionary. AFL itself acknowledged that line of cases. That acknowledgment is important: it undercuts AFL’s central premise that a §1216 investigation would automatically carry with it a statutory entitlement to §1214-style notices. If the decision to apply §1214 procedures in a §1216 investigation is discretionary, there is no statutory command creating a right in complainants to receive those procedures.

## No concrete injury: absence of a legal right defeats informational standing

Because §1216 does not itself create a right to receive the §1214 notice regime, the court found no statutory entitlement to the information AFL claims it was denied. Under the informational‑standing precedents, that absence of a legal entitlement means AFL suffered no cognizable informational injury from OSC’s refusal to investigate. The injury AFL describes is the loss of information that might have been provided if OSC had chosen—as a matter of discretion—to follow §1214 procedures; such an injury is not the denial of a statute‑created informational right, but the denial of a discretionary benefit that the statute does not compel.

## Redressability failure: an order to investigate would not guarantee §1214-style notices

The court also tied the lack of entitlement to a redressability problem. Even assuming a court could order OSC to investigate under §1216, such an order would not force OSC to adopt §1214’s notice provisions in that §1216 investigation because the application of §1214 procedures in §1216 matters is discretionary. Thus a favorable judicial ruling mandating investigation would not be “reasonably likely” to produce the notification and information AFL claims it lost. Under settled standing doctrine, a plaintiff must show that a favorable decision would likely redress the alleged injury; where the remedy would leave the agency free to withhold the very information the plaintiff seeks, redressability is lacking.

## Conclusion

The court therefore rejected AFL’s second standing theory: informational standing requires that the plaintiff be entitled by statute to the information allegedly withheld. AFL identified no such entitlement in §1216, and it acknowledged that §1214 notice procedures are discretionary in §1216 investigations. Because there was no legal right to the notices AFL claims it was denied—and because an order compelling an investigation would not reliably produce those notices—the court concluded AFL suffered no concrete, redressable informational injury and thus lacked Article III standing on this theory.
