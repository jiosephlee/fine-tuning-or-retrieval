Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does Heckler v. Chaney effectively swallow § 1216’s mandatory ‘shall investigate’ language for reviewability purposes?
Question:
The opinion leans on Chaney to highlight the judiciary’s inability to compel agency enforcement choices and uses that to show redressability is speculative. But § 1216(a)(3) says OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of allegations about arbitrary FOIA withholdings. When, if ever, is a ‘shall investigate’ command judicially enforceable post-Chaney? Is the idea that the initial decision to open an investigation might be reviewable if truly mandatory, but choices about seeking corrective/disciplinary action are not—or does Chaney’s presumption extend even to the threshold investigation decision when the statute lacks complainant-facing rights and standards?
Answer:
Short answer
No—Heckler v. Chaney does not automatically nullify every statutory “shall … conduct an investigation” command. But America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer illustrates how Chaney’s presumption against judicially compelling enforcement powers will defeat many challenges unless the statute (1) creates a concrete, complainant‑facing entitlement or (2) imposes a nondiscretionary, judicially manageable duty whose performance would itself redress the plaintiff’s injury. In AFLF the court relied on Chaney plus redressability principles to reject review because §1216 did not create an informational entitlement and any relief would depend on speculative choices by multiple independent actors.

Why the D.C. Circuit resolved AFLF the way it did
- Chaney’s baseline: The opinion reiterates Chaney’s core point—that courts generally may not compel agency enforcement decisions and that speculation about how an agency might exercise enforcement discretion is an insufficient basis for Article III relief (citing Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985)). The panel used that rule to evaluate whether a court order compelling OSC to investigate would actually redress AFL’s claimed injuries.

- Standing/redressability was fatal here: Rather than decide whether §1216 is on its face reviewable, the court held AFL lacked Article III standing because any causal chain from an OSC investigation to production of the requested FOIA records required multiple discretionary steps by independent actors (OSC choosing corrective/disciplinary proceedings, the MSPB imposing sanctions, DOJ deciding to produce records). Because redressability “generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors,” the court found a favorable judgment unlikely to “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s asserted informational injury (citing FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Haaland v. Brackeen).

- Informational‑entitlement problem: The court also emphasized the separate doctrine that an informational injury can support standing only when a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive information (citing Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Akins, Zivotofsky). §1216(a)(3) commands OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but it does not—according to the panel—create a complainant’s entitlement to receive information about or flowing from such an investigation. And OSC need not apply §1214’s notice rules when it acts under §1216 (the opinion cites Carson on that point). That lack of a statutory entitlement further undermined AFL’s ability to show a redressable informational injury.

What this means for “shall investigate” post‑Chaney
- Where “shall investigate” will be enforceable: The opinion points to (and rests its analysis upon) two essential features a plaintiff must show to overcome Chaney’s presumption and redressability hurdles: (1) the statute must create a concrete, plaintiff‑facing right or entitlement to information or relief such that an agency’s adherence to the “shall” would by itself redress the plaintiff’s injury; or (2) the statutory command must be sufficiently mandatory and legally manageable that a court can order the agency to act without relying on further discretionary enforcement steps by other actors. If those features are present, Chaney does not automatically preclude judicial enforcement.

- Where Chaney will block enforcement: When the “shall” is not tied to a complainant‑facing entitlement or when compliance would merely set in motion discretionary enforcement and adjudicative processes (as here, involving OSC’s exercise of prosecutorial tools, MSPB action, and then agency choices), Chaney’s presumption and redressability doctrine will normally prevent a court from ordering relief. That is exactly the posture in AFLF: the panel did not hold §1216 never requires OSC to investigate, but it concluded AFL could not show that a judicial order compelling investigation would likely redress its claimed informational harms.

A final caution from the opinion
The court disposed on standing and did not definitively decide whether §1216 is judicially reviewable as a matter of administrative‑law doctrine. Its reasoning, however, makes clear the practical point: a bare “shall investigate” will be hard to enforce when (a) the statute does not create an individualized entitlement to the information or procedures the plaintiff seeks and (b) meaningful relief would depend on later discretionary acts by independent actors. In such circumstances, Chaney’s presumption—combined with redressability rules—will usually be dispositive.
