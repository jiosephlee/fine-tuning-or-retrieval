Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Is there any scenario where a FOIA requester would have standing to compel OSC action under § 1216?
Question:
The panel emphasizes problems of traceability and redressability and the lack of a statutory entitlement to information from OSC. Hypothetically, could a requester ever show standing to force a § 1216 investigation—for example, if OSC’s own regulations promised complainant updates, or if the requester could allege facts making MSPB or agency action sufficiently likely? Or is Chaney’s enforcement-discretion presumption and the lack of a complainant-facing entitlement under § 1216 a categorical bar?
Answer:
Short answer: Not categorically impossible, but extremely unlikely under the legal rules the panel applied. America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer makes clear that a FOIA requester lacks standing to force OSC to investigate unless the plaintiff can show (1) a concrete informational injury traceable to OSC’s refusal and (2) that a favorable judgment would very likely redress that injury—facts the court found lacking in AFL’s case. The opinion therefore leaves only narrow, fact‑specific pathways to standing.

Why AFL lost (doctrinal roadmap from the opinion)
- Article III standing requires injury in fact, causation, and redressability (Lujan). The court applied the relaxed framework for procedural‑right suits but still required a concrete, particularized interest affected by the procedural deprivation (summarized at II).  
- Informational standing exists only when the plaintiff is denied information to which a statute entitles him (Feld Ent., Akins, Pub. Citizen). The opinion rejects an “informational injury in vacuo.”  
- Causation/redressability failed because any FOIA non‑production was traceable to DOJ (not OSC) and relief would depend on a speculative chain: OSC might investigate → OSC might seek corrective/disciplinary action → MSPB or DOJ might act → DOJ might produce documents. The court stressed that causation cannot rest on speculation about independent actors and that courts generally may not compel an agency’s enforcement discretion (citing Heckler v. Chaney). The opinion also emphasized redressability turns on whether the judgment would bind the defendant, not merely persuade third parties (II).

Practical scenarios where standing might exist (consistent with the opinion)
1) Statutory or regulatory complainant entitlement to information or a non‑discretionary OSC duty. The opinion notes FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which imposes an OSC duty when three predicates are met (court orders production, fees awarded, and a court questions arbitrariness). When statutory language creates a mandatory OSC duty and the statutory predicates are satisfied, denial of the mandated OSC action would be a concrete deprivation of a statutory entitlement and would be more plausibly redressable. In short: standing is more likely where the statute itself creates a mandatory, complainant‑facing right that a court order would enforce.  
2) OSC regulation creating a complainant‑facing entitlement. The court relied on the principle that an informational injury requires a statutory entitlement; that same logic applies to binding agency regulations. If OSC adopted (and legally had) a regulation that affirmatively and non‑discretionarily guaranteed complainants specific notices or investigation outcomes in § 1216 matters, a denial of those promised notices could supply the required concrete injury and the refusal to comply would be directly redressable against OSC. The opinion references § 1214’s notice provisions and the discretionary question whether § 1214’s procedures apply to § 1216 investigations (citing Carson), which suggests the importance of whether any procedural entitlement is mandatory rather than discretionary.  
3) A case where judicial relief would bind OSC (not merely persuade third parties) and produce the records without speculative links. The opinion rejects standing when redress depends on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” By contrast, if a plaintiff could show that a declaratory or injunctive judgment would directly require OSC to take a non‑discretionary step that itself produced the requested information (or required an agency party the court can bind to produce the records), redressability would be stronger.

Why these windows are narrow
- The court explicitly relied on Heckler/Chaney’s teaching that enforcement‑discretion decisions are presumptively non‑reviewable and that courts generally may not compel an agency to exercise enforcement powers. When relief requires forcing OSC to exercise enforcement discretion (e.g., to initiate a disciplinary or MSPB action that might in turn prompt document production), standing will be hard to establish.  
- The opinion finds AFL’s theories failed because they required multiple speculative intervening choices by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ—exactly the kind of attenuated causal chain the court disapproved.

Bottom line
A plaintiff can sometimes have standing to compel OSC under § 1216, but only in narrow situations the panel recognized as different from AFL’s facts: where there is a clear statutory or regulatory entitlement to information or a non‑discretionary OSC duty (or where a court’s ruling would directly bind the defendant and “very likely spur” redress), rather than only a hope that OSC, MSPB, or the producing agency will act. Absent such a complainant‑facing entitlement or a judgment that directly binds the relevant actor, Chaney’s presumption of enforcement discretion plus the causation/redressability principles the court applied will foreclose standing.
