Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How exactly do causation and redressability fail when the requested relief is just an order that OSC investigate?
Question:
The opinion describes a speculative causal chain: if OSC investigated, it might pursue corrective/disciplinary action; the MSPB might sanction; DOJ might change course; and thus AFL might get records. In procedural-injury cases courts relax imminence and redressability somewhat. Why didn’t that relaxed standard suffice here? Are there examples where a court found standing to compel a preliminary procedural step even though ultimate relief depended on independent actors? What distinguishes those cases from this one?
Answer:
Short answer: Because AFL’s injury depended on a highly speculative chain of discretionary acts by multiple independent actors, the relaxed standing standard for procedural injuries could not bridge the fatal causation and redressability gaps the court identified. The opinion rests on three linked points: (1) the alleged informational harm is traceable to DOJ (not OSC), (2) any pathway from an OSC investigation to receipt of records requires multiple discretionary choices by other agencies (OSC’s enforcement choices, the MSPB’s sanctions, DOJ’s production), and (3) Congress did not create an entitlement to the investigative “fruits” that would give AFL an informational right enforceable against OSC.

Why the relaxed “procedural-injury” rule didn’t save AFL
- Governing law. Article III requires injury-in-fact that is caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable judgment. Lujan. Courts do “relax” imminence and redressability in some procedural-right cases—but not the requirements of injury-in-fact or causation. The opinion cites Center for Law & Educ. and Fla. Audubon for that limited relaxation, and Summers for the principle that a procedural right alone (a “procedural right in vacuo”) is not enough.
- Traceability. The court held AFL’s informational injury from DOJ’s withholding is “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.” A plaintiff cannot bootstrap standing against one agency by pointing to a separate agency’s alleged wrongdoing unless the complaint shows a direct causal link.
- Speculative causal chain and independent actors. The opinion lays out the chain AFL would need: OSC investigates → OSC pursues corrective/disciplinary action → MSPB sanctions employees → DOJ produces records. The court rejected that as “too speculative and attenuated,” invoking the general rule that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (citing All. for Hippocratic Med.) and Heckler v. Chaney for the principle that courts generally may not compel discretionary enforcement decisions.
- Informational-entitlement failure. For an “informational” injury to support standing, the plaintiff must be denied information to which a statute entitles it (Akins, Pub. Citizen, ASPCA). The court found no statutory entitlement here: even if section 1216 requires OSC to investigate, it does not itself create a right to receive the investigatory notices or reports; OSC’s obligation to follow section 1214’s procedural-notice regime in a section 1216 probe is discretionary (Carson), so AFL cannot claim a statutory informational entitlement that a judicial order would vindicate.

When courts do find standing to compel a preliminary step (and why those cases differ)
- The opinion points to the class of procedural-right decisions where courts relax imminence/redressability because the statute’s procedure protects a concrete interest. In those cases (see Center for Law & Educ., Fla. Audubon), the procedural right either: (a) is closely tailored to protect a concrete, particularized interest the plaintiff alleges has been harmed, or (b) creates a clear entitlement to information or process such that ordering the preliminary step would itself redress the injury.  
- By contrast, AFL’s claim lacks those features: there is no statutory entitlement to the investigatory information it wants, the injury is traceable to another agency (DOJ), and any remedy would rely on discretionary actions by OSC and independent agencies (MSPB, DOJ). Those distinctions are precisely why the court rejected standing here.

Bottom line
The relaxed standard for procedural injuries only helps where the procedural violation is tied to a concrete, particularized interest or where the statute creates an entitlement whose enforcement would itself redress the injury. Here, the causal path from an OSC investigatory order to AFL’s receipt of records is too speculative (dependent on discretionary acts by multiple independent actors), and there is no statutory entitlement to the investigatory outputs AFL seeks—so causation and redressability fail.
