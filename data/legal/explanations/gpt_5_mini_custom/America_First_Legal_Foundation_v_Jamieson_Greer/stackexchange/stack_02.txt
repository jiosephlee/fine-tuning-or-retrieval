Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why doesn’t informational standing attach to the notices and updates AFL hoped to receive if OSC investigated under § 1216?
Question:
The court says informational standing requires a statute that entitles the plaintiff to receive information, and concludes § 1216 creates no such entitlement. AFL argued that if OSC investigated under § 1216, it might choose to provide the notifications that § 1214 requires in prohibited personnel practice cases. Given § 1216(c) says OSC may investigate and seek corrective action “under § 1214 … in the same way,” why doesn’t that incorporate § 1214’s notice requirements? Is Carson v. OSC controlling nationwide that § 1214’s procedures are discretionary in § 1216 investigations, and if so, what statutory text makes those notices optional rather than mandatory?
Answer:
Short answer
Informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the information. The D.C. Circuit held that §1216 does not create any such entitlement to the notices and updates that §1214 provides. That is why AFL’s claimed “informational” injury fails: AFL conceded and the court treated as correct that OSC’s decision whether to apply §1214’s procedural rules in a §1216 investigation is discretionary (see Carson), and §1216’s text does not convert those §1214 procedures into a statutory right to receive notice.

Why (doctrinally) informational standing fails here
The court rests its ruling on the settled principle that a plaintiff can obtain standing based on denial of information only when a statute “entitle[s] him to receive” that information (Zivotofsky/Akins/Pub. Citizen line of cases). The opinion repeats that rule (citing American Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc., and other authorities) and then asks whether the statutes at issue actually create an entitlement to receive the §1214-style notices AFL wanted. It concludes they do not. Without a statutory entitlement to the information, there is no concrete informational injury for Article III purposes.

What the opinion says about §1214 and §1216
- §1214 contains procedural protections for complaining parties in forbidden-personnel-practice cases, including specific notice provisions (see 5 U.S.C. §1214(a)(1)(B)–(D)). The opinion acknowledges those notice provisions exist in §1214.
- §1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings; §1216(c) states OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The court treated that language as permissive in effect for purposes of creating an entitlement to information.
- Crucially, the court observed that AFL did not claim §1216 itself requires OSC to “engraft” §1214’s notice regime onto every §1216 investigation; AFL instead accepted the line of cases that treats OSC’s choice whether to apply §1214’s procedures in a §1216 matter as discretionary (the court cites Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011), as an example). Given that concession and the statutory text, the court concluded §1216 creates no entitlement to receive §1214-style notices. Because no entitlement exists, AFL lacks informational standing.

Is Carson “controlling nationwide”?
No—Carson is a Sixth Circuit decision, so it is not binding outside that circuit. The D.C. Circuit opinion does not treat Carson as binding nationwide either; it cites Carson as one of the cases recognizing that OSC’s application of §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations has been treated as discretionary, and it notes AFL’s concession that such cases exist. The D.C. Circuit’s own holding here reaches the same practical result (no entitlement, no informational standing), but that is the D.C. Circuit’s holding, not Sixth Circuit precedent imposed nationwide.

What in the statute makes the notices optional rather than mandatory?
Two textual points the opinion relies on (either expressly or by reasonable implication) support the conclusion that §1214 notice procedures are not an automatic entitlement in §1216 investigations:
1. §1216(c) uses permissive language (“may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way …”), which authorizes OSC to use §1214 procedures but does not itself command that OSC must do so in every §1216 investigation. The permissive “may” is the primary textual hook for optionality.
2. Congress elsewhere imposed a clear, mandatory OSC duty when it intended one: FOIA’s separate enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. §552(a)(4)(F)(i), says OSC “shall promptly initiate” a proceeding if a court orders production, awards fees, and questions arbitrary withholding. The opinion highlights that Congress knows how to make OSC’s duties mandatory when it wants to; the absence of a parallel mandatory command in §1216 supports the view that §1214 procedures are not automatically entitling outsiders to notices in §1216 investigations.

Bottom line
Informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the information. The D.C. Circuit concluded §1216 does not create such an entitlement to §1214-style notices: §1216(c) authorizes OSC to apply §1214 procedures but does not compel OSC to do so, AFL acknowledged precedent treating that choice as discretionary (Carson), and FOIA contains other, explicit mandatory commands where Congress intended them. Because there was no statutory right to the notices, AFL lacked the concrete informational injury needed for Article III standing.
