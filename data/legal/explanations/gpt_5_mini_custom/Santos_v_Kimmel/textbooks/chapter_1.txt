Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and platform: George Santos; Jimmy Kimmel; ABC and Disney; Cameo and JKL

This case arises from a dispute between former Congressman George Santos (the plaintiff) and several media-related defendants: Jimmy Kimmel (a late-night television host), American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (ABC, which airs Jimmy Kimmel Live!), and The Walt Disney Company (ABC’s parent). The contested interactions occurred through Cameo, an online platform that permits users to pay for personalized video messages from public figures. Kimmel used Cameo to obtain videos from Santos and then incorporated those videos into segments on his television program, Jimmy Kimmel Live! (JKL).

## Factual setup: Kimmel used fictitious names to order Santos Cameo videos; aired them in JKL segments titled “Will Santos Say It?”

According to the complaint (and the exhibits the parties submitted), Kimmel—using a series of fictitious names—placed multiple requests on Cameo for personalized videos from Santos. Santos accepted and produced the requested clips. Kimmel then broadcast those clips on JKL as part of a recurring, mocking segment labeled “Will Santos Say It?” The gist of Santos’s allegation is that the JKL broadcasts transformed the videos into satirical criticism and, in doing so, infringed Santos’s asserted copyrights and injured him under various state-law theories.

The operative factual record for the pleading-stage analysis included the complaint’s allegations, the Cameo terms (as relevant to the contract-related claims), the videos themselves (as exhibits or incorporated material), and the copyright registrations for the videos—which the courts treated as proper subjects of judicial notice.

## Claims asserted: federal copyright infringement; state claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement

Santos brought a federal copyright infringement action asserting that he owned copyrights in the Cameo videos and that Kimmel (with ABC and Disney) unlawfully used those videos on JKL. He also asserted three state-law claims: breach of contract (alleging violation of Cameo’s Terms of Service), breach of an implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The complaint sought relief against Kimmel and the corporate defendants for their role in ordering, obtaining, and broadcasting the videos.

## District court ruling: dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6); fair use bars copyright claims; state claims preempted or otherwise deficient

The Southern District of New York dismissed the entire complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim. As to the copyright counts, the District Court concluded that the defendants’ use of the videos on JKL was protected by the affirmative defense of fair use. The court found the broadcasts were transformative commentary/criticism and that the remaining statutory fair-use factors did not weigh against the defense. For the state-law claims, the District Court held that some contract-based claims were preempted by the federal Copyright Act and that the others, in any event, were deficiently pleaded (for example, failing to allege essential terms or an adequate showing of out-of-pocket loss for the fraudulent inducement claim). The District Court therefore entered final judgment dismissing all claims.

On appeal, the Second Circuit affirmed. The appellate court agreed that fair use barred the copyright claims and concluded independently that each state-law claim failed to state a viable cause of action under governing state-law standards (so it did not need to resolve preemption as to all claims).

## Standard of review: de novo review of dismissal

The Second Circuit reviewed the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal de novo. That is the usual standard for appellate review of a dismissal for failure to state a claim: the appellate court reexamines whether the complaint, taken in the light most favorable to the plaintiff and accepting well-pleaded factual allegations as true, adequately alleges a plausible claim for relief. The court applied governing Second Circuit authority to determine whether dismissal was appropriate at the pleading stage for each asserted claim.

A particular wrinkle in this case is that fair use is an affirmative defense. Even so, the Second Circuit and other courts recognize that a fair-use defense may be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint and any materials properly before the court (including the works themselves and related registrations) make fair use plainly apparent. The court relied on that principle when affirming dismissal of the copyright claims.

## Materials considered on 12(b)(6): complaint, exhibits, incorporated materials, and judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations

In conducting its de novo review, the Second Circuit considered the complaint’s factual allegations and the documents attached to or incorporated by reference in the complaint. The opinion cites precedent allowing courts to consider such materials at the pleading stage without converting the motion into one for summary judgment. Importantly for a copyright case, the court also took judicial notice of the Copyright Office registrations for the Cameo videos as published in the Copyright Office’s public registry. Those registrations and the videos themselves were central to the fair-use analysis because they established key, dispositive facts—such as the content and publication status of the works—that allowed the court to evaluate the statutory fair-use factors on the face of the pleadings and accompanying materials.

Together, these procedural and evidentiary choices explain how the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal became the subject of de novo appellate review and why the Second Circuit could affirm dismissal based on fair use and deficiencies in the pleaded state-law claims without requiring further factual development.
