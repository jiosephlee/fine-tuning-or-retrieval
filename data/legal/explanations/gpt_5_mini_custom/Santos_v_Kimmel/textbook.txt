Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and platform: George Santos; Jimmy Kimmel; ABC and Disney; Cameo and JKL

This case arises from a dispute between former Congressman George Santos (the plaintiff) and several media-related defendants: Jimmy Kimmel (a late-night television host), American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (ABC, which airs Jimmy Kimmel Live!), and The Walt Disney Company (ABC’s parent). The contested interactions occurred through Cameo, an online platform that permits users to pay for personalized video messages from public figures. Kimmel used Cameo to obtain videos from Santos and then incorporated those videos into segments on his television program, Jimmy Kimmel Live! (JKL).

## Factual setup: Kimmel used fictitious names to order Santos Cameo videos; aired them in JKL segments titled “Will Santos Say It?”

According to the complaint (and the exhibits the parties submitted), Kimmel—using a series of fictitious names—placed multiple requests on Cameo for personalized videos from Santos. Santos accepted and produced the requested clips. Kimmel then broadcast those clips on JKL as part of a recurring, mocking segment labeled “Will Santos Say It?” The gist of Santos’s allegation is that the JKL broadcasts transformed the videos into satirical criticism and, in doing so, infringed Santos’s asserted copyrights and injured him under various state-law theories.

The operative factual record for the pleading-stage analysis included the complaint’s allegations, the Cameo terms (as relevant to the contract-related claims), the videos themselves (as exhibits or incorporated material), and the copyright registrations for the videos—which the courts treated as proper subjects of judicial notice.

## Claims asserted: federal copyright infringement; state claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement

Santos brought a federal copyright infringement action asserting that he owned copyrights in the Cameo videos and that Kimmel (with ABC and Disney) unlawfully used those videos on JKL. He also asserted three state-law claims: breach of contract (alleging violation of Cameo’s Terms of Service), breach of an implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The complaint sought relief against Kimmel and the corporate defendants for their role in ordering, obtaining, and broadcasting the videos.

## District court ruling: dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6); fair use bars copyright claims; state claims preempted or otherwise deficient

The Southern District of New York dismissed the entire complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim. As to the copyright counts, the District Court concluded that the defendants’ use of the videos on JKL was protected by the affirmative defense of fair use. The court found the broadcasts were transformative commentary/criticism and that the remaining statutory fair-use factors did not weigh against the defense. For the state-law claims, the District Court held that some contract-based claims were preempted by the federal Copyright Act and that the others, in any event, were deficiently pleaded (for example, failing to allege essential terms or an adequate showing of out-of-pocket loss for the fraudulent inducement claim). The District Court therefore entered final judgment dismissing all claims.

On appeal, the Second Circuit affirmed. The appellate court agreed that fair use barred the copyright claims and concluded independently that each state-law claim failed to state a viable cause of action under governing state-law standards (so it did not need to resolve preemption as to all claims).

## Standard of review: de novo review of dismissal

The Second Circuit reviewed the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal de novo. That is the usual standard for appellate review of a dismissal for failure to state a claim: the appellate court reexamines whether the complaint, taken in the light most favorable to the plaintiff and accepting well-pleaded factual allegations as true, adequately alleges a plausible claim for relief. The court applied governing Second Circuit authority to determine whether dismissal was appropriate at the pleading stage for each asserted claim.

A particular wrinkle in this case is that fair use is an affirmative defense. Even so, the Second Circuit and other courts recognize that a fair-use defense may be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint and any materials properly before the court (including the works themselves and related registrations) make fair use plainly apparent. The court relied on that principle when affirming dismissal of the copyright claims.

## Materials considered on 12(b)(6): complaint, exhibits, incorporated materials, and judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations

In conducting its de novo review, the Second Circuit considered the complaint’s factual allegations and the documents attached to or incorporated by reference in the complaint. The opinion cites precedent allowing courts to consider such materials at the pleading stage without converting the motion into one for summary judgment. Importantly for a copyright case, the court also took judicial notice of the Copyright Office registrations for the Cameo videos as published in the Copyright Office’s public registry. Those registrations and the videos themselves were central to the fair-use analysis because they established key, dispositive facts—such as the content and publication status of the works—that allowed the court to evaluate the statutory fair-use factors on the face of the pleadings and accompanying materials.

Together, these procedural and evidentiary choices explain how the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal became the subject of de novo appellate review and why the Second Circuit could affirm dismissal based on fair use and deficiencies in the pleaded state-law claims without requiring further factual development.

# Fair Use Framework and the Transformative Purpose Inquiry

This chapter explains how the Second Circuit applied the statutory fair use framework at the pleading stage in Santos v. Kimmel, why the court treated the transformative-purpose inquiry as central, and how those principles compelled dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims. The court’s analysis rested on statutory text, established precedent about transformative use and pleading-stage resolution, and the specific allegations and exhibits before the court (the Cameo videos and the JKL segments). The result is a compact example of when a defendant can win on fair use at Rule 12(b)(6).

## The fair use statute and its flexible application

The Copyright Act's fair use provision, 17 U.S.C. § 107, supplies a non‑exclusive list of four factors courts weigh: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use on the potential market for or value of the original. The statute is deliberately flexible: fair use is an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior” so courts can avoid rigid applications of the Act that would chill creativity. The Second Circuit in Santos invoked this statutory framework and emphasized that the factors are context‑sensitive—courts evaluate them together, not mechanically.

## When fair use can be resolved at the pleading stage

Although fair use is ordinarily a fact‑intensive inquiry, the Second Circuit has recognized that fair use can be so plainly established by the complaint (and by documents properly considered with the complaint) that dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) is appropriate. The court cited prior authority holding that when the alleged infringing work and the allegedly infringing use are before the court, the court may decide fair use without discovery. In Santos, the relevant original works (Santos’s Cameo videos) and the secondary works (the JKL segments) were attached to or incorporated in the complaint and thus properly before the court. Because the fair use question could be decided by comparing those works, the court concluded the defense could be resolved at the pleading stage.

## Transformative use: the central focus of the first fair use factor

The first factor—purpose and character of the use—centers on whether the secondary use is “transformative”: does it merely supersede the original, or does it add something new with a further purpose or different character? The Second Circuit quoted its own definition of “transformative” and emphasized that this inquiry is context dependent. Importantly, the court adopted the reasonable‑observer standard for determining the purpose of the use: what a neutral observer would perceive the secondary use to be doing, not the subjective intent of the creator or the secondary user. The opinion relied on recent Supreme Court and Second Circuit authority underscoring that subjective intent is not determinative of the purpose inquiry.

## Applying the transformative inquiry to Kimmel’s segments

Turning to the facts alleged, the District Court found—and Santos did not dispute—that the JKL segments used the videos for the purpose of satirical commentary: to lampoon “the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” The Second Circuit accepted that characterization and applied the reasonable‑observer standard: a viewer of the JKL segments would understand Kimmel’s use as critical, mocking commentary, not as a continuation of the original videos’ purported inspirational messaging. Santos’s own complaint described his videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” and a reasonable observer would so read them. The JKL segments altered the context and purpose of the clips—they were not presented to perform the same function as the originals but to expose and ridicule Santos’s conduct. On that ground the court treated the first factor as strongly favoring fair use.

## Rejecting the “instigator/designer” argument

Santos argued that Kimmel could not claim fair use because Kimmel allegedly “instigated the creation” of the videos (by submitting Cameo requests under false names) and thus was effectively the designer of their original purpose. The court rejected this line of attack because the transformative‑purpose inquiry depends on what a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use to be, not on the subjective thinking or supposed role of either party in creating the original. In short, even accepting Santos’s factual allegations that Kimmel initiated the transactions, that does not change the perceived purpose of the JKL segments as commentary—so it does not defeat a finding of transformative use.

## The limited role of bad faith after Harper & Row

Santos also relied on Harper & Row for the proposition that fair use presupposes good faith and that bad acts (false identities) should defeat the defense. The Second Circuit explained two points in response. First, the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from treating good faith as a categorical prerequisite to fair use. Second, and more importantly here, Santos’s complaint did not allege facts supporting a conclusion that defendants sought to “supplant” his market for the videos. Under the court’s controlling market‑harm analysis, the relevant question is whether the secondary use usurps the original’s market by being a competing substitute, not whether it merely criticizes or devalues the original. On the face of the complaint, the JKL segments were sarcastic criticism, not an attempt to replace or commercially exploit Santos’s videos. That lack of alleged market usurpation, together with the critical purpose, undermined the force of Santos’s bad‑faith argument.

## Brief note on the other fair use factors

The court addressed the remaining statutory factors succinctly. The opinion noted the copyright registrations and that the videos were published on the day they were created—facts relevant to the second factor (nature of the work) that do not favor heightened protection. As to the fourth factor (market effect), the court relied on settled law that critical or parodic works are unlikely to be in the same licensing market as the originals; a use that lampoons or criticizes typically does not serve as a market substitute for the original work. Thus the fourth factor did not counsel against fair use. The opinion did not find any pleaded facts that would tip these other factors in Santos’s favor.

## First‑factor bottom line and disposition on fair use

Weighing these points together, the Second Circuit concluded that the first fair use factor strongly favored Kimmel and that the other factors did not overcome that result. Because the complaint and the attached works themselves showed transformative critical use, the court affirmed dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6): fair use barred Santos’s copyright claims without need for discovery. The Santos opinion thus illustrates how, when the works and their uses are before the court, the transformative‑use inquiry—evaluated through the reasonable‑observer lens and informed by the market‑usurpation test—can permit dismissal on fair use at the pleading stage.

# The Remaining Fair Use Factors and Overall Balance

This section explains how the Second Circuit applied the three remaining fair use factors—the nature of the copyrighted works, the amount and substantiality of what was taken, and the effect of the use on the market—and why, taken together with the transformative purpose finding, those factors supported resolving fair use against Santos at the pleading stage and affirming dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6).

## Nature of the works: published status supports fair use

The second fair use factor asks whether the copyrighted work is closer to the core of copyright protection (e.g., unpublished, highly creative works receive greater weight against fair use) or is less protected (e.g., factual or already published works). The court relied on the complaint and the copyright registrations (which the court may judicially notice) to establish that Santos’s videos had been published the same day they were created. Under the governing law, that published status weakens the weight of the second factor favoring Santos. In short, unpublished works get special protection; once a work is published, that factor is less likely to tip the balance against a secondary use. Because the complaint itself established publication, the district court and the court of appeals treated factor two as not strongly favoring Santos.

## Amount and substantiality: challenges rejected

The third fair use factor evaluates both the quantitative amount and the qualitative importance of what was taken. Santos challenged the district court’s treatment of this factor, but the Second Circuit rejected those challenges on the pleadings. Two general principles are important to understand why the court was comfortable deciding this factor at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage. First, even substantial or complete copying can weigh toward fair use when the copying serves a different, transformative purpose; the analysis is not strictly mechanical. Second, the factor is judged in context: the necessity of using particular material for the secondary work’s critical or parodic purpose can lessen the weight of substantial copying against the secondary user. Here, the complaint itself described the purpose and content of both the original videos and the JKL segments; given the transformative commentary and the surrounding factual record the court found before it, Santos’s objections to the amount-and-substantiality analysis did not prevent dismissal.

## Market effect: focus on substitution/usurpation, not harm from criticism

The fourth factor—the effect of the secondary use on the potential market for the original—was decisive. The controlling inquiry is not whether the secondary work harms the original by criticizing or devaluing it, but whether the secondary work acts as a market substitute that usurps the original’s economic market. The court emphasized this point by quoting and applying recent precedent: a lampoon, parody, or critical use that makes the original less appealing to some consumers does not automatically produce a cognizable market harm under factor four unless it competes as a functional substitute for the original.

Applying that principle, the court concluded on the face of the complaint that Santos had not plausibly alleged the kind of market usurpation required to defeat fair use. Santos did not, and could not, allege a realistic licensing market in which purchasers would pay for the right to have their videos rebroadcast on a late-night show whose purpose is sarcastic commentary. The complaint itself suggested defendants used the videos to mock Santos; it did not suggest defendants intended to supplant any commercial exploitation of the videos by Santos. Because the alleged secondary use was critical and non-substitutive in nature, factor four favored fair use.

## Licensing markets and the improbability of a licensing demand for criticism

The court drew on an established doctrinal point first articulated in Campbell: courts ask whether the secondary use would reasonably serve as a licensing opportunity that the copyright holder could exploit. For imaginative works, the court observed, it is “unlikely that creators...will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions.” That unlikelihood removes such critical uses from the category of predictable licensing markets that factor four protects. Applying this principle, the court found that Santos failed to identify a plausible licensing market that Kimmel’s segments could have usurped; thus the market-effect factor did not weigh against fair use.

## Scope of the record: only the original videos and the JKL segments were needed

Because fair use is a fact-sensitive inquiry, courts sometimes say it cannot be resolved at the pleading stage. But the Second Circuit reiterated that dismissal on Rule 12(b)(6) is appropriate when the complaint and the works themselves make fair use “so clearly established” that no plausible claim of infringement survives. Citing Cariou, the court explained that when the only materials necessary to evaluate fair use are the original works and the allegedly infringing works—and those materials are before the court—fair use can be resolved as a matter of law at the pleading stage. That was the situation here: the complaint incorporated or attached the videos and the JKL segments (and the registrations were judicially noticed), so the court had the relevant record to decide fair use without proceeding to discovery.

## Overall conclusion: fair use established on the face of the complaint; copyright claims dismissed

Putting the factors together, the court found a strongly transformative purpose (commentary/criticism) and concluded that the published status of the works, the context of the copying, and the absence of any plausible market usurpation all supported fair use. Because those conclusions were supported by the complaint, the attached works, and the publicly available registrations, the Second Circuit held that fair use was established as a matter of law and affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims under Rule 12(b)(6). The upshot for readers learning to read opinions is this: when a complaint itself (together with the copyrighted and allegedly infringing works) shows that the statutory fair-use factors point decisively toward fair use—particularly where the fourth-factor market-usurpation inquiry fails—the affirmative defense can be resolved on the pleadings and justify dismissal without discovery.

# Contract-Based State Claims: Terms of Service and Implied Agreement

This chapter explains why the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of George Santos’s contract-based state-law claims even though the District Court had relied on preemption. The panel did not decide whether the Copyright Act preempted Santos’s contract theories; instead, it “need[ed] not resolve” preemption because both the direct breach and implied-contract theories fail to state a claim under the governing state-law standards. Understanding that procedural posture—affirmance on Rule 12(b)(6) grounds rather than on preemption—frames the court’s substantive analysis of the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS) and the alleged implied-in-fact agreement.

Preemption posture: affirmance on failure-to-state-a-claim grounds without resolving preemption
The District Court dismissed Santos’s breach-of-contract and breach-of-implied-contract claims as preempted by the Copyright Act. On appeal, Santos argued the claims were “qualitatively different” and therefore not preempted. The Second Circuit avoided that broader statutory-preemption question because it concluded the state-law claims independently failed. That choice is important technique: if a federal appeals court can resolve the case on an adequate state-law ground pleaded in the complaint, it may affirm without addressing preemption. Here, the panel applied the Rule 12(b)(6) standard de novo and examined whether Santos pleaded the necessary state-law elements with nonconclusory allegations. Because he did not, the court affirmed regardless of preemption.

Breach of Cameo Terms of Service: Santos was not a party to the TOS users accept
Santos’s first contract theory rested on the Cameo Terms of Service—that Kimmel, by using Cameo, had agreed to terms that Santos could enforce. The critical threshold question was contractual privity: who are the contracting parties, and did the contract create enforceable rights for nonparties? The court treated the TOS as an agreement between users (e.g., Kimmel) and Cameo (the platform). Santos was not a signatory; therefore his ability to enforce provisions of the TOS depends on whether the law recognizes him as a third-party beneficiary.

Illinois third-party-beneficiary law: strong presumption against conferring enforceable rights on nonparties
The TOS was governed by Illinois law. Under Illinois law, there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” The implication that a contract should be enforceable by a third party must be “so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” In other words, Illinois requires either express third-party-beneficiary language or facts that are essentially the same as an express declaration that the parties intended to confer enforceable rights on the nonparty.

Absence of express third-party-beneficiary language for the provisions Santos invokes; contrast with other TOS provisions that do include such language
Applying that rule, the court asked whether the particular TOS provisions Santos invoked contain language showing an intent to benefit and to permit enforcement by third parties. They did not. The opinion emphasizes that the TOS actually contains some provisions with explicit third-party-beneficiary language elsewhere—demonstrating that when Cameo intended third-party enforcement, it said so expressly. That textual contrast undercuts any inference that the drafters implicitly intended the provisions Santos relies on to be enforceable by outsiders. Because Santos pointed to no “express declaration” or equivalent textual manifestation, the Illinois presumption against third-party enforcement was fatal to his direct TOS claim.

Implied-in-fact contract under New York law: need to plead essential terms and a meeting of the minds
Santos’s alternate theory was that the defendants (Kimmel or the broadcasters) entered into an implied-in-fact contract with him—essentially an agreement outside the TOS by which a defendant undertook obligations to Santos. The court applied New York law to that claim. Under New York law, an implied-in-fact contract requires the same core elements as any contract: offer, acceptance, consideration, and a meeting of the minds about essential terms. Crucially at the pleading stage, a claimant must allege the “essential terms” of the putative agreement in nonconclusory language and allege facts supporting a meeting of the minds.

Pleading defects: no nonconclusory essential terms; no plausible allegation that Kimmel agreed to adhere to the TOS or otherwise undertook definite obligations to Santos
The Second Circuit found multiple pleading defects in Santos’s complaint. First, he did not identify the essential terms of any alleged implied contract with the defendants; the complaint lacked nonconclusory allegations spelling out what the defendants promised, what Santos promised in return, and how those promises were to be performed. The court relied on precedent requiring that the complaint specify “those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated” rather than asserting legal conclusions. Second, Santos failed to plausibly allege a meeting of the minds—there were no factual allegations showing the defendants agreed to adhere to the Cameo TOS with respect to Santos or otherwise undertook unequivocal, definite obligations to him. The complaint’s assertions that defendants “should have” or “were required” to follow certain rules were insufficient to show an agreement between the parties; New York authority requires factual allegations that demonstrate mutual assent.

Why these defects are dispositive
Because the TOS claim fails for lack of third-party-beneficiary status under Illinois law, and because the implied-in-fact contract claim fails for lack of pleaded essential terms and mutual assent under New York law, Santos’s contract-based claims do not survive a Rule 12(b)(6) challenge even without reaching preemption. The appellate court therefore affirmed dismissal of those claims. Practically, this result shows two things for litigants: (1) suing on a platform’s TOS is difficult unless you are within the class of intended beneficiaries or the text unmistakably says third parties may enforce it; and (2) pleading an implied contract requires concrete allegations of the agreement’s essential terms and facts demonstrating a meeting of the minds, not bare legal conclusions.

Conclusion
The Second Circuit’s analysis stayed tightly within the complaint and controlling state-law standards. By enforcing Illinois’s strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries and New York’s requirement that contract claims plead essential terms and mutual assent with factual specificity, the court found Santos’s contract theories legally insufficient—providing an independent ground for affirming dismissal and obviating the need to resolve whether federal copyright law would also preempt the claims.

# Fraudulent Inducement and Disposition

## New York fraud damages: out-of-pocket rule requires actual pecuniary loss

Under New York law, a plaintiff bringing a common-law fraudulent inducement claim must plead and prove actual pecuniary loss caused by the defendant’s misrepresentation. The Second Circuit in Santos v. Kimmel applied the familiar five-element framework for fraud (a false representation of a material fact, knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance, and damages) and emphasized the damages element. Citing Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney and Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, the court reiterated that “damages” for fraud requires an out‑of‑pocket loss: the plaintiff must allege that he suffered a concrete, economic detriment as a result of relying on the misrepresentation. New York does not treat speculative harms, emotional distress, or generalized reputational injury as satisfying this element absent special circumstances that give rise to compensable economic loss.

Understanding this rule matters for pleading. To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must do more than assert that a defendant lied and that the plaintiff was deceived; it must allege what money or measurable economic value the plaintiff lost because of that deception and how the loss flowed from the defendant’s conduct.

## Application: Santos alleged no actual out-of-pocket loss from the alleged deception

Applying the out‑of‑pocket requirement, the court focused on Santos’s complaint and found it did not allege any actual pecuniary loss resulting from Kimmel’s use of false names on Cameo. Although the complaint describes how the Cameo requests were allegedly procured under false pretenses and how the videos were later broadcast on Jimmy Kimmel Live!, it contains no nonconclusory allegation that Santos suffered a monetary loss—no outlays, no missed licensing opportunities, no diminished sale price, nor other quantifiable economic harm tied causally to the deceit.

Because pleading the element of damages is necessary for a fraud claim under New York law, Santos’s failure to allege an out‑of‑pocket loss was fatal. The Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s ruling that the fraudulent inducement claim therefore could not stand.

## Result: fraudulent inducement claim dismissed

For the reasons just described, the fraudulent inducement count was dismissed for failure to state a claim. The dismissal was based squarely on the pleading defect as to damages under New York law, not on any broader ruling about the truth or falsity of the alleged misrepresentations or about defendants’ motives.

## Final disposition: judgment affirming dismissal of all claims

The fraud ruling was one piece of the Second Circuit’s larger disposition. The court affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims on fair‑use grounds and concluded each of the state‑law claims failed to state a claim (the contract claims either being implausible or preempted and the fraud claim lacking damages). The Second Circuit therefore affirmed the District Court’s final judgment dismissing the complaint in its entirety.

## Practical takeaways

- Under New York law, fraud requires concrete pecuniary (out‑of‑pocket) damages. Pleadings must allege a specific, causal economic loss, not only deception or reputational harm.
- A complaint that fails to plead out‑of‑pocket loss will be dismissed at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage for failure to state a claim.
- The court’s decision also illustrates that deceptive procurement of a work does not automatically defeat other defenses (here, fair use). Satire, parody, or critical commentary can qualify as fair use even when the secondary user acquired the material by deceptive means if the other fair‑use factors are satisfied.
- Practical implications for creators and platforms: platform terms of service are generally not enforceable by nonparties, and commentators who transform or critique works typically do not usurp the original work’s licensing market—factors courts consider in both copyright and related state‑law claims.
- For plaintiffs alleging fraudulent inducement in a similar context, the safer pleading strategy is to identify and quantify specific economic losses (e.g., unreimbursed costs, lost licensing revenue, or concrete diminution in value) and tie those losses causally to the defendant’s misrepresentation.
