Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How did the "nature of the work" factor apply where the videos are creative but were "published the same day"?
Question:
The panel notes that the copyright registrations show the videos were published the same day they were created and cites 17 U.S.C. § 101. I’m trying to understand the factor-two analysis: Are these Cameo videos considered highly creative (which would weigh against fair use) but with the "published" status mitigating that? How exactly does "publication" under § 101 get determined from the registration record, and why does being published matter here if the works are otherwise expressive and personalized?
Answer:
Short answer
- The “nature of the work” factor (factor two) ordinarily disfavors fair use when the original is highly creative and especially when it is unpublished. The Santos opinion acknowledges the creative, expressive character of the Cameo videos but then points out that the copyright registrations show the videos were published the same day they were created. The court treated that publication status (established by the registration record) as undercutting the argument that the works were unpublished and so reduced the weight of factor two against fair use. Coupled with a strongly transformative use (factor one) and no cognizable market usurpation (factor four), publication helped tip the balance toward fair use.

Why publication matters under factor two
- Federal fair‑use doctrine treats creative, imaginative works as closer to the core of copyright and thus generally more protective (so factor two tends to weigh against fair use). Unpublished works receive extra protection because the author has a right to control the first public appearance of her expression (see Campbell, which the opinion cites for related principles).
- Publication removes that extra layer of protection: once a work has been published the “unpublished” concern is gone, so factor two is less powerful for the copyright holder.

How the court determined publication here
- The panel relied on the copyright registration entries in the record, stating explicitly that “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101.” The court may take judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations (see Island Software & Comput. Servs., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.; TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum), and the registrations themselves record a publication date. That is what the opinion used to treat the videos as published.

Putting it together for Santos v. Kimmel
- The opinion (1) found the JKL segments highly transformative (factor one strongly favored fair use); (2) recognized that the videos are expressive/creative but treated the registration evidence of same‑day publication as diminishing the usual force of factor two; and (3) found no market usurpation (factor four). The court therefore concluded that, even though the originals were creative, their published status (as shown by the registration entries) reduced factor two’s support for Santos and helped the court conclude fair use at the pleading stage.

Practical takeaways
- Creative work ≠ automatic win on factor two; unpublished status is what gives factor two its greatest bite. If a registration or other record shows publication, courts will treat the “unpublished” concern as absent and will give factor two less weight against fair use.
