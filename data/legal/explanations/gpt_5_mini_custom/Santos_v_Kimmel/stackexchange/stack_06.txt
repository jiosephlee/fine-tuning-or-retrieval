Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why couldn’t Santos enforce Cameo’s user Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary under Illinois law?
Question:
For the direct breach claim, the court applies Illinois law and says there’s a strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries unless the contract "practically" says so expressly. Santos argued Kimmel (a user) breached the TOS that users agree to, but the panel says there’s nothing close to an express declaration granting Santos enforcement rights, and notes other provisions do have explicit third-party language. Practically speaking, what sort of language would have been sufficient to let a Cameo creator like Santos enforce user TOS? Does the presence of some express third-party clauses elsewhere in the TOS create a negative inference against implied third-party status for creators?
Answer:
Short answer
Because Illinois law “strong[ly] presum[es] against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties” and requires the implication “to be so strong as to be practically an express declaration,” the panel found the Cameo user Terms lacked the near‑explicit language Illinois requires. The court stressed that other provisions of the same Terms do contain explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language, which shows how the drafters would have granted third‑party enforcement if they intended to do so. See Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) (quoting Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur).

Why the court refused to treat Santos as a third‑party beneficiary
- Governing doctrine: Under Illinois law (which the court applied to the Terms), there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties,” and the contract must be “practically an express declaration” to overcome that presumption. The panel relied on that formulation in rejecting Santos’s direct breach claim. Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II (citing Barry).
- What the opinion found in the Terms: The court found “nothing close to an ‘express declaration’ that the relevant provisions . . . apply to [Santos] or other noncontracting parties.” The opinion also notes that the Terms elsewhere contain explicit third‑party beneficiary language, which “indicat[es] that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Id. (emphasis added).
- Practical effect: Because the Terms did not contain near‑explicit language saying creators like Santos were intended beneficiaries or had enforcement rights, the Illinois presumption against third‑party enforcement was not overcome.

What wording would have been sufficient (practical examples)
The court’s standard — requiring something “practically an express declaration” — means the Terms would need language plainly conveying that creators are intended beneficiaries with a right to enforce. Examples of the kind of wording that would meet that standard (and that the court said was missing) include clauses that:
- expressly name creators/performers as intended third‑party beneficiaries; or
- state that users’ promises are “for the benefit of” creators and that creators “may enforce” the Terms against users; or
- use unambiguous phrases such as “intended third‑party beneficiaries” and explicitly grant those beneficiaries enforcement rights.

(The opinion itself does not give a model clause; it articulates the standard and explains that nothing in the Cameo Terms came close to that “express declaration.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II.)

Does the presence of explicit third‑party clauses elsewhere create a negative inference?
Yes. The panel treated the presence of express third‑party‑beneficiary language in other parts of the Terms as evidence that the drafter knew how to confer enforcement rights when intended, and therefore the absence of such language in the provisions Santos relied on weighs against finding an implied third‑party beneficiary. The opinion states this explicitly: the existence of explicit third‑party language elsewhere “indicat[es] that when the parties . . . intended to allow provisions . . . to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II.

One related point the opinion makes (why Santos’s other contract theories also failed)
Even apart from the third‑party‑beneficiary issue under Illinois law, the court rejected Santos’s attempt to plead an implied‑in‑fact contract (governed by New York law here) because he failed to plead the essential terms or a meeting of the minds. See Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. § II (discussing Caniglia and DG & A citations).

Bottom line
Under the standard the court applied, mere benefits to creators from user agreements are not enough; the Terms must practically (i.e., nearly literally) say the nonparty is an intended and enforceable beneficiary. The Cameo Terms did not do that, and the fact that some provisions did use explicit third‑party language made the omission in the disputed provisions dispositive.
