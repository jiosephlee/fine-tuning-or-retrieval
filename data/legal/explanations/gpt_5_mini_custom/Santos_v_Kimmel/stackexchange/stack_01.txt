Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### If the defendant instigated the creation of the videos, how can his later use still be "transformative" fair use?
Question:
The opinion rejects Santos’s argument that Kimmel can’t claim transformation because he "instigated the creation" of the Cameo videos by submitting the prompts. The panel instead applies a reasonable observer standard (citing Warhol) and says the JKL segments had a different purpose—commenting on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—than the videos’ original purpose of encouragement/positivity. I’m confused how the instigator point fits: If Kimmel designed the prompts to elicit specific statements, doesn’t that make the original purpose the same as his later use? Does it matter that Santos contends his original purpose aligned with Kimmel’s (i.e., to say what the buyer asked for, for money)? How do courts cabin manipulation of the "original" purpose by the secondary user when assessing transformation?
Answer:
Short answer: Because fair-use transformation looks to what a reasonable observer would perceive the original work’s purpose to be, not to who secretly prompted its creation. The Second Circuit (quoting Warhol/Goldsmith) rejected Santos’s “instigator” argument for that reason: even if Kimmel solicited the lines, the published Cameo videos objectively conveyed messages of encouragement/positivity, while the JKL segments re‑purposed those clips for satire/criticism. That difference in purpose/character — judged objectively — supports a finding of transformation.

Why that is so (doctrine and how the opinion applies it)
- The key fair‑use inquiry under the first factor is whether the new work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character,” i.e., is transformative. The court relies on the objective, reasonable‑observer test articulated in Warhol/Goldsmith: subjective intent of the original author or the secondary user does not control the purpose-of-use inquiry. (Opinion at ¶ I, citing Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023).)
- The Santos opinion explains the consequence: even if Kimmel “instigated” or “designed” prompts, what matters is how the videos would be understood by a reasonable observer. The complaint itself alleged that the published Cameo videos conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” A reasonable observer would therefore see the original purpose as encouragement — not as an invitation to say absurd things for money. The JKL segments, by contrast, had the clear purpose of sarcastic criticism (mocking Santos’s willingness to say odd things for money). That objective difference supports transformation. (Opinion at ¶¶ I–II, pp. discussing reasonable‑observer standard and App’x citations.)
- The opinion also notes that the Harper & Row language about good faith is not decisive here: even if bad faith were argued from Kimmel’s misrepresentations, the complaint does not plausibly allege an intent to supplant Santos’s market; the complaint itself paints the defendants’ purpose as criticism/satire, which aligns with fair use. (Opinion at ¶ I, discussing Harper & Row.)

How courts cabin “manipulation” of the original purpose
- By using an objective test: courts look at the content and context of the original, as published, and ask what a reasonable observer would take its purpose to be. That prevents a secondary user from defeating transformation simply by showing they elicited particular wording, and it prevents a copyright holder from insisting on a subjective intent that conflicts with the work’s objective effect.
- That does not mean instigation is irrelevant in every case. If the published original, as reasonably perceived, genuinely served the same expressive purpose as the secondary use, then transformation is less likely. Put another way: instigation won’t convert nontransformative reuse into transformative reuse where the objective purposes are the same; but instigation alone cannot change the objective meaning of the published work if that meaning is plainly different from the secondary use’s purpose.
- The opinion illustrates both points: Santos’s argument that Kimmel “designed” the originals fails because the objective content of the videos (as alleged) is encouraging, while JKL’s use is critical/satirical — an objectively different purpose. The panel therefore treated instigation as immaterial to the first‑factor analysis under the reasonable‑observer standard. (Opinion at ¶ I.)

Bottom line for your hypothetical questions
- Kimmel prompting Santos doesn’t automatically make the original purpose identical to the later use. What matters is how the original came across to a reasonable observer once it was published. If that objective perception differs from the secondary use’s purpose (as the court found here), the secondary use can be transformative despite the instigation.
- If the published original really did communicate the same purpose as the reuse (objectively), then a court could — and should — find less (or no) transformation even if the secondary user instigated the content. The reasonable‑observer test is the guardrail that cabins manipulation.
