Title: Blog about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

# Can a Company “Certify” Without a Signature? Strict Compliance Meets Corporate Attestation in Williams v. GoAuto

## Why this case matters

At first glance Williams v. GoAuto is a garden-variety dispute about cancelled auto-insurance policies and whether the insurer followed Louisiana’s rules when a premium-finance company exercised its power of attorney to cancel on account of nonpayment. But the Fifth Circuit’s decision does something doctrinally interesting: it confronts the tension between Louisiana’s well-established doctrine of strict statutory compliance for cancellations and modern corporate processes that produce unsigned, automated certifications. The panel held that the statute’s requirement that the finance company “certif[y]” certain facts does not implicitly demand a handwritten or human signature on the certification, and that an insurer may “receive” an electronic transmission (an automated e‑mail/web-service request) sufficient to permit cancellation. That resolution matters for two reasons. First, it affects the substantive protection afforded insureds whose coverage may be lost when third‑party financiers default. Second, it signals how courts will reconcile older statutory language—drafted for a paper world—with automated business practices.

## What the Fifth Circuit held and why

The relevant statutory text, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b), permits a premium finance company to request cancellation of an insurance policy upon uncured default “by sending . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” enumerated matters (power of attorney, default, notice to insured, copies to interested parties). The insurer may then cancel “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.”

Plaintiffs argued two related points: (1) the statutory “certifying” requires a signature or identification of a signing individual before cancellation can be effective; and (2) GoAuto never actually “received” the certified statement before its system cancelled the policy, because the physical, signed document was not in GoAuto’s hands when the automated cancellation occurred.

The court rejected both arguments. On the signature point, the panel observed that the statute does not mention a signature. It surveyed Louisiana authority and found no definitive rule that a signature is necessary; some precedents paraphrase the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance,” and only one appellate opinion (Benitez v. Elsayed) noted the absence of a signature as part of a larger finding that a notice was defective—but did not hold lack of a signature alone invalidated cancellation. The Fifth Circuit reasoned that “certifying” can be performed by the corporate entity itself and that the statute contemplates the insurance premium finance company (not an individual employee) effecting cancellation by certifying the requisite facts. The panel therefore refused to “add to the procedures” by importing a signature requirement.

On the “receipt” issue, the court accepted ordinary dictionary definitions of “receipt” and “receive” as involving coming into possession or custody; it then held that GoAuto’s web-service/listener architecture—where APAC’s system sends an electronic cancellation request that GoAuto’s system automatically accepts and cancels the policy—constituted “receipt” sufficient under the statute. The fact that a human employee later stamped or signed the printed documents did not undermine the earlier electronic receipt and resulting cancellation.

The panel declined to certify the unsettled questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, noting that novelty alone does not warrant frequent certification and that the record and precedent did not present a compelling state-law question needing resolution.

## Analytical perspective: statutory text, corporate acts, and strict compliance

The decision sits at a crossroads of two legitimate legal impulses. On one hand, Louisiana courts have long insisted on strict adherence to the cancellation statute because the policyholder’s right to a minimum cure period and a clear time of cancellation are at stake. On the other, modern commerce routinely substitutes corporate attestation and automated transmission for a signature-bearing, paper-based world. The Fifth Circuit navigated this tension by anchoring its analysis in the text (“certifying” by the finance company) and a practical understanding of contemporary corporate processes (web services and automated e‑mails).

There is force to the panel’s reluctance to judicially graft a signature requirement onto the statute. A rigid insistence on an employee’s printed name or inked signature would be anachronistic and could produce gamesmanship—e.g., finance companies could simply backdate signatures, or create post hoc signed forms—even while digital logs show the certification and transmission occurred earlier. Equally, the court’s pragmatic acceptance that a computer listener receiving an authenticated electronic request suffices as “receipt” avoids a rule that would invalidate literally thousands of automated cancellations because a piece of paper did not move from one drawer to another.

That said, the court’s approach does narrow the protective bite of “strict adherence.” Strict compliance has functioned in Louisiana jurisprudence to impose a bright-line check on finance companies’ exercise of a power that can abruptly terminate coverage. Part of that check has been documentary form—identifying who certified and when mailing occurred—so insureds and third parties can verify timelines and challenge defects. Treating a generic corporate-labeled statement and an automated electronic hand-off as the functional equivalent of a signed, individually attributable certification reduces the evidentiary burdens on finance companies and insurers and shifts the risk to insureds who may have less access to the underlying electronic logs.

The Fifth Circuit does not endorse laxity; it explicitly rejects adding requirements not found in the statute. Its reasoning is defensible as a method of faithful statutory interpretation. But it also effectively delegates to private actors the task of designing verification systems: courts will enforce whatever internal corporate attestation the finance company chooses so long as it constitutes a “statement certifying” and is received by the insurer.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several implications follow.

First, litigation over cancelled policies will likely turn more on digital forensics and system logs than on physical papers. Plaintiffs challenging cancellations will need to prove defects in the content or timing of electronic transmissions, or show that the insurer’s automatic acceptance was unauthorized or erroneous. Insurers and finance companies will have incentives to maintain robust, timestamped electronic records and to design clear audit trails that memorialize the certification and receipt events.

Second, this decision may produce a piecemeal evolution of what “certifying” means in Louisiana: future courts will parse whether particular automated attestations suffice in different factual contexts. For example, a bare e-mail from a finance-company domain that simply says “cancel” may be distinguishable from a structured message containing the statutorily enumerated certifications and attachments of mailing affidavits. Plaintiffs may press cases where the automated system fails or where later-signed paper documents contradict the electronic record.

Third, consumer-protection advocates will view this ruling warily. Allowing unsigned certifications and automated cancellations cuts against the protective logic of strict compliance insofar as it reduces the visibility of who (which human agent) actually verified the statements. In close cases—say, where an insured disputes whether the ten‑day cure notice was mailed—lack of a named, signed certifier will complicate discovery and could make it harder for insureds to prevail.

Finally, there is an institutional choice here: courts are, naturally, reticent to rewrite statutes. If the Legislature wishes to require named individual attestations or signatures, or to prescribe precise electronic attestation standards, the onus is on lawmakers to act.

## Practical fixes the Legislature (or regulators) could adopt

If Louisiana policymakers are concerned that the Fifth Circuit’s reading dilutes the protective function of strict compliance, there are several straightforward statutory or regulatory options that would clarify expectations without upending modern business practices:

1. Define what constitutes a valid “certification.” The statute could specify that the certification must be a written or electronic statement made by the premium finance company, and require that the certification include (a) the name and contact information of the individual authorizing or preparing the certification, or (b) an attestation on corporate letterhead together with an electronic or digital signature (e.g., under the E-SIGN/Uniform Electronic Transactions Act paradigm) or a stamped, verifiable electronic log entry created contemporaneously with the certification.

2. Recognize electronic signatures and timestamped logs. A statutory amendment could explicitly permit electronic signatures and system-generated timestamps, provided they meet defined authentication criteria (e.g., unique user credentials, non-repudiation mechanisms, and retained audit logs).

3. Require an affidavit of mailing or an easily discoverable audit trail. The statute could demand that the finance company, at the time of certification, either attach or retain an affidavit proving mailing to the insured and a specific log entry showing when the insurer’s system received the certification.

4. Protect insureds’ cure rights with notice redundancy. Legislators could require that when a finance company seeks cancellation via power of attorney, it must both send the electronic certification to the insurer and serve a contemporaneous, separately tracked notice to the insured (for example, certified mail or verified delivery), thereby preserving the protective policy behind the strict-compliance rule.

5. Clarify “receipt.” To avoid downstream disputes about technicalities, the statute could define “receipt” to include electronic receipt validated by a server log entry from the insurer’s system, or require explicit human acknowledgement in certain borderline cases (e.g., if the insurer’s cancellation systems are configured to accept automated messages without filtering).

These drafting fixes would preserve the statutory objectives—clear time of cancellation and a minimum cure period—while accommodating modern practices.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto is a practical, text-driven response to a problem born of the collision between old statutory language and new technology. The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to read a signature requirement into the “certifying” language, and its recognition that electronic transmission and automated cancellation can satisfy “receipt,” are defensible and sensible outcomes that avoid judicial lawmaking. But the decision narrows the documentary safe harbor that strict compliance doctrine historically provided to consumer‑policyholders. If Louisiana wants to preserve the evidentiary clarity and individual accountability that signatures historically supplied, the Legislature (or regulators) should spell out how certifications must be made and authenticated in an electronic age. Until then, expect lower courts to wrestle with the adequacy of particular electronic attestations and for litigants to lean heavily on system logs—and on statutory reform—to adjudicate these disputes.
