Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Parties, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## Parties
This case involves four principal groups of parties. The plaintiffs are former insureds Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright and a third-party claimant, Nicholas Jenkins. Jenkins is the owner of a vehicle and the victim of an accident caused by a different GoAuto insured (not a plaintiff here); that driver’s GoAuto policy had been financed through the same financing arrangement as Williams’s and Wright’s. The defendants are GoAuto Insurance Company (the insurer) and American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (APAC), the premium-finance company that paid GoAuto the full premium up front and then collected installment payments from the insureds.

## Premium-finance agreements and the power of attorney
The financing arrangement between the insureds and APAC is central to the dispute. Under those agreements APAC advanced the full premium to GoAuto; the insureds, in turn, repaid APAC in monthly installments. Each financing agreement expressly granted APAC a power of attorney authorizing APAC to cancel the insurance policy if the insured defaulted on the installment payments. That power of attorney is the statutory mechanism by which a finance company may request cancellation of a financed policy under the Louisiana statute at issue in the case (La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)). The statute requires the finance company to send the insurer a notice “certifying” certain enumerated items (e.g., that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the agreement is in default, that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to interested third parties).

## What happened: notices, nonpayment, and cancellation mechanics
In each relevant instance the insureds failed to make the installment payment required by APAC. APAC’s practice in that event was to send the insured a “Ten Day Notice of Cancellation” informing the insured that the policy would be cancelled if payment was not received within ten days. If payment was not received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system generated an automated e‑mail to GoAuto containing a copy of the Ten Day Notice and a request for cancellation. The automated request was on APAC letterhead and recited the statutory certifications. GoAuto’s computer system was configured to cancel the policy when it received that e‑mail/cancellation request.

Later that same morning, after the computer-initiated cancellation occurred, an APAC employee would print the cancellation materials, affix a signature stamp to the printed documents, and then place the signed, printed documents in a shared filing cabinet. Prior to 2017 the filing cabinet was located in a shared room managed by GoAuto Management Services; since 2017 an APAC employee delivered the signed, printed materials to a GoAuto employee who stamped them as “received” and placed them in a shared cabinet. Thus, the chronological facts relevant to the statutory dispute are: (1) APAC’s automated electronic request reached GoAuto’s systems and triggered an automated cancellation; (2) only later that morning was any human-signed paper copy produced and filed.

## Claims asserted
The plaintiffs asserted that GoAuto’s cancellations were ineffective under Louisiana law because the statutory procedures for cancellation by a premium‑finance company were not strictly followed. The complaints alleged, in substance, that the technical procedures APAC and GoAuto used—most importantly the timing of any human signature and the manner in which GoAuto “received” the cancellation request—failed to satisfy the statute’s requirements, rendering the cancellations ineffective. From those alleged defects the plaintiffs asserted related claims including misrepresentation and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing (claims that depend on whether the policies were in fact valid at the time of the accidents and communications).

## Procedural path to the Fifth Circuit
The plaintiffs originally sued GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court as a putative class action; Williams and Jenkins were the original plaintiffs and Wright was added by amendment. GoAuto removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties narrowed the litigation to a single legal question for summary-judgment briefing: whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with the Louisiana statute governing premium-finance cancellations. The district court granted GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment, concluding that GoAuto’s procedures satisfied the statutory requirements, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Wright, and Jenkins appealed that adverse judgment to the Fifth Circuit, challenging (among other things) whether the required statutory “certifying” had occurred before cancellation and whether GoAuto had in fact “received” the cancellation requests prior to cancelling the policies. The Fifth Circuit’s decision on those issues determines whether the district court correctly resolved the threshold statutory question that controlled the case.

# Louisiana’s Premium Finance Cancellation Framework and Strict Compliance

## Core statute: what must the premium finance company send

At the center of Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), which authorizes a premium finance company to request cancellation of an insured’s policy upon an uncured default by sending the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four discrete items. Those four items are (i) that the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, (ii) that the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured, (iii) that upon default the finance company sent the insured the required notice of cancellation and specifying the date it was sent, and (iv) that copies of the notice were sent to any persons who, under the finance agreement, have an interest in any loss (including named agencies, lienholders, or other third parties), with names and addresses identified. The statute permits delivery by depositing the notice in the mail, by using a private carrier, or “via electronic mail,” and it frames the certifications as the act of “the insurance premium finance company.” Read together, the statute prescribes both content (the four certifications plus the cancellation notice) and permitted means of transmission (mail, private carrier, or email).

Understanding these textual components is essential: the statute does not, on its face, say how the statement must be signed, who must sign it, or the precise mechanics of delivery beyond the three permitted methods. Those lacunae are the source of litigation over whether particular company practices satisfy the statute’s demands.

## Insurer’s action upon receipt: what triggers cancellation

Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) supplies the consequence of compliance: “Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” The operative trigger is the insurer’s receipt of the notice and certification. In Williams the Fifth Circuit considered what “receipt” means in a modern, largely electronic business environment. Because the statute itself does not define “receipt,” the court consulted a leading dictionary definition (Black’s Law Dictionary) treating “receipt” as the act of receiving — especially by taking physical possession — and “receive” as coming into possession or getting from an outside source. Applying that definition to the record, the court concluded that an insurer may proceed to cancel once it has taken possession, delivery, or custody of the request for cancellation. The court accepted that automated electronic delivery to GoAuto’s systems — an APAC process calling a GoAuto web service that the company’s computer “listener” then executes — constitutes the insurer’s receipt for purposes of the statute in this record. Thus, in the court’s view, receipt can occur by electronic, automated transmission and need not await a human review or an imprimatur placed on paper later in the morning.

## The strict-adherence doctrine and its limits

Louisiana precedent requires strict adherence to the statute’s procedural requirements when a finance company exercises a power of attorney to cancel a policy. The Fifth Circuit rehearsed that doctrine through controlling and persuasive state appellate decisions: Delatte v. Lemotte and Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co. stand for the proposition that any defect in the statutorily-prescribed cancellation process can render the cancellation ineffective. The court emphasized the rationale courts have given for strictness: as Britten v. Reavis explains, exact compliance serves two core functions — it fixes the precise time and date of cancellation, and it preserves a minimum cure period during which the insured can remedy the default. These twin aims explain why Louisiana courts have been vigilant about enforcement of the statute’s procedures.

But Williams also illustrates a doctrinal restraint: courts enforcing strict adherence should not, under the guise of strictness, rewrite the statute or impose requirements the legislature did not include. The Fifth Circuit framed its task as interpreting the statutory text, not adding prerequisites (for example, a legislatively unenumerated signature requirement). Thus, strict compliance is enforced against departures from the statutory prescriptions, but courts should be careful not to manufacture additional steps that the statute does not require.

## Prior case treatments of “certifying” and the signature question

A recurring interpretive knot is what the statute’s command that the finance company “certif[y]” requires in practice. Several Louisiana appellate opinions quoted by the court characterize the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance” or allow cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement.” Those prior decisions (KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood; Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.; Lewis v. Julien) endorse the requirement of a statement certifying certain facts but do not address whether that statement must bear an individual employee’s signature.

One intermediate appellate opinion, Benitez v. Elsayed, did note the absence of a signature on the submitted instrument as a shortcoming, observing that the document “does not identify any particular employee” and “is not signed by any individual,” but the Benitez court did not hold that lack of an individual signature, standing alone, automatically invalidated the cancellation if the other statutory elements were satisfied. In Williams the Fifth Circuit read these cases to mean that while a statement certifying the required facts is essential, Louisiana courts have not established a per se rule that a named or signed individual signature is mandatory. The Fifth Circuit therefore concluded that the statute’s command that “the insurance premium finance company” certify the specified facts can be satisfied without a signature identifying a particular employee, and it declined to graft a signature requirement onto the statute absent clearer state-law authority.

## How the court applied the framework to GoAuto/APAC’s procedures

Applying the statute and the strict-adherence principle to the record, the Fifth Circuit examined APAC’s practices and GoAuto’s receiving process. APAC sent ten‑day notices to insureds and, when the cure period expired, its computer system automatically sent an email to GoAuto with a copy of the notice of cancellation and a certification on APAC letterhead reciting the four required points and stating that copies were enclosed. GoAuto’s web service — an automated listener — received that electronic notice and automatically effected cancellation in the early morning hours. Only later did an APAC employee (using a signature stamp) and, after 2017, a GoAuto employee stamp the printed file as “received” and place the documents in a shared filing cabinet.

The court’s analysis had two key conclusions. First, the word “certifying” in the statute does not, as a matter of Louisiana law as the court read it, impose a separate requirement that the statement be signed by an identifiable individual before cancellation. Second, the insurer’s “receipt” for purposes of § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) can occur when an insurer’s electronic system takes possession of the delivered notice; an automated web-service action that accepts and processes the cancellation request constitutes receipt so the insurer may proceed to cancel. Because the APAC email/statement reached GoAuto’s systems and GoAuto’s system acted on it, the court held the statutory prerequisites were satisfied and that GoAuto’s cancellation was effective. The Fifth Circuit therefore affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for GoAuto.

The court did note an alternative rationale the district court had used — treating the person who later placed the printed notice into the shared filing cabinet as a “private carrier” — but declined to reach that theory since the email-and-receiver analysis resolved the statutory question. Similarly, the plaintiffs’ request that the Fifth Circuit certify the interpretive questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court was denied because the panel found no compelling reason to invoke certification; the state-law question had an answer sufficient for disposition in the federal appeal.

## Pedagogical takeaway

Williams v. GoAuto illustrates how a federal appellate court reads a state statute that prescribes a strict procedural route for legal consequences. The court enforced Louisiana’s rule of strict adherence to the statutory procedure but stayed within the statute’s text: it required the prescribed content of notice and certification, but it declined to create additional formalities (like a mandated individual signature) not present in the statute or authoritatively required by Louisiana courts. The decision also shows how courts will adapt traditional statutory concepts — here, “receipt” and “certifying” — to contemporary electronic business practices when the statute permits electronic transmission and the record demonstrates that an insurer’s systems have taken possession of the communication. For students learning to read opinions, Williams provides a useful model of statutory interpretation that balances fidelity to strict procedural requirements with fidelity to the statute’s plain language and existing state precedent.

# GoAuto/APAC Cancellation Workflow and Evidence

This chapter walks through the factual record about how APAC (the premium finance company) and GoAuto (the insurer) processed cancellations, and explains why those facts mattered to the court’s statutory analysis. The opinion turns on a sequence of automated and manual steps — what was sent, when GoAuto’s systems acted, and what paperwork was later produced — because Louisiana’s statute conditions cancellation on a premium-finance company’s “certifying” certain facts and the insurer’s “receipt” of that certification. As you read the evidence in this case, think in terms of (1) the statute’s discrete requirements, (2) the time line of electronic and human acts, and (3) whether the record supplies the facts the statute calls for at the moment cancellation occurs.

## APAC 10-day notices

The starting point in the record is APAC’s usual default notice to insureds. When an insured misses a payment, APAC sends a ten-day notice warning the insured the policy will be cancelled if payment is not made within ten days. APAC’s process also includes sending copies of the ten-day notice to any persons or entities that the premium finance agreement shows have an interest in losses under the policy — the very set of recipients the statute requires be notified. The content and recipients of those ten-day notices are relevant because the statute requires the premium finance company to certify that (among other things) it sent such a notice and sent copies to interested parties. In this case the record shows APAC’s ten-day notices and that APAC maintained proof of mailing (an affidavit of mailing available on request), which directly corresponds to the statutory elements (i)–(iv) the finance company must “certify.”

## Automated request

The next link in the workflow is the automated request that follows if the insured does not cure the default by midnight on the tenth day. APAC’s computer system is set to generate and transmit to GoAuto an electronic package — an email (or an API/web-service call) that contains (a) a copy of the ten-day notice and (b) a written request for cancellation on APAC letterhead. Critically, the automated request includes a written “certification” that tracks the four statutory statements: (1) that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, (2) that the agreement is in default and uncured, (3) that a ten-day notice was sent to the insured (with copies enclosed and proof of mailing available), and (4) that copies were sent to anyone shown by the finance agreement to have an interest. This alignment between the automated content and the statute’s enumerated items is the principal evidentiary basis for GoAuto’s claim that the required certification was sent.

When you read an opinion like this, note how the court parses the actual language in the automated transmission against the statutory checklist. The presence of the four items in APAC’s automated request is what allows the company to argue it “certified” the necessary facts even though the certification was generated by a computer process rather than hand-signed by a named employee at the moment of transmission.

## Immediate system action

The opinion emphasizes what happens on GoAuto’s side the moment that electronic request arrives. GoAuto’s systems include a web service that listens for incoming calls or messages. Testimony in the record described the web service as “always listening”: when APAC’s process calls the service to request cancellation, the GoAuto system recognizes the request and automatically performs the cancellation. That cancellation occurs in the early morning hours when the e-mail/API call is received; no human reviews or stamps the printed documents until later that morning.

The timing of this automated cancellation is decisive for the statutory “receipt” question. The court treated the electronic arrival and the web service’s immediate cancellation as satisfying the insurer’s “receipt” of the finance company’s notice and statement. The opinion relies on common dictionary definitions of “receipt” as taking possession or coming into possession of something; it concludes that a computer system’s acceptance of an electronic transmission is a form of receipt. For students, the key evidentiary takeaways are (a) contemporaneous system logs, timestamps, or witness testimony that the system “received” the message matter more than later physical paperwork, and (b) the record’s description of how the web service works supplied an adequate factual basis for the court to treat electronic receipt as the operative event.

## Post-cancellation paperwork

After the automated cancellation, the record shows additional human and paper-handling steps later that same morning. An APAC employee printed the cancellation documents and affixed a signature stamp to them. How those printed, stamped documents were filed changed over time. Before 2017 the signed prints were physically taken to a shared filing cabinet in a room associated with GoAuto Management Services; since 2017 the APAC employee has taken the prints to a GoAuto employee who stamps them “received” and places them in a shared filing cabinet. Plaintiffs contested aspects of this post-cancellation handling: they argued that (1) certification requires a signature before cancellation (so a post-cancellation stamp is too late), and (2) the insurer never actually “received” the notice before cancelling because the physical stamps and filing were performed by APAC employees or because the documents were kept in a shared space rather than in GoAuto’s exclusive custody.

The court resolved these evidentiary disputes by focusing on timing and substance. The certification language was present in the electronic request at the moment the web service acted, so the lack of a prior handwritten signature did not defeat the statutorily required certification. And because GoAuto’s systems had an electronic receipt (and took the cancellation action immediately), the later physical stamping and file-placement did not control the statutory “receipt” question. In short, the record’s contemporaneous electronic transmission and GoAuto’s automated response were sufficient, so the subsequent paper-handling did not create a statutory defect.

## District court’s alternative rationale

The district court had offered an alternative, more novel factual theory: it treated the physical delivery of printed notices by the APAC employee as effectively using a “private carrier,” relying on an analogy from federal railroad-carrier jurisprudence to define “private carrier.” That reasoning sought to place the documents into GoAuto’s possession through delivery by a private carrier (i.e., APAC’s agent). The Fifth Circuit did not resolve the merits of that alternative rationale because it concluded the email/web-service receipt alone satisfied the statute and therefore made consideration of the “private carrier” theory unnecessary. The panel also noted there was scant Louisiana authority adapting the “private carrier” concept in this specific statutory context and declined to endorse the district court’s unconventional definition.

For law students, this is an example of an appellate court declining to reach an unnecessary alternative ground. The existence of the e-mail/web-service chain meant the panel did not have to validate a strained analogy or break new ground in state-law definitions.

## Takeaway on how the evidence supported statutory compliance

The appellate decision turned on fitting factual minutiae to discrete statutory elements. The record contained three facts the court deemed dispositive: (1) APAC’s ten-day notices and proof-of-mailing practice put the contents and recipient list the statute expects into play; (2) APAC’s automated e-mail/API request included explicit certification language that mirrored the statute’s four required statements; and (3) GoAuto’s systems “received” that electronic request and immediately effectuated cancellation. Because those facts were established in the summary-judgment record, the court concluded the procedures complied with the statute even though a physical signature and later filing occurred after the electronic cancellation.

When you analyze similar opinions, follow the same method: map each statutory element to a concrete fact in the record and pay close attention to timing (what existed at the moment cancellation occurred). Where technology replaces human acts, contemporaneous electronic transmissions and system behavior often control the legal question of “receipt” and “certification” unless the statute expressly requires a human signature or other formalities.

# Issues on Appeal and the Court’s Analysis: Signature and Receipt

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance turns on two narrow statutory questions under Louisiana law: whether a premium finance company’s “certifying” the required facts under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) must be evidenced by a signature before the insurer may cancel a policy, and what it means for the insurer to have “receipt” of the finance company’s cancellation request under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The court affirms summary judgment for GoAuto by answering both questions in ways that accommodate modern, electronic business practices while remaining faithful to the statute and Louisiana precedent.

## Issue 1 — Does “certifying” require a signature?

The statute says that, to request cancellation, the premium finance company must send the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” a short list of facts (power of attorney, uncured default, that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured, and copies sent to interested parties). The statute does not mention a signature or identify any particular person who must sign a document. Louisiana appellate decisions have repeatedly required “strict adherence” to the statutory procedures when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel a policy, and they have treated any defect in the cancellation process as potentially fatal. But, as the Fifth Circuit emphasizes, strict adherence means following the statutory steps — not adding requirements the text does not contain.

When students read the court’s analysis here, note the methodology. The court first considers the plain text (the statute requires the finance company to “certify” certain facts) and then looks at Louisiana precedent to see whether courts have read “certifying” as imposing a signature requirement. The existing opinions are inconclusive. Some appellate decisions paraphrase the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance” or allow cancellation “upon receipt of such notice and statement,” but they do not identify signature as an independent prerequisite. One case, Benitez, criticized a notice that lacked both an identified author and a signature and noted the document did not “purport to ‘certify’” the statements; Benitez did not hold that the absence of a signature alone invalidated the cancellation. Because the statute speaks of the insurance premium finance company certifying — not an identified individual signing — the Fifth Circuit declines to graft a signature requirement onto the statute.

Applying those principles to the record, the court treats APAC’s cancellation request (sent on APAC letterhead and containing explicit statements certifying each required element) as a valid “statement certifying.” The fact that an APAC employee later applied a signature stamp to a printed copy after the policy had already been cancelled by GoAuto’s automated system does not mean the pre‑cancellation certification was deficient. The key point the opinion teaches is that, under Louisiana law as interpreted by the Fifth Circuit in this case, certification can be made by a company statement rather than by an identified or signed individual, and courts should not impose an extra‑textual signature requirement when the statute is silent.

## Issue 2 — When has the insurer “received” the cancellation request?

The second contested issue is whether GoAuto must have taken physical possession of a paper cancellation request before cancelling a policy, or whether electronic delivery to and processing by GoAuto’s systems suffices as “receipt.” The statute requires that “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested” and may proceed to cancel. The statute does not define “receipt.”

The Fifth Circuit turns to familiar interpretive tools when a statutory term is undefined: it consults standard dictionary definitions and the practical realities of modern communications. The court adopts Black’s Law Dictionary definitions of “receipt” and “receive” — essentially, to take possession or to come into possession of something from an outside source — and applies them in context. The record shows that APAC’s system automatically sent GoAuto an email or web‑service call containing the notice and the certification, and GoAuto’s computer system was configured to cancel the policy upon receiving that electronic message. In short, the insurer’s systems took possession of the cancellation request by accepting the electronic transmission, and the automatic cancellation followed.

This holding emphasizes two related lessons for law students. First, statutory commands about sending and receiving official notices are interpreted in light of contemporary commercial practices; the court does not treat paper as the only valid medium when the statute was enacted in an era before ubiquitous electronic transmission. Second, “receipt” is a functional concept: what matters is that the insurer has the item in its custody or control (here, the GoAuto system had the electronic notice and acted on it), not that a human employee had physically handled a printed form before the cancellation took effect.

The plaintiffs advanced a competing, physical‑possession theory: because the signature stamp and physical filing of the cancellation documents occurred after (and were performed by an APAC employee), GoAuto supposedly never “received” the request prior to cancellation. The court rejects that argument as immaterial. The later affixation of a signature stamp and disputes about which employee stamped or filed the paper copies did not negate the fact that GoAuto’s systems had received the electronic cancellation message and acted upon it. The district court had also proposed an alternative rationale—treating the employee who delivered the notices as a “private carrier”—but because the Fifth Circuit resolves the case on the electronic‑receipt ground, it declines to adopt or analyze that unconventional theory.

## Consequences for the summary judgment motion and remaining claims

Because the Fifth Circuit concluded that APAC’s company‑level certification on its letterhead satisfied the statute’s “statement certifying” requirement and that GoAuto’s automated receipt of the electronic cancellation request satisfied the statute’s “receipt” requirement, the court held that GoAuto complied with § 9:3550(G)(3). With statutory compliance established, there was no basis to find GoAuto had failed to effect a valid cancellation under Louisiana law. Consequently the district court did not err in denying the plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment and in granting GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment. All claims against GoAuto were properly dismissed with prejudice.

For students learning to read opinions, this sequence illustrates how a narrow statutory construction can be dispositive: the panel focuses on two textual terms, applies plain‑meaning and contextual analysis informed by precedent and dictionary definitions, and resolves the appeal without reaching broader or speculative issues. It also shows the courts’ pragmatic approach to electronic communications and the limits of the “strict adherence” doctrine: courts enforce the statute’s requirements carefully, but they will not add procedural formalities—like a pre‑cancellation signature—when the statutory text and the commercial context do not demand them.

# Certification Request, Disposition, and Practical Significance

## Certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court

The plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify unsettled questions of Louisiana law—principally whether a premium finance company must include a signed, pre-cancellation signature on its “statement certifying” required items under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), and what constitutes “receipt” by the insurer. The court declined. As the opinion explains, federal courts use certification sparingly because the device is a limited and important resource; simply showing that a state-supreme-court definition is absent is not a sufficient ground for certification. The court relied on its own precedent (Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n) for the proposition that an unsettled term alone does not compel certification. In short, absent a compelling reason—such as a genuinely determinative, novel state-law question that would benefit from controlling state-court guidance—the Fifth Circuit will resolve the issue itself rather than certify it.

The practical lesson from the opinion is twofold: certification is an extraordinary step, and litigants cannot obtain it merely by pointing to some ambiguity in a statutory term. The panel evaluated Louisiana authorities and the statute’s text and concluded that the questions could be resolved without asking the Louisiana Supreme Court for guidance, so it denied the request.

## Disposition

The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to GoAuto. The appellate court held that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3): (1) APAC’s notices contained the statutorily required “statement certifying” the four listed items; (2) GoAuto’s automated receipt of the e-mailed cancellation request (via its web service) constituted “receipt” of the notice, permitting GoAuto to cancel the policies; and (3) because the statutory cancellation was effective, the plaintiffs’ derivative claims (including alleged misrepresentations and breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing) failed and were properly dismissed with prejudice. The panel therefore affirmed the district court’s judgment in full.

## Practical takeaways for insurers and premium finance companies

This opinion contains concrete procedural guidance grounded in the statute and the panel’s factual analysis:

- What must be certified: The finance company’s statement must affirm the statutory items—(i) existence of a valid power of attorney in the premium finance agreement, (ii) an uncured default, (iii) that the ten-day notice of cancellation was sent to the insured (including the date), and (iv) that copies were sent to persons with an interest (specified by name/address where applicable). The opinion treats a clear, explicit corporate “certification” that recites these elements as fulfilling the statutory requirement.

- Signature not required before electronic cancellation: The Fifth Circuit held that Louisiana law does not impose an independent, pre-cancellation signature requirement on the certifying statement. The statute speaks of the insurance premium finance company “certifying,” and the court saw no basis to graft on an individual employee’s signed name when the company’s notice itself expressly certifies the required facts. Thus, a company-formed electronic notice that unambiguously certifies the statutory items can satisfy the statute even if a human signature is added only later (or not at all).

- Electronic transmission and automated web services can supply “receipt”: The court accepted that an insurer’s automated systems (here, a web service that listens for APAC’s cancellation call and causes an automatic cancellation when the e-mail/request is received) can constitute “receipt” under the statute. Practically, this means that e-mail transmissions, API/web-service calls, or other electronic communications that the insurer’s systems receive and act upon will ordinarily count as giving the insurer possession or custody of the cancellation request.

- Preserve contemporaneous electronic evidence: Because the court’s acceptance of electronic certification and receipt turns on timing and content, companies should preserve and be prepared to produce contemporaneous logs and records showing (a) the content of the notice sent, (b) timestamps for when the finance company sent the message, (c) server or web-service receipt logs showing when the insurer’s system received the message, (d) any automated cancellation actions triggered, and (e) any post hoc physical-stamping or signature events. Affidavits of mailing and copies of the notice, as APAC included, are useful supplements. These records are the practical means of proving strict statutory compliance.

## Boundaries and open questions

Although the opinion relaxes the notion that an employee’s signed name is automatically required and embraces modern electronic communications, it is careful to preserve two important limits:

- Strict adherence remains the rule: The court reaffirmed that Louisiana requires strict compliance with § 9:3550(G)(3) when a finance company exercises a power of attorney to cancel a policy. The panel emphasized that courts should not add procedural requirements beyond the statute’s text, but neither should companies be cavalier about compliance. Failure to certify any of the statutory items, or factual gaps in timing or proof of sending/receipt, can render a cancellation ineffective under longstanding Louisiana authority.

- Some issues left unresolved: The court declined to decide whether other formalities (e.g., a signature in different factual settings) might be required in different circumstances, and it expressly declined to reach the district court’s creative definition of “private carrier.” Because the Fifth Circuit resolved the case on the alternative ground that e-mail/web-service receipt satisfied the statute, the precise contours of “private carrier” under § 9:3550 remain an open question. Likewise, close factual scenarios—such as an e-mail that is not received by the insurer’s system, a notice that fails to recite the four statutory items, or one where the insurer’s automated system acts before any plausible company certification—were not squarely decided and could produce different outcomes.

In sum, Williams v. GoAuto confirms that modern, machine-mediated communications can satisfy Louisiana’s cancellation statute so long as the company’s notice clearly certifies the statutory elements and the insurer can be shown to have taken possession of the cancellation request. At the same time, the decision underscores the need to keep precise, contemporaneous electronic and documentary proof of both transmission and receipt because Louisiana courts continue to insist on strict statutory adherence.
