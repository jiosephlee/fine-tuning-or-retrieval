Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) require a human signature to "certify" a cancellation request?
Question:
The statute says the premium finance company must send a copy of the notice of cancellation "together with a statement certifying" items (i)–(iv), but it never mentions a signature. Plaintiffs argued that "certifying" implies a signed attestation before cancellation. The Fifth Circuit held no signature is required, distinguishing Benitez v. Elsayed because there the notice didn't purport to certify at all. Under Louisiana law, can a corporation "certify" via an unsigned document or automated email that states "APAC certifies..." without any individual's name or signature? Are there Louisiana authorities (statutes, UETA, evidence rules) that define certification in a way that would require a signature or identifiable affiant?
Answer:
Short answer: No — as the Fifth Circuit read and applied Louisiana law in Williams v. GoAuto Ins., La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) does not, by its text or Louisiana precedent cited in that opinion, require a human signature or an identified affiant on the “statement certifying” the listed matters. An unsigned corporate communication or automated email that explicitly states “APAC certifies …” and is received by the insurer sufficed in that case.

Explanation and legal basis (from Williams v. GoAuto Ins., 5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025)

- Statutory text controls: the statute requires that “the insurance premium finance company” send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the listed items (i)–(iv). The Fifth Circuit emphasized that the statute speaks of the company certifying, not of an identified individual doing so, and warned courts against adding requirements that the statute itself does not contain. (Williams opinion, discussion I & II.)

- Louisiana “strict adherence” principle does not automatically add a signature requirement: Louisiana decisions require strict adherence to the cancellation procedures, but that principle is about enforcing the statutory mechanics (fixing the date/time of cancellation and cure period), not about importing extra formalities (like a signature) absent textual support. The Fifth Circuit thus concluded there is “no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified.” (Williams, quoting and distinguishing Benitez.)

- Distinguishing Benitez v. Elsayed: Benitez noted the notice there “is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements.” The Fifth Circuit distinguished Benitez because the notices at issue in Williams did expressly “certify” the required items; the absence of a signature alone was not dispositive in Benitez and was not fatal here. (Williams, discussing Benitez.)

- Electronic/automated communications and “receipt”: the court accepted that an automated email from APAC that included the certification and that was received by GoAuto’s system satisfied the statute’s “receipt” requirement for triggering cancellation. The court treated modern corporate computer-to-computer communications as adequate means of sending/receiving the certifying statement. (Williams, discussion I.)

What about other Louisiana authorities (UETA, evidence rules, etc.)?

- The Williams opinion does not rely on Louisiana’s UETA, evidence rules, or any separate statutory definition of “certify” requiring a signature. The Fifth Circuit based its conclusion on the statutory text and on the absence of any state-court holding imposing a signature requirement. The court expressly declined to seek certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court. (Williams, discussion II.)

Practical implications for practice / pleading

- Under Williams, a premium finance company’s unsigned corporate certification (including an automated email stating “APAC certifies …”) that actually states the four required items and is received by the insurer will satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) as interpreted by the Fifth Circuit.

- Caveat: Williams is a federal appellate interpretation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not definitively ruled that a signature is unnecessary, and some Louisiana appellate opinions (e.g., Benitez) have discussed lack of a signature as a shortcoming in the notice. If you are litigating in Louisiana state court, that slight uncertainty may be relevant, but Williams is strong persuasive authority in the Fifth Circuit and for federal courts applying Louisiana law.

Bottom line: under the reasoning and holdings in Williams v. GoAuto, the statute does not require a human signature or named affiant; a corporate, unsigned (even automated) certification that explicitly states the required items and is received by the insurer suffices.

### What qualifies as "receipt" by the insurer under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) — is an automated web service/email sufficient?
Question:
The statute says that upon "receipt" of the finance company's notice and certification, the insurer may proceed to cancel. Here, GoAuto's system auto-cancelled when an APAC system emailed and/or pinged a GoAuto web service in the early morning, before any human at GoAuto touched the paperwork. The court relied on Black's definitions and treated the computer's intake as "receipt." Is there Louisiana authority on whether electronic delivery to an automated system constitutes the insurer's receipt for statutory purposes? Would it matter if the 'receipt' was through an API/web service call rather than traditional email? And does it matter that a human only stamps the paper later in the morning?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — under Williams v. GoAuto, electronic delivery to an insurer’s automated intake (whether e‑mail to a system or a web‑service/API “call”) can qualify as the insurer’s “receipt” under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The Fifth Circuit held that GoAuto’s computer receiving APAC’s e‑mail/web‑service request and automatically cancelling the policy satisfied the statutory receipt requirement; the later human stamping did not undo that receipt.

Why — governing doctrine and what the opinion says
- Strict‑compliance rule. Louisiana law requires strict adherence to the statutory cancellation procedures; any defect can render a cancellation ineffective. The court reiterated that rule (citing Delatte, Eaglin, Britten), but emphasized courts cannot add requirements the statute does not contain.  
- What “receipt” means. The Louisiana statute does not define “receipt,” so the Fifth Circuit adopted Black’s Law Dictionary definitions — receipt = “the act of receiving … by taking physical possession,” receive = “to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” The court concluded that the insurer may cancel once it takes “possession, delivery, or custody” of the request. (Williams v. GoAuto Ins., No. 24‑30646, slip op. at pp. ___ (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025)).  
- Automated systems count as “receipt.” The court described APAC’s interaction with GoAuto’s system: APAC “makes a call to a GoAuto web service…a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called… and when it hears its name… it performs the activity.” The court held that the e‑mailed notice received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning — which triggered automatic cancellation — satisfied the statute: “GoAuto’s method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute.”  
- Signature and later human handling are immaterial. The court rejected the plaintiffs’ argument that a handwritten signature or human receipt before cancellation was required; it held the statute does not require a signature and that the later human stamping (after the computer cancelled the policy) did not negate the earlier electronic receipt.

Practical takeaways for similar fact patterns
- Electronic delivery to an insurer’s systems will likely be treated as “receipt” if the insurer’s system actually takes custody/possession (for example, an inbound e‑mail that is ingested and processed or an API/web‑service call that the insurer’s listener accepts and acts on).  
- An API/web‑service call is functionally the same as the web service/e‑mail interaction the court described; the opinion explicitly analyzed a web‑service “call” and accepted that as receipt.  
- Late human review, stamping, or a signature applied after an automated cancellation is unlikely (under Williams) to undermine the fact that the insurer already “received” the cancellation request.

Limitations
- Williams is a Fifth Circuit opinion applying Louisiana law; the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined “receipt” in this context. The Fifth Circuit declined to certify the question, so Williams is persuasive (and binding in the Fifth Circuit) but not an express Louisiana Supreme Court holding. The opinion’s reasoning, however, is explicit and practical: delivery into the insurer’s custody via its computers equals “receipt.”

Bottom line: Under Williams, automated electronic delivery (including API/web‑service calls or system‑ingested e‑mails) that places the cancellation request into the insurer’s custody and triggers the insurer’s system to act constitutes “receipt” under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i); a later human stamp or signature does not defeat that receipt.

### How does the "strict adherence" requirement square with the court’s refusal to add a signature requirement not in the text?
Question:
Louisiana courts demand "strict adherence" to § 9:3550 when a finance company cancels under a power of attorney, and any defect invalidates cancellation. Plaintiffs argued that strict adherence means insisting on a signature and other formalities to fix timing and ensure accuracy. The Fifth Circuit responded that strict adherence isn't an invitation to add requirements not in the statute. Do Louisiana cases define 'strict adherence' as compliance with the express text only, or do they sometimes infer procedural safeguards (like signatures or named preparers) from terms such as 'certifying'? How do cases like Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, and Benitez frame the line between strict textual compliance and judicially implied formalities?
Answer:
Short answer
Louisiana’s “strict adherence” doctrine enforces the statute’s express requirements and the statutory purposes those requirements serve (fixing the time/date of cancellation and protecting the insured’s cure period). It does not license courts to invent additional formalities (like a handwritten signature or a named preparer) that the Legislature did not require. That is exactly what the Fifth Circuit held in Williams v. GoAuto: absent clear textual or controlling state-court authority, courts should not graft a signature requirement onto La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Williams also explains the flip side: Louisiana cases will invalidate cancellations for defects that defeat the statute’s functions.

How the cases fit together
- Delatte: the opinion quotes Delatte for the broad principle that “Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Williams treats Delatte as the source of the strict-adherence rule — i.e., courts will enforce the statute’s requirements closely. (Williams opinion, quoting Delatte at 633 So. 2d 686, 689.)

- Eaglin: Williams also cites Eaglin for the related proposition that “[a]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” That language supports invalidating cancellations when statutorily required elements are missing or when the process has a material defect. (Williams quoting Eaglin, 558 So. 2d at 287.)

- Britten: Williams quotes Britten as explaining the function of “strict adherence”: it (1) “provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation,” and (2) “grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Those functional goals are the touchstone for determining whether a procedural shortcoming is fatal. (Williams quoting Britten, 503 So. 2d at 1154.)

- Benitez: Williams discusses Benitez, which criticized a notice for “not identif[ying] any particular employee … [and] is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” But crucially, Williams points out that Benitez did not hold that lack of a signature alone automatically invalidates a cancellation. Instead Benitez identified the absence of signature/named preparer as a shortcoming among others. (Williams citing Benitez, 285 So. 3d at 579.)

What Williams adds and the governing line
Williams articulates the controlling line: strict adherence requires compliance with the statute’s text and the statutory purposes (the Britten functions). Courts will strike down cancellations that fail to meet the statute’s express requirements or that defeat those purposes. But courts will not create additional procedural requirements — such as a mandatory handwritten signature or a requirement that the notice name the individual preparer — unless the statute says so or Louisiana precedent clearly demands it. As Williams puts it, “Strict adherence … is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.” Williams then concludes that an e‑mailed company statement on letterhead that “certifies” the required facts is consistent with the statute and that receipt can occur electronically. (Williams, Oct. 3, 2025.)

Practical implications
- To satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) under current Louisiana authority (as read in Williams), make sure the statutorily listed items are actually stated and that the insurer can show receipt of that statement. Those are the hallmarks courts enforce.  
- A handwritten signature or named preparer is helpful evidence but, standing alone, is not required by the statute as currently interpreted; however, absence of those elements can be cited by a court (as in Benitez) when other defects are present that undermine the statute’s functions.  
- The safe practice is to draft the certification to mirror the statutory text, document delivery/receipt, and preserve contemporaneous evidence (whether electronic or physical) that the insurer actually received the certifying statement.

Bottom line: “Strict adherence” means strict compliance with the statute and its purposes, not judicial creation of extra formalities. The cases cited (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, Benitez) show that courts will invalidate cancellations that fail to accomplish the statute’s objectives, but they will not invent signature or naming requirements that the statutory text does not contain.

### Can hand-delivery by an affiliate’s or the finance company’s employee qualify as delivery "with a private carrier" under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)?
Question:
The district court floated an alternative ground that the APAC employee who physically delivered/stamped the notices was a 'private carrier' under the statute, but the Fifth Circuit called that rationale 'imaginative and unconventional' and didn't reach it. Is there any Louisiana authority defining 'private carrier' in this statutory context? In practice, would hand-delivery by an employee of the finance company (or an affiliate) count as delivery 'with a private carrier,' or does 'private carrier' imply an independent third-party delivery service? If the email route failed, would placing the documents in a shared filing cabinet controlled by a related management company constitute either 'private carrier' delivery or 'receipt' by the insurer?
Answer:
Short answer
- There is no Louisiana appellate authority that definitively defines “private carrier” for La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Williams v. GoAuto Insurance expressly notes that absence and (because it resolved the case on the e‑mail ground) declines to decide whether an APAC employee could be treated as a “private carrier.” 5th Cir. Op. (Oct. 3, 2025).
- Practically, Williams signals that treating an affiliate’s or the finance company’s own employee as a “private carrier” is a legally uncertain and risky theory. The Fifth Circuit described the district court’s finding to that effect as “imaginative and unconventional” and refused to adopt it. Id.
- On “receipt,” Williams adopts a straightforward possession/custody standard (quoting Black’s): the insurer must take possession, delivery, or custody of the finance company’s request before it may cancel. The opinion accepted automated electronic receipt by the insurer’s system as satisfying that requirement. Id.

Legal standard from Williams
- Strict statutory compliance is required for financed-policy cancellations. The statute requires the finance company to send “by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” specified facts. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) (quoted in Williams).
- “Private carrier”: Williams records that Louisiana courts have not defined the term in this context and the Fifth Circuit declined to address the district court’s conclusion that an APAC employee qualified as one. The court therefore left the term unresolved in Louisiana law. Williams, slip op. at Part I & n. (discussing district court’s view and declining to reach it).
- “Receipt”: Williams accepts ordinary dictionary/Black’s definitions — “the act of receiving … by taking physical possession” — and holds that the insurer may cancel once it “takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” The court expressly found GoAuto’s automated receipt of APAC’s e‑mail sufficient. Williams, slip op. (receipt discussion).

Applying Williams to your three questions
1) Is there Louisiana authority defining “private carrier” in this statutory context?
- No controlling Louisiana decision was identified in Williams. The Fifth Circuit states the absence of Louisiana case law and declines to decide the question. Relying on Williams alone, the term remains legally open in Louisiana.

2) Would hand‑delivery by an employee of the finance company (or an affiliate) qualify as delivery “with a private carrier”?
- Under Williams, it’s unresolved. But the opinion’s tone is instructive: the Fifth Circuit criticized the district court’s view that an APAC employee “neatly fits the definition of private carrier” as “imaginative and unconventional” and refused to adopt it. That suggests a court might view a finance‑company/affiliate employee’s hand‑delivery as an internal delivery rather than the kind of independent third‑party “private carrier” the statute contemplates. Put another way: treating an in‑house or affiliate employee as the statutory “private carrier” is legally uncertain and would be a risky position to press without clearer state authority.

3) If e‑mail fails, would placing the documents in a shared filing cabinet controlled by a related management company be (a) delivery “with a private carrier” or (b) “receipt” by the insurer?
- (a) There is no basis in Williams to call such placement “delivery with a private carrier.” The opinion does not endorse that approach and explicitly declines to treat internal/affiliate handling as a substitute for an independent carrier.
- (b) On “receipt,” Williams requires that the insurer actually take possession, delivery, or custody. The court noted that prior to 2017 the filing room belonged to GoAuto Management Services (not GoAuto), and it treated that factual distinction as relevant to the plaintiffs’ argument that GoAuto never “received” the requests. Because Williams resolved the case on electronic receipt (GoAuto’s system actually received the e‑mail and triggered cancellation), it did not hold that placing documents in an affiliate‑controlled shared cabinet equals “receipt” by the insurer. Thus, placing papers in a shared cabinet controlled by a related management company creates doubt whether the insurer has legally “received” the notice unless the insurer itself takes possession or there is clear evidence the insurer exercises custody/control over that cabinet.

Practical takeaways (what to do if you represent a finance company or an insurer)
- Don’t rely on an internal/affiliate employee to qualify as a “private carrier” unless a binding Louisiana authority later so holds. Williams signals courts may reject that theory.
- If you want to use the “private carrier” route, use an independent third‑party carrier (with proof of delivery) or ensure the insurer’s own employee or system actually receives and acknowledges the notice.
- If e‑mail is used (and the insurer’s system demonstrably receives it), Williams says that satisfies the statute. If e‑mail is unavailable or fails, make sure the insurer actually takes custody (signed receipt by an insurer employee, delivery to an address under the insurer’s control, or documented hand‑delivery to a named insurer representative) rather than merely depositing documents in an affiliate’s shared filing cabinet.

Conclusion
Williams leaves “private carrier” undefined for § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) in Louisiana and rejects, by implication, the safer viability of counting an affiliate’s employee as an independent carrier. It does, however, make clear that “receipt” means actual possession or custody by the insurer (including automated electronic receipt). If you want predictability, obtain actual insurer receipt (or use an independent carrier with proof) rather than relying on internal hand‑delivery or placement in an affiliate‑controlled filing cabinet.

### Does § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)’s "insurer may proceed to cancel" language permit immediate, automated cancellation, or does it require a discretionary human act?
Question:
The insurer 'may proceed to cancel' upon receipt of the compliant notice and certification. GoAuto’s system automatically cancels at the moment of electronic receipt (after the 10-day cure period expires). Is that consistent with the statute’s use of 'may' (suggesting discretion) and the purpose of fixing the exact date/time of cancellation? Is there any Louisiana case law on whether the insurer must make a contemporaneous, discretionary decision after receipt, or whether pre-programmed automated cancellation upon receipt is permissible?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Under Williams v. GoAuto, § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) is satisfied when the insurer takes “receipt” of the premium-finance company’s compliant notice, and an insurer may use an automated process that immediately cancels upon electronic receipt. The Fifth Circuit held that the statute does not require a contemporaneous human, discretionary act or a signed, employee-level certification before cancellation.

Why (the court’s reasoning)
- Statutory trigger is “receipt”: the court construed “receipt” to mean taking possession, delivery, or custody (citing ordinary dictionary definitions) and concluded that electronic receipt by GoAuto’s systems satisfies that requirement. The opinion: “We accept these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.”
- Modern technology accomplishes what people once did: the opinion described the APAC→GoAuto interaction (APAC calls a GoAuto web service; the web service is a “listener” that performs the requested activity when called) and expressly approved that process: “This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto’s method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute.”
- No requirement of a contemporaneous, discretionary human decision: the court observed that “No person is involved until later in the morning after the GoAuto computer system effects cancellation” and still held the procedure compliant. It therefore refused to impose a judicially created requirement that a human must first exercise discretion before cancellation.
- Signature/individual identification not required: relatedly, the court rejected a contention that the statute requires a signature or an identified employee to “certify” the statements, finding no basis in the text or existing Louisiana authority to add that requirement.

Doctrine and statutory purpose
- The court applied strict-adherence principles (Louisiana requires strict compliance with the statute), but also emphasized that strictness does not allow courts to add requirements not found in the statute. Two statutory purposes—fixing the exact time/date of cancellation and protecting a minimum cure period—are served by an electronic receipt that timestamps the cancellation request.

What this means for your question
- Under Williams, an insurer may program an automated cancellation to occur at the moment of electronic receipt (after the cure period) and comply with § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The decision shows the court will treat electronic possession/receipt by an insurer’s systems as the operative “receipt,” and it declined to require a contemporaneous human act or signature before the insurer “may proceed to cancel.”
- The opinion also notes there is little Louisiana appellate authority squarely requiring a human signature or contemporaneous discretion, and the Fifth Circuit declined to certify the issue to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

If you’re briefing or litigating, emphasize Williams’ holdings about “receipt,” electronic/web-service receipt, and the court’s explicit approval of automated cancellation as satisfying the statute’s timing and cure-period functions.

### Why did the court’s finding of statutory compliance dispose of the misrepresentation and good-faith claims with prejudice?
Question:
The plaintiffs (including a third-party accident victim, Jenkins) alleged misrepresentations and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The Fifth Circuit affirms dismissal of all claims after concluding GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with § 9:3550. Are these tort/bad-faith theories in Louisiana entirely derivative of an ineffective cancellation (i.e., if cancellation is valid, the claims necessarily fail)? Could an insured or third party still have a viable claim based on how cancellation was communicated or recorded even if the cancellation itself was effective? How does Jenkins, as a non-insured injured by a GoAuto insured whose policy was canceled, have standing or a cause of action tied to alleged cancellation defects?
Answer:
Short answer
Because the plaintiffs’ tort theories in this case were grounded on the premise that the financed cancellations were ineffective under La. R.S. § 9:3550, the Fifth Circuit treated statutory compliance as the dispositive issue. Louisiana law ties the validity of a finance-company cancellation to strict compliance with § 9:3550, and an effective (statutorily proper) cancellation removes the factual and legal predicate for the misrepresentation and bad‑faith theories the plaintiffs alleged. The Fifth Circuit therefore affirmed dismissal with prejudice once it held GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute.

Why that follows from the opinion (doctrine and application)
- The governing rule: the opinion repeatedly states that Louisiana requires “strict adherence” to the statutory procedure for premium‑finance cancellations and that “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation” (citing Delatte and Eaglin). The statute spells out what the finance company must “certify” to the insurer. The purpose of strict adherence is to fix the time of cancellation and the cure period (Britten), so noncompliance is what gives rise to the legal problem.
- How the plaintiffs tied their tort claims to the statute: Williams, Wright and Jenkins alleged GoAuto’s cancellations were ineffective and that GoAuto made misrepresentations and breached the duty of good faith and fair dealing in that process. The opinion shows the plaintiffs’ tort claims were premised on a statutory defect theory — i.e., if the notice/certification procedures failed, the cancellation was ineffective and that failure supported their claims.
- The court’s disposition: the Fifth Circuit analyzed the statutory requirements (signature/certification; “receipt” by the insurer; method of delivery) and concluded GoAuto’s procedures satisfied § 9:3550 (no signature requirement; electronic receipt via GoAuto’s web service sufficed). Because the cancellations complied with the statute, the district court’s grant of summary judgment for GoAuto and dismissal of all claims with prejudice was affirmed. The opinion concludes: “Because GoAuto’s procedures comply with the statute, the district court did not err…” — which is why the tort/bad‑faith theories fell with the statutory ruling.

What this means about derivative claims and communications/recording
- In this case, the plaintiffs’ misrepresentation and bad‑faith theories were derivative of an alleged ineffective cancellation. The court treated the statutory question as dispositive: if the finance-company notices and insurer’s receipt meet § 9:3550, then the cancellation is effective and the plaintiffs’ defect‑based tort theories lack their foundational fact.
- Could a plaintiff still win on how a cancellation was communicated or recorded even if the cancellation itself was effective? The Fifth Circuit’s practical answer here is no — not if the communication/recording satisfies § 9:3550. The opinion rejected the argument that a post‑cancellation stamped signature or the filing‑room handling rendered the cancellation ineffective because the statute does not require a signature and the insurer “receives” the notice when its systems take possession (the web service call). Thus, methods of communication that comply with the statute do not give rise to the defect that would sustain the derivative tort claims in this case.
- The opinion does not broadly hold that every conceivable tort claim about process or misstatement is impossible once a cancellation is otherwise statutorily valid; it resolves only the plaintiffs’ claims as pled and framed here. The court did not accept the plaintiffs’ suggested signature/receipt defects, and it did not create a new independent tort theory divorced from § 9:3550 compliance.

Why Jenkins (a third‑party accident victim) was dismissed with the others
- The opinion treats Jenkins as a plaintiff whose claims rest on the same theory: that APAC/GoAuto’s cancellation procedures were defective and therefore coverage (or the insurer’s obligations) should have been different at the time of the accident. The Fifth Circuit did not separately analyze Jenkins’s standing or a distinct cause of action; instead, it affirmed dismissal of “all claims” after finding statutory compliance.
- Practically, Jenkins’s claim was dependent on proving an ineffective cancellation (the same factual/legal predicate as the insured plaintiffs). Once the court held the cancellations complied with § 9:3550 (i.e., were effective), the factual basis for holding GoAuto liable to a third‑party on that ground evaporated, and his claims fell with the others.

Bottom line
Under the reasoning the Fifth Circuit used in this opinion, misrepresentation and bad‑faith theories that are premised on a finance‑company cancellation being ineffective turn on strict statutory compliance. If the cancellation procedures meet § 9:3550 (as the court found here), the cancellation is effective and the derivative tort claims fail — which explains why the court disposed of those claims with prejudice. The opinion’s analysis focuses on whether the statutory procedure was followed; it does not create a separate rule allowing tort recovery where the statutory requirements themselves are satisfied.

### When will the Fifth Circuit certify unsettled Louisiana-law questions, and did it make an Erie guess here?
Question:
Plaintiffs asked the court to certify the meaning of 'certifying' and 'receipt' to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing industry-wide importance. The Fifth Circuit declined, citing Jefferson v. Lead Industries, and proceeded to rely on Black’s Law Dictionary and intermediate appellate decisions (KMJ Services, Hodges, Lewis, Benitez). Under Erie, how does the Fifth Circuit weigh Louisiana intermediate appellate decisions and secondary sources when the Louisiana Supreme Court hasn't spoken? What factors make a 'compelling reason' for certification, and why didn't the court find one here despite the lack of controlling authority and the strict-adherence doctrine?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Fifth Circuit will certify Louisiana-law questions only sparingly—“chary about certifying” and requiring a “compelling reason” to do so (Jefferson v. Lead Indus.). Mere absence of a Louisiana Supreme Court decision is not, by itself, enough.  
- Here the court did not certify; instead it made the federal-court prediction of Louisiana law (an Erie “guess”) based on Louisiana intermediate appellate decisions and a recognized secondary source (Black’s). The opinion explains why those authorities were sufficient and why certification was not warranted.

Why (and when) the Fifth Circuit certifies: the standard from the opinion
- The court repeats Jefferson v. Lead Industries: it is “chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” The court also quotes Jefferson’s rule that “that a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court ‘is not sufficient to warrant certification.’” Williams v. GoAuto, slip op. at II (citing Jefferson).
- Put simply (as the opinion applies it): the Fifth Circuit asks for more than the mere lack of a Louisiana Supreme Court ruling. It will consider whether existing Louisiana authority and persuasive secondary sources allow a reliable prediction of state law; if they do, the court will usually decide the question rather than certify.

How the court weighs intermediate appellate decisions and secondary sources under Erie
- The opinion shows the court treats Louisiana intermediate appellate decisions as persuasive guidance (not binding state‑supreme rulings) and will follow them when they are clear and on point. The court surveyed KMJ Services, Hodges, Lewis, and Benitez and used their language and holdings to illuminate the statutory phrases at issue. Williams, slip op. I.
- Where those decisions are inconclusive, the court will consider other persuasive authorities (here Black’s Law Dictionary). The panel says “we accept these definitions [from Black’s] and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” Williams, slip op. I (receipt discussion).
- If the intermediate cases plus secondary sources together yield a principled, textually consistent answer that does not require the court to invent new state-law rules, the Fifth Circuit will predict Louisiana law rather than certify.

What counts as a “compelling reason” (as implied by the opinion)
- The opinion does not enumerate a checklist, but applying Jefferson and Williams shows what is not enough and what the court looks for:
  - Not enough: mere absence of a Louisiana Supreme Court decision; a party’s assertion of industry‑wide importance alone. Williams rejects the plaintiffs’ contention that industry importance by itself required certification.
  - What the court will want (implicitly): situations where persuasive guidance is lacking or genuinely conflicting, where the intermediate appellate law is ambiguous or squarely split, or where resolution by the state’s highest court is necessary to avoid widespread, inconsistent application of state law. If those conditions do not obtain and the federal court can make a reasonable, principled prediction from available sources, certification will be denied.
- The Williams panel applied that approach: because intermediate appellate authority and Black’s provided a workable, text-based interpretation (and because strict‑adherence doctrine did not authorize a court to add a signature requirement), there was no “compelling reason” to invoke certification. Williams, slip op. I–II (“Strict adherence … is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.”; “We decline to certify the question.”).

Did the court make an Erie guess here?
- Yes. By declining certification and resolving the statutory questions itself, the Fifth Circuit performed the typical Erie task: it predicted how the Louisiana Supreme Court would interpret the statute using the best available state-law indicators (intermediate appellate decisions and a leading secondary source). The opinion explicitly relies on those state-court decisions and Black’s to reach its conclusions about “certifying” and “receipt,” then applies that prediction to affirm the district court.

Bottom line
- The Fifth Circuit will certify Louisiana questions only when there is a true need that cannot be met by existing state-court decisions or persuasive authorities. In Williams the panel concluded the intermediate appellate decisions plus Black’s provided a sufficiently clear basis to predict Louisiana law; therefore it made an Erie prediction rather than certify to the Louisiana Supreme Court.
