Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 4: # Certification, Signatures, and Corporate Acts: What Does the Statute Require?

This chapter examines what it means for a premium finance company to “certify” under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), whether a natural‑person signature or identification of an individual is required, and how courts treat institutional statements, automated transmissions, and signature devices in the cancellation of financed insurance policies. The legal issues arise at the intersection of a Louisiana statute that conditions insurer action on a finance company’s “certifying” certain facts and the long‑standing Louisiana principle that finance‑company cancellations must be strictly accomplished. The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in the GoAuto appeal (No. 24‑30646) crystallizes the modern approach: a certification may be an institutional act accomplished by the finance company’s systems and business records; neither a handwritten signature nor the printed name of a particular employee is necessary as a matter of statutory interpretation, although practical considerations counsel careful drafting and recordkeeping.

## Textual analysis: the statute speaks in terms of the finance company “certifying”

The statute frames cancellation by the premium finance company in corporate, not personal, terms. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires that the finance company “send[] to the insurer . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” specific facts: (i) the power of attorney exists in the agreement, (ii) the agreement is in default and the default is uncured, (iii) upon default a notice to the insured was sent specifying the sending date, and (iv) copies of the notice were sent to all other interested parties with names and addresses. The statutory text does not use verbs like “sign” or “swear,” nor does it say the certification must be by a named or natural person. The operative actor is the “insurance premium finance company,” and the obligation is framed as the company’s certification of facts.

Textual analysis therefore starts with two points. First, the statute’s corporate idiom suggests the legislature contemplated institutional acts—statements issued by the finance company—rather than narrowly circumscribing the form of authentication. Second, because Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to the procedures laid out in the statute, courts must enforce precisely the elements the statute lists, but that strictness does not license judicial addition of prerequisites not present in the text. The doctrine of strict adherence serves two articulated functions: fixing the exact time and date of cancellation and ensuring a minimum period during which a default may be cured (Britten v. Reavis). That purpose is served by reliable, verifiable statements establishing the statutory elements; reliability can be achieved by means other than a hand‑written signature.

## Benitez v. Elsayed: absence of signature among several deficiencies; not dispositive by itself

Benitez v. Elsayed illustrates how Louisiana appellate courts handle deficiencies in cancellation notices. In Benitez the court observed that the notice “does not identify any particular employee . . . is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” The court’s discussion treated the absence of signature as one among several shortcomings showing the notice failed to satisfy the statutory schema; crucially, Benitez did not hold that an unsigned certification is per se invalid. Rather, the opinion identifies the absence of a named signatory or signature as evidentiary context for finding the overall cancellation defective.

The limited lesson of Benitez is therefore cautionary: a signature or an identified signatory can be probative of authenticity and corporate authorization, but its absence will not automatically defeat a cancellation if the required statutory facts are otherwise properly certified in a form reasonably attributable to the finance company. Benitez should not be read as establishing a categorical, freestanding requirement of a natural‑person signature when the finance company’s institutional statement, business records, and means of transmission suffice to demonstrate the company’s certification.

## Corporate certification without an identified individual: letterhead, institutional statements, and automated processes

Corporate acts are often expressed through documents that do not bear a natural‑person signature. A letterhead, an affidavit bearing an officer’s typed name, a certificate executed by corporate counsel, or a corporate email may be sufficient to attribute a statement to the corporation. In the finance‑company cancellation context the operative question is whether the insurer can reasonably attribute the required “statement certifying” the statutory elements to the finance company itself and whether the insurer received that statement in a form that fixes the time and content of the communication.

Factors that support attribution of a statement to the finance company include: the use of company letterhead; the content of the statement matching the statutory checklist; contemporaneous business records (such as system logs, mailing affidavits, or saved email copies); metadata showing transmission and receipt timestamps; and policies or procedures reflecting that the communication was generated in the regular course of business. These attributes may be as or more persuasive than a hand‑written signature because they can establish both the content and the delivery time—precisely the two functions the statute was designed to protect (fixing time/date and ensuring opportunity to cure).

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in the GoAuto case embodies this reasoning. APAC’s computer system generated and emailed to GoAuto a copy of the ten‑day notice and a cancellation request that included a certification reciting the statutory items. GoAuto’s web service received the email and, operating automatically, executed the cancellation. Later that morning—after the insurer’s system had already effected cancellation—an APAC employee applied a signature stamp to the printed documents. The Fifth Circuit concluded that Louisiana law permits a company to certify without a named individual signing the notice and that the electronic transmission and the insurer’s reception of the notice satisfied the statutory requirement that cancellation be requested “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement.” Thus a corporate certification can be accomplished through institutional statements and automated processes, so long as the insurer’s receipt of the company’s statement is established.

## Signature stamps, electronic signatures, and attestations versus substantive content requirements

Courts distinguish between form (how a statement is authenticated) and substance (what the statement says). La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) sets out substantive requirements—the four items that must be certified. It does not prescribe a particular attestation ritual. Accordingly, judicial analysis focuses on whether the certification conveys the required facts and whether the insurer received that communication in a way that fixed the time and content of the request for cancellation.

Signature stamps and later‑affixed signatures raise timing and authenticity questions. A stamp applied after an insurer’s automated receipt of an unsigned electronic certification will not necessarily defeat cancellation if other evidence shows that the finance company’s system sent the certification and the insurer received it. The key inquiry is whether the insurer had “receipt” of the certification—i.e., possession or control—such that the insurer could rely on it. A signature applied afterward may be convenient corroboration, but courts will examine the record as a whole.

Electronic signatures and electronic records are widely accepted in commercial practice and by many statutes governing transactions and evidence. Where a statute is silent about the form of certification, courts frequently accept electronic transmissions, system logs, timestamps, and authenticated email messages as satisfying any implicit evidentiary need. Practitioners should be aware of both federal (E‑SIGN) and state enactments adopting the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) or similar rules: these regimes typically validate electronic records and electronic signatures as legally effective, absent contrary legislative intent for a particular statute. Even when electronic means are acceptable, parties litigating their sufficiency will point to the same evidentiary themes: can the recipient tie the electronic record to the finance company and to the date and time required by the statutory cancellation cycle?

Substantively, nothing in the statute permits a finance company to certify false content. The “certifying” language contemplates an assertion of fact—authority (power of attorney), default status, notice to the insured, and notice to interested parties. An attestation that parrots the statutory checklist but is untrue in fact will not satisfy the statute’s purpose. Thus, while courts may be forgiving about form, they remain exacting about substance.

## Avoiding judicially adding requirements under a strict‑adherence framework

Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to the statutory procedures when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy. Strict adherence is an analytical rule focused on compliance with the statute’s text and aims, not a license for courts to graft extra-textual requirements (such as a hand‑written signature or identification of a particular employee) that the legislature did not enact. The Fifth Circuit emphasized this point: strict adherence is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures; the statute speaks of the finance company “certifying” and does not demand a signature.

This principle has two practical consequences. First, litigants challenging a cancellation should point to concrete statutory deficiencies—e.g., an absence of any statement that one of the statutory elements (like the existence of a power of attorney or the date of the insured notice) was satisfied—rather than relying on the absence of a signature alone. Second, practitioners seeking to ensure robustness against challenge should nonetheless remember that courts evaluate the record; good practice is to produce clear, verifiable evidence of the finance company’s certification and the insurer’s receipt. The policy-driven rationale behind strict adherence—fixing date/time and giving the insured an opportunity to cure—supports accepting technologically modern, well‑documented corporate processes that perform those functions.

## Practical guidance and a synthesis test

Putting the doctrinal points together yields a practical, testable framework for analyzing whether a finance company’s “certifying” function under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) has been satisfied:

1. Substance: Does the statement sent to the insurer actually recite the four statutory items? If any statutory element is missing or contradicted by the record, strict adherence fails.

2. Attribution: Can the recipient reasonably attribute the statement to the premium finance company (through letterhead, system identifiers, corporate email address, business records, or other indicia of corporate origin)? Absence of a named individual is not dispositive if institutional indicia are present.

3. Receipt: Did the insurer receive the statement in a manner that fixes the time of receipt and permits the insurer to act (electronic receipt, system logs, time‑stamped email, or physical possession)? Courts will treat automated receipt by an insurer’s computer system as “receipt,” provided the transmission is sufficiently verifiable.

4. Timing and opportunity to cure: Do the communications and timestamps establish the period the statute gives for curing a default? The record should show the notice to the insured was sent and that the insurer had the finance company’s cancellation request at a time consistent with statutory deadlines.

5. Authenticity and corroboration: Do contemporaneous records (mailing affidavits, metadata, system logs, retained copies) corroborate the statement? While a hand‑written signature can be such a corroboration, other reliable evidence will suffice.

Under this synthesis, signature stamps, later‑affixed ink signatures, and unsigned but corporately attributable electronic statements can all be adequate, so long as the above criteria are met. The Fifth Circuit’s GoAuto decision applied this approach: the automated email from APAC containing the statutory certification, coupled with system receipt and corroborating business processes, satisfied the statute despite the absence of a named individual’s signature at the moment the insurer’s system effected cancellation.

## Conclusion: doctrine and practice in tension

Doctrinally, Louisiana requires strict compliance with the statutory checklist for finance‑company cancellations, but that doctrine does not compel courts to impose a signature requirement where the text is silent. The operative actor under the statute is the corporate finance company, and corporate certification may be accomplished by institutional statements and electronic processes that reliably communicate the statutory facts and are received by the insurer. Practically, parties should not rely on that doctrinal point alone; prudent practice is to produce clear evidence—letterhead, designated signatories or officers (if possible), authenticated system logs, time‑stamped emails, and mailing affidavits—that firmly tie the certification to the finance company and to the time of receipt. That combination of textual fidelity and robust recordkeeping best secures a cancellation against both statutory scrutiny and litigation challenges.
