Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 2: # Delivery, Certification, and Receipt in a Digital Era

This chapter analyzes how a modern statutory scheme for cancellation of financed insurance policies adapts to electronic communications and automated processing. The focal point is La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3), the provision that governs how a premium finance company may cancel an insured’s policy when the insured defaults on an installment agreement. Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, decided by the Fifth Circuit in 2025, supplies a recent, concrete application of the statute’s text to contemporary practices: e-mail notices, web-service listeners, automatic cancellations, and post‑hoc affixation of signature stamps. Williams is an ideal vehicle to teach three doctrinal problems frequently encountered today: (1) what the statute means by permissible modes of delivery; (2) what it means to “certify” compliance with statutory prerequisites and whether a signature is required; and (3) what it means for an insurer to have “receipt” of a cancellation request in an era when machines increasingly act as intermediaries.

The chapter proceeds in sections that mirror those questions. Each section explains the governing law and the analytic tools courts use to apply it, draws lessons from Williams and related Louisiana cases, and concludes with practical rules and considerations for litigants and practitioners.

## Permissible delivery modes under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a): mail, private carrier, and electronic mail

The statute identifies three modes by which a finance company may effect or request cancellation: (1) depositing in the mail, (2) delivering with a private carrier, or (3) sending via electronic mail. The statutory purpose—repeatedly emphasized by Louisiana courts—is strict adherence to the prescribed procedure so the precise time and date of cancellation is ascertainable and the insured is granted a minimum cure period. See Delatte v. Lemotte; Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.; Britten v. Reavis. Those decisions underscore that compliance is not merely formal; it is designed to protect insureds from unexpected lapses and to fix a bright-line moment when protection ends.

When the statute expressly includes electronic mail as a permissible mode, two analytical moves follow. First, courts treat “electronic mail” as functionally equivalent to the former option of “mail,” but they also must reconcile the differing technical qualities of electronic communications (speed, instantaneous delivery, server receipt, logs, bounce notices). Second, because “[s]trict adherence” is required, courts will examine whether the substantive content of the electronic notice satisfied the statute’s certification and copy requirements and whether adequate evidence of sending and receipt exists. Thus, an e‑mail that lacks the mandated statements or fails to show that copies were sent to interested parties will not meet the statute even if the transmission technically occurred.

Williams is instructive here: the statute’s inclusion of electronic mail allowed the Fifth Circuit to treat APAC’s e‑mail notice and the technical interaction between APAC’s systems and GoAuto’s web services as a permissible delivery mode. The court did not accept arguments that electronic delivery required additional procedural protections beyond those the statute specifies. From this follows a practical rule: when a statute lists electronic mail, courts will generally permit electronic delivery so long as the substantive requirements the statute imposes are satisfied and there is reliable evidence of transmission and receipt.

## What “certifying” means: corporate certification versus individual signatory

The statute requires the premium finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” several enumerated facts—e.g., the financing agreement contains a power of attorney, the agreement is in default, a notice was sent to the insured, and copies were sent to persons with an interest. The word “certify” implies an assertion of fact with some level of formal assurance, but the statute does not explicitly prescribe the formality of that assurance (for instance, a handwritten signature, a notarization, or an affidavit). Louisiana appellate authority has repeatedly required strict compliance with the substance of the certification requirement, but has not uniformly required a particular form of authentication.

Williams draws two important inferences from that silence. First, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the statute contemplates certification by the insurance premium finance company as an entity; the company itself may “certify” the required facts. Second, absent clearer state-law authority to the contrary, the court declined to add a signature requirement where the statute did not provide one. A corporate statement on company letterhead that expresses the required certification language was enough in Williams because it performed the function the statute requires—identifying the elements necessary to fix the time of cancellation and to provide notice to the insured.

From an analytic standpoint, courts will ask two questions when assessing a “certifying” statement: (1) does the statement contain the specific factual elements the statute requires? and (2) is there sufficient indicia that the finance company, as an entity, intended to assume responsibility for those statements (for example, company letterhead, corporate indicia, or an authorization mechanism)? If the answer to both is yes, many courts (including the Fifth Circuit in Williams) will treat the statement as a valid certification even if no named human signatory appears.

But this is not an open-ended rule in favor of form over substance. Louisiana courts remain attentive to the statutory purpose: if the absence of a particular form (for example, no signatory, no identification of to whom copies were addressed) undermines the statute’s ability to fix the date/time or to give the insured a meaningful opportunity to cure, courts may find the certification defective. For that reason, the content and the context of the certification are critical.

## Is a signature required? Survey of Louisiana appellate decisions: KMJ Servs.; Hodges; Lewis; Benitez

The Williams opinion canvasses several Louisiana appellate decisions that touch on signatures and certifications:

- KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood paraphrased the statutory requirement as needing “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute, but did not elaborate on signature requirements.
- Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins. allowed cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company” but did not discuss whether the statement required a signature.
- Lewis v. Julien is similarly noncommittal on signatures.
- Benitez v. Elsayed noted the absence of a signature and the lack of identification of any preparer as shortcomings, but did not hold that absence of a signature, standing alone, invalidated the cancellation.

Taken together, these opinions show that Louisiana courts have not harmonized on a bright-line rule requiring a named or signed individual for certification. Williams follows that pattern: it assumed—based on statutory silence and prior indecisive authority—that courts should not judicially create a signature requirement where the statute does not impose one. The Fifth Circuit observed that one earlier plaintiff-side decision said a cancellation document lacked identification and signature, but that decision stopped short of holding the absence of signature per se invalidating.

Practical synthesis for students: when a statute is textually silent about signatures, courts prefer to enforce the statute’s enumerated content requirements rather than add formalities. Thus, a carefully drafted corporate certification on company letterhead memorializing each statutory element will often satisfy the certification requirement. Nonetheless, parties pushing against electronic or unsigned certifications will emphasize (a) the statute’s purpose in protecting insureds, (b) the evidentiary risks of proving who made the certification, and (c) the potential for fraud when no individual identity is attached. In close cases, the absence of a signature may be one among several defects that together render cancellation ineffective.

## Understanding “receipt” under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i): ordinary meaning, possession, and custody

The statute conditions an insurer’s ability to cancel on its “receipt” of the notice and statement from the premium finance company: “Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” The statute does not define “receipt,” and Louisiana appellate courts have not squarely defined it.

Williams takes its cue from dictionary definitions and ordinary meaning: receipt is the act of receiving, usually by taking physical possession, and receive is to come into possession of or get from some outside source. The Fifth Circuit concluded that “receipt” requires the insurer take possession, delivery, or custody of the request for cancellation. That possession may be physical, but in contemporary practice it suffices for the insurer’s systems—its servers or web services—to take custody of the incoming electronic communication.

The canonical policy reasons for requiring receipt are apparent: an insurer should not be treated as having received a notice unless the insurer or its agent has the message within its sphere of control so that it can act—and so insureds know when coverage is at risk. A “sent but never received” line of argument thus remains viable for plaintiffs: if the notice never reaches the insurer or is misaddressed, then no lawful cancellation has been requested and the insurer cannot rely on a purported cancellation.

From an analytic perspective, courts will look to operational facts when deciding whether an insurer “received” a notice. Useful inquiries include: Was the notice sent to an address or endpoint the insurer maintains? Did the insurer’s systems log the incoming transmission or otherwise process it? Was the transmission acknowledged by an automated system (e.g., a delivery receipt, bounce message, or application-level acknowledgment)? Were human employees aware of or in a position to access the notice within a reasonable time? Evidence such as server logs, time-stamped web-service calls, and employee testimony about standard operating procedures often proves pivotal.

Williams makes two important holdings about receipt. First, an insurer may have “receipt” when its computer systems accept an incoming electronic message and act on it—here when GoAuto’s web service was “listening,” accepted the call from APAC’s system, and effectuated cancellation. Second, “receipt” does not require immediate human review; an automated interaction that places the notice into the insurer’s custody satisfies the statutory predicate because the insurer’s own systems, operating within its control, took possession of the notice.

## Electronic receipt and automated processing: when a server’s intake constitutes receipt

Automated systems complicate traditional notions of receipt. Where a web service automatically consumes messages and triggers processing, is the insurer a receiver in the statutory sense? Williams answers in the affirmative when the system is designed to accept such messages, process them, and maintain electronic records or logs. The Fifth Circuit emphasized the nature of the interaction: APAC’s process “made a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation . . . basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.” That automated call-and-response was enough to constitute insurer receipt for statutory purposes.

Two doctrinal caveats follow.

First, automation does not automatically create receipt in all circumstances. Where an insurer’s system rejects or misroutes messages, or where the system is not configured to accept messages from a particular sender, courts may find no receipt. The crucial fact is whether the insurer (through its agents or systems) had control and custody of the message.

Second, automated receipt is tied to evidentiary records. If the insurer’s system accepted the message, it should—indeed will typically—generate logs, time stamps, database records, or audit trails. Courts will look to those artifacts to determine whether and when receipt occurred. Absent logs, a litigant challenging receipt will point to the absence of proof and to the timing of human involvement (for example, that cancellation occurred at midnight when no human could have read the notice), arguing that the insurer acted without lawful receipt.

For litigators and companies, Williams suggests a robust pragmatic rule: when an insurer intends to rely on automated intake, maintain reliable, tamper-evident logs that show message acceptance, the content accepted, and the action taken upon acceptance. Where practical, include an automatic acknowledgment message or store-and-forward receipts that demonstrate receipt and chain-of-custody.

## Proof of mailing and proof of electronic transmission: evidentiary considerations

The statute also requires an affirmation that copies of the notice were sent to persons with an interest and that proof of mailing is available. Proof of mailing and proof of electronic transmission differ in their mechanics but serve the same evidentiary function: they make possible retrospective verification of whether and when the required communications occurred.

For traditional mail, Louisiana practice recognizes affidavits of mailing, certified mail receipts, and post office records. For electronic communications, proof often rests on system logs, e-mail headers and metadata, SMTP server records, delivery notifications, transactional logs from web services, and third-party e-mail service provider statements. Courts will evaluate whether these records are reliable, adequately authenticated, and free from indicia of tampering.

Williams highlights the practical evidentiary battleground. APAC’s cancellation request stated that “affidavit proof of mailing is available upon request” and included copies of the notices. The Fifth Circuit accepted that statement as a valid corporate certification. But the opinion also underscores the importance of keeping contemporaneous records: if a dispute arises, courts will demand to see the supporting affidavits, server logs, or other documentation.

Best practices drawn from Williams and general evidentiary rules:

- Preserve unaltered server and application logs that show timestamps, sender and recipient endpoints, message contents or hashes, and status codes.
- Use authenticated affidavits from custodians of records explaining the logging process and chain-of-custody.
- Retain copies of notices in their transmitted form and, if attachments are included, preserve those as well.
- If using third-party e-mail or hosting providers, obtain and preserve transactional logs or statements from those providers.

Carefully collected electronic evidence eliminates disputes about whether a notice was sent and whether the insurer received it in its control.

## The unsettled ‘private carrier’ path: what it could mean and why courts may avoid it

One statutory alternative for delivery is “depositing . . . with a private carrier.” The district court in Williams invoked a novel definition of “private carrier” (drawing analogies from a railroad case) to justify receipt, but the Fifth Circuit declined to rest its decision on that ground. Louisiana courts have not thoroughly defined “private carrier” in this statutory context.

The uncertainty stems from two sources. First, “private carrier” can mean different things in different statutory contexts; it might embrace courier services (e.g., FedEx), independent contractors, or even employees acting as agents in some contexts. Second, invoking “private carrier” in cases involving e‑mail or web services risks stretching the term beyond its ordinary meaning in ways that might undermine legislative intent to provide clear methods for cancellation and proof.

Because the e‑mail mechanism in Williams satisfied the statute, the Fifth Circuit avoided developing a general doctrine defining “private carrier.” This reticence is typical: courts prefer not to expand statutory terms in novel directions unless necessary. Practically, parties should avoid litigating the “private carrier” question when electronic transmission clearly satisfies the statutory language; if relying on courier or messenger delivery, ensure proper documentation (tracking numbers, delivery confirmations, chain-of-custody statements).

## Relevance of electronic transactions law (e.g., UETA/ESIGN) to electronic notices and signatures

Two bodies of law validate electronic records and signatures: state versions of the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) and the federal Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act (ESIGN). Louisiana has adopted UETA (Louisiana’s adoption is codified in statutes governing electronic transactions). These laws provide that electronic records and electronic signatures have legal effect with respect to transactions involving interstate commerce and many intra‑state matters, subject to limited exceptions.

Key principles from UETA/ESIGN relevant to § 9:3550:

- An “electronic record” satisfies statutory requirements for writing unless the statute explicitly requires a non-electronic form. Because § 9:3550 expressly authorizes electronic mail, an electronic record is permissible.
- An “electronic signature” can take many forms (typed name, an image of a signature, a digitized signature block, or other electronic sound, symbol, or process executed or adopted by a person with intent to sign). UETA/ESIGN generally validate such signatures if parties intended to sign electronically and agreed to do business electronically.
- The statutes support the position that the absence of a handwritten signature does not, standing alone, render an electronic certification invalid.

Williams implicitly relies on these modern principles: by permitting electronic mail and accepting corporate statements without handwritten signatures, the court’s approach coheres with the principles underlying UETA/ESIGN. But two limitations are important. First, if a particular statutory provision explicitly requires a handwritten or notarized signature, UETA/ESIGN may not preempt that requirement. Second, even when an electronic signature is permissible, courts will scrutinize authentication and intent: was the entity that purportedly issued the certification the party that actually authorized it?

Practitioners should therefore invoke UETA/ESIGN to support the validity of electronic notices, but also preserve contemporaneous evidence of authorization and intent (for example, internal authorizations, records that show system configuration, or published procedures indicating the company’s use of electronic certification).

## Applying the doctrine: tests and practical checklists

Bring together the doctrinal points into concrete, testable rules a student or lawyer can apply to a new fact pattern.

When will a premium finance company’s electronic notice satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)?

1. Substantive compliance: The notice itself must contain, or be accompanied by, the statutorily required certification language addressing (i) the power of attorney, (ii) the default, (iii) that the 10‑day notice was sent to the insured and the date of sending, and (iv) that copies were sent to persons with interest and their names/addresses. A corporate statement on company letterhead that recites these items ordinarily suffices.

2. Sender attribution and intent: The communication should bear indicia that it comes from the premium finance company (letterhead, domain name, authenticated account, or a designated web-service endpoint). Absent a named human signatory, the corporate indicia must still make it reasonable to conclude the company published the certification.

3. Receipt by the insurer: The insurer must take possession or custody. Electronic receipt occurs when the insurer’s designated endpoint (e-mail address, web service) accepts the communication and places it within the insurer’s control, whether by automated processing or by storage on a server. Evidence of receipt—server logs, application acknowledgments, or delivery receipts—will be decisive.

4. Proof preserving and availability: Proof of mailing (physical) or proof of transmission (electronic logs, delivery receipts, affidavits of custodian) should be generated contemporaneously and preserved. A statement that affidavits are “available upon request” is helpful but not dispositive; courts will expect to see the underlying records if challenged.

5. Cure period integrity: The fact of receipt must enable the statute’s goal—fixing a date/time to measure any cure period. If the method of delivery subverts the insured’s opportunity to cure (for example, the message was routed to an inaccessible inbox or rejected by spam filters and the finance company never followed up), a court may find the cancellation ineffective.

If these elements are met, courts inclined to follow Williams will validate the cancellation even in the absence of a handwritten signature and even if automated systems acted without immediate human review.

## Unresolved questions and areas for litigation

Williams answers important questions but leaves others open, presenting fertile ground for future litigation:

- When does an insurer’s automated system not constitute “receipt”? Facts that might produce a different result include unacknowledged server rejections, misconfiguration that causes drop/queue retention without insurer control, or messages routed to a third-party address not controlled by the insurer.
- How much identification must a certification include to be meaningful? A mere boilerplate statement that “certifies” the items may be insufficient if there is evidence of fraud or lack of authorization.
- What is the role of “private carrier” for non‑electronic but non-postal methods of delivery? Courts may eventually define the term, but Williams demonstrates judicial reluctance to expand statutory meanings unless necessary.
- How will courts apply UETA/ESIGN’s authentication and intent requirements when parties dispute whether an entity intended to be bound by an electronic certification? Technical authentication standards and contemporaneous internal documentation will be key.

## Practical takeaways for counsel and companies

- Draft certification notices to mirror the statutory language verbatim, and include explicit references to the statute so the communication leaves no doubt about the intended legal effect.
- Use corporate letterhead, designated sender domains, and process documentation that demonstrates the finance company’s authorization to send the notice.
- Design system interactions so that the insurer’s endpoint both accepts and logs incoming cancellation requests; preserve those logs and obtain automatic acknowledgments where feasible.
- Maintain affidavits of mailing or authenticated export of electronic transmission records and custodian affidavits explaining log creation and retention.
- If possible, include a named custodian or authorized agent in the notice (even if not strictly required) to reduce litigation risk—this small step can avoid argument over the absence of a human signature.
- When relying on courier or other nonpostal physical delivery, preserve tracking numbers and delivery confirmations to support a “private carrier” chain-of-custody argument if needed.

Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance illustrates how a court approaches statutory delivery and certification requirements in a digital age. The statute’s text, the purpose of strict compliance, and contemporary electronic-transactions law together produce a framework in which corporate certifications delivered electronically and accepted by insurer-controlled systems can satisfy statutory prerequisites even without a handwritten signature. The controlling themes are substance over added formalities, reliable evidence of transmission and receipt, and preservation of the institutional capacity to fix the date/time of cancellation and to protect insureds’ cure rights. As automated processing and electronic communications proliferate, litigators and companies will need to focus on designing defensible procedures and on collecting contemporaneous evidentiary artifacts to demonstrate that the law’s protections were respected.
