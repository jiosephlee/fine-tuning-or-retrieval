Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Louisiana Statutory Doctrine—Strict Compliance, Notice, Certification, and Receipt

This chapter explains the Louisiana statutory regime that governs cancellation of insurance policies when the premiums are financed by a premium-finance company. The statute at issue — La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) — creates a detailed procedure that a premium-finance company must follow to exercise a power of attorney to cancel an insured’s policy after an uncured default. Louisiana courts have long described adherence to that procedure as “strict,” and they have treated defects as having potentially dispositive consequences. Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, a recent Fifth Circuit decision applying Louisiana law, tests two interpretive points that commonly arise in contemporary practice: what it means for the finance company to “certify” the required facts, and what it means for the insurer to have “receipt” of the finance company’s notice — issues complicated by electronic communications and automated insurer systems. The chapter sets out the statute’s text and structure, explains the logic of strict compliance, analyzes the contested terms “certifying” and “receipt,” and offers practical rules for application to new fact patterns.

## Text and structure of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)

Section 9:3550(G) governs the interplay of insurance and premium financing. The provision recognizes that a premium-finance agreement can contain a power of attorney that allows the financer to cancel an insured’s policy on the financer’s default. But that power is not unfettered; the statute conditions the financer’s exercise of the power on procedural steps designed to fix the date of cancellation and to preserve the insured’s opportunity to cure. The critical subsection, § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b), requires the premium finance company, “by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail,” to send to the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four enumerated facts: that (i) the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; (ii) the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; (iii) a ten-day notice of cancellation was sent to the insured upon default and specifying the date of sending; and (iv) copies of the ten-day notice were sent to all persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest in any loss, with those names and addresses specified. Upon “receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement,” the insurer may treat cancellation as requested and proceed to cancel the policy. The statute thus imposes two linked obligations on the finance company — sending both the ten-day notice to the insured and a separate certified statement and copy of the notice to the insurer — and it conditions the insurer’s authority to cancel on its receipt of the latter.

Understanding what the statute literally demands is the first step in any dispute. The statute tells us what must be in the finance company’s statement and to whom it must be sent, and it enumerates methods of transmission that include postal, private carrier, and electronic mail. The statute’s text does not expressly require a signature, nor does it define “certifying” or “receipt.” Louisiana jurisprudence fills some of those gaps by insisting on strict observance of the statutory sequence and substance, but it leaves open how courts should treat modern transmission methods and electronic attestations.

## Preconditions for cancellation after uncured default

The statute creates a series of preconditions that must be satisfied before a financed policy can be cancelled by the insurer at the direction of the finance company. First, the finance agreement must contain a valid power of attorney authorizing cancellation upon default. Second, the financer must give the insured a ten-day notice of cancellation on default, thereby creating a minimum cure period. Third, the finance company must send the insurer, by one of the statutorily authorized means, a copy of that notice together with the statutorily required “statement certifying” the enumerated facts. Finally, the insurer must have “receipt” of that notice and statement; upon receipt, the insurer is authorized to consider cancellation requested and may cancel the contract. The statute therefore creates a tripartite sequence: (1) notice to the insured with a ten-day cure window; (2) statement and copy of the notice transmitted to the insurer; and (3) insurer’s receipt and exercise of its cancellation authority. Each step matters because the statute’s twin purposes are to fix the precise cancellation moment and to insure the insured’s realistic opportunity to cure.

## Required contents of the finance company’s statement certifying statutory facts

The statute prescribes the content of the “statement certifying.” That statement must affirm, in substance, the four core matters identified above: the existence of a power of attorney in the finance agreement; that the finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; that the ten-day notice was sent to the insured and the date of sending; and that copies of the ten-day notice were sent to anyone shown in the finance agreement to have an insurable interest, with their names and addresses specified. Practitioners should read the statute literally: the statement must put the insurer on notice of each required fact so that the insurer can determine the authenticity and timeliness of the cancellation request and so that the date of cancellation can be fixed.

That the statute uses the word “certifying” signals something more than a perfunctory notice: it requires the finance company to make an explicit attestation that the steps were taken. But the word itself does not, on its face, define the formal mechanics of the attestation — whether it must be signed by a particular individual, whether it must be under oath, or whether it may be made through an automated or corporate-level statement. Williams v. GoAuto engages that interpretive question directly.

## The insured’s ten-day notice and opportunity to cure

A central protection for the insured is the ten-day notice requirement. The statute requires that, upon default, the premium finance company send a ten-day notice of cancellation to the insured, which creates the minimum period for the insured to cure the default. Louisiana courts have repeatedly emphasized that this cure period is not a mere formality; it is an essential element of the statutory protection. The required content for the finance company’s statement to the insurer includes specifying the date on which the ten-day notice was sent, so that the cancellation date can be determined and the insured and the insurer can know whether the cure window had run. As later discussions show, any defect that undermines the existence or the date of that notice threatens to defeat the cancellation entirely.

## Notice to other interested parties and loss payees

The statute also requires that copies of the ten-day notice be sent to “all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur thereunder,” with the names and addresses specified in the certifying statement. This protects lienholders, governmental agencies, or other third parties whose interest in the insured property or policy could be affected by cancellation. Failure to send these notices or to identify the recipients in the certifying statement is a statutory defect that, under Louisiana decisions, may invalidate the exercise of the finance company’s power to cancel.

## The insurer’s authority to cancel upon receipt of notice and statement

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) conditions the insurer’s authority to cancel on “receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” The statute therefore distinguishes between the finance company’s act of sending and the insurer’s act of receiving; cancellation power arises only upon the latter. That distinction matters in practice: if a court finds the insurer did not receive the required statement and copy, the insurer’s cancellation will be ineffective regardless of the finance company’s intent. The statute’s sequencing thus assigns the decisive role in effectuating cancellation to the insurer’s possession of the statement, not merely to the finance company’s transmission.

## Louisiana decisions requiring strict adherence to premium-finance cancellation statutes

Louisiana appellate courts have repeatedly held that strict adherence to § 9:3550(G) is required when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel. Cases such as Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686 (La. App. Cir. 1993), and Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284 (La. App. Cir. 1990), articulate the basic rule: any defect in the prescribed process can render a cancellation ineffective. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149 (La. App. Cir. 1987), explains the policy behind strictness: bringing certainty to the exact time and date of cancellation and preserving the insured’s minimum cure period. Lower courts have applied strict adherence in a variety of contexts, invalidating cancellations where the finance company failed to send the ten-day notice, failed to show the date of mailing, or omitted required recipients from its statement.

That doctrinal posture means litigants frequently frame disputes as one of statutory compliance rather than as an open equitable question. When a plaintiff shows a meaningful statutory defect, Louisiana courts have been willing to treat the cancellation as a nullity and to reinstate coverage or otherwise protect the insured’s rights.

## The purposes of strict compliance: fixing the cancellation date and preserving cure time

The twin rationales that recur in the caselaw are (1) fixing the cancellation’s precise time and (2) providing a guaranteed minimum time for cure. Fixing the cancellation date guards against retroactive or ambiguous termination of coverage; it prevents finance companies and insurers from claiming that cancellation occurred before the insured could reasonably act. Ensuring a cure period protects insureds from losing coverage before they had a real opportunity to remedy the default. These objectives explain why courts treat deviations from the statutory procedure as more than technicalities: defects are viewed as undermining the statute’s fundamental protective function.

## Consequences of defects in the cancellation process under Louisiana law

Because of the rule of strict adherence, courts often treat defects as rendering cancellations ineffective. Typical consequences are reinstatement of coverage (retroactively, if necessary), continued insurer liability for claims arising during the purportedly canceled period, and exposure to statutory or tort claims by insureds. The practical consequence for insurers and finance companies is that failure to show strict compliance — to produce a convincing record that the ten-day notice was sent on a particular date, that the insurer received the required statement, and that interested third parties were notified — can be fatal in litigation. In practice, then, the finance company’s record-keeping, mailing affidavits, and electronic logs often determine outcomes.

## Statutory interpretation of “certifying”: statement, attestation, and possible signature questions

Williams v. GoAuto squarely posed the question whether the statute’s requirement that the finance company “certify” certain facts implies a further, textual requirement of signature or of the identification of a specific individual who actually made the certification. The statute itself refers to “a statement certifying” and identifies the entity doing the certifying as “the insurance premium finance company.” The Louisiana cases that previously addressed the statute have generally required a statement that contains the substantive elements, but they have not uniformly required that a named employee sign the certification.

In Williams, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the statute does not require a signature on the financing company’s certification. The court reasoned from the statute’s text: the certifying actor is the finance company itself, and therefore a corporate-level “statement certifying” is what the statute demands. The court observed that requiring a signature would add to the process in a way the statute does not demand, and that lower-court decisions that commented on the absence of a signature — for example, Benitez v. Elsayed, 285 So. 3d 572 (La. App. 5 Cir. 2019) — did not hold that absence of signature alone invalidated cancellation when the substantive certifying elements were present.

How should students and practitioners apply that holding? The working rule is that “certifying” requires an attestation by the premium-finance company that the statutory elements occurred; the attestation may be made through a corporate communication, an automated transmission, or other means so long as it functionally communicates the required facts and the finance company can be identified as the source. But two important caveats follow. First, the substance matters: courts will reject a purported certification that omits a statutory element, misstates the date of mailing, or fails to list required interested parties. Second, the form may matter in close cases where identity and authenticity are contested: if the insured demonstrates a plausible risk of fraud or forgery — for example, where the purported “certification” is a blank form left unsigned with uncertain provenance — a court might require stronger proof of who actually made the attestation. The Benitez court’s concern about the absence of any identified certifier is instructive: the absence of any indicia of authenticity or provenance can be problematic, even if a signature is not strictly required by the statute.

Consequently, practitioners who represent finance companies or insurers should ensure that the certification plainly identifies the sender company, is traceable in records, and, where practical, includes some person or departmental identifier as a matter of best practice even if not strictly required by the statute. Insurance defense counsel should be prepared to produce logs, system metadata, affidavits of corporate officers, and other contemporaneous proof tying the certification to the finance company’s processes.

## Statutory interpretation of “receipt”: mailing, delivery, custody, possession, and electronic transmission

The word “receipt” — the trigger for the insurer’s ability to cancel — is the other core interpretive battleground. The statute’s text requires that the insurer have “receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement.” The statute does not define receipt, and Louisiana courts have not exhaustively interpreted the term. Traditionally, “receipt” is understood to mean the act of taking possession or coming into custody of something. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession” and “receive” as “to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.”

Williams adopts that ordinary usage and extends it to modern electronic contexts. The Fifth Circuit held that an insurer’s automatic electronic systems receiving an e-mail or a web-service call from the finance company — and reacting by cancelling the policy — satisfy the statute’s requirement of “receipt.” The court emphasized the real-world operation of those systems: a finance-company process triggers a call to a GoAuto web service that is “listening” for such calls and, when it receives a valid call, performs the cancellation. In the court’s view, that automated receipt is the insurer’s receipt for purposes of the statute, because the insurer’s system actually took possession of the request and acted upon it.

From this holding a practical rule emerges: receipt occurs when the insurer, or its agent or system, actually comes into possession of the notice and statement — including by electronic transmission — such that the insurer could have acted upon the notice. Electronic receipt is sufficient if the insurer’s system actually receives the transmission and the system’s receipt can be proven by logs, metadata, timestamps, or other objective records. Equally important, the statute distinguishes receipt from mere transmission: the finance company’s sending a statement is necessary but not sufficient to create cancellation authority unless and until the insurer receives it. The practical consequence is that the timing of transmission and of actual receipt can be determinative of whether the insured enjoyed the statutory ten-day cure period prior to cancellation.

Two subtleties deserve attention. First, whether an agent’s actions count as the insurer’s receipt turns on agency law and the statute’s context. If a third party acts as a true agent of the insurer — a designated recipient or employee of the insurer — its receipt will ordinarily be imputed to the insurer. If the person who physically receives or stamps the documents is an employee of the finance company and not of the insurer, a court may scrutinize whether such act constituted insurer receipt. Williams dealt with that argument and resolved the dispute by focusing on the insurer’s actual electronic receipt rather than on any later physical handling by an APAC employee. Second, where cancellation occurs automatically upon electronic receipt — as with a web-service implementation — courts will look to system logs and contemporaneous records to verify what the insurer’s system received and when. Litigation often turns on those technical records.

## The relationship between formalism and modern business practices in insurance administration

Williams illustrates the tension between two reliable judicial instincts. On the one hand, Louisiana courts have demanded strict compliance with the cancellation statute’s protective structure. On the other, courts must interpret and apply statutes in an era when business is largely electronic. The Fifth Circuit’s approach in Williams reconciles these instincts by adhering to the statute’s text while allowing modern means of certifying and transmitting to qualify so long as they accomplish the statute’s purpose. That is, courts should not add procedural requirements — such as a handwritten signature — that the legislature did not write, but they should demand reliable proof that the required steps occurred.

The practical effect is to preserve the statute’s substantive protections (date-fixation and cure time) while recognizing that corporations commonly communicate and act through automated systems. Thus, a finance company’s automated e-mail or a call to an insurer’s web service may suffice for certification and receipt if the transaction is traceable, if the transmitted statements contain the statutorily required elements, and if the insurer’s receipt is objectively verifiable. At the same time, courts remain willing to treat defective processes as invalidating cancellations where the defects are material to the statutory purpose — for example, where the ten-day notice was never sent, where the date of mailing is unknowable, or where the required interested parties were not identified or notified.

## Comparing explicit statutory requirements with arguments that would add nontextual procedural steps

A recurrent theme of judicial review of regulatory processes is resistance to judicially imposing requirements that the legislature did not enact. Courts regularly decline to read into statutes requirements that would be helpful or prudent but that do not appear in the text. Williams embodies that principle in rejecting the argument that “certifying” necessarily requires a signature. The court cautioned that “strict adherence” to the statute should not be an invitation for courts to add to the procedures the legislature prescribed.

That principle does not mean courts will countenance sloppy compliance. The line between adding requirements and insisting on substantive compliance tracks the statute’s purpose: courts must require what the statute requires, but they should not impose additional formalities that do not further the statutory objectives. If legislatures want a signature requirement, they may adopt one explicitly; until then, courts should focus on whether the required attestation occurred, whether required notices were sent, and whether receipt by the insurer can be demonstrated.

## Applying the doctrine: tests and practical guidance for litigants and courts

From the statute, the caselaw, and Williams, the following practical rules and tests will help students apply the doctrine to new fact patterns:

1. The statutory checklist. To evaluate whether a financed cancellation complied with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G), ask whether: (a) the finance agreement contained a valid power of attorney; (b) the finance company sent the ten-day notice to the insured and can identify the date of sending; (c) the finance company sent to the insurer a copy of the ten-day notice together with a statement “certifying” the four statutory items, including the names and addresses of interested third parties; and (d) the insurer had “receipt” of that notice and statement prior to cancellation. Failure on any one point can be dispositive.

2. What counts as “certifying”? A certification must be a statement by the finance company that the statutory elements are satisfied. A signature is not strictly required under the statute according to Williams, but the certifying statement must be authentic, attributable to the finance company, and complete in content. Where authenticity is contested, contemporaneous records, corporate logs, and metadata will be decisive.

3. What counts as “receipt”? Receipt occurs when the insurer, or an agent acting for the insurer, actually takes possession or custody of the notice and statement. Electronic receipt — including e-mail or web-service transmissions that the insurer’s systems process — qualifies if objectively verifiable by system logs, timestamps, or comparable records. Receipt by a person who is not an agent of the insurer may not suffice; agency must be proven if the recipient is a third party.

4. Burdens of proof. Because the statute conditions cancellation on the finance company’s compliance, the finance company (and the insurer that relies on its certification) should ordinarily be prepared to prove compliance by producing records of the notices, mailing affidavits, system logs, and contemporaneous business records. Plaintiffs challenging cancellation must produce enough evidence of defect to create a triable issue; under Louisiana’s strict-adherence line, even minor defects can be significant if they affect the statute’s protective purposes.

5. Electronic systems and automatic cancellations. When an insurer’s system cancels a policy automatically upon receiving a transmitted request, courts will look to proof that the insurer’s system actually received the request before cancellation and that the finance company’s accompanying statement contained the required certifying elements. Automatic cancellation does not, by itself, render the cancellation invalid; the decisive question is whether the statutory sequence and content requirements were satisfied in timing and substance.

6. When a court might require more formality. Although a handwritten signature is not typically required, courts may demand more formal evidence in unusual circumstances: where the purported certification is internally inconsistent, where there is credible evidence of fabrication, where the identity of the sender is unclear, or where the absence of any indicia of provenance makes the certification unreliable. Benitez is an example where the lack of an identified certifier was treated as a serious defect in the notice’s form and content.

## Checklist for compliance and for litigating challenges

Practitioners should use a practical checklist to avoid disputes and to litigate effectively:

- For finance companies: prepare a written certifying statement that plainly recites the four statutory items, identify the sending company, maintain contemporaneous proof of mailing or transmission of the ten-day notice to the insured (with date), maintain proof of transmission and system logs for transmission to the insurer, and retain copies of notices sent to interested third parties with addresses.

- For insurers relying on certifications: maintain system logs, metadata, timestamps, and internal records showing when the insurer’s system received the finance company’s communication and when cancellation was processed. If the insurer relies on a paper process, preserve physical receipts, stamp dates, and chain-of-custody evidence showing receipt.

- For insureds contesting cancellation: demand and seek production of the ten-day notice, mailing affidavits, the finance company’s certifying statement, electronic logs showing transmission and receipt, and proof of which parties were sent notices. Emphasize defects that affect the date of the ten-day notice or the identity/authenticity of the certifying statement.

## Conclusion: doctrine and practical consequences

Williams v. GoAuto confirms three central principles under Louisiana law. First, the statutory protections for insureds embodied in § 9:3550(G) must be respected; courts will not mechanically allow cancellations that subvert the statute’s two core purposes of fixing cancellation date and preserving cure time. Second, the statute’s prescribed procedure is to be interpreted according to its text; courts should not add additional formalities (such as a blanket signature requirement) that the legislature did not include. Third, modern electronic communications — including e-mail and web-service calls — can satisfy the statute’s transmission and receipt requirements when they accomplish the same substantive functions as traditional mail and can be demonstrated by objective records.

For students and practitioners, the lesson is straightforward: strict adherence remains the operative standard, but adherence must be judged against the statute’s actual provisions and the realities of contemporary business practices. Litigation about cancellation will turn not on theoretical preferences for formality, but on whether the required statements and notices were made, attributed to the finance company, transmitted, and actually received in a way that preserves the insured’s statutory cure opportunity.
