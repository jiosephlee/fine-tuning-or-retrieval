Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance—Facts, Procedure, and the Fifth Circuit’s Analysis

This chapter uses Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, 5th Cir. No. 24-30646 (Oct. 3, 2025), to explore how a federal court sitting in diversity interprets an older state statute in light of modern electronic business practices. The opinion illustrates several recurring problems in contemporary insurance-dispute litigation: (1) how to apply a statutory “strict adherence” requirement developed in a paper-based era to automated, electronic cancellation workflows; (2) how federal courts rely on intermediate state appellate decisions (and when they may certify unresolved questions to the state’s highest court); and (3) the evidentiary and practical consequences of statutory wording — in particular, what it means for a premium finance company to “certify” information and for an insurer to have “receipt” of that certification.

The Fifth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for GoAuto, holding that (i) the statutory requirements for a premium finance company’s cancellation request were satisfied without a handwritten or individually signed certification, and (ii) electronic transmission to and processing by GoAuto’s web service constituted “receipt” by the insurer for purposes of the statute. The court declined to add requirements that the statute does not contain and refused the plaintiffs’ request to certify the statutory questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

## The parties and the financing relationship

The plaintiffs in the litigation were former insureds (Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright) and an injured third party (Nicholas Jenkins). The defendants were an insurer, GoAuto Insurance Company, and a premium finance company, American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). APAC purchased the full premium from GoAuto and then financed the insureds’ payments in monthly installments. Each insured entered into a premium finance agreement that included (1) a grant of power of attorney authorizing APAC to request cancellation of the insured’s policy on specified grounds (principally nonpayment), and (2) a contractual right for APAC to effect cancellation by sending a notice and a certification to the insurer.

This financing structure is common: the insurer remains the policyholder; the finance company advances the premium and acquires a contractual mechanism (often including a power of attorney) to protect its interest if the insured defaults. Louisiana’s statute regulating this process — La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3) — sets forth the mechanics a premium finance company must follow to effect cancellation by using its delegated power.

## The statutory framework and the “strict adherence” principle

Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv), a premium finance company, to request cancellation upon an uncured default, must send to the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four specified matters: (i) that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; (ii) that the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; (iii) that upon default a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured (with the date of sending specified); and (iv) that copies of the insured notice were sent to any other parties with an interest, with names and addresses specified. The insurer, upon “receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company,” may proceed to cancel the policy. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

Louisiana courts have repeatedly described these requirements as subject to “strict adherence.” The cases emphasized by the Fifth Circuit — Delatte v. Lemotte, Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., and Britten v. Reavis — explain the policy behind strictness: the statutory procedure fixes the exact time and date of cancellation and guarantees a minimum cure period for the insured. Because of those twin functions, Louisiana courts treat any defect in the prescribed process as potentially fatal to an effective cancellation. In short, the statute creates a narrow, delineated pathway; missteps can mean that a cancellation never legally occurred.

That strict-compliance principle governs judicial inquiry in disputed cancellations, but it does not authorize courts to read additional requirements into the statute simply because the statute originated in a paper-driven period. The Fifth Circuit’s approach in Williams illustrates that courts will respect strict compliance doctrine while also adhering to ordinary principles of statutory construction: judges enforce the text as written and avoid imposing judicially manufactured formalities.

## The procedural posture: removal and the limited summary-judgment dispute

The class action began in Louisiana state court; GoAuto and APAC removed to federal district court in the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties limited the case at the summary-judgment stage to a single question: whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana law. That narrowing is important: the district court did not resolve all factual disputes about notice practices or about whether particular insureds were harmed; it decided only whether the statutory mechanics were observed as a matter of law. The district court granted GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment and dismissed plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. The plaintiffs appealed.

On appeal, the plaintiffs identified three discrete errors they believed the district court made: (1) it erred in finding that the statutory procedures for delivery of the notice of cancellation were followed, (2) it erred in finding GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests, and (3) it erred in denying their motion for partial summary judgment. These are essentially legal questions about statutory interpretation and the application of undisputed documentary evidence, so the Fifth Circuit reviewed them de novo.

## APAC and GoAuto’s cancellation workflow before and after 2017

To understand the statutory issues, it is necessary to reconstruct the parties’ operational process. APAC’s workflow (as in many finance companies) had several stages. First, when an insured missed a scheduled installment, APAC sent the insured a ten-day notice warning that failure to pay within ten days would result in cancellation. If the insured did not cure by the end of the ten-day period, APAC’s computer system (automatically) sent GoAuto an email with a copy of the notice of cancellation and a cancellation request. The emailed cancellation request was on APAC letterhead and included a four-part certification that tracked the statutory elements.

GoAuto’s systems were set up to respond to those emailed requests: GoAuto operated a web service (a server-side application that listens for incoming calls), and APAC’s system called that web service. When the web service was called, it performed the cancellation and the policy was canceled automatically in the early morning hours. Notably, no human employee at APAC or GoAuto applied a signature to the emailed cancellation before the automated cancellation occurred. Later that same morning, however, an APAC employee printed the documents and used a signature stamp to sign and then — depending on the time period — placed the documents in a shared physical filing cabinet. Prior to 2017, the shared file area belonged to GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto itself; since 2017, the printed and stamped documents were taken to a GoAuto employee who stamped them “received” and placed them in a cabinet that both companies could access.

This chronology — electronic transmission triggering a machine-implemented cancellation, followed by later human handling and a signature stamp — is the core factual template the court had to assess against the statutory text.

## Plaintiffs’ theories: ineffective cancellation, misrepresentation, and breach of good faith

The plaintiffs alleged that GoAuto’s cancellations were ineffective because APAC failed to comply with the statute’s certification and delivery requirements. They argued two linked points. First, “certifying” in the statute should, in their view, be understood to require an actual (handwritten or at least individually identifiable) signature on the certification before the insurer can cancel the policy; because APAC’s signature stamping occurred after the electronic cancellation, the certification was not made prior to the cancellation, rendering the cancellations ineffective. Second, the statute requires that the insurer have “receipt” of the finance company’s notice and certification before it may cancel; plaintiffs argued GoAuto did not “receive” the certification because the human “receipt” (the stamping and placement in the shared file) happened after cancellation and the employee who stamped the documents was an APAC, not a GoAuto, employee. Those procedural theories underpinned plaintiffs’ claims for misrepresentation and breach of the insurer’s duty of good faith.

Because the parties submitted materials on cross-motions for summary judgment confined to these legal questions, the district court and the Fifth Circuit treated the factual record (about the timing of emails, automatic cancellations, and later signature-stamping) as sufficiently established for deciding whether, as a matter of law, the procedures satisfied the statute.

## The Fifth Circuit’s analysis: “certifying” does not demand a signature

The Fifth Circuit began with the textual command: the statute requires that the premium finance company send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the listed items. The term “certifying” appears in a statute that references the finance company as the actor. The plaintiffs’ signature argument had two steps: (a) “certifying” requires a signed statement, and (b) the signature must exist before the insurer cancels the policy.

The court surveyed Louisiana case law. It acknowledged that Louisiana appellate decisions require “strict adherence” to the statutorily prescribed process, and that many cases have invalidated cancellations where required steps were missing. But the court observed that earlier opinions do not uniformly or explicitly treat a named individual’s signature as an indispensable element. Some Louisiana cases use language such as a “statement certifying compliance,” and others speak of “receipt” of a notice “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” One Fifth Circuit panel of decisions cited (and the district court attempted to rely upon) is not helpful on whether an individual signature is required. The Fifth Circuit noted Benitez v. Elsayed — a Louisiana appellate opinion that flagged the lack of a signature as a shortcoming — but Benitez did not establish that absence of a signature, by itself, invalidated a cancellation.

Faced with uncertain and mostly nonbinding state appellate authority and an unambiguous statutory text stating that “the insurance premium finance company” must certify, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the statute contemplates certification by the company and does not require an individually signed personal attestation before cancellation may occur. Courts should not add a signature requirement absent textual or authoritative state-law support. The Fifth Circuit explained the point by reference to ordinary principles of statutory interpretation: if the legislature wanted a signature, it could have said so; where the statute vests the duties in the finance company itself, corporate acts authorized by personnel and automated processes can satisfy the statutory command so long as the substantive elements are present.

This holding expresses two distinct rules students should carry forward. First, when a statute speaks of a corporate actor certifying something, courts will not automatically read in a requirement that a particular human agent sign the certificate. Second, the strict-adherence doctrine prevents courts from excusing noncompliance with the procedures the statute does require, but it does not permit courts to manufacture new procedural prerequisites not found in the statutory text.

## The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of “receipt” and electronic processes

The second major contested point was whether GoAuto had “receipt” of the cancellation notice and certification prior to effecting cancellation. Plaintiffs emphasized that the physical, human-stamped “receipt” of printed materials occurred later and was performed by an APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee. The question was therefore whether an electronically delivered notice that triggered GoAuto’s automatic cancellation constituted the kind of “receipt” the statute demands.

The Fifth Circuit began with dictionary definitions. A leading law dictionary defines “receipt” as the act of receiving, especially by taking possession, and “receive” as “to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Relying on these ordinary-meaning definitions, the court concluded that “receipt” includes taking possession, custody, or delivery through whatever effective mechanism the parties use. The court explicitly recognized modern operational realities — corporate computers and web services can be the means by which an insurer takes possession of a communication. In Williams, APAC’s system sent an email or otherwise called GoAuto’s web service; GoAuto’s listener/web service received the message and immediately processed the cancellation. That instantaneous electronic exchange meant the insurer had “receipt” for purposes of the statute at the time its web service processed the request.

The opinion thus teaches two interrelated points about statutory interpretation in the information age. One is methodological: when a state statute uses common words with no specialized technical meaning, courts may use common-law and dictionary definitions to supply meaning, particularly where the legislative text is silent about new technology. The second is practical: electronic transmissions that reach an insurer’s systems and trigger automatic corporate action can constitute the “delivery” or “receipt” that statutes historically associated with physical possession.

The Fifth Circuit broadened the statutory path to include modern communications. But important caveats remain: the decision does not eliminate the strict-adherence rule; it simply situates the statutory requirements within the realities of electronic processing. If an e-mail fails to reach the insurer’s system, is altered, or lacks the statutorily required substantive statements, the cancellation could still be ineffective. Likewise, the opinion does not hold that any electronic message will suffice; the substance of the certification and the demonstrable delivery to the insurer’s systems were critical here.

## Secondary rationales and what the appellate court declined to decide

The district court had offered an alternative reasoning: it characterized the APAC employee who delivered the printed documents as a “private carrier” and treated delivery to the shared filing space as satisfying the statute. That theory rested on an expansive reading of “private carrier,” and the district court imported analogies from unrelated contexts (including a railroad case) to justify it. The Fifth Circuit explicitly declined to adopt or rely upon this unconventional characterization. Because the appellate court concluded that the emailed notices and the insurer’s web service were sufficient under the statute, it had no need to resolve whether the later physical handling of documents by a private carrier-like employee could or would satisfy the statute.

This procedural posture illustrates a common appellate practice: when a lower court advances multiple grounds, an appellate court will affirm on any adequate ground that the parties have briefed and the record supports, but it may decline to reach alternative or unsettled rationales that are unnecessary to the disposition.

## Certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court: why the Fifth Circuit refused

When a federal court sitting in diversity confronts an unsettled question of state law — particularly where the state’s highest court has not spoken — it has three basic options: (1) predict how the state supreme court would decide the issue, following binding intermediate appellate decisions where the law is clear; (2) certify the question to the state supreme court if the forum allows it; or (3) apply federal principles if the question is procedural rather than substantive Erie-wise (not at issue here).

The plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify to the Louisiana Supreme Court the two questions central to the appeal: whether a signature is required and whether the statutory “receipt” requires physical possession by a human agent. The Fifth Circuit declined to certify. The controlling precedent the court cited — Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n — explains that federal courts should be cautious about certifying questions to state high courts and should do so only where there is a compelling reason. The mere absence of a state supreme court decision, standing alone, does not warrant certification. Here, the Fifth Circuit believed the ordinary-meaning approach combined with the existing body of intermediate appellate authority provided a sufficient basis to resolve the statutory questions without resorting to certification. Consequently, the court answered the questions itself and affirmed the district court.

This disposition demonstrates the federal standard for certification: certification is available but discretionary and reserved for cases where prediction is unusually difficult, state-law uncertainty is pervasive and dispositive, or the question is of exceptional importance to the state. That threshold is not met simply because a statute is old or because modern technology presents interpretive questions.

## Practical and doctrinal takeaways

Williams v. GoAuto teaches several lessons relevant to practitioners and students of insurance and civil procedure:

1. Statutory text controls, but ordinary-meaning tools are legitimate. Where a state statute uses general terms and the state supreme court has not spoken, federal courts will rely on dictionary definitions and the plain meaning of words in context. The Fifth Circuit read “certifying” and “receipt” in that way.

2. Corporate certification can be satisfied without a manually written individual signature. When a statute instructs that a company must “certify” certain facts, courts may accept an unsigned corporate communication (especially when it is on company letterhead, explicitly states the required elements, and is authored through the company’s established processes). That is particularly true where intermediate state authority is not to the contrary.

3. Electronic delivery to and processing by an insurer’s systems can constitute “receipt.” Statutory notions of possession and receipt adapt to contemporary commercial practices. A web service that “listens” and acts upon a transmitted cancellation request effectively takes custody of that communication when it processes it; the insurer cannot avoid the legal consequences by pointing to later human paperwork steps.

4. Strict compliance doctrine still matters. Louisiana maintains a strict-adherence rule for finance-company cancellations. The Williams decision preserves that doctrine’s application but refuses to expand it beyond the legislature’s text. A company that fails to include required information (for example, omitting the date of the notice to the insured) would still risk invalidation of the cancellation.

5. Best practices remain important. Although the court declined to require signatures, a company wishing to minimize litigation risk should keep robust, contemporaneous audit trails: machine-generated timestamps, retained electronic copies of outgoing messages, confirmation logs from recipients’ servers, and an identifiable chain of custody for later printed documents. These practices provide documentary proof a court can evaluate under the strict-adherence standard.

6. Certification to a state supreme court is discretionary and sparingly used. Parties seeking certification must show that the state-law uncertainty is acute and that certification is necessary to fairly adjudicate the dispute.

## Applying Williams to new fact patterns — brief hypotheticals

These short hypotheticals illustrate how Williams would apply in related circumstances:

- Hypothetical A: APAC sends an automated SMS to an insurer’s publicized cancellation phone number, and the insurer’s system immediately processes the request. Under Williams, the electronic message, if it reaches the insurer’s system and contains the statutorily required certification, would likely be treated as “receipt,” since the principle is that delivery to and processing by the insurer’s systems constitute possession.

- Hypothetical B: APAC’s system emails the insurer, but the message is blocked by the insurer’s spam filter and never reaches the web service; later APAC prints and hand-delivers a signed notice to a mailbox shared with the insurer, but this occurs after the date of the alleged cancellation. That would present a stronger case for plaintiffs: the key factual issue would be whether the insurer actually took possession of the emailed message prior to cancellation; if not, the administrative process could be defective under the strict-adherence doctrine.

- Hypothetical C: APAC’s emailed cancellation omits the names or addresses of third parties with an interest (required by § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv)). Even if the insurer’s system receives the email and processes cancellation, the omission of required information could render the cancellation ineffective under Louisiana’s strict-compliance rule.

These hypotheticals show that Williams does not eliminate litigation risk for finance companies and insurers; it merely clarifies that modern electronic workflows can satisfy the statute where they demonstrably include the required content and where the insurer’s systems take custody of the communication.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance affirms two interlocking propositions. First, Louisiana’s statutory cancellation procedure, interpreted in light of the “strict adherence” principle, governs the effectiveness of a premium finance company’s use of its power of attorney to cancel a policy. Second, the textual requirements are met when the finance company provides the statutorily required certification and when the insurer takes “receipt” of that certification — and receipt may occur through modern electronic transmission to and processing by the insurer’s systems. The Fifth Circuit refused to import a signature requirement the statute did not contain and declined to certify the questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. Practitioners should read Williams as adapting statutory interpretation to contemporary corporate practices while leaving intact the underlying strict-compliance protections historically afforded to insureds.
