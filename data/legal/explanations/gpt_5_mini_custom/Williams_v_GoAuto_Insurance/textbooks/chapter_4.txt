Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 4: # Issues on Appeal and the Court’s Analysis: Signature and Receipt

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance turns on two narrow statutory questions under Louisiana law: whether a premium finance company’s “certifying” the required facts under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) must be evidenced by a signature before the insurer may cancel a policy, and what it means for the insurer to have “receipt” of the finance company’s cancellation request under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The court affirms summary judgment for GoAuto by answering both questions in ways that accommodate modern, electronic business practices while remaining faithful to the statute and Louisiana precedent.

## Issue 1 — Does “certifying” require a signature?

The statute says that, to request cancellation, the premium finance company must send the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” a short list of facts (power of attorney, uncured default, that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured, and copies sent to interested parties). The statute does not mention a signature or identify any particular person who must sign a document. Louisiana appellate decisions have repeatedly required “strict adherence” to the statutory procedures when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel a policy, and they have treated any defect in the cancellation process as potentially fatal. But, as the Fifth Circuit emphasizes, strict adherence means following the statutory steps — not adding requirements the text does not contain.

When students read the court’s analysis here, note the methodology. The court first considers the plain text (the statute requires the finance company to “certify” certain facts) and then looks at Louisiana precedent to see whether courts have read “certifying” as imposing a signature requirement. The existing opinions are inconclusive. Some appellate decisions paraphrase the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance” or allow cancellation “upon receipt of such notice and statement,” but they do not identify signature as an independent prerequisite. One case, Benitez, criticized a notice that lacked both an identified author and a signature and noted the document did not “purport to ‘certify’” the statements; Benitez did not hold that the absence of a signature alone invalidated the cancellation. Because the statute speaks of the insurance premium finance company certifying — not an identified individual signing — the Fifth Circuit declines to graft a signature requirement onto the statute.

Applying those principles to the record, the court treats APAC’s cancellation request (sent on APAC letterhead and containing explicit statements certifying each required element) as a valid “statement certifying.” The fact that an APAC employee later applied a signature stamp to a printed copy after the policy had already been cancelled by GoAuto’s automated system does not mean the pre‑cancellation certification was deficient. The key point the opinion teaches is that, under Louisiana law as interpreted by the Fifth Circuit in this case, certification can be made by a company statement rather than by an identified or signed individual, and courts should not impose an extra‑textual signature requirement when the statute is silent.

## Issue 2 — When has the insurer “received” the cancellation request?

The second contested issue is whether GoAuto must have taken physical possession of a paper cancellation request before cancelling a policy, or whether electronic delivery to and processing by GoAuto’s systems suffices as “receipt.” The statute requires that “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested” and may proceed to cancel. The statute does not define “receipt.”

The Fifth Circuit turns to familiar interpretive tools when a statutory term is undefined: it consults standard dictionary definitions and the practical realities of modern communications. The court adopts Black’s Law Dictionary definitions of “receipt” and “receive” — essentially, to take possession or to come into possession of something from an outside source — and applies them in context. The record shows that APAC’s system automatically sent GoAuto an email or web‑service call containing the notice and the certification, and GoAuto’s computer system was configured to cancel the policy upon receiving that electronic message. In short, the insurer’s systems took possession of the cancellation request by accepting the electronic transmission, and the automatic cancellation followed.

This holding emphasizes two related lessons for law students. First, statutory commands about sending and receiving official notices are interpreted in light of contemporary commercial practices; the court does not treat paper as the only valid medium when the statute was enacted in an era before ubiquitous electronic transmission. Second, “receipt” is a functional concept: what matters is that the insurer has the item in its custody or control (here, the GoAuto system had the electronic notice and acted on it), not that a human employee had physically handled a printed form before the cancellation took effect.

The plaintiffs advanced a competing, physical‑possession theory: because the signature stamp and physical filing of the cancellation documents occurred after (and were performed by an APAC employee), GoAuto supposedly never “received” the request prior to cancellation. The court rejects that argument as immaterial. The later affixation of a signature stamp and disputes about which employee stamped or filed the paper copies did not negate the fact that GoAuto’s systems had received the electronic cancellation message and acted upon it. The district court had also proposed an alternative rationale—treating the employee who delivered the notices as a “private carrier”—but because the Fifth Circuit resolves the case on the electronic‑receipt ground, it declines to adopt or analyze that unconventional theory.

## Consequences for the summary judgment motion and remaining claims

Because the Fifth Circuit concluded that APAC’s company‑level certification on its letterhead satisfied the statute’s “statement certifying” requirement and that GoAuto’s automated receipt of the electronic cancellation request satisfied the statute’s “receipt” requirement, the court held that GoAuto complied with § 9:3550(G)(3). With statutory compliance established, there was no basis to find GoAuto had failed to effect a valid cancellation under Louisiana law. Consequently the district court did not err in denying the plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment and in granting GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment. All claims against GoAuto were properly dismissed with prejudice.

For students learning to read opinions, this sequence illustrates how a narrow statutory construction can be dispositive: the panel focuses on two textual terms, applies plain‑meaning and contextual analysis informed by precedent and dictionary definitions, and resolves the appeal without reaching broader or speculative issues. It also shows the courts’ pragmatic approach to electronic communications and the limits of the “strict adherence” doctrine: courts enforce the statute’s requirements carefully, but they will not add procedural formalities—like a pre‑cancellation signature—when the statutory text and the commercial context do not demand them.
