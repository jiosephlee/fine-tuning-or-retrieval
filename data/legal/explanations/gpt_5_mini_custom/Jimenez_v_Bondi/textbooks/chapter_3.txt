Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Past Persecution: Threats, Psychological Harm, and Cumulative Assessment

This chapter explains how the Tenth Circuit evaluated whether the petitioners’ experiences amounted to past persecution in Jimenez v. Bondi. The court framed the question under settled Tenth Circuit precedent: what level and type of harm—considered individually and cumulatively—suffices to qualify as “persecution” for asylum purposes. The analysis demonstrates how the court applies familiar doctrinal markers (severity, offensiveness, and cumulative effect) to a fact pattern of repeated threats, armed confrontations, property-related disputes with criminal actors, and alleged psychological injury.

## Persecution concept: more than mere restrictions or threats; offensiveness and severity requirement

The court began from the basic rule that “persecution” is not defined in the INA, so courts look to what sort of suffering is “regarded as offensive” and that is more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats. The opinion relies on Wiransane v. Ashcroft for this principle: persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm on those who differ in some protected respect in a way that is “regarded as offensive” and that rises above mere threats or restrictions. This standard means courts evaluate both the quality (how offensive or severe) and quantity (how chronic or cumulative) of mistreatment. The court also reiterated that whether private persecution occurred is a factual question reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard, so the BIA’s factual findings are conclusive unless no reasonable adjudicator could reach the same conclusion.

Two important corollaries follow from the Tenth Circuit’s precedent and are applied in this opinion. First, physical injury is not strictly necessary to show persecution—the absence of bodily harm does not automatically doom a claim (see Karki)—but the overall harm must still be sufficiently severe. Second, mere threats, standing alone, typically do not qualify; the threats must be part of a pattern or accompanied by other serious mistreatment that together cross the persecution threshold.

## Evidence presented: repeated threats; armed confrontations; brochure; assault of employee; child intimidation; absence of actual physical injury to petitioners

The petitioners’ factual record included a sequence of hostile acts: repeated threatening phone calls (weekly), distribution of a brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” two in-person armed confrontations (one holding Jimenez at gunpoint and ordering him to leave the neighborhood; another assault on a factory employee seeking the petitioners), and a frightening child-intimidation episode in which a stranger put his arm around the petitioners’ young son and covered his mouth. Petitioners also had a prior confrontation between Jimenez and a local criminal figure (Don Rafa) over a property dispute; the criminal’s associates later told petitioners they had received orders from Don Rafa.

Crucially, petitioners conceded before the IJ and elsewhere that they themselves were never physically injured. That concession is important because, although physical injury is not required, it is a significant factor in assessing severity. The court emphasized the absence of actual physical harm to the petitioners in applying the persecution standard.

## Petitioners’ claims of emotional and psychological harm (insomnia; child’s panic attacks)

Petitioners relied in part on psychological effects—Duarte’s insomnia and the son’s panic attacks—to argue the cumulative harm met the persecution threshold. The court acknowledged that emotional and psychological harms are relevant to the persecution inquiry, but treated those allegations as insufficient here. It pointed out that the Tenth Circuit has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone,” citing Brandy and other precedent. In short, while such harms can contribute to a broader showing, they seldom tip the scales when the underlying incidents consist principally of threats and non-injurious confrontations.

## Cumulative-evidence approach vs. isolated incidents

The court reiterated that incidents are not analyzed in isolation; instead, courts consider the cumulative effect of multiple incidents (Ritonga). That cumulative approach recognizes that a pattern of less-severe harms can, together, amount to persecution. But the cumulative test is not automatic: the combined incidents must, in total, meet the qualitative and quantitative thresholds—i.e., be sufficiently severe and offensive. Here the court applied that cumulative lens and still concluded the aggregate of threats, confrontations, and psychological stress did not reach persecution.

To understand the line the court drew, it is helpful to compare petitions the opinion discussed. The court cited Karki (threats alone insufficient), Chhetri (telephone threats and a vague in-person threat insufficient), and Carias-Mejia (machete threats and repeated robberies insufficient because the threats were conditional on compliance). Those cases show that even multiple threats or episodic criminal acts do not necessarily constitute persecution absent a level of harm or particularized targeting that meets the statutory test.

## Tenth Circuit guidance: threats alone typically insufficient; physical harm not required but severity matters

The Jimenez opinion synthesizes Tenth Circuit guidance: threats alone generally do not constitute persecution; physical injury is not a sine qua non but is a highly probative indicator of severity; and psychological injury—while relevant—rarely suffices by itself. The court also reaffirmed that the record must compel a different outcome to overturn the BIA under the substantial-evidence standard. The opinion notes that petitioners’ counsel conceded at oral argument that Tenth Circuit precedent has rejected asylum claims even in more extreme factual permutations, signaling that the bar is not easily met.

## Comparative case references: Karki; Brandy; Chhetri; Carias-Mejia

The opinion anchors its analysis with prior Tenth Circuit decisions. Karki establishes that threats alone are ordinarily insufficient. Brandy underscores the courts’ hesitancy to grant asylum based solely on emotional or psychological harms stemming from threats. Chhetri and Carias-Mejia provide concrete examples where multiple threats, detentions, or property-related violence did not suffice to show persecution because either the harms were not severe enough, were conditional, or lacked the requisite nexus and severity. Jimenez applies those precedents to conclude the present facts fall on the non-persecution side of the line.

## Holding on this element: substantial evidence supports BIA’s finding that, even in the aggregate, the harms did not rise to persecution

Applying the law to the facts, the Tenth Circuit concluded that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s finding that petitioners did not suffer past persecution. The court emphasized the petitioners’ own concessions (no physical harm), the nature of the incidents (threats, intimidation, an assault on an employee, and child-targeting), and the limited probative force of alleged psychological effects. Even when viewed cumulatively, the harms did not meet the threshold established in Tenth Circuit precedent. Because the petitioners failed to establish past persecution, they could not rely on the rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution that such a finding would create—an outcome that also bears on the separate but related withholding-of-removal claim.

Takeaway for students: when reading asylum opinions like Jimenez, focus on (1) the court’s articulation of the persecution standard (severity/offensiveness, more than threats), (2) whether incidents are considered cumulatively, (3) any concessions or absences of physical harm in the record, (4) how alleged psychological harms are weighed, and (5) the standard of review (substantial evidence). The Jimenez decision illustrates how a string of serious but non-injurious threats, even with distressing psychological consequences, can still fall short of the statutory concept of persecution under controlling Tenth Circuit authority.
