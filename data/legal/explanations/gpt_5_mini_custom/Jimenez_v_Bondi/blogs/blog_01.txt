Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# When Death Threats Aren’t Enough: The Tenth Circuit’s Rigorous Persecution Threshold in Jimenez v. Bondi

## Why this case matters
Jimenez v. Bondi is a reminder that, in the Tenth Circuit, even repeated, armed death threats and an incident involving a child may fall short of asylum’s persecution threshold unless accompanied by more concrete indicia of severe harm or a clearer showing of motive and state failure. The case puts a fine point on three recurring asylum battlegrounds: what counts as “persecution”; how to establish nexus to a protected ground in mixed‑motive harassment by criminals; and when government protection or internal relocation is unreasonable. For practitioners and advocates, Jimenez signals that compiling documentary and corroborative evidence remains decisive—threats, standing alone and without stronger corroboration, are at perennial risk of being discounted on appeal.

## The court’s reasoning, in brief
The petitioners—Colombian nationals who campaigned for a presidential candidate and whose husband-law‑student later intervened in a property dispute with a narco-trafficking leader (“Don Rafa”)—suffered a series of menacing events: telephone threats, a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” two armed confrontations at the family’s factory (one directly telling them to leave), a later assault of an employee who was asked where the petitioners were, and a frightening encounter in which a man put his arm around the petitioners’ younger son and told him to “run to your dad.” The family briefly hid with relatives and ultimately fled to the United States after a police patrol officer said “nothing could be done” and advised them to leave.

The Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review, affirming the BIA’s decision on multiple grounds. First, on past persecution, the court applied its settled framework: persecution requires “infliction of suffering or harm . . . more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty,” and threats alone are generally insufficient. Although the panel acknowledged the incidents collectively, it concluded that (1) petitioners suffered no physical injury or lasting physical harm, a concession they made at the merits hearing, and (2) their psychological harms—sleep loss, panic attacks—were not the type of corroborated, severe psychological injury that the court has treated as rising to persecution. The court relied on Karki, Brandy, and related precedents to hold that the cumulative pattern here did not compel a different result.

Second, on nexus, the court treated this as a mixed‑motive case and required that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for the harm. The panel found substantial evidence that the driving motive was not political opinion but retaliation and financial protection of Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise after Jimenez’s involvement in the property dispute. The escalation of threats only after Jimenez confronted the tenant/Don Rafa, the references by the perpetrators to searches and seizures at the property, and the absence of proof that the earlier politically‑tinged threats were attributable to Don Rafa all supported the BIA’s finding that political opinion was tangential.

Third, on government protection and relocation, the court emphasized the importance of reporting and notice: without a contemporaneous report or evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, there is no basis to conclude the Colombian government was categorically unable or unwilling to protect the family. The record showed police had attempted searches at the property and that family members safely remained in Colombia, and the petitioners themselves had a short safe stay at a relative’s home—facts the court used to support the BIA’s conclusions that internal relocation was reasonable and that the state was not shown to be complicit or helpless.

The upshot: substantial‑evidence review sustained the BIA’s factual findings across these points, and the petitioners failed to meet the asylum (and, consequently, the higher withholding) standards.

## Why the court came out this way — and where that reasoning is contestable
Jimenez is faithful to the Tenth Circuit’s cautious posture on threat‑only claims. Two doctrinal currents drive the result. First, the court draws a line between menacing conduct and the type of targeted, severe infliction of suffering historically understood as persecution. That line matters for adjudicatory clarity and curbs overbroad asylum grants, but it also risks starving meritorious claims where non‑physical violence—terrorizing, repeated threats, or attempts to abduct a child—has life‑altering consequences.

Second, the court’s mixed‑motive analysis is rigorous: it will treat criminal motives (revenge, profit, silencing) as dispositive unless an applicant can point to evidence that political opinion was a central reason for targeting. Here, the timing of escalation after the property confrontation and the perpetrators’ statements about searches at Don Rafa’s house undercut a political‑motive theory. That focus on temporal sequencing and specific statements is legally defensible, but it imposes a high evidentiary hurdle for applicants who face amorphous, overlapping motives in contexts where criminal actors and political conflict intertwine.

There are plausible critiques. The court’s skepticism toward psychological harms, while anchored in precedent, can obscure situations where terrorizing tactics produce debilitating mental illness that effectively deprives victims of normal life and safety. Likewise, giving significant weight to the absence of contemporaneous police reports assumes reporting is feasible and safe; though the court recognized the futility exception, it demanded more concrete proof of futility here than the petitioners produced. That is often a hard evidentiary task for asylum seekers who flee quickly or fear reprisals from corrupt local officials.

## Evidence that might tip a similar case in the Tenth Circuit
Jimenez implicitly sketches the contours of what would have been persuasive. Representatives of such claims should, where possible, develop:

- Contemporaneous police reports, formal complaints, or other official documentation showing notice and the government’s inability or refusal to act. If reporting is dangerous or futile, an applicant should establish that with specific country‑condition evidence or prior instances showing systemic collusion or impunity.
- Medical records or psychological evaluations tying depression, panic disorder, or PTSD to the incidents. The Tenth Circuit permits psychological harm to be considered, but wants corroboration that the harm is severe and enduring.
- Corroboration of physical attempts to abduct or injure (photographs, surveillance footage, witness statements, hospital records), or other objective indicators (e.g., evidence of kidnapping rings operating in the area, contemporaneous media reports of similar attacks) showing the persecutor’s capacity and intent to cause severe harm.
- Clear evidence linking the persecutor’s motive to a protected ground—explicit statements by attackers (“we’re killing Liberal Party people”) or documentation of systemic targeting of party members in the applicant’s locale may satisfy the “central reason” requirement in mixed‑motive cases.
- Country‑condition reports and expert testimony establishing that local law enforcement is complicit with, or unable to control, particular criminal organizations—this helps make the futility argument when reporting is dangerous.

In short, the court wants tangible, contemporaneous, and corroborative evidence—especially where the alleged harms are largely threats and where motive is contested.

## Doctrinal and practical implications
Jimenez reaffirms several settled points in Tenth Circuit asylum jurisprudence: threats alone rarely suffice, psychological harm demands strong corroboration, mixed-motive inquiries require a protected ground to be a central reason, and applicants bear the burden of showing government inability or unwillingness to protect them. For asylum seekers from countries with violent non‑state actors who also have political dimensions to their targeting, the decision means litigators must be strategic and evidentiary: build the record early, document incidents contemporaneously when feasible, and marshal medical, expert, and country‑condition evidence.

More broadly, the case underscores cross‑circuit frictions. Some courts have been more receptive to threat‑based claims when threats were extreme, accompanied by near‑abductions, or set against a backdrop of demonstrated state complicity. The Tenth Circuit’s approach narrows that path, and will likely produce repeated appeals, particularly in factual patterns where criminal and political motives overlap. Immigration attorneys practicing in the Tenth Circuit should anticipate that adjudicators will probe motive and corroboration and that appellate courts will give substantial deference to the BIA’s factual conclusions.

## Conclusion
Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful, if sobering, exemplar of the evidentiary expectations the Tenth Circuit enforces in asylum cases centered on threats and intimidation. The panel did not deny the terror the family experienced, but it required more—either physical harm, stronger documentary corroboration of severe psychological injury, clearer linkage of motive to a protected ground, or firmer proof that reporting would have been futile—before finding persecution. For advocates, the lesson is practical: collect documentation, secure contemporaneous reporting when safe, obtain corroborating medical and expert assessments, and frame motive with precision. Absent that architecture of proof, even harrowing threats may not clear the circuit’s high bar.
