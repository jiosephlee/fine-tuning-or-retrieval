Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Unable or Unwilling, or Just Unasked? Failure to Report, Police Apathy, and Internal Relocation in Jimenez

## Why this case matters

Jimenez v. Bondi is a compact but instructive decision about a recurring fault line in U.S. asylum law: when does a victim’s failure to secure police protection undercut a claim that her home government is “unable or unwilling” to protect her? The Tenth Circuit denied relief to a Colombian family who fled threats from narco-traffickers after a local dispute, accepting the Board of Immigration Appeals’ view that their limited outreach to authorities and a couple of unhelpful police encounters did not establish government inability or unwillingness to protect. The court reached that conclusion while tying it to its finding that internal relocation was reasonable — the family had safely stayed with relatives and others remained in Colombia unmolested.

The opinion is consequential for practitioners because it applies and clarifies the Singh v. Bondi framework in a fact pattern that will be familiar to many asylum seekers: threats from nonstate armed actors who may have, or be alleged to have, ties to local authorities. It underscores what the court treats as necessary evidence to trigger the “futility” exception to the ordinary requirement that applicants seek state protection, and it shows how a weak record on government complicity can carry through to defeat both asylum and withholding claims by supporting an internal-relocation finding.

## How the court reasoned

The panel applied settled Tenth Circuit law: to obtain asylum via past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution, an applicant must show (among other things) that the persecutor acted “on account of” a protected ground, and that the government is unable or unwilling to control the persecutor. The court affirmed the BIA on three interlocking conclusions.

First, on persecution, the court reiterated the familiar principle that threats and emotional distress, standing alone and even in the aggregate, do not always meet the threshold of persecution. Jimenez and his wife experienced threats, an assault on a factory employee, and a frightening encounter involving their child; the IJ found them credible. But the court relied on its precedents (Karki, Brandy, Chhetri) that place a relatively high bar on when threats and psychological harms suffice.

Second, on nexus, the court found substantial evidence that the persecutors were primarily motivated by Jimenez’s confrontation with “Don Rafa” over property and revenue, not by his—and his family’s—political activities. The timing of escalated threats after the property dispute, and the capture in the record of the attackers’ statements blaming Jimenez for searches, supported the BIA’s conclusion that political opinion was at best tangential.

Third, and most relevant for practitioners, the court rejected the argument that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect the family. The decision leaned on two related points. The record showed the Colombian authorities had attempted searches and seizures at the problematic property, undermining an assertion of state capture. And crucially, Petitioners barely engaged with the state: they reported only limited contacts with two police officers who told them not to seek trouble, and they did not pursue broader avenues of protection (including using Jimenez’s law-enforcement contacts). Under Singh v. Bondi, the court emphasized notice to the state and the opportunity to respond; an applicant’s failure to report can undercut an inability/unwillingness claim unless the applicant shows that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. The Tenth Circuit found no such showing here. Finally, the court tied that result to internal relocation: family members still lived safely in Colombia, the family had spent three weeks safely at a relative’s home, and the threats were localized to their neighborhood — factors that supported the IJ’s conclusion that relocation within Colombia was reasonable.

## What the opinion signals about the “futility” and “unwilling or unable” inquiry

Jimenez illustrates the evidentiary contours that the Tenth Circuit expects when a claimant seeks to invoke the futility exception. A few discrete police encounters with indifferent officers will not, by themselves, establish systemic government failure. The court conspicuously distinguished isolated officer apathy from the sort of pattern or systemic evidence that courts find probative of inability or unwillingness. It cited Osorio‑Morales (7th Cir.) for the proposition that “a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show” government failure, and it relied on the presence of government efforts against the same criminal enterprise as counterevidence.

Put differently, to demonstrate that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, an applicant needs more than a generalized fear or a short, unproductive contact with police. Courts will look for indicia that the state had notice and either turned a blind eye or actively colluded: specific evidence of official complicity, a pattern of failed or abusive responses in the relevant region, prior reprisals for reporting, credible evidence that police take bribes from the persecutors, or expert testimony and country reports showing pervasive local corruption. Absent that record, courts are likely to insist that applicants at least attempt to obtain protection before conceding the state off-limits.

## How the government-protection analysis dovetailed with internal-relocation findings

The Tenth Circuit’s unwilling-or-unable analysis did double work: it not only negated a well-founded fear but also supported the BIA’s conclusion that internal relocation was feasible. The court relied on concrete indicators that relatives remained safely in Colombia and that the family had a prior safe stay in the Bogota countryside. It was consequential that the men who threatened the family told them to leave the neighborhood — not the country — a fact the court read as consistent with the availability of local relocation. The presence of civil‑service ties (Jimenez’s father’s continued work) and the government’s activity against the property amplified the court’s willingness to treat internal relocation as a realistic option.

This linkage illustrates a critical practical point: the weaker an applicant’s proof of systemic state failure, the easier it is for the government to rebut a fear-of-persecution claim both by denying a protection failure and by showing alternative safe avenues within the country. The two analyses are often mutually reinforcing.

## Practical implications and downstream effects

For asylum applicants and counsel, Jimenez is a reminder that the administrative record must include an affirmative showing on government notice and response — or persuasive proof of why reporting would have been futile or dangerous. Attorneys should document every effort to seek protection, preserve copies of police reports, and, when direct reporting is impracticable, assemble corroboration: contemporaneous communications, witness statements, proof of reprisals against others who reported, and expert affidavits explaining local patterns of corruption or collusion. Country reports, local NGO documentation, and prior litigation showing systemic failures in a particular municipality can help bridge a sparse personal reporting record.

Practically, evidence of prior reprisals for reporting, corroborated testimony that the persecutors retaliate against complainants, or proof that police are paid off are the kinds of facts courts find persuasive for the futility exception. Conversely, isolated evidence of apathy or a few unhelpful officers will likely fall short absent broader indicia of official complicity.

For courts, Jimenez follows a mainstream line — it aligns the Tenth Circuit with other circuits that require more than minimal contacts to establish systemic official failure. But it also signals the need for careful fact-sensitive work: the difference between “one unhelpful officer” and “systemic official complicity in [a region]” is often a matter of record development, not doctrine. The opinion therefore indirectly puts pressure on immigration adjudicators and advocates to develop fuller records early, lest appellate courts apply substantial-evidence review to an underdeveloped factual showing.

One potential downstream concern is a chilling effect: victims who reasonably fear retaliation for reporting may still be forced by courts to try to obtain protection and thereafter be penalized for not having done so. Jimenez reaffirms the futility exception but narrows the practical circumstances in which it will apply. Unless district‑ or country‑level evidence of systemic corruption becomes routine in the record, applicants from settings with localized or semi-covert collusion may face higher hurdles.

## Takeaways

Jimenez is a useful, if sobering, primer on how courts treat failures to report and isolated police apathy in asylum cases. It makes clear that (1) notice to the state and an opportunity to respond are central to the unable-or-unwilling inquiry; (2) isolated, one-off unhelpful police encounters are rarely enough to prove systemic state failure; (3) evidence that government authorities have acted against the same criminal enterprise undercuts claims of state capture; and (4) a weak record on government protection can dovetail with, and reinforce, an internal-relocation finding. For practitioners, the path to overcoming Jimenez is straightforward in theory — build the record on reporting, document reprisals, and secure expert or country-level corroboration — but demanding in practice.

In short, Jimenez reaffirms longstanding law in a familiar factual setting and puts a premium on meticulous record building in cases where applicants contend the state will not or cannot protect them.
