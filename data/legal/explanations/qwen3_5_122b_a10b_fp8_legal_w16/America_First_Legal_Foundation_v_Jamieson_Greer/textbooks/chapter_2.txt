Chapter 2: # Threshold Issue: Article III Standing Requirements

## Case or Controversy Requirement in Federal Courts

Before a federal court can reach the merits of a dispute—such as interpreting complex statutes governing the Office of Special Counsel—it must first ensure it possesses the fundamental constitutional authority to hear the case. This threshold inquiry stems directly from Article III, Section 2 of the U.S. Constitution, which limits the "judicial Power" to actual "Cases" and "Controversies." The Supreme Court has long held that this language imposes a strict limitation on the judiciary: federal courts cannot act as advisory bodies issuing opinions on abstract legal questions or disputes where there is no genuine stakeholder suffering real harm. Consequently, the doctrine of standing serves as the primary mechanism to enforce this constitutional mandate.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit emphasizes that establishing standing is not merely a technicality but a prerequisite for subject matter jurisdiction. Even though neither party raised standing during initial arguments below, the appellate court recognized its duty to raise the issue sua sponte (on its own initiative). Under *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, if a plaintiff fails to demonstrate standing, the district court lacked jurisdiction to enter judgment in the first place, regardless of whether the underlying statutory argument regarding the Office of Special Counsel's duties was correct. Therefore, in reading this opinion, one sees that the court bypasses all discussion of whether Congress intended for OSC to investigate FOIA denials under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 because it never reaches that question; it stops immediately upon determining that America First Legal Foundation (AFL) cannot clear the jurisdictional hurdle of Article III standing.

## Three Prongs of Standing: Injury, Causation, Redressability

To survive dismissal and prove they possess standing, a plaintiff must satisfy three distinct elements derived from *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*. These prongs function together to ensure that litigation remains tethered to specific harms rather than generalized grievances shared by all citizens. First, **injury-in-fact** requires an invasion of a legally protected interest that is both concrete and particularized, meaning it affects the plaintiff personally and is not abstract or widely dispersed. Furthermore, this injury must be actual or imminent; it cannot be conjectural or hypothetical. Second, **causation** demands a direct link showing that the defendant's conduct actually caused or contributed to the plaintiff's injury. Third, **redressability** requires proof that a favorable judicial decision is likely to remedy or alleviate that specific injury. If winning in court would change nothing practical regarding the harm complained of, there is no justiciable case.
