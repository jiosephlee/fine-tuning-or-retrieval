Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why did the D.C. Circuit treat the MSPB's potential future actions as an intervening cause breaking causation?
Question:
In analyzing redressability, the court noted that even if OSC investigated and found a violation, they would have to go before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), which might then sanction employees, who might then release records. The court called this too speculative because it relies on choices by independent actors (MSPB and DOJ). Isn't this true of almost every administrative enforcement scheme? Why is this specific chain considered too attenuated compared to other cases where courts order agencies to take steps knowing downstream effects depend on third parties?
Answer:
The D.C. Circuit treated the potential actions of the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and the Department of Justice (DOJ) as an intervening cause breaking causation because the chain of events relied entirely on the **unfettered discretion** of independent actors not bound by a court judgment in this specific litigation.

While it is true that many administrative schemes involve downstream effects dependent on third parties, the distinction lies in the nature of that dependence and whether a court order can legally bind those third parties to act.

### The Specific Attenuation in *America First Legal*
In this opinion, Judge Katsas identifies a "speculative" causal chain consisting of four distinct steps, each requiring a discretionary choice by an entity not before the court:
1.  **OSC Discretion:** Even if compelled to investigate, OSC retains discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney* regarding whether to actually pursue corrective or disciplinary action (*Opinion* at II.B).
2.  **MSPB Action:** If OSC brings a case, the MSPB must independently decide to impose sanctions (*Opinion* at II.B).
3.  **DOJ Compliance:** Finally, even if sanctioned, the DOJ (the actual holder of the records) must independently decide to produce them (*Opinion* at II.B).

The court held that this chain fails the redressability test because it requires speculation about "choices by independent actors." Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the court emphasized that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts" (*Opinion* at II.B). Crucially, a judgment against OSC does not bind the MSPB or DOJ; therefore, a ruling ordering OSC to investigate would not "very likely spur" the subsequent releases of records (*Opinion* at II.B).

### Distinction from Other Administrative Schemes
You correctly note that courts often order agencies to take steps knowing downstream effects depend on others. However, those scenarios typically differ from *America First Legal* in two key ways:

1.  **Direct vs. Indirect Redressability:** In cases where standing is granted despite downstream dependencies (e.g., ordering an agency to issue permits), the relief sought is usually directed at a party over whom the court has jurisdiction, and that party's compliance directly resolves the injury without needing another agency's voluntary cooperation. Here, the plaintiff seeks records held by DOJ but sues only OSC. The remedy (investigation) does not directly produce records; it merely initiates a process where two other entities must voluntarily cooperate to achieve that result.
2.  **Binding Effect vs. Persuasion:** As noted in *Haaland v. Brackeen*, redressability depends on how a judgment binds the parties before it (*Opinion* at II.B). In typical administrative enforcement cases, if Agency X is ordered to act and its action automatically triggers a statutory duty for Agency Y (or if Agency X holds direct control over the remedy), causation exists. Here, there is no statutory mechanism forcing DOJ to release records simply because OSC investigates; DOJ remains free to withhold them regardless of OSC's findings unless it chooses otherwise or faces separate litigation.

### Conclusion
The court did not reject standing simply because third parties were involved; it rejected standing because the plaintiff's theory required guessing whether those third parties would exercise their discretion favorably after being pressured by an indirect judicial order against a different actor. The chain was deemed too attenuated because it relied on "speculation about... unfettered choices" rather than a legal mandate compelling immediate relief (*Opinion* at II.B).
