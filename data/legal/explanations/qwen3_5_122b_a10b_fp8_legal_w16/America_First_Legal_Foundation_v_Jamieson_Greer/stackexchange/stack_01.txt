Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does the 'relaxed' standing standard for procedural injuries eliminate the requirement to prove concrete injury in fact?
Question:
I'm struggling with the distinction between procedural rights and substantive injuries in Article III standing. The opinion mentions that for procedural injuries, courts 'relax' the requirements for imminence and redressability, but explicitly states they do not relax the requirement for 'injury in fact.' Does this mean a plaintiff can still sue solely because a statute granted them a procedure (like an investigation), even if they suffer no actual harm from the outcome? Or is there always a threshold requirement to show that the procedure protects some underlying concrete interest that was actually threatened?
Answer:
No, the "relaxed" standing standard for procedural injuries does **not** eliminate the requirement to prove a concrete injury in fact. As the D.C. Circuit explicitly held in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, a plaintiff cannot sue solely because a statute granted them a procedure if they suffer no actual harm from the outcome or lack an underlying concrete interest that the procedure was designed to protect.

The court reaffirmed the Supreme Court's precedent in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer Article III standing. The "relaxation" applies strictly to the elements of **imminence** and **redressability**, not to **injury in fact**. 

To establish standing in a procedural injury case, a plaintiff must demonstrate two things:
1.  They possess a **concrete, particularized interest** that is threatened or injured; and
2.  The statutory procedure was designed to protect that specific interest (*Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep't of Educ.*).

In *Jamieson Greer*, the court rejected America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) argument that it had standing simply because OSC failed to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. The court found AFL failed both prongs:
*   **Regarding the FOIA records:** AFL could not show that an OSC investigation would likely result in the release of records (failing redressability due to speculative chains involving non-parties like the MSPB and DOJ). More importantly, any injury regarding the records was traceable to DOJ's denial, not OSC's refusal to investigate (failing causation).
*   **Regarding information about the investigation:** AFL claimed an injury based on not receiving notices during an investigation. However, since § 1216 does not mandate that OSC provide § 1214-style notices, AFL had no statutory entitlement to that information. Without an entitlement, there was no concrete informational injury (*Am. Soc'y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc.*).

Therefore, the opinion makes clear that a "procedure in vacuo" is never enough. There is always a threshold requirement showing that the procedure protects an underlying concrete interest (such as access to specific documents) and that this interest has been concretely threatened or impaired by the defendant's failure to follow procedure.
