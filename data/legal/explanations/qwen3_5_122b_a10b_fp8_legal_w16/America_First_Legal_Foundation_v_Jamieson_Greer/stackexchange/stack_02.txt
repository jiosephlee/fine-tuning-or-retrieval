Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does Heckler v. Chaney prevent a plaintiff from establishing causation when seeking to compel an agency investigation?
Question:
The court rejected AFL's argument that OSC's failure to investigate caused their inability to get documents from DOJ. The opinion cites *Heckler v. Chaney* regarding enforcement discretion. My confusion lies in the causal chain: If OSC *must* investigate under § 1216, doesn't forcing that investigation break the chain of speculation? Why isn't it enough to say, 'If OSC investigates, they might find a violation,' rather than requiring proof that OSC will definitely force DOJ to release records? How does *Chaney* apply here if the statute allegedly makes the investigation mandatory rather than discretionary?
Answer:
*Heckler v. Chaney* prevents the establishment of causation in this context not by negating the statutory duty to *investigate*, but by severing the link between an investigation and any specific *outcome* (such as document disclosure or disciplinary action). Even if you accept the premise that § 1216 mandates an investigation, *Chaney* dictates that what happens after the investigation begins remains within the agency's unreviewable enforcement discretion.

Under *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985), an agency's decision not to take enforcement action is "presumptively committed to agency discretion" and generally immune from judicial review. The D.C. Circuit applies this presumption strictly to break causal chains in standing analysis where a plaintiff relies on a sequence of future discretionary acts by third parties.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court identifies a multi-step causal chain required to redress AFL's injury:
1.  OSC must investigate;
2.  OSC must choose to pursue corrective or disciplinary action;
3.  The Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) must impose sanctions; and
4.  The Department of Justice (DOJ) must voluntarily produce records following those sanctions.

The court explicitly notes that relying on this chain requires "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit." Crucially, step two depends entirely on how OSC chooses to exercise its **enforcement discretion**. While § 1216 may mandate the *initiation* of an inquiry into arbitrary withholdings, it does not mandate that OSC ultimately file a complaint with the MSPB or seek disciplinary action upon finding a violation. 

Therefore, even if a court orders OSC to open an investigation (breaking the first link), *Chaney* prevents the plaintiff from asserting that this order will "very likely" result in record production because it compels speculation on whether OSC will actually prosecute the case once opened. As the opinion states, "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about... how [an] agency might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion." Because the ultimate relief sought (DOJ releasing documents) hinges on a series of discretionary decisions that are presumptively unreviewable under *Chaney*, the injury is not fairly traceable to OSC's refusal to investigate, but rather to DOJ's independent withholding and the subsequent discretionary choices of OSC and MSPB.
