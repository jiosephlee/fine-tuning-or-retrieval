Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Can a statutory grant of authority to investigate create an 'informational injury' if no specific right to receive data exists?
Question:
AFL tried to argue they had standing because they were denied access to the 'fruits' of an OSC investigation (notices and updates). The court said § 1216 doesn't entitle anyone to receive information unless § 1214's notice requirements are applied, which seems discretionary. If Congress gives an agency power to investigate, does that automatically create a private right for citizens to know what happens during that investigation? Or is 'informational standing' strictly limited to statutes that explicitly say 'X person shall receive Y document'?
Answer:
No, a statutory grant of authority to investigate does not automatically create a private "informational injury" or a corresponding right to receive data regarding that investigation. Under the D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 WL (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), an "informational injury" only exists when a statute **explicitly** entitles the plaintiff to receive specific information.

The court rejected America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) argument that the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) failure to investigate created an actionable injury simply because AFL wanted the "fruits" of that investigation (such as notices and status updates). The court held that while 5 U.S.C. § 1216 mandates OSC to investigate certain allegations, it is silent on whether the complainant is entitled to receive information during that process.

Crucially, the opinion distinguishes between an agency's duty to *act* and a citizen's right to *know*. While § 1214 imposes specific notice requirements for investigations into "prohibited personnel practices," § 1216—which covers arbitrary FOIA withholdings—does not incorporate those requirements by default. As the court noted, precedent establishes that OSC's decision whether to apply § 1214's procedural protections in a § 1216 investigation is **discretionary**.

Consequently, because no statute granted AFL a right to receive the investigation's findings or updates, there was no "legally protected interest" invaded by OSC's silence. The court clarified that "informational standing" is strictly limited to scenarios where Congress has explicitly stated that "X person shall receive Y document." Without such an express entitlement, the denial of information remains a mere grievance rather than a concrete Article III injury capable of supporting standing.
