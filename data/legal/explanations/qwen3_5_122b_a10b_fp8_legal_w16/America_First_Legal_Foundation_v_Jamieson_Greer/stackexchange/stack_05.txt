Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What is the practical difference between dismissing for failure to state a claim versus dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction here?
Question:
The district court originally dismissed AFL's case for failing to state a claim on the merits after finding OSC made a legal error but acted within its discretion. On appeal, however, the D.C. Circuit reversed and dismissed based on Article III standing (lack of subject-matter jurisdiction). Since both result in dismissal without reaching the ultimate merits of whether DOJ violated FOIA policies, why is distinguishing between these two grounds critical? Specifically, how does characterizing this as a jurisdictional issue change how lower courts must handle similar cases in the future?
Answer:
The distinction between a dismissal for **failure to state a claim** (Rule 12(b)(6)) and a dismissal for **lack of subject-matter jurisdiction** (Rule 12(b)(1) due to Article III standing) is critical because it determines whether the court is addressing the plaintiff's legal rights or its fundamental power to hear the case. As demonstrated in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, this distinction dictates the scope of judicial review, the binding nature of the precedent, and the ability of lower courts to revisit the issue.

### 1. The Nature of the Error: Law vs. Power
When a district court dismisses for failure to state a claim, it assumes it has jurisdiction but concludes that, even accepting all factual allegations as true, the plaintiff has no legal remedy. In *AFL*, the district court held that OSC acted within its statutory discretion not to investigate, meaning AFL's claim failed on the merits of administrative law principles (*Heckler v. Chaney* deference).

However, when an appellate court dismisses for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction based on Article III standing, it holds that **no federal court has the constitutional authority** to adjudicate the dispute because there is no "Case" or "Controversy." As Judge Katsas noted in *AFL*, before reaching any merits—including whether OSC made a legal error regarding its statutory duties—the court must ensure the plaintiff suffered a concrete injury caused by the defendant and redressable by a favorable ruling. Because AFL could not trace its injury to OSC's refusal (due to intervening discretionary acts by DOJ and MSPB) nor show redressability, the federal judiciary lacked power entirely.

### 2. Preclusive Effect and Future Litigation
This distinction fundamentally changes how lower courts must handle similar cases in the future:

*   **Res Judicata / Claim Preclusion:** A dismissal on the merits (failure to state a claim) typically bars the plaintiff from refiling the same suit if they can cure their pleading defects or if new evidence arises that supports a valid legal theory. Conversely, a dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction is **not** preclusive on the merits. Under *Steel Co.* (cited in *AFL*), if standing is cured—for example, if AFL later obtains records directly from DOJ or shows a direct link between OSC's action and their injury—they could theoretically refile because they were never barred from asserting their rights; they simply weren't allowed into court *this time*.
*   **Binding Scope:** A merits-based decision interprets statutes (e.g., interpreting § 1216 vs. § 552(a)(4)(F)). Such interpretations bind future litigants regarding what Congress intended. A jurisdictional decision like *AFL* only establishes that **under current facts**, there is no concrete injury. It leaves open whether different facts (e.g., specific harm caused solely by OSC's non-investigation) could create standing. Lower courts are bound by *AFL*'s application of standing doctrine but are not necessarily bound by any implied interpretation of OSC's statutory powers, as those were explicitly left undecided ("we need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive").

### 3. Judicial Economy and Deference
Finally, characterizing this as jurisdictional prevents courts from issuing advisory opinions on executive branch discretion. By dismissing for lack of jurisdiction rather than stating that OSC had discretion not to act, the D.C. Circuit avoided making a potentially contentious ruling on administrative law principles (§ 1216 mandates vs. § 1214 discretion). Instead, it affirmed that without a concrete stake in the outcome, federal judges cannot compel an agency investigation regardless of how "mandatory" Congress wrote the statute appears to be on paper.

In summary, while both outcomes end in dismissal, treating this as a jurisdictional failure signals that AFL has no right to sue *at all* under these circumstances, whereas treating it as a failure to state a claim would imply AFL had a right but failed to articulate it correctly under existing law. This preserves AFL's theoretical right to return if their factual situation changes while preventing federal courts from speculating on hypothetical chains of causation involving third-party agencies like DOJ and MSPB.
