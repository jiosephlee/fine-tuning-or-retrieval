Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does the 'relaxed' standing standard for procedural injuries eliminate the requirement to prove concrete injury in fact?
Question:
I'm struggling with the distinction between procedural rights and substantive injuries in Article III standing. The opinion mentions that for procedural injuries, courts 'relax' the requirements for imminence and redressability, but explicitly states they do not relax the requirement for 'injury in fact.' Does this mean a plaintiff can still sue solely because a statute granted them a procedure (like an investigation), even if they suffer no actual harm from the outcome? Or is there always a threshold requirement to show that the procedure protects some underlying concrete interest that was actually threatened?
Answer:
No, the "relaxed" standing standard for procedural injuries does **not** eliminate the requirement to prove a concrete injury in fact. As the D.C. Circuit explicitly held in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, a plaintiff cannot sue solely because a statute granted them a procedure if they suffer no actual harm from the outcome or lack an underlying concrete interest that the procedure was designed to protect.

The court reaffirmed the Supreme Court's precedent in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" to confer Article III standing. The "relaxation" applies strictly to the elements of **imminence** and **redressability**, not to **injury in fact**. 

To establish standing in a procedural injury case, a plaintiff must demonstrate two things:
1.  They possess a **concrete, particularized interest** that is threatened or injured; and
2.  The statutory procedure was designed to protect that specific interest (*Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep't of Educ.*).

In *Jamieson Greer*, the court rejected America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) argument that it had standing simply because OSC failed to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. The court found AFL failed both prongs:
*   **Regarding the FOIA records:** AFL could not show that an OSC investigation would likely result in the release of records (failing redressability due to speculative chains involving non-parties like the MSPB and DOJ). More importantly, any injury regarding the records was traceable to DOJ's denial, not OSC's refusal to investigate (failing causation).
*   **Regarding information about the investigation:** AFL claimed an injury based on not receiving notices during an investigation. However, since § 1216 does not mandate that OSC provide § 1214-style notices, AFL had no statutory entitlement to that information. Without an entitlement, there was no concrete informational injury (*Am. Soc'y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc.*).

Therefore, the opinion makes clear that a "procedure in vacuo" is never enough. There is always a threshold requirement showing that the procedure protects an underlying concrete interest (such as access to specific documents) and that this interest has been concretely threatened or impaired by the defendant's failure to follow procedure.

### How does Heckler v. Chaney prevent a plaintiff from establishing causation when seeking to compel an agency investigation?
Question:
The court rejected AFL's argument that OSC's failure to investigate caused their inability to get documents from DOJ. The opinion cites *Heckler v. Chaney* regarding enforcement discretion. My confusion lies in the causal chain: If OSC *must* investigate under § 1216, doesn't forcing that investigation break the chain of speculation? Why isn't it enough to say, 'If OSC investigates, they might find a violation,' rather than requiring proof that OSC will definitely force DOJ to release records? How does *Chaney* apply here if the statute allegedly makes the investigation mandatory rather than discretionary?
Answer:
*Heckler v. Chaney* prevents the establishment of causation in this context not by negating the statutory duty to *investigate*, but by severing the link between an investigation and any specific *outcome* (such as document disclosure or disciplinary action). Even if you accept the premise that § 1216 mandates an investigation, *Chaney* dictates that what happens after the investigation begins remains within the agency's unreviewable enforcement discretion.

Under *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985), an agency's decision not to take enforcement action is "presumptively committed to agency discretion" and generally immune from judicial review. The D.C. Circuit applies this presumption strictly to break causal chains in standing analysis where a plaintiff relies on a sequence of future discretionary acts by third parties.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court identifies a multi-step causal chain required to redress AFL's injury:
1.  OSC must investigate;
2.  OSC must choose to pursue corrective or disciplinary action;
3.  The Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) must impose sanctions; and
4.  The Department of Justice (DOJ) must voluntarily produce records following those sanctions.

The court explicitly notes that relying on this chain requires "speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit." Crucially, step two depends entirely on how OSC chooses to exercise its **enforcement discretion**. While § 1216 may mandate the *initiation* of an inquiry into arbitrary withholdings, it does not mandate that OSC ultimately file a complaint with the MSPB or seek disciplinary action upon finding a violation. 

Therefore, even if a court orders OSC to open an investigation (breaking the first link), *Chaney* prevents the plaintiff from asserting that this order will "very likely" result in record production because it compels speculation on whether OSC will actually prosecute the case once opened. As the opinion states, "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about... how [an] agency might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion." Because the ultimate relief sought (DOJ releasing documents) hinges on a series of discretionary decisions that are presumptively unreviewable under *Chaney*, the injury is not fairly traceable to OSC's refusal to investigate, but rather to DOJ's independent withholding and the subsequent discretionary choices of OSC and MSPB.

### Can a statutory grant of authority to investigate create an 'informational injury' if no specific right to receive data exists?
Question:
AFL tried to argue they had standing because they were denied access to the 'fruits' of an OSC investigation (notices and updates). The court said § 1216 doesn't entitle anyone to receive information unless § 1214's notice requirements are applied, which seems discretionary. If Congress gives an agency power to investigate, does that automatically create a private right for citizens to know what happens during that investigation? Or is 'informational standing' strictly limited to statutes that explicitly say 'X person shall receive Y document'?
Answer:
No, a statutory grant of authority to investigate does not automatically create a private "informational injury" or a corresponding right to receive data regarding that investigation. Under the D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 WL (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), an "informational injury" only exists when a statute **explicitly** entitles the plaintiff to receive specific information.

The court rejected America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) argument that the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) failure to investigate created an actionable injury simply because AFL wanted the "fruits" of that investigation (such as notices and status updates). The court held that while 5 U.S.C. § 1216 mandates OSC to investigate certain allegations, it is silent on whether the complainant is entitled to receive information during that process.

Crucially, the opinion distinguishes between an agency's duty to *act* and a citizen's right to *know*. While § 1214 imposes specific notice requirements for investigations into "prohibited personnel practices," § 1216—which covers arbitrary FOIA withholdings—does not incorporate those requirements by default. As the court noted, precedent establishes that OSC's decision whether to apply § 1214's procedural protections in a § 1216 investigation is **discretionary**.

Consequently, because no statute granted AFL a right to receive the investigation's findings or updates, there was no "legally protected interest" invaded by OSC's silence. The court clarified that "informational standing" is strictly limited to scenarios where Congress has explicitly stated that "X person shall receive Y document." Without such an express entitlement, the denial of information remains a mere grievance rather than a concrete Article III injury capable of supporting standing.

### Why did the D.C. Circuit treat the MSPB's potential future actions as an intervening cause breaking causation?
Question:
In analyzing redressability, the court noted that even if OSC investigated and found a violation, they would have to go before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), which might then sanction employees, who might then release records. The court called this too speculative because it relies on choices by independent actors (MSPB and DOJ). Isn't this true of almost every administrative enforcement scheme? Why is this specific chain considered too attenuated compared to other cases where courts order agencies to take steps knowing downstream effects depend on third parties?
Answer:
The D.C. Circuit treated the potential actions of the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and the Department of Justice (DOJ) as an intervening cause breaking causation because the chain of events relied entirely on the **unfettered discretion** of independent actors not bound by a court judgment in this specific litigation.

While it is true that many administrative schemes involve downstream effects dependent on third parties, the distinction lies in the nature of that dependence and whether a court order can legally bind those third parties to act.

### The Specific Attenuation in *America First Legal*
In this opinion, Judge Katsas identifies a "speculative" causal chain consisting of four distinct steps, each requiring a discretionary choice by an entity not before the court:
1.  **OSC Discretion:** Even if compelled to investigate, OSC retains discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney* regarding whether to actually pursue corrective or disciplinary action (*Opinion* at II.B).
2.  **MSPB Action:** If OSC brings a case, the MSPB must independently decide to impose sanctions (*Opinion* at II.B).
3.  **DOJ Compliance:** Finally, even if sanctioned, the DOJ (the actual holder of the records) must independently decide to produce them (*Opinion* at II.B).

The court held that this chain fails the redressability test because it requires speculation about "choices by independent actors." Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the court emphasized that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts" (*Opinion* at II.B). Crucially, a judgment against OSC does not bind the MSPB or DOJ; therefore, a ruling ordering OSC to investigate would not "very likely spur" the subsequent releases of records (*Opinion* at II.B).

### Distinction from Other Administrative Schemes
You correctly note that courts often order agencies to take steps knowing downstream effects depend on others. However, those scenarios typically differ from *America First Legal* in two key ways:

1.  **Direct vs. Indirect Redressability:** In cases where standing is granted despite downstream dependencies (e.g., ordering an agency to issue permits), the relief sought is usually directed at a party over whom the court has jurisdiction, and that party's compliance directly resolves the injury without needing another agency's voluntary cooperation. Here, the plaintiff seeks records held by DOJ but sues only OSC. The remedy (investigation) does not directly produce records; it merely initiates a process where two other entities must voluntarily cooperate to achieve that result.
2.  **Binding Effect vs. Persuasion:** As noted in *Haaland v. Brackeen*, redressability depends on how a judgment binds the parties before it (*Opinion* at II.B). In typical administrative enforcement cases, if Agency X is ordered to act and its action automatically triggers a statutory duty for Agency Y (or if Agency X holds direct control over the remedy), causation exists. Here, there is no statutory mechanism forcing DOJ to release records simply because OSC investigates; DOJ remains free to withhold them regardless of OSC's findings unless it chooses otherwise or faces separate litigation.

### Conclusion
The court did not reject standing simply because third parties were involved; it rejected standing because the plaintiff's theory required guessing whether those third parties would exercise their discretion favorably after being pressured by an indirect judicial order against a different actor. The chain was deemed too attenuated because it relied on "speculation about... unfettered choices" rather than a legal mandate compelling immediate relief (*Opinion* at II.B).

### What is the practical difference between dismissing for failure to state a claim versus dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction here?
Question:
The district court originally dismissed AFL's case for failing to state a claim on the merits after finding OSC made a legal error but acted within its discretion. On appeal, however, the D.C. Circuit reversed and dismissed based on Article III standing (lack of subject-matter jurisdiction). Since both result in dismissal without reaching the ultimate merits of whether DOJ violated FOIA policies, why is distinguishing between these two grounds critical? Specifically, how does characterizing this as a jurisdictional issue change how lower courts must handle similar cases in the future?
Answer:
The distinction between a dismissal for **failure to state a claim** (Rule 12(b)(6)) and a dismissal for **lack of subject-matter jurisdiction** (Rule 12(b)(1) due to Article III standing) is critical because it determines whether the court is addressing the plaintiff's legal rights or its fundamental power to hear the case. As demonstrated in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, this distinction dictates the scope of judicial review, the binding nature of the precedent, and the ability of lower courts to revisit the issue.

### 1. The Nature of the Error: Law vs. Power
When a district court dismisses for failure to state a claim, it assumes it has jurisdiction but concludes that, even accepting all factual allegations as true, the plaintiff has no legal remedy. In *AFL*, the district court held that OSC acted within its statutory discretion not to investigate, meaning AFL's claim failed on the merits of administrative law principles (*Heckler v. Chaney* deference).

However, when an appellate court dismisses for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction based on Article III standing, it holds that **no federal court has the constitutional authority** to adjudicate the dispute because there is no "Case" or "Controversy." As Judge Katsas noted in *AFL*, before reaching any merits—including whether OSC made a legal error regarding its statutory duties—the court must ensure the plaintiff suffered a concrete injury caused by the defendant and redressable by a favorable ruling. Because AFL could not trace its injury to OSC's refusal (due to intervening discretionary acts by DOJ and MSPB) nor show redressability, the federal judiciary lacked power entirely.

### 2. Preclusive Effect and Future Litigation
This distinction fundamentally changes how lower courts must handle similar cases in the future:

*   **Res Judicata / Claim Preclusion:** A dismissal on the merits (failure to state a claim) typically bars the plaintiff from refiling the same suit if they can cure their pleading defects or if new evidence arises that supports a valid legal theory. Conversely, a dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction is **not** preclusive on the merits. Under *Steel Co.* (cited in *AFL*), if standing is cured—for example, if AFL later obtains records directly from DOJ or shows a direct link between OSC's action and their injury—they could theoretically refile because they were never barred from asserting their rights; they simply weren't allowed into court *this time*.
*   **Binding Scope:** A merits-based decision interprets statutes (e.g., interpreting § 1216 vs. § 552(a)(4)(F)). Such interpretations bind future litigants regarding what Congress intended. A jurisdictional decision like *AFL* only establishes that **under current facts**, there is no concrete injury. It leaves open whether different facts (e.g., specific harm caused solely by OSC's non-investigation) could create standing. Lower courts are bound by *AFL*'s application of standing doctrine but are not necessarily bound by any implied interpretation of OSC's statutory powers, as those were explicitly left undecided ("we need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive").

### 3. Judicial Economy and Deference
Finally, characterizing this as jurisdictional prevents courts from issuing advisory opinions on executive branch discretion. By dismissing for lack of jurisdiction rather than stating that OSC had discretion not to act, the D.C. Circuit avoided making a potentially contentious ruling on administrative law principles (§ 1216 mandates vs. § 1214 discretion). Instead, it affirmed that without a concrete stake in the outcome, federal judges cannot compel an agency investigation regardless of how "mandatory" Congress wrote the statute appears to be on paper.

In summary, while both outcomes end in dismissal, treating this as a jurisdictional failure signals that AFL has no right to sue *at all* under these circumstances, whereas treating it as a failure to state a claim would imply AFL had a right but failed to articulate it correctly under existing law. This preserves AFL's theoretical right to return if their factual situation changes while preventing federal courts from speculating on hypothetical chains of causation involving third-party agencies like DOJ and MSPB.

### Does § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limit OSC's broader mandate under § 1216(a)(3)?
Question:
The facts mention a split in interpretation: OSC claimed it couldn't investigate unless specific FOIA court predicates were met (§ 552), while AFL argued § 1216 gave them independent authority regardless of those predicates. Although the appellate court didn't reach this statutory interpretation issue due to standing concerns, how should I read these two sections together? Is § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) meant to be an exhaustive list of triggers for OSC involvement in FOIA matters, or just one pathway among many created by § 1216?
Answer:
Based on the text of *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the short answer is that **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is not an exhaustive list limiting OSC's authority under § 1216**, though the D.C. Circuit explicitly declined to rule definitively on this statutory interpretation issue.

Here is how you should read the relationship between these two statutes based on the opinion:

**1. The Statutory Distinction**
The opinion clearly distinguishes the two mechanisms as serving different purposes and triggered by different events:
*   **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** creates a specific, mandatory trigger for OSC only after a *court* has already litigated a FOIA case, awarded fees, and specifically found the withholding "arbitrary or capricious." It is a reactive mechanism tied to judicial findings.
*   **§ 1216(a)(3)**, conversely, grants OSC independent authority to investigate allegations of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings directly upon receipt of an allegation. The statute uses the mandatory language "shall... conduct an investigation," suggesting a proactive role distinct from the judicial-triggered process in § 552.

**2. The Lower Court's Reasoning (Adopted as Context)**
While the appellate court dismissed the case on standing grounds, it noted that the district court correctly interpreted the statutes as non-exclusive. The district court held that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does **not** limit OSC's power under § 1216 (*Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner*, 2023 WL 9546644). The appellate panel accepted this premise enough to proceed with its analysis of standing, acknowledging that OSC's argument—that it lacked authority absent the specific § 552 predicates—was based on a "legal error" regarding statutory construction, even if OSC retained discretion on *how* to exercise that authority once triggered.

**3. Why OSC Argued Otherwise (and why they lost on Standing)**
OSC attempted to conflate the two sections, arguing it could not act under § 1216 unless the strict prerequisites of § 552 were met. However, because the appellate court found AFL lacked Article III standing (due to speculative causation regarding redressability), they vacated the lower court's order without reaching this statutory question directly.

**Conclusion for Your Analysis**
You should interpret these sections as creating **two parallel pathways**:
*   One path exists when a private citizen wins a FOIA suit and meets all three criteria in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).
*   A second, independent path exists whenever there is an allegation of arbitrary withholding under § 1216(a)(3), regardless of prior litigation or fee awards.

The opinion suggests that reading § 552 as an exclusive limitation would render § 1216 superfluous for pre-litigation scenarios, violating basic principles of statutory construction (though *Greer* stops short of formally declaring this). Therefore, § 552 is best understood as one specific trigger among others available to OSC, rather than a ceiling on its mandate under § 1216.
