Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## The Attenuated Chain: Why Speculation About Agency Enforcement Breaks Causation

The D.C. Circuit's October 3, 2025, decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a stark reminder that statutory mandates for agency investigations do not automatically translate into judicially cognizable rights for private litigants. While the case ostensibly concerns the scope of the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) duty to investigate Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations, the real story lies in the court's rigorous application of Article III standing doctrine. Writing for a unanimous panel, Circuit Judge Katsas dismantled America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) claim not by debating the merits of OSC's interpretation of the Civil Service Reform Act, but by exposing the "attenuated" nature of the causal link between an OSC investigation and the production of government records. In doing so, the court reinforced a critical boundary: federal courts cannot bootstrap jurisdiction over agency enforcement discretion merely by hypothesizing that a third-party actor might eventually act favorably if pressured by another agency.

The factual backdrop involves a classic FOIA dispute where AFL sought emails regarding judicial recusals from the Department of Justice (DOJ). When DOJ denied the request based on a policy requiring employee consent to search personal email accounts, AFL turned to OSC, invoking 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which commands OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary or capricious withholding of information under FOIA. OSC declined, arguing that its mandatory duty was triggered only when specific predicates under FOIA itself were met—predicates that included a prior court order finding arbitrary withholding—which were absent here. The district court found OSC's legal reasoning flawed but ultimately dismissed AFL's suit because it viewed OSC's ultimate decision to pursue corrective action as discretionary. On appeal, however, Judge Katsas sidestepped this statutory debate entirely, focusing instead on whether AFL could clear the threshold hurdle of Article III standing at all.

Judge Katsas' analysis zeroes in on the causation and redressability prongs of standing, applying what is becoming increasingly strict scrutiny to plaintiffs attempting to sue over procedural injuries. AFL attempted to argue two theories: first, that without an OSC investigation, they would never get their documents; and second, that they were denied access to internal OSC investigative materials. Both theories collapsed under the weight of speculative chains involving independent actors not present in court. Regarding the first theory, AFL posited that if OSC had investigated DOJ's policy, it might have pursued corrective action against specific employees; those employees might then have been sanctioned by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB); and finally, DOJ might have released the records in response to such pressure. Judge Katsas rightly identified this sequence as legally insufficient under current Supreme Court precedent. Relying heavily on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), the court emphasized that causation cannot rest on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors." Here, those actors include both OSP and DOJ—entities whose future discretionary decisions remain unbound by any ruling in this litigation.

This reasoning marks a significant tightening of the screws on "informational standing" when paired with enforcement mechanisms controlled by others. The court noted that even if Congress mandated an investigation into arbitrary withholdings, it did not mandate a specific outcome like record production or disciplinary action against employees. Because OSC retains broad enforcement discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*, and because any subsequent remedial action would depend on voluntary compliance or separate adjudications by other bodies, there is no "likely" path from a judicial order compelling an investigation to redressing AFL's injury. As Judge Katsas wrote, redressability depends on how a judgment binds the parties before it; it does not turn on whether a court's rationale might "persuade" non-parties like DOJ or MSPB to change their behavior later. This distinction is vital: it prevents plaintiffs from using federal courts as leverage points to influence complex inter-agency dynamics where direct relief is impossible for anyone except perhaps indirectly through political pressure rather than legal compulsion.

The implications of *Greer* extend beyond FOIA disputes into any area where Congress has created overlapping regulatory schemes with multiple layers of administrative discretion. By rejecting AFL's attempt to treat an investigatory mandate as a guaranteed pipeline to substantive relief, the D.C. Circuit has insulated agencies' enforcement priorities from being hijacked through third-party lawsuits seeking procedural triggers elsewhere in government operations. Future litigants wishing to challenge an agency's failure to investigate will face a steep uphill battle; they must demonstrate not just that an investigation occurred or failed to occur due to statutory error, but that such failure directly caused their injury without relying on a chain reaction involving other agencies' discretionary choices. In an era where statutory schemes often create intricate webs of oversight and reporting requirements, *Greer* suggests that unless Congress explicitly ties an investigative duty to a specific remedy for a private citizen, those duties may remain largely immune from judicial enforcement via Article III standing challenges filed by interested observers rather than direct victims of non-compliance.
