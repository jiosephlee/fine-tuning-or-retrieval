Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## Procedural Rights in Vacuo: The D.C. Circuit Reinforces the 'Concrete Interest' Requirement for Standing

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit delivered a sharp reminder that a statutory command directed at an agency does not automatically translate into a judicially cognizable injury for every interested observer. Writing for the panel, Judge Gregory Katsas dismissed America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) attempt to force the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Department of Justice's handling of a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the underlying dispute involves high-stakes transparency issues regarding judicial recusal investigations, the court's disposition rests on a foundational threshold question: can a plaintiff sue solely because an agency failed to perform a duty owed to "the public" rather than to them? The answer, according to Judge Katsas, is a resounding no. By rigorously applying *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, the court reaffirmed that procedural rights cannot exist in a vacuum; they require a tether to a concrete, particularized interest that is actually harmed by the procedural defect.

The factual backdrop involves AFL's request for emails between an outside attorney and the DOJ's Civil Division. When the DOJ denied parts of the request based on internal email-search policies, AFL sought relief not just through traditional FOIA litigation—which was already underway—but by invoking 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). This statute directs the OSC to investigate allegations that information was "arbitrarily or capriciously withheld." AFL argued this mandate was self-executing and required OSC to launch an inquiry regardless of whether a court had first found a FOIA violation. The district court agreed that OSC erred in refusing to investigate but stopped short of ordering one, citing discretion in enforcement. On appeal, however, Judge Katsas sidestepped the statutory interpretation battle entirely, concluding that AFL lacked Article III standing before reaching any merits analysis. This approach underscores a growing judicial skepticism toward plaintiffs attempting to use procedural statutes as end-runs around traditional standing hurdles.

Judge Katsas dismantled AFL's argument by dissecting the two distinct theories of injury offered: first, that OSC's failure deprived AFL of the underlying records from DOJ; and second, that it deprived AFL of information regarding OSC's own investigative process. Both theories collapsed under scrutiny regarding causation and redressability. Regarding the records themselves, the court noted that any denial stemmed from DOJ, not OSC. To link OSC's inaction to DOJ's non-disclosure required a speculative chain involving multiple independent actors: OSC would have to initiate an investigation, choose corrective action over dismissal, the Merit Systems Protection Board would have to impose sanctions, and finally, DOJ would have to alter its behavior in response. As Judge Katsas pointed out, relying on such speculation violates core principles articulated in *Heckler v. Chaney* and recent Supreme Court precedent like *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. Courts generally cannot compel executive enforcement discretion, nor can they base standing on hypothetical choices made by third-party agencies not before the court.

The second prong of AFL's argument—that it had an informational injury regarding OSC's internal processes—fared even worse under existing D.C. Circuit jurisprudence. Plaintiffs often argue that when Congress grants them specific procedural rights (like notice or participation), being denied those rights constitutes an injury-in-fact sufficient for standing under cases like *Federal Election Commission v. Akins*. However, *Summers* established a critical limitation: there must be some concrete interest threatened by the procedure itself; otherwise, it is merely "a procedural right in vacuo." Here, Judge Katsas emphasized that while § 1216 mandates an investigation into arbitrary withholdings, it does not explicitly grant private citizens a right to receive notices or updates during that investigation unless it mirrors proceedings under § 1214 (prohibited personnel practices). Since previous case law confirms OSC retains discretion over whether to apply § 1214 procedures in § 1216 cases, AFL could not claim an entitlement to information it never possessed under statute or regulation without making new law from scratch—a task reserved for Congress or higher courts via different mechanisms than simple standing assertions.

This ruling carries significant doctrinal weight for future "informational standing" litigants across administrative law contexts. In recent years, advocacy groups have increasingly attempted to leverage broad statutory duties imposed on agencies—such as environmental impact statements or data collection mandates—to secure access to courts whenever those duties are allegedly ignored by regulators who fail to act against third parties. *Greer* signals that this strategy faces steep headwinds in Washington D.C., particularly where the chain of causation relies on uncoordinated enforcement actions by multiple entities within the executive branch. By insisting on a direct link between the procedural violation and a concrete harm specific to the plaintiff—rather than generalized grievance about regulatory inertia—the D.C. Circuit narrows the pathway for citizen suits aimed at policing agency compliance with internal management statutes like CSRA § 1216.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a clarifying moment for how courts distinguish between statutory commands intended for systemic governance and individual rights actionable in federal court. Judge Katsas did not rule on whether OSC should have investigated; indeed, he left open whether such an investigation was legally required under § 1216(a)(3).
