Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## Procedural Rights in Vacuo: The D.C. Circuit Reinforces the 'Concrete Interest' Requirement for Standing

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit delivered a sharp reminder that a statutory command directed at an agency does not automatically translate into a judicially cognizable injury for every interested observer. Writing for the panel, Judge Gregory Katsas dismissed America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) attempt to force the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Department of Justice's handling of a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the underlying dispute involves high-stakes transparency issues regarding judicial recusal investigations, the court's disposition rests on a foundational threshold question: can a plaintiff sue solely because an agency failed to perform a duty owed to "the public" rather than to them? The answer, according to Judge Katsas, is a resounding no. By rigorously applying *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, the court reaffirmed that procedural rights cannot exist in a vacuum; they require a tether to a concrete, particularized interest that is actually harmed by the procedural defect.

The factual backdrop involves AFL's request for emails between an outside attorney and the DOJ's Civil Division. When the DOJ denied parts of the request based on internal email-search policies, AFL sought relief not just through traditional FOIA litigation—which was already underway—but by invoking 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). This statute directs the OSC to investigate allegations that information was "arbitrarily or capriciously withheld." AFL argued this mandate was self-executing and required OSC to launch an inquiry regardless of whether a court had first found a FOIA violation. The district court agreed that OSC erred in refusing to investigate but stopped short of ordering one, citing discretion in enforcement. On appeal, however, Judge Katsas sidestepped the statutory interpretation battle entirely, concluding that AFL lacked Article III standing before reaching any merits analysis. This approach underscores a growing judicial skepticism toward plaintiffs attempting to use procedural statutes as end-runs around traditional standing hurdles.

Judge Katsas dismantled AFL's argument by dissecting the two distinct theories of injury offered: first, that OSC's failure deprived AFL of the underlying records from DOJ; and second, that it deprived AFL of information regarding OSC's own investigative process. Both theories collapsed under scrutiny regarding causation and redressability. Regarding the records themselves, the court noted that any denial stemmed from DOJ, not OSC. To link OSC's inaction to DOJ's non-disclosure required a speculative chain involving multiple independent actors: OSC would have to initiate an investigation, choose corrective action over dismissal, the Merit Systems Protection Board would have to impose sanctions, and finally, DOJ would have to alter its behavior in response. As Judge Katsas pointed out, relying on such speculation violates core principles articulated in *Heckler v. Chaney* and recent Supreme Court precedent like *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. Courts generally cannot compel executive enforcement discretion, nor can they base standing on hypothetical choices made by third-party agencies not before the court.

The second prong of AFL's argument—that it had an informational injury regarding OSC's internal processes—fared even worse under existing D.C. Circuit jurisprudence. Plaintiffs often argue that when Congress grants them specific procedural rights (like notice or participation), being denied those rights constitutes an injury-in-fact sufficient for standing under cases like *Federal Election Commission v. Akins*. However, *Summers* established a critical limitation: there must be some concrete interest threatened by the procedure itself; otherwise, it is merely "a procedural right in vacuo." Here, Judge Katsas emphasized that while § 1216 mandates an investigation into arbitrary withholdings, it does not explicitly grant private citizens a right to receive notices or updates during that investigation unless it mirrors proceedings under § 1214 (prohibited personnel practices). Since previous case law confirms OSC retains discretion over whether to apply § 1214 procedures in § 1216 cases, AFL could not claim an entitlement to information it never possessed under statute or regulation without making new law from scratch—a task reserved for Congress or higher courts via different mechanisms than simple standing assertions.

This ruling carries significant doctrinal weight for future "informational standing" litigants across administrative law contexts. In recent years, advocacy groups have increasingly attempted to leverage broad statutory duties imposed on agencies—such as environmental impact statements or data collection mandates—to secure access to courts whenever those duties are allegedly ignored by regulators who fail to act against third parties. *Greer* signals that this strategy faces steep headwinds in Washington D.C., particularly where the chain of causation relies on uncoordinated enforcement actions by multiple entities within the executive branch. By insisting on a direct link between the procedural violation and a concrete harm specific to the plaintiff—rather than generalized grievance about regulatory inertia—the D.C. Circuit narrows the pathway for citizen suits aimed at policing agency compliance with internal management statutes like CSRA § 1216.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a clarifying moment for how courts distinguish between statutory commands intended for systemic governance and individual rights actionable in federal court. Judge Katsas did not rule on whether OSC should have investigated; indeed, he left open whether such an investigation was legally required under § 1216(a)(3). ## The Attenuated Chain: Why Speculation About Agency Enforcement Breaks Causation

The D.C. Circuit's October 3, 2025, decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a stark reminder that statutory mandates for agency investigations do not automatically translate into judicially cognizable rights for private litigants. While the case ostensibly concerns the scope of the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) duty to investigate Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violations, the real story lies in the court's rigorous application of Article III standing doctrine. Writing for a unanimous panel, Circuit Judge Katsas dismantled America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) claim not by debating the merits of OSC's interpretation of the Civil Service Reform Act, but by exposing the "attenuated" nature of the causal link between an OSC investigation and the production of government records. In doing so, the court reinforced a critical boundary: federal courts cannot bootstrap jurisdiction over agency enforcement discretion merely by hypothesizing that a third-party actor might eventually act favorably if pressured by another agency.

The factual backdrop involves a classic FOIA dispute where AFL sought emails regarding judicial recusals from the Department of Justice (DOJ). When DOJ denied the request based on a policy requiring employee consent to search personal email accounts, AFL turned to OSC, invoking 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which commands OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary or capricious withholding of information under FOIA. OSC declined, arguing that its mandatory duty was triggered only when specific predicates under FOIA itself were met—predicates that included a prior court order finding arbitrary withholding—which were absent here. The district court found OSC's legal reasoning flawed but ultimately dismissed AFL's suit because it viewed OSC's ultimate decision to pursue corrective action as discretionary. On appeal, however, Judge Katsas sidestepped this statutory debate entirely, focusing instead on whether AFL could clear the threshold hurdle of Article III standing at all.

Judge Katsas' analysis zeroes in on the causation and redressability prongs of standing, applying what is becoming increasingly strict scrutiny to plaintiffs attempting to sue over procedural injuries. AFL attempted to argue two theories: first, that without an OSC investigation, they would never get their documents; and second, that they were denied access to internal OSC investigative materials. Both theories collapsed under the weight of speculative chains involving independent actors not present in court. Regarding the first theory, AFL posited that if OSC had investigated DOJ's policy, it might have pursued corrective action against specific employees; those employees might then have been sanctioned by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB); and finally, DOJ might have released the records in response to such pressure. Judge Katsas rightly identified this sequence as legally insufficient under current Supreme Court precedent. Relying heavily on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), the court emphasized that causation cannot rest on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors." Here, those actors include both OSP and DOJ—entities whose future discretionary decisions remain unbound by any ruling in this litigation.

This reasoning marks a significant tightening of the screws on "informational standing" when paired with enforcement mechanisms controlled by others. The court noted that even if Congress mandated an investigation into arbitrary withholdings, it did not mandate a specific outcome like record production or disciplinary action against employees. Because OSC retains broad enforcement discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*, and because any subsequent remedial action would depend on voluntary compliance or separate adjudications by other bodies, there is no "likely" path from a judicial order compelling an investigation to redressing AFL's injury. As Judge Katsas wrote, redressability depends on how a judgment binds the parties before it; it does not turn on whether a court's rationale might "persuade" non-parties like DOJ or MSPB to change their behavior later. This distinction is vital: it prevents plaintiffs from using federal courts as leverage points to influence complex inter-agency dynamics where direct relief is impossible for anyone except perhaps indirectly through political pressure rather than legal compulsion.

The implications of *Greer* extend beyond FOIA disputes into any area where Congress has created overlapping regulatory schemes with multiple layers of administrative discretion. By rejecting AFL's attempt to treat an investigatory mandate as a guaranteed pipeline to substantive relief, the D.C. Circuit has insulated agencies' enforcement priorities from being hijacked through third-party lawsuits seeking procedural triggers elsewhere in government operations. Future litigants wishing to challenge an agency's failure to investigate will face a steep uphill battle; they must demonstrate not just that an investigation occurred or failed to occur due to statutory error, but that such failure directly caused their injury without relying on a chain reaction involving other agencies' discretionary choices. In an era where statutory schemes often create intricate webs of oversight and reporting requirements, *Greer* suggests that unless Congress explicitly ties an investigative duty to a specific remedy for a private citizen, those duties may remain largely immune from judicial enforcement via Article III standing challenges filed by interested observers rather than direct victims of non-compliance.



## Statutory Interpretation vs. Jurisdictional Gatekeeping: The OSC Investigation Mandate Remains Unresolved

The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a stark reminder that even the most compelling statutory arguments can vanish into the ether if they fail to clear the threshold of Article III standing. In a unanimous opinion authored by Judge Katsas, the court dismissed America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) suit seeking to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Department of Justice's alleged arbitrary denial of a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the lower court had waded into the weeds of statutory interpretation—suggesting that 5 U.S.C. § 1216 might mandate an OSC investigation independent of specific FOIA court findings—the appellate panel never reached that substantive question. Instead, it held that AFL lacked a concrete, redressable injury, effectively leaving the scope of OSC's investigative powers over FOIA violations in a state of suspended animation.

The core factual dispute arose from AFL's attempt to secure emails regarding judicial recusals from the DOJ. When the DOJ denied the request, citing a policy against searching employee emails without consent, AFL turned not just to traditional FOIA litigation—which was already underway—but to the OSC. They invoked 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which directs the OSC "to conduct an investigation" of allegations concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under FOIA. The OSC declined, arguing its duty was triggered only after a court found an agency acted arbitrarily and awarded fees under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The district court disagreed with this reading, ruling that § 1216 created an independent mandate, but ultimately dismissed AFL's claim anyway because it viewed the ultimate decision to investigate as discretionary and non-reviewable. On appeal, however, Judge Katsas sidestepped this interpretive tug-of-war entirely, focusing instead on whether AFL had any business being in federal court at all.

Judge Katsas's analysis zeroes in on the distinction between a procedural right and a concrete injury. Under Supreme Court precedent like *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, a plaintiff cannot rely solely on the deprivation of a procedural right; there must be some underlying concrete interest that is actually threatened by that deprivation. AFL attempted to bridge this gap by pointing to two types of informational injuries: first, the continued lack of access to the DOJ emails themselves, and second, a lack of updates regarding any potential OSC investigation into those emails. The D.C. Circuit rejected both theories with surgical precision regarding causation and redressability.

Regarding the missing emails, the court reasoned that any injury flowing from non-disclosure is traceable to the DOJ's initial refusal, not the OSC's subsequent inaction. To link OSC's failure to investigate with DOJ eventually producing documents requires a "speculative chain" involving multiple independent actors: assuming OSC investigates, assumes it pursues corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), assumes the MSPB sanctions employees, and finally assumes those sanctions cause DOJ compliance. As Judge Katsas noted, relying on such speculation violates fundamental principles of causation established in cases like *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*. Furthermore, courts generally cannot compel enforcement discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*, making it impossible for a judicial order here to guarantee redress for AFL's lost data access.

The second prong of AFL's argument—that they were injured by not receiving status reports during an investigation—fared no better because no statute explicitly grants them that right in this context. While § 1214 outlines notification procedures for "prohibited personnel practices," § 1216 investigations are distinct precedents where prior case law suggests OSC has discretion over whether to adopt those procedural safeguards absent a specific mandate. Without a statutory entitlement to receive information about an investigation that hasn't happened yet—and one that might not happen at all—there is no concrete injury sufficient for Article III standing.

The doctrinal implications of this dismissal are significant for administrative law and FOIA enforcement alike. By stopping short of addressing whether § 1216 creates an independent trigger for OSC investigations outside the narrow confines of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), the D.C. Circuit leaves open a potentially critical loophole in federal transparency oversight. If Congress intended for OSC to act as an independent watchdog capable of initiating inquiries into arbitrary record-holding without waiting for judicial confirmation or fee awards—as suggested by some readings of § 1216(a)(3)—that intent remains untested in federal jurisprudence because no litigant could currently demonstrate standing to challenge an agency's refusal to act on it today.

This outcome highlights a persistent tension in modern administrative litigation: Congress often writes statutes designed to empower private citizens or organizations to police agency behavior through mandatory reporting or investigation clauses (*mandamus*-style triggers), but Article III standing doctrine frequently acts as a gatekeeper preventing those mechanisms from ever seeing daylight unless there is already tangible harm flowing directly from agency action rather than agency omission by third parties like OSC or MSPB until they take affirmative steps toward discipline? Until Congress clarifies whether individuals have an enforceable right to demand these investigations—or until future plaintiffs can articulate how an investigatory delay causes immediate, particularized harm beyond mere frustration—the gap between statutory text and judicial enforceability will remain wide open.*Greer* ensures that while agencies may face political pressure or internal reviews regarding their FOIA policies, they will rarely face judicial compulsion via third-party oversight bodies unless someone can prove they are personally suffering from those policies in real-time.*
