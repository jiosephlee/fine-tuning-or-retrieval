Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Statutory Framework and Procedural History

## The Mandate and Powers of the Office of Special Counsel Under 5 U.S.C. §§ 1214–1216

To understand the dispute in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, one must first grasp the statutory architecture governing the Office of Special Counsel (OSC). Established by the Civil Service Reform Act, the OSC functions as an independent investigative and prosecutorial agency dedicated to maintaining a "fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." Its primary statutory engine is found in 5 U.S.C. § 1214, which empowers the OSC to investigate "prohibited personnel practices"—such as discrimination or retaliation against whistleblowers—and, where appropriate, to seek corrective action before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) or disciplinary action against offending employees. Crucially, § 1214 includes specific procedural mandates, requiring the OSC to notify complaining parties about the progress of an investigation.

However, the scope of the OSC's authority extends beyond traditional personnel disputes through 5 U.S.C. § 1216. This provision grants the OSC broad investigative power over other forms of government misconduct, including allegations that an agency has engaged in "arbitrary or capricious withholding of information" prohibited under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). When acting under § 1216(a)(3), the statute directs that the OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation" of such allegations. Furthermore, § 1216(c) explicitly authorizes the OSC to utilize the enforcement mechanisms of § 1214 (corrective action) and § 1215 (disciplinary action) for FOIA-related violations just as they would for standard prohibited personnel practices. This statutory linkage creates a potential avenue for third-party litigants like America First Legal Foundation (AFL) to seek enforcement against agencies that allegedly violate FOIA policies, bypassing direct litigation in favor of administrative oversight by the OSC.

## Distinction Between 'Prohibited Personnel Practices' and Arbitrary FOIA Withholdings

While § 1216 allows the OSC to apply personnel practice remedies to FOIA violations, a critical distinction exists between a standard "prohibited personnel practice" and an "arbitrary FOIA withholding." A prohibited personnel practice typically involves harm directed at a specific individual within the civil service system due to their employment status or protected activity. In contrast, an arbitrary FOIA withholding represents a systemic or policy-based refusal to release public records, often affecting members of the public rather than current employees.

The opinion highlights that while Congress intended for these two categories to share remedial pathways—allowing for corrective or disciplinary measures—their triggers differ significantly in operation. The key friction point in this case arises from whether § 1216 operates as an independent mandate that compels an investigation whenever an arbitrary withholding is alleged, or whether it functions merely as a bridge mechanism dependent on other conditions being met first. The text suggests that while § 1216 grants power over arbitrary withholdings, it does not exist in a vacuum; it interacts with specific limitations found elsewhere in federal law regarding how and when such investigations are triggered relative to existing judicial proceedings or administrative findings. Understanding this distinction is vital because AFL's argument rests on treating every allegation of arbitrary withholding as a self-executing command for OSC intervention, whereas opposing arguments suggest additional prerequisites must be satisfied before such power can be activated.

## The Specific Trigger for OSC Investigation Under FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

Beyond § 1216, FOIA itself contains its own distinct provision addressing agency non-compliance: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). This section establishes a narrow but potent trigger for OSC involvement that differs fundamentally from a simple complaint about non-disclosure. Under this subsection, the OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding" only after three specific conditions are simultaneously met following court litigation: (1) a court has ordered an agency to produce records improperly withheld; (2) the court has awarded attorney's fees against that agency; and (3) the court has specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously regarding the withholding. Only when all three prongs are satisfied does this specific statutory provision mandate immediate disciplinary proceedings against responsible officers.

This judicial-triggered mechanism stands in tension with AFL's interpretation of § 1216(a)(3), which seemingly requires an investigation upon any allegation of arbitrary withholding without reference to prior judicial orders or fee awards. The opinion notes that when AFL petitioned for an investigation into DOJ's email-search policy, OSC declined precisely because these three predicates under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been satisfied. Consequently, understanding this specific trigger is essential: it represents one pathway where congressional intent clearly demands OSC action based on judicial findings, raising questions about whether Congress intended *another* mandatory pathway via § 1216 that operates independently of such judicial preconditions—a central ambiguity driving this litigation.

## Factual Background: AFL's Email Request to DOJ and Alleged Denial Policy

The factual genesis of this case lies in America First Legal Foundation's attempt to uncover communications regarding judicial recusals involving outside counsel at the Department of Justice (DOJ). Specifically, AFL submitted a FOIA request seeking emails exchanged between an outside attorney and officials within DOJ's Civil Division. According to AFL's complaint, DOJ denied this request based on what they characterized as a dubious internal policy: asserting that they do not search employee email accounts for responsive records unless they obtain consent from those employees first. AFL viewed this policy itself as an arbitrary and capricious violation of FOIA obligations effectively creating a blanket exemption for unsearched emails without consent.

Following this denial via administrative channels within DOJ—which did not result in full compliance immediately—AFL pursued dual tracks: filing suit directly against DOJ under FOIA and separately requesting that OSI investigate DOJ's underlying policy under § 1216(a)(3). Notably, during parallel litigation with DOJ over these same records, DOJ began producing responsive documents voluntarily; however, AFL maintained that their core grievance remained unresolved because DOJ continued to adhere to its contested policy rather than acknowledging it as unlawful through formal adjudication or correction by OSI. Thus, while some relief was achieved informally through ongoing production in federal court litigation with DOJ itself, AFL sought official confirmation and correction via OSI's investigatory powers over what they deemed an entrenched institutional violation of transparency laws.

# Threshold Jurisdictional Question: Article III Standing

Before a federal court can reach the merits of a dispute—in this case, whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) erroneously refused to investigate the Department of Justice—it must first ensure it possesses the constitutional authority to hear the case. This threshold inquiry is governed by the doctrine of Article III standing. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit paused its analysis of the statutory arguments to address whether America First Legal Foundation (AFL) had suffered a harm sufficient to trigger the "judicial Power" defined in Article III of the Constitution. The court's conclusion that AFL lacked standing underscores a fundamental principle of administrative law: a plaintiff cannot sue merely because an agency allegedly failed to follow a procedure; they must demonstrate that this failure caused a concrete injury to their own legally protected interests.

## Constitutional Basis for Standing: Article III Case or Controversy Clause

The foundation of standing doctrine lies in Article III, Section 2, Clause 1 of the U.S. Constitution, which restricts federal courts to resolving actual "Cases" and "Controversies." This limitation prevents federal judges from issuing advisory opinions or acting as overseers of executive branch compliance in abstract disputes where no specific harm has occurred. As noted by Judge Katsas in *Greer*, standing is not merely a technical pleading requirement but a jurisdictional prerequisite derived directly from this separation-of-powers mandate. If a plaintiff fails to establish standing, the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction entirely, regardless of how strong the plaintiff's legal arguments might be on the merits. Consequently, courts often examine standing before addressing substantive issues, ensuring that judicial resources are devoted only to genuine conflicts where a real stake exists between litigants.

## The Tripartite Test for Standing: Injury, Causation, Redressability

To satisfy Article III's case-or-controversy requirement, a plaintiff must clear three distinct hurdles established by Supreme Court precedent and reiterated in *Greer*. First, there must be an **injury-in-fact**: an invasion of a legally protected interest that is both "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent," rather than conjectural or hypothetical. Second, there must be **causation**: the injury must be fairly traceable to the challenged action of the defendant. Third, there must be **redressability**: it must be likely, not merely speculative, that a favorable court decision will remedy the injury. In *Greer*, AFL attempted to bypass these rigorous requirements by arguing that OSC's refusal to investigate deprived them of a procedural right created by statute. However, as Judge Katsas explained, citing *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, the deprivation of a procedural right alone is insufficient if it does not affect some concrete interest; a "procedural right in vacuo" cannot form the basis for federal jurisdiction.

## 'Procedural Right in Vacuo': Why Bare Violations Are Insufficient

A critical concept illustrated in this opinion is that statutes granting procedural protections do not automatically grant every citizen who alleges their violation access to federal court. AFL argued that Section 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act gave them an absolute right to demand an OSC investigation into arbitrary FOIA withholdings by DOJ officers. Even assuming for argument's sake that such a statutory right existed independently of other factors, AFL could not proceed because they failed to link this procedural omission to any tangible harm affecting them personally. The court emphasized that without showing how OSC's silence concretely injured AFL beyond simply denying them what they wanted procedurally (an investigation), there was no justiciable case. This distinction separates mere organizational grievance from personal legal injury; one cannot sue simply because an agency broke its own internal rules unless breaking those rules actually hurts someone in a concrete way recognized by law.

## Relaxed Standards for Procedural Injuries: Application and Limits

Recognizing that some statutory schemes are designed specifically to protect public interests alongside private ones, courts sometimes apply "relaxed" standards when evaluating standing for alleged procedural violations. Under this framework, articulated in cases like *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education*, plaintiffs alleging procedural injuries need not prove strict imminence or redressability with the same rigor as those claiming direct physical or economic harms. However—and this is crucial—the relaxation applies only after proving two things remain intact: **injury-in-fact** and **causation**. The plaintiff must still demonstrate that they have a concrete interest threatened by the agency's action and that violating their procedural right actually harmed that interest. 

In *Greer*, AFL tried to invoke this relaxed standard but ultimately failed on both fronts regarding their claimed injuries. Their first theory—that failing to investigate prevented them from getting FOIA documents—collapsed due to causation issues; any delay in receiving records was traceable to DOJ's initial denial and subsequent litigation strategy, not necessarily OSC's decision not to investigate further down an unbroken chain involving multiple independent agencies (MSPB and DOJ). Their second theory—that they were denied notices during an investigation—failed because Section 1216 does not explicitly entitle complainants to specific notifications unless OSC chooses discretionary application under Section 1214 rules; since no such entitlement existed statutorily for Section 1216 investigations themselves, there was no concrete informational injury either way. Thus, even with lenient scrutiny reserved for procedural claims, AFL could not bridge the gap between abstract statutory noncompliance and personal legal damage required for standing relief sought today!

# Analysis of AFL's First Theory: Informational Injury from Unreleased Records

To understand the D.C. Circuit's rejection of the America First Legal Foundation's (AFL) first standing theory, one must dissect the specific legal requirements for **causation** and **redressability**. AFL attempted to argue that the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) refusal to investigate the Department of Justice (DOJ) caused an informational injury: namely, that AFL remained deprived of records it sought via a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the Supreme Court has recognized that a denial of statutorily mandated information can constitute a concrete injury, that injury must still be fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct and likely to be remedied by a favorable court decision. The court found AFL's theory failed on both counts because it relied on a speculative chain of events involving independent third-party actors and unreviewable executive discretion.

## Tracing injury to Defendant vs. Third Parties: The disconnect between OSC and DOJ

The foundational flaw in AFL's first theory is its inability to properly attribute blame for the missing records. Under Article III standing doctrine, a plaintiff must demonstrate that their injury is "fairly traceable" to the challenged action of the defendant, rather than the result of some independent action taken by a third party not before the court. In this case, AFL sought records directly from the DOJ. Consequently, any failure to produce those records is undeniably traceable to the DOJ's own decisions regarding what constitutes responsive material or how it interprets its search obligations.

The court emphasized that OSC is merely an investigative body with no direct authority over record retention or disclosure by other agencies. OSC did not possess, hold, or withhold the emails AFL requested; only the DOJ did. Therefore, attempting to link AFC's lack of documents to OSC's inaction requires ignoring the direct actor—the DOJ—who actually controls the information. The court noted that attributing an informational injury regarding FOIA disclosures to OSC creates a fundamental disconnect: OSC never possessed the information, nor did it have statutory power to command its immediate release without going through a complex enforcement process initiated against another sovereign entity.

## Speculation vs. Probability: Why intervening choices break causal chains

Even if one assumes OSC could eventually influence DOJ behavior, AFL's argument collapses under scrutiny due to excessive speculation regarding intermediate steps. To connect OSC's failure to investigate with AOL ultimately receiving records, one must posit a highly improbable sequence of events involving multiple independent government entities making discretionary choices. The court outlined this broken chain as follows: first, OSC would have to decide to pursue corrective or disciplinary action; second, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB)—another agency entirely—would have to decide to sanction DOJ employees; and finally, upon such sanctions being imposed (or perhaps even before), DOJ would voluntarily decide to alter its search policies and produce the documents.

This reasoning violates established precedents regarding **causation**, which prohibit plaintiffs from relying on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors." Here, both the MSPB and DOJ are non-parties whose potential future actions are entirely unknown and uncontrollable by either AFC or this court. The likelihood that an investigation by OSC would lead all these specific downstream agencies to act in precisely the way AFC hopes is too attenuated to satisfy constitutional requirements for standing. A causal link cannot rest on a hypothesis where every single step in a multi-agency process proceeds exactly as predicted despite having no obligation or incentive to do so otherwise.

## *Heckler* deference and judicial inability to compel enforcement discretion

A critical component breaking AFC's causal chain is the principle derived from *Heckler v. Chaney*, which holds that agency decisions not to enforce laws are generally committed to agency discretion and presumptively unreviewable by courts. Even if OSC had conducted an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), there was no guarantee it would elect to bring corrective or disciplinary proceedings under §§ 1214 or 1215. Agencies possess broad discretion in determining whether prosecution or enforcement actions are warranted based on resource allocation, policy priorities, and evidentiary strength.

The appellate court pointed out that asking a judge to mandate an investigation effectively asks them to speculate on how an agency would exercise its enforcement discretion once opened up for business. Since courts generally may not compel such discretionary enforcement decisions, any prediction that an investigation would inevitably lead to sanctions is baseless in legal terms. If OSC retains absolute discretion on whether—and how vigorously—to pursue a case after opening an inquiry, then AFC cannot claim with certainty that opening that inquiry will result in any tangible change in behavior by DOJ employees or management sufficient to release records.

## Redressability analysis: Whether a court order binding OSC would likely force DOJ disclosure

Building on these causation failures leads directly into a fatal defect in **redressability**. For standing exists, there must be a substantial likelihood that a favorable judicial ruling will remedy the plaintiff's injury—in this instance, obtaining FOIA records. However, redressability turns on how much control a judgment gives over actual outcomes versus mere rhetoric affecting non-parties. A judgment binding only OSC would not bind DOJ or MSPB; therefore, ordering OSC to investigate does not logically compel DOJ production unless one assumes those other agencies will react predictably and favorably without legal compulsion from this suit itself.

The court reiterated that redressability depends on whether relief granted against *these* defendants resolves *this* plaintiff's harm—not whether their victory might indirectly persuade unrelated actors elsewhere in government bureaucracy later down the line because they were persuaded by logic rather than law ("persuade actors who are not before"). # Analysis of AFL's Second Theory: Informational Injury Regarding Investigation Process

Having determined that America First Legal Foundation (AFL) could not establish standing based on its desire to obtain the underlying emails from the Department of Justice, the D.C. Circuit turned to AFL's alternative theory. This second theory posited that AFL suffered a concrete injury not because it lacked access to the requested records, but because the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) failed to provide it with specific procedural updates and notices during an investigation. Under this framework, AFL argued that by refusing to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA violations, OSC deprived AFL of the information regarding the status and progress of an investigation that it claimed a statute entitled it to receive. The court rejected this argument, finding that no statutory entitlement existed and therefore no concrete injury occurred.

## The Definition of 'Informational Standing' Based on Statutory Entitlements

To evaluate AFL's second theory, one must first understand the doctrine of "informational standing." Generally, a plaintiff alleging an informational injury can satisfy the injury-in-fact requirement of Article III standing if they can demonstrate that a statute granted them a specific right to receive certain information and that they were denied that information. As the Supreme Court has held in cases like *FEC v. Akins*, a plaintiff suffers a concrete injury when they are denied information that a statute explicitly entitles them to receive. However, this right is not limitless; it is strictly tethered to the text of the statute in question. A mere generalized grievance about transparency or a desire for information without a corresponding statutory command does not constitute a legally protected interest. The court emphasized in *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* that the deprivation of a procedural right alone is insufficient for standing unless there is some concrete interest affected by that deprivation—a "procedural right in vacuo" confers no standing. Therefore, for AFL to succeed here, it had to prove that Congress intended § 1216 to grant it a private right to receive specific investigative notices or reports.

## Whether § 1216(a)(3) Grants a Private Right to Receive Investigation Notices

The central legal inquiry was whether 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which mandates that OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation" into allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, implicitly creates an affirmative entitlement for complainants to receive updates on those investigations. The court scrutinized the statutory text and found no such mandate within § 1216 itself. While § 1214—which governs investigations into "prohibited personnel practices"—explicitly outlines procedures for notifying the complaining party about investigation progress, § 1216 contains no parallel language requiring OSC to notify complainants or provide investigative findings when acting under its FOIA-specific authority (§ 1216(a)(3)). The court noted that although § 1216(c) allows OSC to seek corrective action "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved," this language authorizes OSC's remedial powers rather than imposing mandatory disclosure obligations on OSC toward third-party complainants like AFL. Without explicit statutory language granting an entitlement to information, AFL could not claim that its failure to receive such data constituted an invasion of a legally protected interest.

## Discretionary Nature of Importing § 1214 Procedural Safeguards into § 1216 Investigations

AFL attempted to bridge this gap by arguing that if OSC were conducting an investigation under § 1216, it would likely apply the notification procedures found in § 1214 out of convenience or consistency. Consequently, AFL contended that OSC's refusal to investigate meant it never received these potential notifications. The court firmly rejected this reliance on potential administrative practice over statutory mandate. Relying on precedent from the Sixth Circuit (*Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*), the opinion clarified that while OSC *may* choose to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation, it is under no obligation to do so; doing so remains entirely within OSC's discretion. Because AFC acknowledged this discretionary nature in its own filings, it could not assert that it had been denied information it was legally entitled to possess. An expectation based on how an agency *might* act does not rise to the level of a legal right enforceable under Article III standing doctrines.

## Why Lacking an Entitlement Negates Both Injury-in-Fact and Redressability

The absence of a statutory entitlement proved fatal on two fronts: injury-in-fact and redressability regarding AFL's second theory. First, without a specific legal right defined by Congress under either § 1216 or applicable regulations requiring disclosure, AFL suffered no "concrete and particularized" invasion of interest simply because OSC did not send notices during an investigation it refused to start one could argue is non-existent without discretion exercised). Second, even assuming arguendo that such notices would have been sent if an investigation occurred, ordering OSC to investigate would not necessarily redress AFL's alleged injury because OSC retains discretion over whether those specific notice protocols are triggered during such inquiries. Since there was no guarantee—or even strong likelihood—that an ordered investigation would result in the delivery of specific documents or notices (given their discretionary status), any causal link between judicial relief and the receipt of information remained too speculative to satisfy constitutional requirements for redressability. # Disposition and Broader Implications for Administrative Litigation

## Final Holding: Dismissal for Lack of Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
The D.C. Circuit's decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* concludes with a definitive procedural resolution: the court vacates the district court's judgment and remands the case with instructions to dismiss the complaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This outcome rests entirely on the court's determination that America First Legal Foundation (AFL) failed to satisfy the constitutional requirements for Article III standing. Because standing is a threshold jurisdictional prerequisite, the appellate court never reached the substantive legal arguments regarding whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) correctly interpreted its statutory mandate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 or whether OSC abused its discretion in declining to investigate the Department of Justice's FOIA policies. Under Supreme Court precedent, specifically *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, federal courts must address standing before addressing the merits of a case. Consequently, the dismissal is not a ruling on whether OSC acted unlawfully, but rather a declaration that AFL lacks the legal capacity to bring this suit in federal court at all.

## Why Merits Review Was Avoided Despite Potential Legal Errors
A critical lesson from this opinion is that a plaintiff may successfully demonstrate that an agency committed a clear legal error without ever having their case heard on those merits if they cannot prove standing. The district court below had already found "persuasive" evidence that OSC erred in believing it lacked authority to investigate alleged FOIA violations under § 1216 unless specific triggers under FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were met. The district judge explicitly stated that OSC's refusal to investigate was based on "legal error." However, even assuming this legal error existed, Judge Katsas explains that such an error is irrelevant to the disposition if AFL cannot show it suffered a concrete injury traceable to that error and capable of being redressed by a judicial order. The court emphasizes that Article III limits federal judicial power to actual "Cases" or "Controversies"; therefore, even a meritorious claim regarding statutory interpretation cannot proceed if the plaintiff is merely asserting a generalized grievance about government administration rather than protecting their own concrete interests. This reinforces the principle that standing acts as an absolute barrier: without it, the judiciary has no power to correct administrative mistakes, regardless of how egregious those mistakes appear on paper.

## Significance of Treating 'Speculative' Enforcement Outcomes as Non-Redressable
The opinion provides a rigorous application of the redressability prong of standing analysis, particularly when a plaintiff attempts to link an agency's failure to act with future actions by other independent actors. AFL argued that if OSC were compelled to investigate DOJ under § 1216, it would eventually lead to corrective action forcing DOJ to release records. The D.C. Circuit rejected this chain of causation as too "speculative and attenuated." The court detailed how such relief would require multiple hypothetical steps: OSC would have to decide to pursue enforcement; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would have to impose sanctions; and finally, DOJ would have to change its behavior based on those sanctions. Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Heckler v. Chaney*, Judge Katsas notes that courts cannot base causation on speculation regarding the "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts," especially when those actors involve discretionary enforcement decisions by executive agencies like OSC and MSPB. For redressability to exist, a favorable judgment must be likely to directly remedy the injury; here, ordering OSC to open an investigation does not guarantee any specific outcome for AFL's information request, rendering any potential relief purely conjectural.

## Implications for Organizations Seeking to Enforce Agency Compliance via Third-Party Statutes
This decision serves as a cautionary tale for organizations attempting to use one statute (§ 1216) as a vehicle to enforce rights created by another statute (FOIA) against third-party agencies they are not directly suing for relief in that specific action. The core implication is that an organization cannot simply assert a right derived from Congress granting an agency oversight powers unless that organization can show how its own concrete interests are uniquely harmed by the lack of oversight beyond what any member of the public could claim. Even where statutes grant entities like OSC broad investigatory mandates ("shall conduct an investigation"), this does not automatically create private rights enforceable by third parties absent specific statutory language entitling them to notice or participation that confers individualized harm upon denial. The court noted AFL acknowledged precedents holding that extending § 1214 procedural protections into § 1216 investigations is discretionary, meaning there was no statutory entitlement violated by OSP failing these notifications either thereby negating any distinct informational injury AFL claimed it sustained from missing out on process details during potential inquiry phase itself alone insufficiently tied back original data loss anyway since no guaranteed result followed anyway! Thus groups hoping trigger external watchdog mechanisms must carefully map exactly how their personal stake differs general citizenry concern otherwise face immediate dismissal!
