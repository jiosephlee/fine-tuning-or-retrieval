Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## The 911 Call and Initial Police Response at 4:00 a.m.
To understand the legal dispute in *United States v. Coleman*, one must first grasp the high-stakes environment in which the police action occurred. The case arises from a domestic disturbance call received just after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, in Marshfield, Wisconsin. A woman reported that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother and remained inside the residence. Officer Jamie Kizer responded to this emergency call, a scenario the Seventh Circuit notes involves some of the most difficult responsibilities facing law enforcement. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and identified himself. Lisa Coleman emerged onto the porch, acknowledging that her children were inside but immediately displaying hesitation when pressed about their safety. Her initial responses were ambiguous; she stated only that the children were in her bedroom and later expressed merely that she "hope[d]" they were okay. This initial ambiguity set the stage for a complex interaction regarding whether the officers had lawful authority to cross the threshold of the home without a warrant.

## Analysis of Body Camera Footage: The Evolving Dialogue
The factual record in this case is uniquely robust because it relies heavily on body camera footage rather than conflicting witness testimony. As noted by the court citing *United States v. Norville*, video evidence can "evaporate any factual dispute" regarding what actually transpired between an officer and a citizen. The footage reveals a nuanced evolution in Officer Kizer's approach as he attempted to secure entry to ensure child safety. Initially, when asked directly if he could enter after Lisa claimed she did not know where her husband was located, Lisa explicitly declined, stating, "um … I'd prefer you not." However, after a pause during which Lisa went inside to retrieve her shoes but failed to return promptly, Officer Kizer grew concerned enough to crack the door and call out to verify her well-being. When Lisa eventually re-emerged, identifying Jaison Coleman as the subject of threats and admitting uncertainty about his current state of mind or location relative to the children, the dynamic shifted again toward determining immediate safety risks inside the dwelling.

## The Critical Exchange: 'I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside'
The central factual pivot point of this opinion occurs during a specific verbal exchange captured on video after Lisa returned to the doorway for a second time. Officer Kizer sought clarification on whether Coleman was calmer and specifically asked if the children were okay. Lisa struggled verbally before stating, "They're okay right this second," though her speech trailed off into unintelligibility. It was at this precise moment that Officer Kizer stated, "Okay, um, I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Immediately following this statement—which Coleman argues constitutes coercion due to its use of mandatory language ("need")—Lisa replied simply with "Okay." She then provided specific details about where her children were located (her bedroom), assured officers that their dogs would jump on them but not bite them, and physically opened the door while brushing her pets aside to facilitate entry. This sequence is critical because it represents the alleged moment of consent that validates the officers' subsequent presence in private areas of the home without a warrant or exigent circumstances justification at that exact instant.

## Police Entry, Welfare Check, and Discovery of Firearms
Once admitted based on Lisa's apparent consent, Officer Kizer and his colleagues conducted a welfare check consistent with their stated purpose of ensuring child safety and assessing potential danger from Coleman. Lisa led them directly to one back bedroom where she indicated her children were staying; however, Coleman was not present there. When asked where he might be located inside the house again receiving an uncertain answer from Lisa regarding his whereabouts—the officers proceeded to canvass other rooms quickly out of necessity for officer safety and victim protection. # Governing Legal Standards for Third-Party Consent Searches

## Consent as an Exception to the Warrant Requirement
In *United States v. Coleman*, the central constitutional question turns on whether the police officers violated Jaison Coleman's Fourth Amendment rights by entering his home without a warrant. Generally, the Fourth Amendment requires law enforcement to obtain a judicial warrant based on probable cause before searching a private dwelling. However, the Supreme Court has long recognized that a search conducted pursuant to valid consent constitutes a well-delineated exception to this warrant requirement. As noted in *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte* and reiterated by the Seventh Circuit in *United States v. Banks*, if an individual voluntarily consents to a search, the government does not need to secure a warrant or demonstrate probable cause. For consent to serve as a valid exception, however, it must be "freely and voluntarily" given, rather than obtained through coercion, duress, or submission to authority. The court emphasizes that voluntariness is not determined by whether the suspect knew they could refuse consent—a specific warning is not constitutionally mandated—but rather by assessing whether the consent was the product of free will under the specific facts of the encounter.

## Third-Party Authority: The Matlock Doctrine Applied
A unique complexity arises in *Coleman* because the person who granted consent was not the defendant himself, but his wife, Lisa Coleman. The law permits third parties to authorize searches of shared premises provided they possess "common authority" over the property. Citing *United States v. Matlock* and applying it through *United States v. Cellitti*, the court explains that a third party may give valid consent for a search of a place where both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy. Because spouses typically share joint access and control over their marital home, Lisa possessed sufficient authority to permit Officer Kizer's entry even though Jaison objected or was unaware at that precise moment. Consequently, Jaison retained standing to challenge the validity of his wife's consent; he could argue that her authorization was invalid because it was coerced or exceeded her actual authority relative to him. The burden rests on proving that the third-party consentor had mutual use of the property and joint access or control for most purposes at issue during the relevant timeframe.

## Voluntariness Standard: Totality of Circumstances Factors
Once it is established that a third party had authority to consent, the court must determine whether that consent was voluntary. In *Coleman*, both parties acknowledged that voluntariness is assessed using a "totality of the circumstances" test derived from cases like *United States v. Han*. There is no single dispositive factor; instead, courts weigh various elements surrounding the interaction between police and civilians. As outlined in *United States v. Figueroa-Espana*, pertinent considerations include (1) the person's age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether they were advised of their constitutional rights; (3) how long they were detained prior to consenting; (4) whether consent was immediate or prompted by repeated requests; (5) whether any physical coercion was employed; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when granting consent. In this specific case, Officer Kizer's demeanor played a critical role in this analysis. Although he expressed urgency regarding child safety—using phrases like "we almost need to come inside"—the court found his tone remained concerned rather than coercive. Crucially, Lisa demonstrated she understood she could refuse when she initially declined permission ("I'd prefer you not") before eventually agreeing after further discussion about her children's safety without any threat or physical force from officers.

## Scope of Consent: Objective Reasonableness (*Florida v.* Jimeno)
Even if initial entry is authorized by voluntary third-party consent, police officers cannot roam freely through a home; their actions must remain within the boundaries of what was actually permitted by the consenting party. To evaluate these boundaries, courts apply an "objective reasonableness" standard established in *Florida v.* Jimeno and adopted by Seventh Circuit precedent such as *United States v.* Saucedo*. The inquiry asks: What would a typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent? In *Coleman*, Lisa explicitly stated where her children were located ("They're in my bedroom") and identified where she believed her husband might be hiding ("back bedroom"). # Judicial Analysis of Voluntary Consent

## Evaluating Officer Demeanor: Concern vs. Coercion

The core of the Seventh Circuit's analysis in *United States v. Coleman* rests on distinguishing between a police officer legitimately expressing concern for public safety and one using that concern as a tool for coercion. Under the Fourth Amendment, consent to search must be "freely and voluntarily" given, a standard established by the Supreme Court in *Bumper v. North Carolina*. However, the presence of police authority does not automatically render consent involuntary; rather, courts look to the "totality of the circumstances" to determine if the individual's will was overborne. In this case, the appellate court placed significant weight on the visual and auditory evidence provided by Officer Kizer's body camera footage. The court observed that Kizer's tone and demeanor remained consistently "concerned" rather than threatening or aggressive throughout the interaction. By characterizing Kizer as someone acting out of a genuine worry for the children's welfare rather than an intent to bypass Fourth Amendment protections through intimidation, the court established a factual baseline that negated claims of coercion. This distinction is vital because an officer's professional duty to respond to domestic violence calls often necessitates urgent communication, which can sound forceful without actually being legally coercive.

## Significance of Lisa's Initial Refusal ('I'd prefer you not')

Perhaps the most critical factual indicator supporting the finding of voluntary consent was Lisa Coleman's explicit refusal during the initial stages of the encounter. Before eventually agreeing to let Officer Kizer enter, Lisa clearly stated, "um … I'd prefer you not." This moment serves as powerful evidence that she possessed both the knowledge of her right to refuse and the actual capacity to exercise that right against police authority. The Seventh Circuit reasoned that because Lisa successfully denied entry once without suffering any adverse consequences or escalation from Officer Kizer, her subsequent agreement could not be deemed coerced by mere persistence. If an individual demonstrates they understand they can say "no," their eventual "yes" carries greater evidentiary weight regarding voluntariness. The court noted that Officer Kizer accepted this initial refusal and did not attempt to force entry immediately after; instead, he waited while Lisa went inside, only re-engaging when she returned to the doorway hours later (in real-time minutes) with new information about her husband's location and status. This sequence underscores that Lisa was an active participant in determining access to her home, rather than a passive subject overwhelmed by state power.

## Addressing Coleman's Argument Regarding the Word 'Need'

On appeal, Jaison Coleman focused his challenge on a single phrase uttered by Officer Kizer: "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Coleman argued that using the word "need" transformed a request into a command or assertion of legal authority, thereby invalidating consent under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*. The Seventh Circuit rejected this narrow textual analysis in favor of looking at how a reasonable person would interpret the statement within its full context. The court acknowledged that while "need" implies necessity, it did not equate to a threat or a claim of lawful compulsion in this specific scenario. Instead, viewed through the lens of total circumstances, Kizer's phrasing reflected an expression of urgent concern regarding child safety—a legitimate justification for wanting to enter quickly—but fell short of asserting he had no choice but to enter regardless of Lisa's wishes. The court emphasized that Lisa's reaction confirmed this interpretation; she simply replied "Okay," provided details about her children's location, and actively facilitated entry by moving her dogs aside. Had she perceived Kizer's words as a non-negotiable order based on false pretenses of legal authority, her cooperative behavior likely would have differed significantly from what was captured on video.

## Totality of Circumstances Assessment: Duration, Custody Status, and Tone

To reach its final conclusion on voluntariness, the district court—and subsequently the Seventh Circuit—applied the multi-factor test derived from cases like *United States v. Figueroa-Espana*, weighing various elements beyond just specific words spoken. First, neither party disputed that Lisa was never in police custody during these interactions; she stood freely on her own porch and retained control over her movements until she invited officers inside. Second, the duration of the detention prior to consent was minimal; despite multiple exchanges about safety and locations spanning several minutes across two distinct visits by Lisa returning outside, there was no prolonged holding pattern designed to wear down resistance. Third, as previously noted regarding demeanor, there was no physical coercion used against Lisa at any point. The appellate court stressed that while an individual might feel psychological pressure when speaking with armed officers responding to a domestic disturbance call—a feeling common in such high-stakes environments—that subjective pressure does not rise to the level of unconstitutional coercion unless amplified by official misconduct or threats. Because none of these aggravating factors were present in *Coleman*, and because every objective factor pointed toward a willing participant engaging with law enforcement out of concern for family safety, no clear error existed in denying suppression based on lack of consent.

## Conclusion: Why Consent Was Deemed Freely Given

Ultimately, *United States v. Coleman* illustrates how courts validate third-party consent by scrutinizing whether a reasonable person would perceive themselves as free to decline police entry before agreeing otherwise. The Seventh Circuit affirmed the lower court's decision because Officer Kizer maintained a respectful tone consistent with emergency response protocols rather than interrogation tactics capable of overpowering wills; because Lisa demonstrated her autonomy through an initial refusal; and because her eventual compliance included cooperative actions like revealing room layouts and managing pets for officer safety—all without any indication she believed she lacked alternatives or faced punishment for saying no. # Analysis: Scope of Consent and Final Disposition

## The Stated Purpose: Checking Child Welfare as Scope Limitation

Once a court determines that valid consent has been given to enter a home, the analysis does not end; the inquiry shifts immediately to the scope of that consent. Under the Fourth Amendment, a search authorized by consent is lawful only if the officers remain within the bounds of what a reasonable person would understand the consent to cover. The Supreme Court established in *Florida v. Jimeno* that the test for scope is "objective reasonableness," asking what a typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the consenting party. In *United States v. Coleman*, this principle serves as the dividing line between a lawful investigation and an unconstitutional search. The specific parameters of Lisa Coleman's consent were defined by her interaction with Officer Kizer regarding her children's safety. When Officer Kizer expressed concern that they might "need to come inside to make sure" the children were okay, and Lisa replied "Okay," she effectively granted permission for an entry limited to verifying child welfare. This purpose became the objective boundary against which all subsequent police actions were measured. If the officers had entered solely to look for drugs or weapons unrelated to immediate safety concerns, their actions likely would have exceeded this scope. However, because their stated goal was explicitly tied to ensuring the children were safe from potential harm caused by Jaison Coleman, their presence was tethered directly to Lisa's authorization.

## Justification for Scanning Rooms and Locating Occupants

The central question in *Coleman* was whether an inspection for child welfare necessitated a broad sweep of the home or if it allowed officers to locate individuals within it. The Seventh Circuit held that checking on children's safety inherently requires certain investigative steps that might otherwise seem intrusive in a purely consensual search for contraband. To verify that children were safe and not being threatened by Jaison Coleman, who was reported to be aggressive and armed, officers logically needed to determine where everyone was located within the residence. The court reasoned that it would be impossible to confirm child welfare without scanning rooms and identifying occupants, particularly when there was an active threat from a specific adult male believed to be hiding elsewhere in the house. Consequently, when Officers entered, asked where Colemen was, scanned multiple bedrooms, and eventually located him in a separate room, these actions fell squarely within what Lisa implicitly authorized by agreeing to let police ensure her children's safety. Furthermore, once Jaison Coleman was found, his agreement to a protective pat-down further solidified that the interaction remained consistent with safety protocols rather than expanding into an unauthorized general search. The court emphasized that under these specific facts—a domestic disturbance involving threats of violence—the "scope" naturally expanded enough to allow officers to secure the scene and account for all persons present without violating Lisa's original grant of permission.

## Irrelevance of Exigent Circumstances Due to Valid Consent

A common feature in criminal procedure cases involves defendants challenging police entries on multiple grounds while governments offer alternative justifications should one theory fail. In *Coleman*, Jaison argued that his wife did not consent at all, forcing an evaluation of whether emergency exceptions applied instead. However, because the Seventh Circuit affirmed that Lisa provided valid consent covering both entry and scope, there was no legal necessity for the court to address alternative doctrines such as exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery. The doctrine of exigent circumstances allows warrantless entry when there is an immediate threat to life or property that precludes obtaining a warrant; similarly, inevitable discovery argues evidence would have been found legally regardless of any misconduct. In this case, Judge Scudder noted explicitly that these arguments became moot once voluntary consent was established as factually sufficient and legally valid. By resolving the case on consent grounds alone, the court avoided creating potentially broader precedents regarding emergency responses in domestic violence situations or speculative theories about future warrants. This judicial economy underscores a fundamental rule in appellate review: if a dispositive issue resolves in favor of one party based on clear statutory or constitutional grounds—here, valid third-party consent—the court need not reach secondary arguments unless required by precedent or unique legal ambiguity absent from this record.

## Holding Summary: Affirmance of Conviction

The Seventh Circuit concluded its analysis by affirming the district court's denial of Jaison Coleman's motion to suppress and upholding his conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The appellate panel reviewed the district court's findings under the "clear error" standard regarding both voluntariness and scope of consent, determining that no such error existed based on the body camera footage presented at trial. Specifically, Judge Scudder found that Officer Kizer's demeanor remained non-coercive despite using urgent language about needing entry for child safety purposes—a nuance deemed acceptable under total circumstances jurisprudence—and that Lisa retained control over her decision-making process throughout the encounter prior to granting final approval.
