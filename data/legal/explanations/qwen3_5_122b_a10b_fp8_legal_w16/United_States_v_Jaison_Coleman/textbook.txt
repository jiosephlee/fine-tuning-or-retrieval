Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Factual Context: The 911 Call and Domestic Violence Allegations
The factual foundation of *United States v. Jaison Coleman* arises from a high-stakes domestic disturbance involving threats of lethal violence. Early on the morning of April 20, 2023, shortly after 4:00 a.m., emergency services received a call reporting that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill his wife, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside their Marshfield, Wisconsin home. Such calls represent some of the most volatile situations police officers face, requiring rapid assessment of potential danger to victims and children within the residence. The allegations included not only verbal threats against Lisa but also reports that Coleman possessed a firearm and had previously pointed it at her while threatening to set the house on fire. These specific details regarding weapons and arson created an urgent context for Officer Jamie Kizer's arrival, framing every subsequent interaction around the immediate safety of the occupants rather than routine investigation.

## Chronology of Police Interaction: Initial Contact through Entry
The sequence of events unfolded entirely on the front porch and threshold of the Colemans' home before police entered. Upon arrival, Officer Kizer knocked and identified himself. Lisa appeared on the porch but displayed significant hesitation when questioned about her children's safety; she admitted they were in her bedroom but refused to confirm if they were "okay," stating only that she "hoped" so. When Kizer attempted to clarify where Coleman was located, Lisa claimed ignorance. Crucially, when Kizer first requested permission to enter, Lisa explicitly declined, stating, "um ... I'd prefer you not." However, after a pause during which she went inside ostensibly to retrieve her shoes and failed to return immediately, Kizer grew concerned enough to crack the door open and call out to ensure she was safe. When Lisa eventually re-emerged, identifying Coleman as her husband who had made threats, a pivotal exchange occurred regarding the children's status. After Lisa stammered that they were "okay right this second," Kizer expressed his professional judgment: "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." To this statement implying necessity due to child safety concerns, Lisa replied simply, "Okay." She then actively facilitated entry by opening the door herself and directing officers toward her children's location while reassuring them that her dogs posed no biting threat despite jumping on visitors. Once inside, officers conducted a protective sweep consistent with checking for danger and locating family members.

## Key Evidence: Body Camera Footage Analysis
Central to resolving disputes about what actually happened during this encounter is the objective evidence provided by body-worn cameras worn by Officer Kizer and his colleagues. As noted by the Seventh Circuit citing *United States v. Norville*, such video records can "evaporate any factual dispute" regarding the events at issue because they provide an immutable account of tone, timing, and behavior unavailable through mere witness testimony or recollection alone. For students analyzing this opinion, it is vital to note how the appellate court relied heavily on these recordings to determine Officer Kizer's demeanor as "concerned" rather than coercive and to verify that Lisa retained agency throughout the interaction until her final affirmative response. The footage captured not just words spoken but also non-verbal cues—such as Lisa's hesitation versus her eventual cooperation—that informed the district court's credibility determinations regarding whether consent was truly voluntary or merely submitted under duress. Without this visual record, many nuances essential to Fourth Amendment analysis might remain ambiguous or subject to conflicting narratives between law enforcement witnesses and defendants' families.

## Criminal Charges: Violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)
Following their lawful entry based on consent obtained from Lisa Coleman—and subsequently gathering additional information indicating past aggression—the officers secured a search warrant authorized by probable cause developed from statements made by both adults and children inside the home. This warrant-led search uncovered multiple firearms hidden within various rooms of the residence. Because Jaison Coleman was already known to be a convicted felon at large prior thereto—an established fact rendering him legally prohibited from owning or possessing firearms under federal statute—he faced prosecution pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). # Governing Legal Framework for Third-Party Consent Searches

## Consent as an Exception to the Warrant Requirement
In *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit begins its analysis by grounding the case in a fundamental principle of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence: while the Constitution generally requires law enforcement to obtain a warrant before searching a private home, there are specific exceptions to this rule. One such exception is consent. Both parties in *Coleman* acknowledged that if a valid consent exists, the police do not need a warrant to enter and search a dwelling. This doctrine allows individuals to waive their constitutional protection against unreasonable searches, provided the waiver meets certain legal criteria. Without establishing that consent was given freely and voluntarily, any evidence discovered during such an entry would likely be suppressed as fruit of an illegal search. Therefore, the threshold question in cases involving consensual entries is whether the homeowner or resident actually authorized the police presence and investigation.

## Third-Party Authority under *Matlock* and *Cellitti*
A unique complication arises when the person granting consent is not the criminal defendant themselves, but rather a third party residing in the same home. In *Coleman*, Officer Kizer obtained permission from Lisa Coleman, Jaison Coleman's wife, to enter their shared residence. The court relied on established precedent, citing *United States v. Matlock* and its application in *United States v. Cellitti*, to clarify that a third party possesses the authority to consent to a search of common areas if they share "legitimate expectations of privacy" with the defendant alongside other occupants. Consequently, Jaison Coleman retained standing to challenge his wife's consent; however, if Lisa possessed actual authority over the premises—which she did as a co-resident—the police action became lawful regardless of Jaison's objections at that moment. This framework ensures that police can act efficiently in multi-occupant dwellings based on the apparent authority of those present, balancing individual privacy rights against practical law enforcement needs during emergencies like domestic disturbances.

## 'Freely and Voluntarily' Standard (*Bumper*) vs. Coercion
Once it is established that a third party has the potential authority to consent, the court must determine if that consent was legally effective. The Supreme Court set forth in *Bumper v. North Carolina* that for consent to validate a search without a warrant, it must be "freely and voluntarily" given. This standard distinguishes true authorization from mere submission to official authority or acquiescence born of fear or intimidation. If an officer coerces a resident through threats or overwhelming force, any resulting agreement is invalid because it does not represent a genuine choice by the citizen. In *Coleman*, Defendant argued that Officer Kizer's language suggested he intended to enter anyway ("we almost need to come inside"), potentially rendering Lisa's compliance involuntary submission rather than free consent. However, under *Bumper*, mere ambiguity or assertiveness does not automatically equate to coercion; instead, courts look for affirmative acts of duress or explicit commands that strip the resident of their freedom of choice regarding entry into their own home.

## 'Totality of Circumstances' Test Factors
To resolve disputes over whether consent was truly voluntary, appellate courts apply a flexible "totality of circumstances" test rather than relying on rigid rules or isolated phrases spoken during an encounter. Citing precedents such as *United States v. Han* and *United States v. Figueroa-Espana*, the Seventh Circuit outlined specific factors relevant to this evaluation: (1) the age, intelligence, and education of the consenting party; (2) whether they were informed of their right to refuse; (3) the duration of detention prior to consent; (4) whether multiple requests were made; (5) any use of physical force; and (6) whether the individual was in custody at the time of consent applied these factors holistically to Officer Kizer's interaction with Lisa Coleman despite her hesitation earlier in the conversation and her eventual refusal ("I'd prefer you not") before finally agreeing ("Okay"). Crucially, no single factor is dispositive; for instance, while Officers did not explicitly inform Lisa she could say no—a factor weighing slightly against voluntariness—it was outweighed by her demonstrated ability to refuse initially and her lack of custodial status during the exchange. # Judicial Review Standards and Evidentiary Weight

In criminal appeals challenging the admissibility of evidence obtained through a warrantless search, the path to reversal depends heavily on distinguishing between questions of law and questions of fact. The Seventh Circuit's decision in *United States v. Coleman* provides a precise illustration of how an appellate court navigates these distinctions when reviewing a district court's determination that a third party provided valid consent to enter a home. Before addressing the substantive merits of whether Lisa Coleman's consent was voluntary or whether the officers stayed within its scope, the court establishes the framework for judicial review. This framework dictates that factual findings are reviewed under the highly deferential "clear error" standard, while legal principles remain subject to independent analysis. Understanding this hierarchy is essential because it limits the appellate court's power to overturn a trial judge's assessment of witness credibility and evidentiary weight unless that assessment is fundamentally unsupported by the record.

## Clear Error Standard for Voluntary Consent Findings

When determining whether consent to search was "freely and voluntarily" given, courts apply a totality-of-the-circumstances test rather than looking for specific magic words or rigid formalities. Because this inquiry relies heavily on assessing human behavior, demeanor, and context, it is treated primarily as a question of fact. Consequently, under *United States v. Jones*, an appellate court reviews such factual determinations for clear error. This standard is exceptionally demanding; it does not permit the appellate panel to simply substitute its own judgment for that of the district court merely because it might have weighed the evidence differently. Instead, reversal is warranted only if the reviewing court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made.

In *Coleman*, this standard proved decisive in rejecting Jaison Coleman's argument regarding Officer Kizer's language. Coleman focused intensely on the officer's statement, "we almost need to come inside," arguing that using the word "need" constituted coercion or an assertion of authority that vitiated consent. However, applying the clear error standard, the Seventh Circuit declined to disturb the district court's finding that no coercion existed. The appellate judges acknowledged that while "need" could imply compulsion in isolation, viewing it within the totality of circumstances—the officer's calm tone, Lisa's prior ability to refuse entry, her lack of custody status, and her cooperative actions immediately following her agreement—did not lead to a conclusion that consent was involuntary. The district court was entitled to weigh these surrounding facts against Colemen's narrow textual critique, and absent a showing that this weighing process was irrational or contrary to logic, the finding stood firm.

## Clear Error Standard for Scope Determinations

Just as with voluntariness, determining whether police officers remained within the bounds of granted consent involves interpreting objective facts about what was communicated during an encounter. The Supreme Court established in *Florida v. Jimeno* that scope inquiries rely on an objective reasonableness standard: what would a typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange? Despite involving an element of interpretation regarding social norms and communication, courts generally classify these determinations as factual findings subject to clear error review on appeal. This means that if multiple reasonable interpretations exist based on the testimony or recording at hand, the appellate court must accept whichever view aligns with the district court's resolution unless it finds none supportable by evidence at all.

In *Coleman*, this principle protected the district court's conclusion that Officer Kizer and his team did not exceed Lisa's authorization once inside her home. Lisa had invited officers in specifically to verify child safety amidst reports of domestic violence threats requiring immediate attention from those inside back bedrooms toward front doors). Officers entered scanning rooms locating children verifying locations checking potential dangers posed by husband—all activities logically flowing directly from purpose originally articulated ("make sure kids okay").
