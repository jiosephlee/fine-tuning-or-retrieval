Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## The Contractual Wall: Why Santos Couldn't Enforce Cameo's Terms of Service

While the headline-grabbing aspect of *Santos v. Kimmel* involves a former congressman suing over the fair use of his own "personalized" videos on a late-night talk show, the Second Circuit's October 6, 2025, decision offers a far more instructive—and perhaps permanent—lesson for the digital economy regarding contract law. While the court's unanimous affirmation on the copyright front reinforces the robust protections afforded to satirists and critics under the First Amendment and fair use doctrine, it is the dismissal of George Santos's state law contract claims that provides a critical roadmap for litigants trying to weaponize platform Terms of Service (ToS). The Second Circuit made clear: just because a platform like Cameo has strict rules governing how users behave toward content creators does not mean those creators can step into the shoes of a contracting party to sue for breach when those rules are violated.

The core of Santos's argument rested on a familiar but legally fragile theory: that as a provider of services through Cameo, he was an intended third-party beneficiary of the contract between Cameo and its users (in this case, Jimmy Kimmel). When Kimmel allegedly breached Cameo's ToS by submitting fake orders to mock Santos rather than fulfilling genuine requests, Santos argued he could enforce those terms directly. The Second Circuit dismantled this theory by applying a rigorous dual-state analysis that highlights the high bar for establishing third-party beneficiary status in commercial contracts involving digital platforms.

Under Illinois law, which governed the interpretation of Cameo's ToS, the court applied a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." As Judge Lohier noted, for a third party to enforce a contract provision, there must be language so explicit that it amounts to an "express declaration" of intent to benefit them. The court found exactly the opposite in Cameo's agreement structure. The ToS contained specific clauses granting rights to certain defined groups while conspicuously omitting any such language for independent video providers. By pointing out that other provisions explicitly granted third-party rights where intended, while these particular ones did not, the court utilized standard contract interpretation principles to find that Cameo never intended its user agreements to create an actionable cause of action for every creator on its roster. This is a vital distinction for platform operators: silence in a ToS regarding third-party enforcement is generally interpreted as an intentional exclusion, not an ambiguity waiting to be resolved in favor of plaintiffs.

The analysis became even stricter when looking at New York law regarding implied-in-fact contracts and general contract formation principles applicable to the relationship between Santos and Kimmel. Even if one attempted to bypass the written ToS and argue that Kimmel implicitly agreed to abide by platform norms simply by using the site, Santos failed to plead sufficient facts to survive dismissal. New York requires proof of a "meeting of minds"—a mutual understanding and agreement on specific terms—to form an implied contract. The complaint offered no evidence that Kimmel ever communicated with Santos about adhering to specific ToS provisions or that there was any mutual assent beyond the transactional exchange of money for video delivery. Without alleging specific terms or conduct demonstrating an agreement outside the statutory framework, Santos was left with nothing more than conclusory assertions that cannot sustain a breach of contract claim.

Furthermore, Santos's attempt to pivot to fraudulent inducement ran aground on New York's requirement for proving actual economic damages distinct from consequential losses or reputational harm known as "out-of-pocket loss." Since Santos received payment for every video he recorded—even if requested under false pretenses—the court reasoned he had suffered no tangible financial loss attributable solely to the deception in a way that satisfies New York tort standards. This highlights another common trap in digital litigation: assuming that being mocked or deceived automatically translates into compensable damages under state consumer protection laws when no direct monetary injury occurred.

The doctrinal implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* extend well beyond this singular political satire saga. For digital platforms operating global marketplaces connecting independent contractors with consumers, this ruling serves as both a shield and a warning sign. It confirms that platforms can maintain strict internal codes of conduct without inadvertently creating millions of private causes of action among their user base through third-party beneficiary theories. However, it also signals that litigants hoping to piggyback on platform rules must look closely at whether those rules actually intend to confer rights upon them before filing suit. In an era where gig economy disputes often turn on who owes whom what based on app interfaces and click-through agreements, *Santos* reminds us that unless you are named in the contract or can prove you were specifically intended as its beneficiary, you likely remain outside its protective walls entirely.
