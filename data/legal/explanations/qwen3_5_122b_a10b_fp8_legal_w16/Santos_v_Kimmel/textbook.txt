Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## The Cameo Deception: Facts Regarding Fictitious Name Submissions and Video Creation

To understand the legal conflict in *Santos v. Kimmel*, one must first grasp the unique factual matrix involving the digital marketplace known as Cameo. Cameo is a platform that allows individuals to purchase personalized video messages from celebrities, politicians, and public figures. In this case, former Congressman George Santos utilized this platform to offer services where he would record videos conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity" for paying customers. However, the defendant, Jimmy Kimmel—host of the late-night program *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* ("JKL")—did not approach Santos directly as himself. Instead, Kimmel employed a deceptive strategy: he submitted multiple requests for these personalized videos using various fictitious names.

When Santos received these requests, he had no knowledge of Kimmel's identity or his intentions. Acting in good faith according to the standard operation of the platform, Santos fulfilled each request by recording and delivering the requested content. Crucially, at the moment of creation and delivery via Cameo, the videos were intended solely for private consumption by the purchasers who paid for them. This transactional history is vital because it establishes that Kimmel did not simply appropriate existing footage found online; rather, he actively instigated the creation of new works through misrepresentation before acquiring them for his own purposes. These facts form the bedrock of Santos's argument that his creative output was stolen and repurposed without consent or compensation.

## The 'Will Santos Say It?' Segments: Contextualizing the Broadcast Usage

Once Kimmel obtained the videos through his fictitious personas, he integrated them into a recurring segment on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* titled "Will Santos Say It?" The purpose of this segment was explicitly satirical; it involved airing the personalized messages to mock Santos's reputation and his perceived willingness to say anything for money. By broadcasting these clips on national television as part of a comedic critique, Kimmel transformed the nature of the content from a private greeting into a tool for public commentary and ridicule.

The distinction between Santos's original intent and Kimmel's ultimate use is central to the litigation. While Santos created the videos with a positive tone aimed at encouraging viewers or celebrating life events, Kimmel recontextualized them to highlight what he viewed as Santos's absurdity or hypocrisy. The complaint alleges that this usage constituted copyright infringement because it reproduced protected expression without authorization. Furthermore, Santos argued that this conduct violated state laws governing contracts and fraud, asserting that he was induced into creating these works under false pretenses that ultimately led to his humiliation on air. The core tension lies in whether this satirical broadcast constitutes an infringing act or falls within permissible boundaries of free speech and commentary under copyright law.

## District Court Dismissal: Grounds Based on Fair Use and Preemption/Failure to State a Claim

Before reaching the Court of Appeals, this dispute was adjudicated in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York before Judge Cote. The District Court granted a motion to dismiss Santos's complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), which tests whether a plaintiff has pleaded sufficient facts to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. In its ruling, the District Court concluded that Santos could not prevail on his copyright infringement claims because they were barred by the affirmative defense of fair use as a matter of law at the pleading stage. Additionally, regarding Santus's state law claims—which included breach of contract (both express and implied) and fraudulent inducement—the District Court held that they were either preempted by federal Copyright Act protections or failed to meet basic pleading requirements under applicable state law. Consequently, all claims were dismissed with prejudice, prompting Santos to appeal this final judgment to the Second Circuit.

## Standard of Review: De Novo Analysis Under Rule 12(b)(6) and Judicial Notice

On appeal, United States Circuit Judge Lohier outlined the precise standard by which Second Circuit reviews such decisions: *de novo* review. This means the appellate court accepts no deference from lower court rulings but instead examines all questions anew with fresh eyes. When applying *de novo* review to a 12(b)(6) dismissal decision, appellate courts must accept all well-pleaded factual allegations in the complaint as true and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of non-moving parties like Mr. Santos (*DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*). However, this favorable treatment extends only to factual assertions; conclusions drawn by plaintiffs are not binding on reviewing courts unless they rise above mere speculation (*Caniglia v. Chi.-Trib.-N.Y.*).

Furthermore, while appellate courts generally look strictly at what is contained within "four corners" of a complaint during Rule 12(b)(6) motions (*DiFolco*), there are limited exceptions where external materials may be considered without converting a motion to dismiss into one for summary judgment (*TCA Television Corp.*). Specifically in cases involving intellectual property disputes like *Santos*, courts may take judicial notice of official records such as copyright registrations published in Copyright Office registries (*Island Software & Comput.*). This procedural mechanism allowed Judge Lohier's panel to verify dates regarding when certain videos were registered relative to their publication date—a fact critical later in analyzing statutory # Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Doctrine

## Statutory Framework: The Four Factors of Fair Use Under 17 U.S.C. § 107

When a plaintiff like George Santos brings a copyright infringement claim, the defendant often raises "fair use" as an affirmative defense. This doctrine serves as a safety valve in the Copyright Act, excusing conduct that might otherwise constitute infringement to prevent the rigid application of copyright law from stifling the very creativity the statute aims to promote. As the Second Circuit noted in *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, fair use allows courts to balance protection with free expression. To determine whether a use qualifies as fair, courts analyze four non-exclusive factors set forth in 17 U.S.C. § 107: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. These factors are not a checklist where every item must weigh in favor of fairness; rather, they are elements to be weighed together based on the specific context of the case. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, this framework was applied at the motion to dismiss stage, demonstrating that fair use can sometimes be so clearly established by the facts alleged in a complaint that it warrants immediate dismissal of an infringement claim before discovery occurs.

## Purpose and Character: Transformative Use vs. Subjective Intent

The most critical inquiry under the first statutory factor is whether the secondary use is "transformative." A use is transformative if it does not merely supersede or replace the original work but instead adds something new, possessing a further purpose or different character. In *Santos*, Jimmy Kimmel's team obtained videos from Santos by posing as fans requesting personal messages through a platform called Cameo. When aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*, these clips were presented as part of a segment mocking Santos's willingness to say anything for money, contrasting sharply with Santos's original delivery style which conveyed "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity."

A pivotal legal lesson from this section of the opinion concerns how courts determine this "purpose." Santos argued that his subjective intent was simply to provide encouraging messages for hire, just as Kimmel intended to mock him for taking money to say absurd things; therefore, he claimed there was no transformation because both parties shared an underlying commercial motive regarding money-for-video exchanges. The Second Circuit rejected this argument entirely, citing *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*. The court clarified that whether a use is transformative depends on what a **reasonable observer** perceives from the work itself, not on the subjective intent of either copyright holder or secondary user. While Santos subjectively viewed his videos as inspirational messages delivered for pay, a reasonable viewer watching them on Kimmel's show would perceive them as vehicles for satire and social commentary on Santos's behavior. Because Kimmel added new meaning—critiquing Santos's gullibility and greed—the first factor strongly favored fair use regardless of Santos's internal motivations or Kimmel's deceptive methods in obtaining them initially.

## Good Faith Defense: Why Misrepresentation Did Not Invalidate Fair Use

Santos attempted to defeat the fair use defense by arguing that Kimmel acted in bad faith because he lied about his identity when ordering the videos through Cameo under fictitious names. Relying on older Supreme Court precedent in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, which suggested that "fair use presupposes good faith," Santos contended that this deception should bar him from asserting fair use protections for himself? No, wait—that phrasing needs correction based on logic: Santos argued that because Kimmel deceived him into creating the content, Kimmel lacked good faith and thus could not claim fair use immunity for broadcasting it.

The court clarified that while *Harper & Row* mentioned good faith, subsequent jurisprudence has significantly narrowed its relevance in modern fair use analysis. More importantly, bad faith generally refers to an intent to steal or supplant a competitor's market share rather than mere trickery used to gather material for critique or news reporting. In *Santos*, even if one accepts that Kimmel engaged in deception to acquire the footage, his ultimate goal was not to sell copies of Santos's videos or compete directly with Santos's business model; rather, he sought to criticize and lampoon him. The complaint depicted Defendants as motivated by sarcastic criticism rather than an attempt to appropriate commercially valuable rights for themselves alone. Consequently, any lack of "good faith" regarding how Kimmel obtained access did not negate his ability to assert fair use once he transformed those materials into a comedic commentary segment designed for broadcast television distinct from their original function as personal greetings sold online. Thus,the district court correctly held that bad faith allegations did not automatically disqualify defendants from relying upon equitable defenses rooted deeply within intellectual property policy # Analysis of State Law Claims: Contract Preemption and Pleading Standards

## Preemption Overview: Why Resolving Preemption Was Unnecessary Due to Pleading Defects

When a plaintiff brings both federal copyright claims and related state law claims, a recurring threshold issue is whether the state law claims are "preempted" by the Copyright Act. Under Section 301 of the Copyright Act, state laws that grant rights equivalent to copyright are generally superseded by federal law to ensure national uniformity in intellectual property protection. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, former Congressman Santos argued that his state law claims for breach of contract and breach of implied contract were qualitatively different from a standard copyright infringement claim and therefore should survive preemption. However, the Second Circuit employed a strategic approach often seen in appellate practice: it declined to reach the complex constitutional and statutory questions of preemption entirely. Instead, the court determined that even if these state law claims were *not* preempted, they still failed independently under applicable state pleading standards. By resolving the case on the narrower grounds of insufficient factual allegations, the court avoided issuing a broad ruling on the scope of preemption in digital platform disputes. This illustrates a key principle for reading opinions: courts frequently dismiss claims on alternative grounds where possible, rendering discussions of more sweeping legal doctrines like preemption dicta rather than holding. Consequently, students must recognize that while preemption is a vital theoretical framework in IP litigation, it is not always necessary to resolve every case involving overlapping federal and state causes of action.

## Direct Breach Claim (Illinois Law): Third-Party Beneficiary Presumption in Terms of Service Agreements

Santos's first surviving state law theory was a direct breach of contract claim based on the Cameo platform's Terms of Service (ToS). His argument relied on the premise that Jimmy Kimmel violated these terms by submitting fake requests using fictitious identities, thereby breaching the agreement between Kimmel and Cameo. The crux of this legal battle turned on whether Santos could enforce a contract to which he was not a signatory. Under Illinois law, which governed the interpretation of Cameo's ToS due to contractual choice-of-law provisions, there exists a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The court emphasized that for a non-signatory like Santos to enforce such an agreement, there must be evidence that the contracting parties intended to benefit him specifically—specifically requiring an "express declaration" or language so strong it amounts to one. The opinion highlights that when parties intend for third parties to enforce contract terms, they typically include explicit third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere in the document. In analyzing Cameo's ToS, the court noted that while some provisions contained such language for other contexts, no provision extended enforcement rights to content creators like Santos against users like Kimmel. Without this express intent, Santos remained outside the protective umbrella of the contract. This section teaches students that in digital economy cases involving platform terms of service, standing is strictly limited; unless a contract explicitly grants rights to third parties, creators cannot sue platforms or users for breaches of those terms simply because they performed services facilitated by them.

## Implied Contract Claim (New York Law): Failure to Allege Essential Terms and Meeting of Minds

Turning to Santos's claim for breach of an implied-in-fact contract under New York law, the court applied rigorous pleading standards typical of modern civil procedure jurisprudence. An implied-in-fact contract arises not from written words but from conduct suggesting an agreement between parties where there was a mutual intent to enter into a bargain and consideration exchanged. To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), a complaint alleging such a claim must go beyond mere conclusions; it must plead specific facts demonstrating an offer, acceptance, and consideration sufficient to form a binding agreement. The Second Circuit found Santos's complaint deficient because it failed to allege "in nonconclusory language... the essential terms" of any such agreement with Kimmel or ABC regarding adherence to Cameo's policies or specific performance expectations beyond what was already covered by public interaction on the platform. Crucially, New York law requires proof of a "meeting of minds"—a genuine consensus between parties regarding their obligations. The court reasoned that Santos did not plausibly allege that Kimmel ever indicated an intention to abide by any specific set of unwritten rules governing their interaction beyond standard commercial norms on Cameo. Because Kimmel's actions were framed as deceptive tactics within an open marketplace rather than violations of a private understanding with Santos, no mutual assent existed. This analysis underscores that in federal courts applying state substantive law via diversity jurisdiction or supplemental jurisdiction, plaintiffs cannot rely on vague assertions of fairness or expectation; they must articulate precise factual scenarios showing how two distinct parties agreed upon specific duties before claiming those duties were breached.
