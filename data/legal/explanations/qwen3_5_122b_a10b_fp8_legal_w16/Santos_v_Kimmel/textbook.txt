Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## The "Will Santos Say It?" Segments and the Creation of Videos
The factual genesis of *Santos v. Kimmel* lies in the intersection of social media commerce and late-night television satire. Former Congressman George Santos utilized the "Cameo" platform, a service that allows individuals to sell personalized video messages to fans for specific occasions or purposes. Jimmy Kimmel, host of the late-night program *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* ("JKL"), along with American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. ("ABC") and its parent company, The Walt Disney Company, sought to critique Santos's public persona. Rather than requesting videos directly under their own names, the defendants engaged in a coordinated effort to procure content from Santos through the Cameo platform. Once Santos recorded these personalized videos—believing them to be genuine commissions for private consumption—the defendants acquired them and subsequently broadcast them on JKL. These broadcasts were edited into a recurring segment titled "Will Santos Say It?", wherein Kimmel mocked Santos by highlighting his willingness to record messages containing absurd statements simply for financial compensation. This sequence of events forms the core factual matrix: Santos created expressive works for private sale, while the defendants appropriated those works for public broadcast as part of a satirical commentary on Santos himself.

## The Deception Strategy and Solicitation
A critical element of the factual background involves the method by which the defendants obtained the videos. The complaint details that Kimmel did not approach Santos openly but rather instigated the creation of the videos by submitting requests through Cameo using various fictitious names. This strategy allowed Kimmel to bypass any expectation that Santos might refuse to record content knowing it would be used for mockery on national television. By concealing his identity, Kimmel secured videos that Santos produced with an expectation of privacy or limited commercial distribution consistent with standard Cameo transactions. However, once obtained, these videos were repurposed entirely; they were no longer private greetings but became central props in a public segment designed to ridicule their creator. While this deceptive solicitation raises questions about fairness in human interaction, it sets up the legal conflict regarding whether such conduct negates statutory protections like fair use or creates independent liabilities under state contract law.

## District Court Proceedings: Dismissal Under Rule 12(b)(6)
Following these interactions, former Congressman Santos filed suit in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York against Kimmel, ABC, and Disney. His complaint asserted federal copyright infringement claims alongside state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The district court responded by granting a motion to dismiss pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, which tests whether a plaintiff has stated a legally cognizable claim upon which relief can be granted. In its analysis, the district court concluded that Santos's copyright claims were barred as a matter of law by the doctrine of fair use, recognizing that Kimmel's use was transformative commentary rather than mere copying. Furthermore, regarding the state law claims, the court determined they were either preempted by federal copyright law or failed to meet basic pleading requirements necessary to survive dismissal. Consequently, all claims were dismissed with prejudice before any discovery took place or evidence was presented beyond what was contained in the complaint itself.

## Standard of Review: De Novo Analysis and Judicial Notice
On appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, both parties operate under a specific framework dictated by procedural rules governing appellate review of 12(b)(6) dismissals. When reviewing such a dismissal *de novo*, an appellate court accepts all well-pleaded factual allegations in the complaint as true and draws all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party—in this case, Santos. Crucially, this review extends beyond just the text of the complaint; it encompasses documents attached as exhibits or incorporated by reference into the pleading. In intellectual property cases specifically, courts may also take "judicial notice" of certain external records without converting the motion into one for summary judgment. As seen in *Santos*, this includes examining copyright registrations published in official government registries to verify dates and publication status. This procedural mechanism allows appellate courts to assess whether fair use is so clearly established based on undisputed facts (such as registration data showing simultaneous creation and publication) that no reasonable jury could find infringement at trial effectively ending litigation at this early stage without needing further evidentiary development regarding market harm or subjective intent.

# Copyright Infringement and the Transformative Nature of Parody

## Overview of the Four Fair Use Factors
When a defendant in a copyright infringement suit raises the affirmative defense of fair use, the court engages in a nuanced balancing test designed to prevent the rigid application of copyright law from stifling creativity. As articulated in *Santos v. Kimmel*, fair use "excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior," serving as a safety valve to ensure the Copyright Act promotes, rather than hinders, artistic and expressive innovation. Under 17 U.S.C. § 107, courts must weigh four non-exclusive factors: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. While these factors are distinct, they are not applied mechanically; rather, they function together to assess whether a secondary use adds something new or merely superseded the original work. Crucially, as noted in *Santos*, fair use can sometimes be so clearly established based on the facts alleged in a complaint that it warrants dismissal under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), preventing frivolous litigation from proceeding further.

## 'Purpose and Character': Determining Transformativeness Through a Reasonable Observer Lens
The most significant inquiry in *Santos* concerned the first factor: "the purpose and character of the use." The central question here is whether Kimmel's use of Santos's videos was "transformative." A use is transformative if it adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, altering the original with new expression, meaning, or message. In this case, former Congressman Santos argued that because he voluntarily created the videos after being solicited by Kimmel (using pseudonyms), Kimmel was effectively co-designer of their original purpose. Therefore, Santos contended, Kimmel could not claim his subsequent broadcast transformed that purpose.

The Second Circuit rejected this subjective argument, establishing a critical principle for students reading copyright opinions: transformativeness is determined by what a **reasonable observer** perceives, not by the subjective intent of either party involved. The court emphasized that while Santos subjectively intended to convey messages of "hope, strength, perseverance," and positivity—a fact reflected in his own allegations—the objective reality presented to an audience watching *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* was entirely different. A reasonable viewer understood that Kimmel's segment was designed to mock Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. By repurposing sincere personal greetings into tools for satirical critique regarding political gullibility or commercial opportunism, Kimmel changed both the purpose and character of the works. This shift from sincere encouragement to sarcastic commentary satisfied the requirement for transformation under *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*. Consequently, even though Kimmel instigated the creation of the videos through deception regarding his identity, this did not prevent his later broadcast from having a fundamentally different communicative goal than Santos originally intended.

## Addressing the 'Bad Faith' Argument: Why Deceptive Solicitation Does Not Negate Fair Use
A unique factual complication in *Santos* was Kimmel's deceptive conduct in soliciting the videos under false pretenses. Santos argued that this "bad faith" should disqualify him from asserting fair use as a defense. Relying on older Supreme Court dicta suggesting that "fair use presupposes good faith," Santos claimed that lying about one's identity constituted misconduct severe enough to strip away fair use protections entirely.

The Second Circuit clarified that while early jurisprudence like *Harper & Row* mentioned good faith more prominently, modern precedent has moved away from treating bad faith as an automatic bar to fair use protection in cases involving criticism and commentary. The court reasoned that focusing on bad faith risks conflating ethical missteps with legal rights under copyright law. More importantly, in this specific context, there was no evidence that Kimmel sought to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable right to exploit his own videos for profit or licensing purposes. Instead, Kimmel's actions were driven by satire and criticism—core values protected by free speech principles embedded within copyright law's fair use doctrine. The court noted that allowing a creator to block parody simply because they were tricked into providing material would grant them veto power over legitimate social commentary based solely on their subjective reaction to being deceived. Thus, while deceptive solicitation might give rise to other legal claims (such as fraud), it does not automatically negate a valid fair use defense when the ultimate use serves a transformative commentary function.

## 'Nature,' 'Amount,' and 'Market Effect': Analysis of Published Works and Lack of Market Substitution
Beyond transformativeness and bad faith, *Santos* offers clear guidance on how courts analyze three additional fair use factors when faced with video content used for parody or news reporting regarding public figures. First, regarding **the nature of the copyrighted work**, both parties agreed—and judicial notice confirmed—that Santos had published these videos on Cameo immediately upon creation under 17 U.S.C. § 101 definitions of publication. Published works generally receive thinner protection against fair use than unpublished ones because society has an interest in facilitating access to publicly available information once released by its author.

# Preemption vs. Independent Failure to State a Claim for Contract Actions

## The Preemption Question: Why the court declined to resolve whether Copyright Act preempts these specific claims

When a plaintiff brings state law claims that overlap with rights protected by federal copyright law, a threshold jurisdictional question often arises: is the state claim "preempted" by the Copyright Act? Under Section 301 of the Act, state laws that grant rights equivalent to exclusive copyright rights are generally preempted unless they include an extra element that changes the nature of the action. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, former Congressman Santos attempted to maintain state law causes of action for breach of contract and breach of implied contract alongside his federal copyright claim. He argued that these contract claims were qualitatively different from copyright infringement and therefore survived preemption.

The Second Circuit, however, explicitly chose not to resolve this complex doctrinal debate. The court noted that while it could potentially find the claims preempted, it did not need to reach that conclusion because Santos's complaints failed on independent grounds unrelated to federal preemption. This approach illustrates a fundamental principle of appellate review: courts prefer to decide cases on the narrowest possible grounds available. By finding that Santos failed to state a valid claim under applicable state contract law principles, the court rendered the preemption inquiry moot for purposes of this appeal. Consequently, students reading this opinion should understand that a dismissal based on substantive pleading failures can be just as dispositive as a dismissal based on federal statutory preemption, sparing the court from having to define the precise boundaries between state contract obligations and federal intellectual property rights in this specific context.

## Direct Breach Claim Analysis: Third-party beneficiary status under Illinois law applied to Cameo Terms of Service

To sustain a direct breach of contract claim regarding the Cameo platform's Terms of Service, Santos faced a significant hurdle regarding privity—the legal relationship required between parties to enforce a contract. The terms of service in question were agreements entered into solely between users (such as Jimmy Kimmel) and Cameo. Santos was neither a signatory nor a party to these agreements; rather, he was the creator who received video orders through the platform. To overcome this lack of privity, Santos had to prove he was an intended third-party beneficiary of those contracts.

The court analyzed this issue under Illinois law, which governed the interpretation of Cameo's Terms of Service due to choice-of-law provisions within those terms. Under Illinois jurisprudence, there exists a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." To rebut this presumption, a plaintiff must show that the contracting parties intended for the benefit to extend specifically to them. This intent cannot be inferred lightly; it must be so strong as to be "practically an express declaration." Upon reviewing the complaint and the Terms of Service themselves, the Second Circuit found no such expression in favor of Santos or other content creators who were not parties to the user agreement. The court pointed out that other sections of the Terms of Service did contain explicit third-party beneficiary language where appropriate, demonstrating that when Cameo intended for outsiders to enforce contract provisions, it knew how to say so clearly. Because Santos identified no such express declaration benefiting him, his direct breach of contract claim failed as a matter of law regardless of whether Kimmel violated any internal policies regarding fake accounts or deceptive practices.

## Implied-in-Fact Contract Analysis: The requirement for specific terms and a mutual meeting of minds under New York law

Santos alternatively argued that an implied-in-fact contract existed between himself and Kimmel/ABC under New York law. Unlike express contracts formed by written signatures or spoken words, an implied-in-fact contract is inferred from conduct showing a mutual intention to enter into an agreement. However, proving such an arrangement requires more than simply alleging that one party performed services and another accepted them; it demands evidence of a "meeting of minds" regarding specific contractual terms.

The Second Circuit emphasized that New York courts scrutinize implied contracts carefully in commercial contexts involving media and entertainment. To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), a plaintiff must allege specific facts establishing: (1) performance by one party; (2) acceptance or receipt by another; (3) knowledge by both parties that compensation was expected; and (4) sufficient facts implying an agreement on material terms such as price or scope. In *Santos*, the complaint fell short on multiple fronts. First, it failed to plead non-conclusory language describing the essential terms of any alleged agreement between Santos and Kimmel specifically regarding adherence to Cameo's policies or restrictions on video usage for satire. Second, there was no plausible allegation suggesting Kimmel ever indicated an intent to form an agreement with Santos limiting how he could use Santoses' videos once purchased via Cameo's standard transaction process. Without evidence pointing toward a mutual understanding or meeting of minds distinct from standard marketplace transactions facilitated by Cameo's existing framework, no implied contract could be legally recognized under New York precedent cited by the court. # Fraudulent Inducement Damages Requirement

## Elements of Fraudulent Inducement under New York Law
To sustain a claim for fraudulent inducement under New York law, a plaintiff must satisfy several rigorous elements, one of which serves as a strict gatekeeper for recovery: the demonstration of actual out-of-pocket loss. As the Second Circuit noted in *Santos v. Kimmel*, citing the seminal decision *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996), New York tort law requires more than just a showing that a defendant made a false representation or that the plaintiff relied upon it to their detriment in a broad sense. Instead, the plaintiff must prove they suffered a specific, quantifiable financial injury directly resulting from the fraud. This requirement distinguishes actionable fraud from mere disappointment or general commercial dissatisfaction. In the context of former Congressman George Santos's appeal, the court affirmed the district court's dismissal specifically because Santos failed to meet this threshold evidentiary burden regarding damages, rendering his fraudulent inducement claim legally insufficient regardless of whether Kimmel engaged in deceptive conduct.

The legal standard articulated in *Lama Holding* establishes that "out-of-pocket" loss refers to the difference between what the plaintiff parted with and what they received in exchange due to the misrepresentation. It is a concrete metric designed to ensure that damages are compensatory rather than punitive or speculative. When analyzing a complaint at the motion to dismiss stage, as was done here under Rule 12(b)(6), the court looks strictly at whether the plaintiff has plausibly alleged facts showing such a loss occurred. If the complaint fails to identify any actual expenditure or asset depletion caused by the defendant's deception, it cannot survive dismissal because it lacks an essential element of the cause of action. The court emphasized that this is not merely a technicality but a substantive requirement rooted in New York common law, which refuses to award damages for fraud where no tangible economic harm can be traced back to the defendant's lies.

## 'Out-of-Pocket Loss': Distinguishing Compensable Damages from Speculative Harms
A critical distinction in understanding why Santos's claim failed lies in separating compensable out-of-pocket losses from speculative or intangible harms such as reputational injury or lost future business opportunities. Under New York precedent cited by the Second Circuit, specifically *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017), courts do not accept allegations that simply assert a loss of potential revenue or abstract diminution in value as sufficient proof of fraud damages unless those losses manifest as immediate financial depletion. An "out-of-pocket" loss implies that money left the plaintiff's pocket and did not return; it is not satisfied by showing that a plaintiff might have earned more money had they known better, nor does it cover injuries where no funds were exchanged at all.

This distinction is particularly vital when dealing with creative works and services provided on platforms like Cameo. While Santos argued that Kimmel's use of false identities harmed his reputation and potentially devalued his ability to sell future videos, these are generally categorized as consequential or reputational damages rather than direct out-of-pocket losses arising from an induced transaction. The law views reputational harm differently; while it may support defamation claims or certain unfair competition theories, it does not automatically constitute the "pecuniary loss" required for fraudulent inducement unless tied directly to a specific transaction where assets were transferred based on deceit. By failing to articulate how Kimmel's deception caused Santos to lose specific funds he otherwise would have retained—or conversely, spent money he would not have spent—Santos could not bridge the gap between Kimmel's mockery and a recoverable monetary injury under New York tort principles.

## Application: Why No Actual Pecuniary Loss Was Alleged
Applying these standards to the facts alleged in Santos's complaint reveals exactly why his fraudulent inducement claim collapsed on independent grounds unrelated to copyright preemption or fair use. The core factual scenario involved Kimmel submitting video requests through Cameo using fictitious names before airing them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* for comedic effect and social commentary. While this conduct arguably involved deception regarding identity, Santos's complaint did not successfully allege that he paid anything extra because of this deception, nor did he allege that he transferred rights or services he would not have otherwise provided absent some specific financial inducement beyond his standard participation on an open platform.

As the appellate court observed, Santos failed to plead facts connecting Kimmel's false representations to any actual pecuniary loss distinct from general reputational harm or hypothetical lost licensing opportunities inherent in being mocked publicly on television.
