Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What constitutes sufficient pleading of an implied-in-fact contract regarding third-party terms of service under NY law?
Question:
I don't understand why Santos couldn't rely on Kimmel breaching Cameo's Terms of Service (ToS). The court says Santos wasn't a party to ToS and Illinois law presumes against third-party beneficiaries unless explicitly declared. Then it switches to NY law for implied contracts but says Santos failed to plead essential terms or a 'meeting of minds.' Since Kimmel clicked 'agree' to ToS which included rules about not misrepresenting identity or using content commercially outside agreed scope, isn't that an objective manifestation of assent? Why does NY law require more than just one party adhering to known public terms when interacting with another person who relies on those terms?
Answer:
The short answer is that under New York law, an **implied-in-fact contract** requires proof of a mutual exchange where both parties objectively manifested an intention to be bound by specific terms. Merely knowing that *one* party (Kimmel) agreed to a set of rules (the Cameo Terms of Service) does not automatically create a binding contractual relationship between that party and a third party (Santos) who did not sign those terms.

Here is the breakdown based on the *Santos v. Kimmel* opinion:

### 1. The Distinction Between Express and Implied Contracts
The court distinguishes between two types of contract theories, applying different standards to each:
*   **Express Contract (Third-Party Beneficiary):** Santos tried to enforce the written Cameo Terms of Service directly. However, under Illinois law (which governs the ToS), there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The court noted that unless the ToS contains an "express declaration" benefiting Santos, he cannot sue for their breach. Since the ToS contained third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere but not for these specific provisions, Santos failed this prong.
*   **Implied-in-Fact Contract:** Because the express route was blocked, Santos argued that his conduct combined with Kimmel's conduct created an *implied* contract under New York law. This is where your confusion lies: you argue that Kimmel clicking "agree" is an objective manifestation of assent to those rules.

### 2. Why "Clicking Agree" Was Insufficient for an Implied Contract
Under New York law, as cited in *Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc.*, establishing an implied-in-fact contract requires looking at **"the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances"** to determine if a meeting of minds occurred.

The Second Circuit held that Santos failed to plead this for two specific reasons found in paragraph II of the opinion:

**A. Failure to Plead Essential Terms**
An implied contract still requires defined terms. The court states that Santos failed to allege, in "nonconclusory language," the **essential terms** of the agreement between him and Kimmel specifically regarding how they interacted off-platform or via video requests.
> *"Santos's complaint does not 'allege... the essential terms of the parties' ... contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.'"* (*Caniglia v. Chi Chicago Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate*)

Simply pointing to the general Cameo ToS is insufficient because those terms were never negotiated or communicated as part of a bilateral agreement between Santos and Kimmel personally during their interaction.

**B. Lack of Mutual Assent ("Meeting of Minds")**
This addresses your core question about "objective manifestation." While Kimmel manifestly assented to Cameo's rules by clicking "agree," **Santos did not manifest assent to be bound by those same rules vis-à-vis Kimmel.**
*   An implied-in-fact contract arises from conduct showing a **mutual** intent to enter into an agreement with specific obligations.
*   The court notes there was no indication that Kimmel's click constituted an offer or promise made *directly* to Santos that Santos accepted by performing the video services under those specific conditions.
*   As stated in *DG & A Mgmt.:*, there must be an "indication of a meeting of minds." One party adhering to internal platform rules does not constitute a meeting of minds with a creator who simply fulfills orders without agreeing to abide by those same platform restrictions as a condition of payment or performance in their personal transaction.

### Conclusion
The court rejected Santos's argument because **unilateral adherence to public terms does not equal bilateral consent.** Even though Kimmel knew (and agreed) he shouldn't misrepresent himself per Cameo's ToS, Santos did not allege facts showing he relied on *those specific promises* as part of a private bargain with Kimmel, nor did he allege that he performed his services on the condition that Kimmel strictly follow Cameo's internal policies rather than just delivering what was requested. Without alleging these essential elements—specifically mutual intent and defined terms—the complaint failed to state a claim for breach of an implied-in-fact contract under NY law.
