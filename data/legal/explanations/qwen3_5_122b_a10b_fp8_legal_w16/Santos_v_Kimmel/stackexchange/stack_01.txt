Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a defendant's deceptive conduct (using fake identities to solicit content) ever negate the 'good faith' requirement for fair use?
Question:
I'm confused about the court's handling of the 'bad faith' argument. Santos argued that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to trick him into making the videos constituted bad faith, citing *Harper & Row* which states fair use 'presupposes good faith.' However, the Second Circuit seemed to dismiss this by saying *Harper & Row*'s language on good faith has been 'distanced' by later cases and that the subjective intent doesn't matter. Does this mean that even egregious deception in obtaining the underlying work is irrelevant if the subsequent use is transformative? Or is there still a threshold where bad faith can kill a fair use defense regardless of transformativeness?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes, under current Second Circuit precedent, deceptive conduct in obtaining the underlying work generally cannot negate a fair use defense if the subsequent use is sufficiently transformative and serves a different purpose than the original. While *Harper & Row* famously stated that fair use "presupposes good faith," the *Santos v. Kimmel* opinion clarifies that this language has been effectively "distanced" by later Supreme Court and Circuit authority. Consequently, subjective bad faith or deception regarding how a work was acquired does not automatically bar fair use; the inquiry remains focused on whether the new work adds something new with a further purpose or different character.

### Analysis of "Bad Faith" and Transformative Use

The core of your confusion lies in reconciling the *Harper & Row* dictum with modern fair use jurisprudence. In *Santos*, the Second Circuit explicitly addressed Santos's argument that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to solicit videos constituted bad faith sufficient to defeat the defense. The court rejected this, noting that while *Harper & Row* contained the phrase "fair use presupposes good faith," subsequent case law has moved away from treating subjective intent as a dispositive factor.

The court anchored its reasoning in the Supreme Court's decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, emphasizing that **"whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user."** 

This objective standard is crucial. Even if Kimmel acted with deceptive intent (subjective bad faith) by creating fake personas to trick Santos into making videos, this does not alter how a reasonable observer perceives the *purpose* of Kimmel's eventual broadcast. The court found that a reasonable observer would view the JKL segments as commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money—a purpose distinct from Santos's original intent of conveying "hope, strength, perseverance." Because the new work was transformative (adding a new meaning/message), Kimmel's initial deception did not render the ultimate use non-transformative or preclude fair use.

### Is There Still a Threshold Where Bad Faith Kills Fair Use?

*Santos* suggests that while egregious deception is factually significant, it is likely **irrelevant to the legal analysis of fair use** unless it directly impacts one of the four statutory factors—specifically, whether the defendant attempted to "supplant" or compete with the original work rather than critique it.

The opinion distinguishes between:
1.  **Deception in acquisition:** This was present in *Santos* (fake names), yet deemed insufficient to bar fair use because it did not change the transformative nature of the final product.
2.  **Bad faith in exploitation:** The court noted that Santos could not prove Kimmel intended to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable rights or act as a market substitute (*Swatch Group*). If a defendant uses deception specifically to steal content solely for republication without adding new expression (i.e., no transformation), that might constitute bad faith sufficient to deny fair use because it fails Factor One (transformative purpose) and potentially Factor Four (market harm).

However, based strictly on *Santos*, once a work is determined to be highly transformative—such as satire or parody—the defendant's prior dishonesty in obtaining the material does not independently invalidate the defense. The court affirmed dismissal at the pleading stage precisely because Kimmel's motivation was "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary," rendering his earlier deceptive tactics legally immaterial to the fair use determination.

### Conclusion for Your Studies
When analyzing this doctrine post-*Santos*:
*   **Subjective Intent is Secondary:** Courts look at what an objective observer sees regarding the new work's purpose, not why or how it was obtained.
*   **Transformative Purpose Trumps Acquisition Methods:** If Use B transforms Work A into social commentary/parody, Use B is likely protected even if User B lied to get Work A.
*   **Limitation:** Bad faith remains relevant only if it demonstrates an intent to merely supersede or compete with the original market (non-transformative copying), but mere trickery in solicitation does not meet this bar under current Second Circuit law.
