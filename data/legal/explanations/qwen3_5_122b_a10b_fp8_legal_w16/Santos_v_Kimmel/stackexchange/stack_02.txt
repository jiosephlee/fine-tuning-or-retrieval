Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the court distinguish between 'market substitution' and 'devaluation via parody' in the fourth fair use factor?
Question:
The opinion states that for the fourth factor, we look at whether the second work usurps the market by being a competing substitute, not whether it damages the market by devaluing the original through criticism. The court notes that creators won't typically license parodies anyway. But isn't there a risk here? If Kimmel's segment effectively destroys Santos's ability to sell these specific types of inspirational videos because people now associate them with mockery rather than sincerity, wouldn't that be market harm? How exactly does the court draw the line between legitimate criticism that hurts sales (which is allowed) and a derivative market usurpation (which isn't) in this specific context?
Answer:
The distinction you are asking about lies at the heart of the Fourth Factor's inquiry into **market substitution** versus **critical devaluation**. The *Santos* court draws this line by focusing strictly on whether the secondary work serves as a functional replacement for the original, rather than whether it negatively impacts the original's reputation or sales through criticism.

Under the Second Circuit's analysis in *Santos*, relying heavily on *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* and *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive*, the court clarifies that "market harm" for fair use purposes does not include harm caused by the success of a parody or critique. The court explicitly states: "[W]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute."

Here is how the court applies this standard to your hypothetical concern regarding "association with mockery":

1.  **No Licensing Market for Criticism:** The court notes that creators generally do not license their works to critics or satirists who intend to mock them. Citing *Campbell*, the opinion explains that "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." Therefore, if Kimmel's segment reduces Santos's sales because viewers no longer find his videos sincere, that is not a lost licensing opportunity; it is simply an expected consequence of successful criticism.
2.  **Substitution vs. Commentary:** The test asks if Kimmel's segment could satisfy the demand for Santos's original videos. Would a viewer seeking an inspirational message of "hope, strength, perseverance" go to Kimmel's show instead? The court finds they would not. Kimmel's segment had a "different character"—specifically, to "comment on the willingness of Santos... to say absurd things for money." Because the segment served a different purpose (satire/commentary) rather than fulfilling the original function (inspirational content), it did not act as a substitute.
3.  **The Line Drawn:** The line is drawn where the secondary work ceases to be a vehicle for commentary and begins to compete directly with the original for its intended audience and utility. If Kimmel had created his own "inspirational video" that replaced Santos's in consumers' minds without adding critical commentary, that would be usurpation. However, because his work relies on exposing the absurdity of Santos's performance, any resulting decline in sales due to public ridicule falls outside the scope of cognizable market harm under copyright law.

In short, *Santos* confirms that copyright law protects against competitors stealing your market share by selling a similar product; it does not protect authors from losing customers because their product has been successfully mocked or criticized.
