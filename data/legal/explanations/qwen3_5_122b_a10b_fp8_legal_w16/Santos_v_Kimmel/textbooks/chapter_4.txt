Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 4: # Fraudulent Inducement Damages Requirement

## Elements of Fraudulent Inducement under New York Law
To sustain a claim for fraudulent inducement under New York law, a plaintiff must satisfy several rigorous elements, one of which serves as a strict gatekeeper for recovery: the demonstration of actual out-of-pocket loss. As the Second Circuit noted in *Santos v. Kimmel*, citing the seminal decision *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996), New York tort law requires more than just a showing that a defendant made a false representation or that the plaintiff relied upon it to their detriment in a broad sense. Instead, the plaintiff must prove they suffered a specific, quantifiable financial injury directly resulting from the fraud. This requirement distinguishes actionable fraud from mere disappointment or general commercial dissatisfaction. In the context of former Congressman George Santos's appeal, the court affirmed the district court's dismissal specifically because Santos failed to meet this threshold evidentiary burden regarding damages, rendering his fraudulent inducement claim legally insufficient regardless of whether Kimmel engaged in deceptive conduct.

The legal standard articulated in *Lama Holding* establishes that "out-of-pocket" loss refers to the difference between what the plaintiff parted with and what they received in exchange due to the misrepresentation. It is a concrete metric designed to ensure that damages are compensatory rather than punitive or speculative. When analyzing a complaint at the motion to dismiss stage, as was done here under Rule 12(b)(6), the court looks strictly at whether the plaintiff has plausibly alleged facts showing such a loss occurred. If the complaint fails to identify any actual expenditure or asset depletion caused by the defendant's deception, it cannot survive dismissal because it lacks an essential element of the cause of action. The court emphasized that this is not merely a technicality but a substantive requirement rooted in New York common law, which refuses to award damages for fraud where no tangible economic harm can be traced back to the defendant's lies.

## 'Out-of-Pocket Loss': Distinguishing Compensable Damages from Speculative Harms
A critical distinction in understanding why Santos's claim failed lies in separating compensable out-of-pocket losses from speculative or intangible harms such as reputational injury or lost future business opportunities. Under New York precedent cited by the Second Circuit, specifically *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017), courts do not accept allegations that simply assert a loss of potential revenue or abstract diminution in value as sufficient proof of fraud damages unless those losses manifest as immediate financial depletion. An "out-of-pocket" loss implies that money left the plaintiff's pocket and did not return; it is not satisfied by showing that a plaintiff might have earned more money had they known better, nor does it cover injuries where no funds were exchanged at all.

This distinction is particularly vital when dealing with creative works and services provided on platforms like Cameo. While Santos argued that Kimmel's use of false identities harmed his reputation and potentially devalued his ability to sell future videos, these are generally categorized as consequential or reputational damages rather than direct out-of-pocket losses arising from an induced transaction. The law views reputational harm differently; while it may support defamation claims or certain unfair competition theories, it does not automatically constitute the "pecuniary loss" required for fraudulent inducement unless tied directly to a specific transaction where assets were transferred based on deceit. By failing to articulate how Kimmel's deception caused Santos to lose specific funds he otherwise would have retained—or conversely, spent money he would not have spent—Santos could not bridge the gap between Kimmel's mockery and a recoverable monetary injury under New York tort principles.

## Application: Why No Actual Pecuniary Loss Was Alleged
Applying these standards to the facts alleged in Santos's complaint reveals exactly why his fraudulent inducement claim collapsed on independent grounds unrelated to copyright preemption or fair use. The core factual scenario involved Kimmel submitting video requests through Cameo using fictitious names before airing them on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* for comedic effect and social commentary. While this conduct arguably involved deception regarding identity, Santos's complaint did not successfully allege that he paid anything extra because of this deception, nor did he allege that he transferred rights or services he would not have otherwise provided absent some specific financial inducement beyond his standard participation on an open platform.

As the appellate court observed, Santos failed to plead facts connecting Kimmel's false representations to any actual pecuniary loss distinct from general reputational harm or hypothetical lost licensing opportunities inherent in being mocked publicly on television.
