Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Doctrine

## Statutory Framework: The Four Factors of Fair Use Under 17 U.S.C. § 107

When a plaintiff like George Santos brings a copyright infringement claim, the defendant often raises "fair use" as an affirmative defense. This doctrine serves as a safety valve in the Copyright Act, excusing conduct that might otherwise constitute infringement to prevent the rigid application of copyright law from stifling the very creativity the statute aims to promote. As the Second Circuit noted in *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, fair use allows courts to balance protection with free expression. To determine whether a use qualifies as fair, courts analyze four non-exclusive factors set forth in 17 U.S.C. § 107: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. These factors are not a checklist where every item must weigh in favor of fairness; rather, they are elements to be weighed together based on the specific context of the case. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, this framework was applied at the motion to dismiss stage, demonstrating that fair use can sometimes be so clearly established by the facts alleged in a complaint that it warrants immediate dismissal of an infringement claim before discovery occurs.

## Purpose and Character: Transformative Use vs. Subjective Intent

The most critical inquiry under the first statutory factor is whether the secondary use is "transformative." A use is transformative if it does not merely supersede or replace the original work but instead adds something new, possessing a further purpose or different character. In *Santos*, Jimmy Kimmel's team obtained videos from Santos by posing as fans requesting personal messages through a platform called Cameo. When aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*, these clips were presented as part of a segment mocking Santos's willingness to say anything for money, contrasting sharply with Santos's original delivery style which conveyed "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity."

A pivotal legal lesson from this section of the opinion concerns how courts determine this "purpose." Santos argued that his subjective intent was simply to provide encouraging messages for hire, just as Kimmel intended to mock him for taking money to say absurd things; therefore, he claimed there was no transformation because both parties shared an underlying commercial motive regarding money-for-video exchanges. The Second Circuit rejected this argument entirely, citing *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*. The court clarified that whether a use is transformative depends on what a **reasonable observer** perceives from the work itself, not on the subjective intent of either copyright holder or secondary user. While Santos subjectively viewed his videos as inspirational messages delivered for pay, a reasonable viewer watching them on Kimmel's show would perceive them as vehicles for satire and social commentary on Santos's behavior. Because Kimmel added new meaning—critiquing Santos's gullibility and greed—the first factor strongly favored fair use regardless of Santos's internal motivations or Kimmel's deceptive methods in obtaining them initially.

## Good Faith Defense: Why Misrepresentation Did Not Invalidate Fair Use

Santos attempted to defeat the fair use defense by arguing that Kimmel acted in bad faith because he lied about his identity when ordering the videos through Cameo under fictitious names. Relying on older Supreme Court precedent in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, which suggested that "fair use presupposes good faith," Santos contended that this deception should bar him from asserting fair use protections for himself? No, wait—that phrasing needs correction based on logic: Santos argued that because Kimmel deceived him into creating the content, Kimmel lacked good faith and thus could not claim fair use immunity for broadcasting it.

The court clarified that while *Harper & Row* mentioned good faith, subsequent jurisprudence has significantly narrowed its relevance in modern fair use analysis. More importantly, bad faith generally refers to an intent to steal or supplant a competitor's market share rather than mere trickery used to gather material for critique or news reporting. In *Santos*, even if one accepts that Kimmel engaged in deception to acquire the footage, his ultimate goal was not to sell copies of Santos's videos or compete directly with Santos's business model; rather, he sought to criticize and lampoon him. The complaint depicted Defendants as motivated by sarcastic criticism rather than an attempt to appropriate commercially valuable rights for themselves alone. Consequently, any lack of "good faith" regarding how Kimmel obtained access did not negate his ability to assert fair use once he transformed those materials into a comedic commentary segment designed for broadcast television distinct from their original function as personal greetings sold online.
