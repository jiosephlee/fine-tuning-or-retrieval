Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # Analysis of State Law Claims: Contract Preemption and Pleading Standards

## Preemption Overview: Why Resolving Preemption Was Unnecessary Due to Pleading Defects

When a plaintiff brings both federal copyright claims and related state law claims, a recurring threshold issue is whether the state law claims are "preempted" by the Copyright Act. Under Section 301 of the Copyright Act, state laws that grant rights equivalent to copyright are generally superseded by federal law to ensure national uniformity in intellectual property protection. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, former Congressman Santos argued that his state law claims for breach of contract and breach of implied contract were qualitatively different from a standard copyright infringement claim and therefore should survive preemption. However, the Second Circuit employed a strategic approach often seen in appellate practice: it declined to reach the complex constitutional and statutory questions of preemption entirely. Instead, the court determined that even if these state law claims were *not* preempted, they still failed independently under applicable state pleading standards. By resolving the case on the narrower grounds of insufficient factual allegations, the court avoided issuing a broad ruling on the scope of preemption in digital platform disputes. This illustrates a key principle for reading opinions: courts frequently dismiss claims on alternative grounds where possible, rendering discussions of more sweeping legal doctrines like preemption dicta rather than holding. Consequently, students must recognize that while preemption is a vital theoretical framework in IP litigation, it is not always necessary to resolve every case involving overlapping federal and state causes of action.

## Direct Breach Claim (Illinois Law): Third-Party Beneficiary Presumption in Terms of Service Agreements

Santos's first surviving state law theory was a direct breach of contract claim based on the Cameo platform's Terms of Service (ToS). His argument relied on the premise that Jimmy Kimmel violated these terms by submitting fake requests using fictitious identities, thereby breaching the agreement between Kimmel and Cameo. The crux of this legal battle turned on whether Santos could enforce a contract to which he was not a signatory. Under Illinois law, which governed the interpretation of Cameo's ToS due to contractual choice-of-law provisions, there exists a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The court emphasized that for a non-signatory like Santos to enforce such an agreement, there must be evidence that the contracting parties intended to benefit him specifically—specifically requiring an "express declaration" or language so strong it amounts to one. The opinion highlights that when parties intend for third parties to enforce contract terms, they typically include explicit third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere in the document. In analyzing Cameo's ToS, the court noted that while some provisions contained such language for other contexts, no provision extended enforcement rights to content creators like Santos against users like Kimmel. Without this express intent, Santos remained outside the protective umbrella of the contract. This section teaches students that in digital economy cases involving platform terms of service, standing is strictly limited; unless a contract explicitly grants rights to third parties, creators cannot sue platforms or users for breaches of those terms simply because they performed services facilitated by them.

## Implied Contract Claim (New York Law): Failure to Allege Essential Terms and Meeting of Minds

Turning to Santos's claim for breach of an implied-in-fact contract under New York law, the court applied rigorous pleading standards typical of modern civil procedure jurisprudence. An implied-in-fact contract arises not from written words but from conduct suggesting an agreement between parties where there was a mutual intent to enter into a bargain and consideration exchanged. To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), a complaint alleging such a claim must go beyond mere conclusions; it must plead specific facts demonstrating an offer, acceptance, and consideration sufficient to form a binding agreement. The Second Circuit found Santos's complaint deficient because it failed to allege "in nonconclusory language... the essential terms" of any such agreement with Kimmel or ABC regarding adherence to Cameo's policies or specific performance expectations beyond what was already covered by public interaction on the platform. Crucially, New York law requires proof of a "meeting of minds"—a genuine consensus between parties regarding their obligations. The court reasoned that Santos did not plausibly allege that Kimmel ever indicated an intention to abide by any specific set of unwritten rules governing their interaction beyond standard commercial norms on Cameo. Because Kimmel's actions were framed as deceptive tactics within an open marketplace rather than violations of a private understanding with Santos, no mutual assent existed. This analysis underscores that in federal courts applying state substantive law via diversity jurisdiction or supplemental jurisdiction, plaintiffs cannot rely on vague assertions of fairness or expectation; they must articulate precise factual scenarios showing how two distinct parties agreed upon specific duties before claiming those duties were breached.
