Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a defendant's deceptive conduct (using fake identities to solicit content) ever negate the 'good faith' requirement for fair use?
Question:
I'm confused about the court's handling of the 'bad faith' argument. Santos argued that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to trick him into making the videos constituted bad faith, citing *Harper & Row* which states fair use 'presupposes good faith.' However, the Second Circuit seemed to dismiss this by saying *Harper & Row*'s language on good faith has been 'distanced' by later cases and that the subjective intent doesn't matter. Does this mean that even egregious deception in obtaining the underlying work is irrelevant if the subsequent use is transformative? Or is there still a threshold where bad faith can kill a fair use defense regardless of transformativeness?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes, under current Second Circuit precedent, deceptive conduct in obtaining the underlying work generally cannot negate a fair use defense if the subsequent use is sufficiently transformative and serves a different purpose than the original. While *Harper & Row* famously stated that fair use "presupposes good faith," the *Santos v. Kimmel* opinion clarifies that this language has been effectively "distanced" by later Supreme Court and Circuit authority. Consequently, subjective bad faith or deception regarding how a work was acquired does not automatically bar fair use; the inquiry remains focused on whether the new work adds something new with a further purpose or different character.

### Analysis of "Bad Faith" and Transformative Use

The core of your confusion lies in reconciling the *Harper & Row* dictum with modern fair use jurisprudence. In *Santos*, the Second Circuit explicitly addressed Santos's argument that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to solicit videos constituted bad faith sufficient to defeat the defense. The court rejected this, noting that while *Harper & Row* contained the phrase "fair use presupposes good faith," subsequent case law has moved away from treating subjective intent as a dispositive factor.

The court anchored its reasoning in the Supreme Court's decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, emphasizing that **"whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user."** 

This objective standard is crucial. Even if Kimmel acted with deceptive intent (subjective bad faith) by creating fake personas to trick Santos into making videos, this does not alter how a reasonable observer perceives the *purpose* of Kimmel's eventual broadcast. The court found that a reasonable observer would view the JKL segments as commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money—a purpose distinct from Santos's original intent of conveying "hope, strength, perseverance." Because the new work was transformative (adding a new meaning/message), Kimmel's initial deception did not render the ultimate use non-transformative or preclude fair use.

### Is There Still a Threshold Where Bad Faith Kills Fair Use?

*Santos* suggests that while egregious deception is factually significant, it is likely **irrelevant to the legal analysis of fair use** unless it directly impacts one of the four statutory factors—specifically, whether the defendant attempted to "supplant" or compete with the original work rather than critique it.

The opinion distinguishes between:
1.  **Deception in acquisition:** This was present in *Santos* (fake names), yet deemed insufficient to bar fair use because it did not change the transformative nature of the final product.
2.  **Bad faith in exploitation:** The court noted that Santos could not prove Kimmel intended to "supplant" Santos's commercially valuable rights or act as a market substitute (*Swatch Group*). If a defendant uses deception specifically to steal content solely for republication without adding new expression (i.e., no transformation), that might constitute bad faith sufficient to deny fair use because it fails Factor One (transformative purpose) and potentially Factor Four (market harm).

However, based strictly on *Santos*, once a work is determined to be highly transformative—such as satire or parody—the defendant's prior dishonesty in obtaining the material does not independently invalidate the defense. The court affirmed dismissal at the pleading stage precisely because Kimmel's motivation was "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary," rendering his earlier deceptive tactics legally immaterial to the fair use determination.

### Conclusion for Your Studies
When analyzing this doctrine post-*Santos*:
*   **Subjective Intent is Secondary:** Courts look at what an objective observer sees regarding the new work's purpose, not why or how it was obtained.
*   **Transformative Purpose Trumps Acquisition Methods:** If Use B transforms Work A into social commentary/parody, Use B is likely protected even if User B lied to get Work A.
*   **Limitation:** Bad faith remains relevant only if it demonstrates an intent to merely supersede or compete with the original market (non-transformative copying), but mere trickery in solicitation does not meet this bar under current Second Circuit law.

### How does the court distinguish between 'market substitution' and 'devaluation via parody' in the fourth fair use factor?
Question:
The opinion states that for the fourth factor, we look at whether the second work usurps the market by being a competing substitute, not whether it damages the market by devaluing the original through criticism. The court notes that creators won't typically license parodies anyway. But isn't there a risk here? If Kimmel's segment effectively destroys Santos's ability to sell these specific types of inspirational videos because people now associate them with mockery rather than sincerity, wouldn't that be market harm? How exactly does the court draw the line between legitimate criticism that hurts sales (which is allowed) and a derivative market usurpation (which isn't) in this specific context?
Answer:
The distinction you are asking about lies at the heart of the Fourth Factor's inquiry into **market substitution** versus **critical devaluation**. The *Santos* court draws this line by focusing strictly on whether the secondary work serves as a functional replacement for the original, rather than whether it negatively impacts the original's reputation or sales through criticism.

Under the Second Circuit's analysis in *Santos*, relying heavily on *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* and *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive*, the court clarifies that "market harm" for fair use purposes does not include harm caused by the success of a parody or critique. The court explicitly states: "[W]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute."

Here is how the court applies this standard to your hypothetical concern regarding "association with mockery":

1.  **No Licensing Market for Criticism:** The court notes that creators generally do not license their works to critics or satirists who intend to mock them. Citing *Campbell*, the opinion explains that "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." Therefore, if Kimmel's segment reduces Santos's sales because viewers no longer find his videos sincere, that is not a lost licensing opportunity; it is simply an expected consequence of successful criticism.
2.  **Substitution vs. Commentary:** The test asks if Kimmel's segment could satisfy the demand for Santos's original videos. Would a viewer seeking an inspirational message of "hope, strength, perseverance" go to Kimmel's show instead? The court finds they would not. Kimmel's segment had a "different character"—specifically, to "comment on the willingness of Santos... to say absurd things for money." Because the segment served a different purpose (satire/commentary) rather than fulfilling the original function (inspirational content), it did not act as a substitute.
3.  **The Line Drawn:** The line is drawn where the secondary work ceases to be a vehicle for commentary and begins to compete directly with the original for its intended audience and utility. If Kimmel had created his own "inspirational video" that replaced Santos's in consumers' minds without adding critical commentary, that would be usurpation. However, because his work relies on exposing the absurdity of Santos's performance, any resulting decline in sales due to public ridicule falls outside the scope of cognizable market harm under copyright law.

In short, *Santos* confirms that copyright law protects against competitors stealing your market share by selling a similar product; it does not protect authors from losing customers because their product has been successfully mocked or criticized.

### At what point does 'transformative use' become apparent enough to justify dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6)?
Question:
The court affirmed dismissal at the pleading stage based on fair use. Usually, fair use involves factual disputes about how much was used or what the effect on the market is. Here, the court relied heavily on taking judicial notice of copyright registrations and looking at the complaint itself. What is the standard for deciding that a fair use defense is 'so clearly established' that no discovery is needed? Specifically, how did they determine that a reasonable observer would view Kimmel's purpose as commentary without needing evidence beyond what Santos himself alleged in his complaint?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under Second Circuit precedent, a fair use defense justifies dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) when the "facts alleged in the complaint" (including documents attached or incorporated by reference) make the defense "so clearly established" that no factual development through discovery is necessary. *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 178 (2d Cir. 2016). While fair use often involves disputed questions of fact regarding market impact or the nature of the work, courts may resolve these issues at the pleading stage if the record before them—specifically the complaint and judicially noticed materials like copyright registrations—is sufficient to demonstrate that a reasonable observer would find the use transformative and non-infringing as a matter of law.

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit applied this standard by focusing on the **objective perception of a "reasonable observer"** rather than the subjective intent of either party. The court determined that transformative use was apparent enough for dismissal based on three key elements derived directly from the complaint and public records:

1.  **Objective Purpose Over Subjective Intent:** The court rejected Santos's argument that Kimmel's deception (using fake identities to solicit videos) negated fair use or dictated the video's purpose. Citing *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, the court held that "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks." (*Santos* at p. 4). Even though Santos subjectively intended to create videos conveying "hope, strength, [and] positivity," his own complaint acknowledged that Kimmel's segments mocked Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." Because a reasonable viewer would perceive the new work as satire/commentary distinct from the original inspirational message, the first factor favored fair use immediately upon reading the complaint.
2.  **Judicial Notice of Facts:** The court utilized its ability to take judicial notice of copyright registrations filed with the Copyright Office (*Island Software & Comput. Serv.*, 413 F.3d at 261). These registrations confirmed that Santos's videos were published immediately upon creation, supporting the conclusion that they were not unpublished creative works deserving heightened protection under factor two, and helping clarify that no separate licensing market existed for critical parodies (factor four).
3.  **Absence of Market Harm on Face:** Regarding the fourth factor, the court found no need for discovery because it is legally settled that parody and criticism generally do not constitute market substitution unless they act as a competing substitute for the original work (*Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*). Since Santos's complaint described Kimmel's use as mockery rather than a replacement for purchasing Santos's videos, market harm was impossible as a matter of law based solely on the allegations.

Therefore, *Santos* illustrates that dismissal is appropriate when the plaintiff's own allegations reveal a clear disparity between the original work's purpose and the defendant's new purpose, rendering any inquiry into subjective intent or complex market data unnecessary to resolve whether a reasonable observer sees a transformation. When those objective differences are evident on the face of the pleading and supported by public records like registration dates, further discovery serves no purpose in defeating a valid fair use defense at this stage.

### Does preemption analysis require resolving whether state contract claims are qualitatively different from copyright claims before dismissing for failure to state a claim?
Question:
Regarding Santos's breach of contract and implied contract claims, I'm stuck on why the court didn't actually decide if these were preempted by Section 301 of the Copyright Act. The district court dismissed them on preemption grounds, but Santos appealed arguing they were qualitatively different. The appellate court said they 'need not resolve this issue' because the claims failed under state law anyway. Is it procedurally correct to skip over a major federal preemption question entirely just because there's an independent state-law reason for dismissal? Shouldn't preemption be addressed first since it goes to federal jurisdiction/authority?
Answer:
No, you are not required to resolve the preemption question before dismissing state law claims based on independent state-law deficiencies. The Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* illustrates a well-established principle of judicial economy: when a court can dispose of a case on non-constitutional (or non-preemptive) grounds, it generally should do so without reaching broader questions of federal authority or statutory scope.

In *Santos*, the District Court had held that Santos's breach of contract and implied contract claims were preempted by § 301 of the Copyright Act. On appeal, Santos argued these claims were "qualitatively different" from copyright infringement and thus escaped preemption. However, the Second Circuit explicitly declined to rule on this substantive preemption issue. As the opinion states: "We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos's state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law."

This approach is procedurally sound for several reasons rooted in Article III justiciability and prudential restraint:

1.  **Avoidance of Unnecessary Constitutional/Statutory Questions:** Courts traditionally avoid deciding federal questions—such as whether a statute preempts state law—if the case can be resolved on narrower, alternative grounds. Since the claims failed independently under Illinois and New York contract law (specifically regarding third-party beneficiary status and the failure to plead essential terms), there was no live controversy requiring a determination of whether Congress intended to displace those specific state remedies.
2.  **State Law Grounds Are Dispositive:** Preemption analysis asks whether a state claim is equivalent to a copyright claim covered by federal law. If the plaintiff cannot even satisfy the elements of the state cause of action (as Santos could not prove privity or mutual assent), the claim fails regardless of whether it is preempted or not. Resolving the preemption question would be an advisory opinion on a hypothetical scenario where valid state claims exist but are nonetheless barred by federal law.
3.  **Efficiency and Finality:** By affirming on the failure-to-state-a-claim basis alone, the appellate court provided final resolution for all parties without expanding the record or engaging in complex comparative analysis between state contract doctrines and federal copyright exclusivity.

Therefore, skipping the qualitative difference analysis was not only permissible but preferable. The court correctly identified that because Santos's allegations did not meet basic pleading standards under applicable state laws (citing *Barry* for third-party beneficiary presumptions and *Caniglia* for pleading requirements), any discussion of § 301 preemption was superfluous to the disposition of the appeal.

### What constitutes sufficient pleading of an implied-in-fact contract regarding third-party terms of service under NY law?
Question:
I don't understand why Santos couldn't rely on Kimmel breaching Cameo's Terms of Service (ToS). The court says Santos wasn't a party to ToS and Illinois law presumes against third-party beneficiaries unless explicitly declared. Then it switches to NY law for implied contracts but says Santos failed to plead essential terms or a 'meeting of minds.' Since Kimmel clicked 'agree' to ToS which included rules about not misrepresenting identity or using content commercially outside agreed scope, isn't that an objective manifestation of assent? Why does NY law require more than just one party adhering to known public terms when interacting with another person who relies on those terms?
Answer:
The short answer is that under New York law, an **implied-in-fact contract** requires proof of a mutual exchange where both parties objectively manifested an intention to be bound by specific terms. Merely knowing that *one* party (Kimmel) agreed to a set of rules (the Cameo Terms of Service) does not automatically create a binding contractual relationship between that party and a third party (Santos) who did not sign those terms.

Here is the breakdown based on the *Santos v. Kimmel* opinion:

### 1. The Distinction Between Express and Implied Contracts
The court distinguishes between two types of contract theories, applying different standards to each:
*   **Express Contract (Third-Party Beneficiary):** Santos tried to enforce the written Cameo Terms of Service directly. However, under Illinois law (which governs the ToS), there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The court noted that unless the ToS contains an "express declaration" benefiting Santos, he cannot sue for their breach. Since the ToS contained third-party beneficiary clauses elsewhere but not for these specific provisions, Santos failed this prong.
*   **Implied-in-Fact Contract:** Because the express route was blocked, Santos argued that his conduct combined with Kimmel's conduct created an *implied* contract under New York law. This is where your confusion lies: you argue that Kimmel clicking "agree" is an objective manifestation of assent to those rules.

### 2. Why "Clicking Agree" Was Insufficient for an Implied Contract
Under New York law, as cited in *Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc.*, establishing an implied-in-fact contract requires looking at **"the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances"** to determine if a meeting of minds occurred.

The Second Circuit held that Santos failed to plead this for two specific reasons found in paragraph II of the opinion:

**A. Failure to Plead Essential Terms**
An implied contract still requires defined terms. The court states that Santos failed to allege, in "nonconclusory language," the **essential terms** of the agreement between him and Kimmel specifically regarding how they interacted off-platform or via video requests.
> *"Santos's complaint does not 'allege... the essential terms of the parties' ... contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.'"* (*Caniglia v. Chi Chicago Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate*)

Simply pointing to the general Cameo ToS is insufficient because those terms were never negotiated or communicated as part of a bilateral agreement between Santos and Kimmel personally during their interaction.

**B. Lack of Mutual Assent ("Meeting of Minds")**
This addresses your core question about "objective manifestation." While Kimmel manifestly assented to Cameo's rules by clicking "agree," **Santos did not manifest assent to be bound by those same rules vis-à-vis Kimmel.**
*   An implied-in-fact contract arises from conduct showing a **mutual** intent to enter into an agreement with specific obligations.
*   The court notes there was no indication that Kimmel's click constituted an offer or promise made *directly* to Santos that Santos accepted by performing the video services under those specific conditions.
*   As stated in *DG & A Mgmt.:*, there must be an "indication of a meeting of minds." One party adhering to internal platform rules does not constitute a meeting of minds with a creator who simply fulfills orders without agreeing to abide by those same platform restrictions as a condition of payment or performance in their personal transaction.

### Conclusion
The court rejected Santos's argument because **unilateral adherence to public terms does not equal bilateral consent.** Even though Kimmel knew (and agreed) he shouldn't misrepresent himself per Cameo's ToS, Santos did not allege facts showing he relied on *those specific promises* as part of a private bargain with Kimmel, nor did he allege that he performed his services on the condition that Kimmel strictly follow Cameo's internal policies rather than just delivering what was requested. Without alleging these essential elements—specifically mutual intent and defined terms—the complaint failed to state a claim for breach of an implied-in-fact contract under NY law.

### Is out-of-pocket loss strictly required for fraudulent inducement in New York when no direct financial transaction occurred?
Question:
The fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed because Santos allegedly lacked 'out-of-pocket loss.' Santos made free videos; he didn't pay money to Kimmel directly (Kimmel paid Cameo). So where is his pecuniary loss? My confusion stems from whether providing labor/content in exchange for exposure/promotion counts as an investment where losing it due to fraud creates recoverable damages. If he spent time creating high-quality videos expecting genuine promotion but got mocked instead, shouldn't that lost opportunity cost or wasted effort count as an economic injury under *Lama Holding Co.* standards?
Answer:
Yes, under current New York law as applied in *Santos v. Kimmel*, **out-of-pocket loss is strictly required** to sustain a fraudulent inducement claim, and "lost opportunity" or wasted labor alone does not satisfy this element absent a direct financial expenditure or a reduction in the plaintiff's existing assets.

### The Legal Standard: Out-of-Pocket Loss vs. Benefit-of-the-Bargain
New York distinguishes between two primary measures of damages in fraud cases: "benefit-of-the-bargain" (which seeks to give the plaintiff the value of what was promised) and "out-of-pocket" (which seeks to restore the plaintiff to the position they held before the fraud). 

As explicitly noted by the Second Circuit in *Santos*, citing *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), New York generally requires a showing of **actual out-of-pocket loss**. This standard mandates that the plaintiff prove they parted with money or property based on the defendant's misrepresentation. The doctrine is designed to compensate for an actual depletion of wealth, rather than merely enforcing a promise that turned out to be worthless or unfulfilled.

### Application in *Santos v. Kimmel*
In *Santos*, you correctly identified that Santos did not pay Kimmel directly; rather, he provided labor (creating videos) via the Cameo platform in exchange for what he believed would be genuine promotion. However, the Court affirmed the dismissal precisely because Santos could not demonstrate an **out-of-pocket loss**.

The Court's reasoning rests on two key points derived from the opinion:
1.  **No Direct Expenditure:** The Court found that Santos "failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss." Because Santos did not pay Kimmel money nor transfer pre-existing assets to him (the payment flowed from Kimmel to Cameo, and from Cameo to Santos), there was no immediate financial depletion attributable to Kimmel's alleged fraud.
2.  **Labor is Not Out-of-Pocket Loss:** While Santos argued that his time and effort constituted an investment, New York courts do not typically classify voluntary labor performed without a prior monetary obligation as "money or property" lost in the strict sense required for out-of-pocket damages unless that labor resulted in a direct surrender of value equivalent to cash paid away. In this context, providing content for free (even if induced by fraud) did not constitute a pecuniary loss under *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017), which reaffirmed the necessity of proving actual financial detriment beyond mere disappointment or unmet expectations.

### Why Opportunity Cost Does Not Suffice
Your hypothesis regarding "opportunity cost" or "wasted effort" aligns more closely with a **benefit-of-the-bargain** theory—i.e., arguing that had he known the truth, he would have sold his services elsewhere or charged differently. However, because New York requires proof of *out-of-pocket* loss for many tort-based fraud claims (particularly where no contract exists to enforce), speculative losses like missed opportunities or unpaid labor do not meet the threshold established in *Lama*.

The Court in *Santos* treated these allegations as insufficient because they described a situation where Santos gave something up voluntarily without receiving anything of tangible value in return *from Kimmel*, but crucially, he did not suffer a net reduction in his financial estate relative to his starting position caused by paying Kimmel or transferring assets directly to him based on lies. Without an allegation that he paid money he wouldn't have otherwise paid, or transferred property he wouldn't have otherwise transferred, the claim fails as a matter of law under New York precedent cited by the Second Circuit.

### Conclusion
To survive a motion to dismiss for fraudulent inducement in New York post-*Santos*, a plaintiff must allege facts showing they parted with money or property they otherwise would have retained. Providing free services based on false promises constitutes a broken expectation but does not automatically equate to an actionable **out-of-pocket loss** unless those services can be quantified as money/property actually surrendered and depleted from the plaintiff's holdings due solely to reliance on the fraud. Since Santos alleged neither payment nor asset transfer to Kimmel, his claim was properly dismissed.
