Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## The Objective Observer in Fair Use: Why Subjective Intent Doesn't Save Santos

The Second Circuit's recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* offers a crisp, necessary reminder about where the line between deception and expression lies in copyright law. At first glance, the facts seem tailor-made for a plaintiff seeking sympathy: Former Congressman George Santos hired comedian Jimmy Kimmel to record personalized greeting videos via the Cameo platform, only to discover later that Kimmel had used fake identities to solicit them. When these videos aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a satirical segment mocking Santos's propensity for saying absurd things, Santos sued for copyright infringement and state law violations. While the district court dismissed the case, the appeal provided an opportunity for the Second Circuit to clarify how "good faith" operates within the fair use framework—and more importantly, why a defendant's initial dishonesty does not automatically strip away First Amendment protections once the work is created.

Writing for a unanimous panel, Circuit Judge Lohier affirmed the dismissal, grounding the decision firmly in the objective reality of how a reasonable observer perceives the secondary work. The core of Santos's appeal rested on a novel theory: that Kimmel's use of pseudonyms constituted "bad faith," which should negate the fair use defense entirely under *Harper & Row*. However, the Second Circuit pushed back hard against this argument, signaling that while bad faith remains a theoretical consideration in fair use analysis, it cannot override the fundamental inquiry into whether a new work is transformative. By anchoring its reasoning in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, the court made clear that transformative use is judged by what an objective viewer sees, not by what either party secretly intended during procurement.

The court's rejection of Santos's "subjective intent" argument is perhaps the most significant doctrinal takeaway from *Santos*. Santos argued that because he originally created the videos with an intent to convey "hope and strength," and because Kimmel knew this when he tricked him into making them, Kimmel's subsequent use could not be transformative. The panel dismantled this logic by pointing out that copyright law looks outward, not inward. Citing *Goldsmith*, Judge Lohier noted that "[t]he subjective intent of the user... [does not] determine the purpose of the use." Instead, the inquiry focuses on whether a reasonable observer would perceive a different message or purpose in the new work compared to the original. In this context, while Santos saw messages of perseverance, any reasonable person watching *JKL* would see satire and social commentary on political gullibility and performative absurdity. That shift in meaning—the difference between a sincere greeting and a punchline—is exactly what makes a use transformative under current precedent.

This distinction allows for a nuanced separation between deceptive conduct and expressive content. The court acknowledged that Kimmel did act deceptively to procure the footage—a fact Santos highlighted repeatedly—but held that such misconduct does not inherently destroy fair use if the resulting broadcast serves a distinct communicative function. The panel reasoned that *Harper & Row*'s reference to "good faith" has been largely contextualized by modern jurisprudence; it does not mean that every instance of sharp practice voids fair use protection. Rather, bad faith is relevant primarily when it suggests an attempt to "supplant" or directly compete with the original market value of the work. Since Kimmel's segment was clearly parody and criticism—uses that no rational copyright holder would expect to license—the element of market substitution was absent regardless of how Kimmel obtained his material.

Beyond copyright, *Santos* provides a masterclass in procedural discipline regarding state law claims as well. The Second Circuit efficiently sidestepped complex preemption questions regarding contract claims by simply holding that no valid contract existed between Santos and ABC/Kimmel under applicable Illinois or New York law. Without privity of contract or sufficiently pleaded terms for an implied agreement, Santos had no basis to enforce Cameo's Terms of Service against third parties who merely utilized its platform through subterfuge. Similarly, the fraudulent inducement claim collapsed because New York law requires proof of actual out-of-pocket loss—a hurdle Santos couldn't clear when he voluntarily performed services he believed were legitimate until they aired publicly. These dismissals reinforce that while moral outrage over being tricked is understandable, it does not translate into legal liability where statutory elements are missing or federal defenses are robustly established.

The broader implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* extend beyond late-night television satire; they serve as a guardrail for future cases involving creative appropriation born from questionable means. If courts were allowed to invalidate fair use based solely on how a defendant acquired source material—even if that acquisition involved lying—it would create a chilling effect on investigative journalism and political commentary where subjects often conceal their true intentions or identities until exposed by media scrutiny. By insisting on an objective "reasonable observer" standard for transformativeness, Judge Lohier ensured that copyright law continues to protect speech that adds new meaning to existing works, even when those works are procured through less-than-savory tactics upstream. Ultimately, *Santos* confirms that while one can lie to get your foot in someone else's door, you don't necessarily lose your right to speak once you're inside—if what you say changes everything about how people view what was originally said there before you arrived



## Pleading Fair Use at 12(b)(6): When Deception Becomes Commentary

The intersection of celebrity, satire, and intellectual property rarely produces clean legal lines, but *Santos v. Kimmel*, decided by the Second Circuit on October 6, 2025, offers a strikingly crisp resolution. Former Congressman George Santos sued late-night host Jimmy Kimmel and ABC for copyright infringement after Kimmel orchestrated a sting operation where he posed as various individuals requesting personalized video messages from Santos via the Cameo platform. Once received, these videos—ostensibly delivering messages of "hope" and "positivity"—were broadcast on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a segment mocking Santos's history of fabrication. The District Court dismissed the suit at the pleading stage, and now the Second Circuit has affirmed.

This decision is significant not just for its outcome, but for its procedure. In an era where fair use disputes often drag through years of discovery before reaching a jury verdict—or summary judgment—the Second Circuit reaffirmed that fair use can be dispositive under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). By holding that the transformative nature of Kimmel's satire was evident on the face of the complaint alone, Judge Lohier's opinion signals that when the gap between an original work and a satirical recontextualization is wide enough, courts need not wait for factual development to protect free speech interests.

### The Transformative Nature of Satire on the Face of the Complaint

The core of the Second Circuit's analysis rests on the first fair use factor: the purpose and character of the use. Citing *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, Judge Lohier emphasized that transformativeness is judged by what a "reasonable observer" perceives, not by the subjective intent of either creator or user. Santos argued that because he originally made the videos with positive intent, and Kimmel knew this (having instigated their creation), there could be no transformation. The court flatly rejected this logic.

A reasonable observer watching *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* would not view these clips as inspirational greetings; they would see them as evidence of Santos's gullibility or complicity in absurdity for financial gain. As Judge Lohier noted, while Santos claimed his original purpose was to convey "feelings of hope," Kimmel's usage added something entirely new: a critique of Santos's willingness to say anything for money. This distinction allowed the court to find transformativeness without needing to look beyond the complaint itself. The deception employed by Kimmel—who used pseudonyms to solicit the videos—did not negate fair use; rather, it was instrumental to creating a new expressive purpose distinct from the original work's function.

### Good Faith and Market Harm: Debunking Myths

Santos attempted to revive his claim by leaning on older Supreme Court dicta suggesting that fair use requires "good faith." He pointed to Kimmel's deceptive solicitation tactics as proof that he acted in bad faith, thereby forfeiting his defense under *Harper & Row*. The Second Circuit pushed back firmly, noting that modern jurisprudence has largely moved away from using "bad faith" as a standalone bar to fair use unless it involves plagiarism or theft designed to supplant the original market. Here, there was no attempt by Kimmel to sell competing greeting cards or replace Santos's services; he sought only to mock him through criticism and parody.

Furthermore, regarding market harm—the fourth statutory factor—the court applied standard doctrine regarding parodies: creators generally cannot claim a licensing market exists for works they would never license themselves (i.e., critical reviews or lampoons). Since no one expects an artist or performer to license their work specifically for being mocked on national television in this manner, there was no cognizable market injury. Crucially, because both sides agreed on these facts based on publicly available materials (the complaint and copyright registrations), discovery was unnecessary to resolve these issues as matters of law.

### State Law Claims and Procedural Efficiency

Beyond copyright, the court efficiently dismantled Santos's state law claims for breach of contract and fraudulent inducement. Regarding contract claims against Cameo's Terms of Service, Judge Lohier highlighted basic principles of third-party beneficiary status under Illinois law: absent an express declaration benefiting non-signatories like Santos, no cause of action exists. Similarly, under New York law governing implied contracts, Santos failed to allege specific terms or a "meeting of minds" regarding how his videos would be treated once posted online. Finally, his fraud claim collapsed because he could not allege any out-of-pocket financial loss—a requisite element under New York precedent—which makes sense given that he voluntarily performed services for money (via Cameo) even if tricked about who ordered them.

By resolving all claims at 12(b)(6), the Second Circuit avoided dragging ABC into costly litigation over questions that seemed legally settled from day one. This approach underscores a vital tension in current IP litigation: how much should courts defer to juries? While some recent trends suggest expanding jury roles in complex IP cases involving digital media decisions like *Santos* suggest that when speech is clearly protected commentary disguised as commercial exploitation (in reverse), early dismissal remains appropriate to prevent chilling effects on robust public discourse.

## The Contractual Wall: Why Santos Couldn't Enforce Cameo's Terms of Service

While the headline-grabbing aspect of *Santos v. Kimmel* involves a former congressman suing over the fair use of his own "personalized" videos on a late-night talk show, the Second Circuit's October 6, 2025, decision offers a far more instructive—and perhaps permanent—lesson for the digital economy regarding contract law. While the court's unanimous affirmation on the copyright front reinforces the robust protections afforded to satirists and critics under the First Amendment and fair use doctrine, it is the dismissal of George Santos's state law contract claims that provides a critical roadmap for litigants trying to weaponize platform Terms of Service (ToS). The Second Circuit made clear: just because a platform like Cameo has strict rules governing how users behave toward content creators does not mean those creators can step into the shoes of a contracting party to sue for breach when those rules are violated.

The core of Santos's argument rested on a familiar but legally fragile theory: that as a provider of services through Cameo, he was an intended third-party beneficiary of the contract between Cameo and its users (in this case, Jimmy Kimmel). When Kimmel allegedly breached Cameo's ToS by submitting fake orders to mock Santos rather than fulfilling genuine requests, Santos argued he could enforce those terms directly. The Second Circuit dismantled this theory by applying a rigorous dual-state analysis that highlights the high bar for establishing third-party beneficiary status in commercial contracts involving digital platforms.

Under Illinois law, which governed the interpretation of Cameo's ToS, the court applied a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." As Judge Lohier noted, for a third party to enforce a contract provision, there must be language so explicit that it amounts to an "express declaration" of intent to benefit them. The court found exactly the opposite in Cameo's agreement structure. The ToS contained specific clauses granting rights to certain defined groups while conspicuously omitting any such language for independent video providers. By pointing out that other provisions explicitly granted third-party rights where intended, while these particular ones did not, the court utilized standard contract interpretation principles to find that Cameo never intended its user agreements to create an actionable cause of action for every creator on its roster. This is a vital distinction for platform operators: silence in a ToS regarding third-party enforcement is generally interpreted as an intentional exclusion, not an ambiguity waiting to be resolved in favor of plaintiffs.

The analysis became even stricter when looking at New York law regarding implied-in-fact contracts and general contract formation principles applicable to the relationship between Santos and Kimmel. Even if one attempted to bypass the written ToS and argue that Kimmel implicitly agreed to abide by platform norms simply by using the site, Santos failed to plead sufficient facts to survive dismissal. New York requires proof of a "meeting of minds"—a mutual understanding and agreement on specific terms—to form an implied contract. The complaint offered no evidence that Kimmel ever communicated with Santos about adhering to specific ToS provisions or that there was any mutual assent beyond the transactional exchange of money for video delivery. Without alleging specific terms or conduct demonstrating an agreement outside the statutory framework, Santos was left with nothing more than conclusory assertions that cannot sustain a breach of contract claim.

Furthermore, Santos's attempt to pivot to fraudulent inducement ran aground on New York's requirement for proving actual economic damages distinct from consequential losses or reputational harm known as "out-of-pocket loss." Since Santos received payment for every video he recorded—even if requested under false pretenses—the court reasoned he had suffered no tangible financial loss attributable solely to the deception in a way that satisfies New York tort standards. This highlights another common trap in digital litigation: assuming that being mocked or deceived automatically translates into compensable damages under state consumer protection laws when no direct monetary injury occurred.

The doctrinal implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* extend well beyond this singular political satire saga. For digital platforms operating global marketplaces connecting independent contractors with consumers, this ruling serves as both a shield and a warning sign. It confirms that platforms can maintain strict internal codes of conduct without inadvertently creating millions of private causes of action among their user base through third-party beneficiary theories. However, it also signals that litigants hoping to piggyback on platform rules must look closely at whether those rules actually intend to confer rights upon them before filing suit. In an era where gig economy disputes often turn on who owes whom what based on app interfaces and click-through agreements, *Santos* reminds us that unless you are named in the contract or can prove you were specifically intended as its beneficiary, you likely remain outside its protective walls entirely.
