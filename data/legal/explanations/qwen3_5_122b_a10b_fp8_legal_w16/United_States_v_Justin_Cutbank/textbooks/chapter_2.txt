Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 2: # Admissibility of Prior Possession as Intrinsic Evidence

## Rule 404(b): The Prohibition on Character Versus Exceptions for Intrinsic Acts
One of the most critical distinctions a law student must master when analyzing evidentiary rulings is the line drawn by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) between prohibited "character" evidence and admissible "intrinsic" evidence. Under Rule 404(b), evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts is generally inadmissible if offered solely to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion they acted in accordance with that character—often referred to as propensity evidence. For instance, introducing proof that a defendant stole money last year to suggest they are likely stealing today would violate this rule. However, the rule explicitly permits such evidence for non-propensity purposes, such as proving motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, or lack of accident. Furthermore, courts distinguish these "other acts" from acts that are so closely linked to the charged offense that they form part of the story itself; these are termed "intrinsic" acts and fall entirely outside the scope of Rule 404(b)'s restrictions.

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the central evidentiary dispute arose from the government's attempt to introduce testimony from victims D.F. and C.F. regarding their observation of Cutbank possessing the specific sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle days and weeks before the night of the alleged kidnapping and assault. Before trial, Cutbank filed a motion in limine seeking to exclude this testimony, arguing it constituted impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). His logic followed a common defense strategy: suggesting that showing he owned the gun earlier merely paints him as a dangerous individual prone to carrying weapons, thereby prejudicing the jury against him regardless of whether he possessed it on the specific date charged. The district court rejected this argument, classifying the prior sightings not as separate "bad acts," but as intrinsic evidence directly relevant to proving an essential element of the charged crime: possession. Because the prosecution had to prove that Cutbank knowingly possessed a firearm as a felon at a specific moment in time, evidence establishing his relationship with that specific weapon leading up to that moment was deemed integral to completing the narrative of possession rather than an extraneous act used to infer guilt through character assassination.

## Continuous Possession: Establishing Ownership and Control
The concept of "continuous possession" serves as the theoretical bridge allowing prosecutors to introduce prior sightings without running afoul of character exclusion rules. In felon-in-possession cases under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant knew they were holding a firearm at the time of arrest or discovery by authorities. A gap in time between when a witness last saw a suspect with a gun and when police recovered it can create ambiguity regarding whether the suspect retained actual control or ownership during that interval. If a defendant denies ever having touched the weapon found near them—a plausible denial given his status—he might argue he never possessed it on that specific day despite its proximity. Therefore, testimony confirming that witnesses saw Cutbank handling or displaying this exact rifle repeatedly prior to November 19–20 helps establish an unbroken chain of custody and control. It transforms isolated instances into proof of ongoing dominion over the instrumentality of crime.

During trial in *Cutbank*, D.F. testified she had seen Cutbank with this specific sawed-off rifle on "a handful of occasions" before the incident occurred where he held her hostage at gunpoint similarly C.F., another resident in the home, corroborated seeing Cutbank use or handle this gun once or twice previously. By establishing these prior moments of contact with this very object—the Marlin .22 with its distinctive sawed-off barrel and stock—the testimony fortified the inference that when police eventually discovered DNA-matched remains on similar weaponry nearby (and subsequently located D.F.'s stolen phone along with cutlery), Cutbank maintained continuous ownership throughout those intervening periods.
