Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 6: # Final Synthesis: Affirmance Based on Record Strength

## Cumulative Effect Doctrine in Eighth Circuit Jurisprudence
In appellate practice, defendants often argue that while individual trial errors might be harmless in isolation, their combined impact deprives the defendant of a fair trial. This argument relies on the "cumulative effect" doctrine. However, as demonstrated in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit applies a stringent standard when evaluating such claims. To succeed, a defendant must demonstrate that the aggregate of alleged errors resulted in "substantial prejudice." The court explicitly rejects the notion that a conviction should be overturned merely because multiple evidentiary rulings were challenged; rather, reversal is warranted only if the cumulative errors significantly influenced the verdict against the defendant.

In Cutbank's case, the appellate court analyzed his various challenges to evidentiary rulings—including those regarding prior possession testimony, hearsay instructions, and cross-examination limitations—and concluded that none constituted reversible error individually. Consequently, there was no basis to find that these non-errors collectively amounted to reversible prejudice. The court emphasized that where the government presents overwhelming evidence of guilt, such as DNA matches on the sawed-off rifle and direct eyewitness identifications by victims who testified at trial, any theoretical cumulative error fails to meet the threshold for reversing a conviction. The record showed that Cutbank's defense was unable to show that improper admission of evidence substantially swayed the jury, rendering his cumulative error argument legally insufficient under established precedent like *United States v. Keys*.

## Presumption of Reasonableness for Within-Guidelines Sentences
When reviewing sentencing decisions, appellate courts operate under specific presumptions designed to respect the district court's role as the fact-finder best positioned to assess the defendant's conduct and history. A central tenet of Eighth Circuit jurisprudence is that a sentence imposed within the properly calculated advisory Guidelines range is presumed reasonable on appeal. This presumption shifts the burden to the appellant to prove that the sentence is substantively unreasonable or procedurally flawed. In *Cutbank*, this framework dictated much of the court's analysis regarding his 292-month sentence.

The district court correctly calculated Cutbank's Guidelines range by applying a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense—in this instance, kidnapping and domestic assault—and by accounting for his status as an armed career criminal with multiple violent priors. Because Cutbank received a sentence at the low end of this calculated range (292 months out of a possible 292–365 months), he benefited from the presumption of reasonableness articulated in cases like *United States v. Goodhouse*. Furthermore, procedural requirements mandate only that the district court demonstrate it considered the factors listed in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a); they do not require a mechanical recitation of every factor weighed at length. Since Cutbank faced severe circumstances involving domestic violence, kidnapping, and assault on law enforcement officers, and because he had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions involving violence and firearms, his within-guidelines sentence aligned perfectly with statutory goals of punishment and public safety, satisfying both procedural and substantive review standards.

## Summary Judgment Logic in Appellate Review
While criminal appeals differ fundamentally from civil summary judgments—which resolve cases based on undisputed facts before trial—the logic employed by Judge Loken in affirming Cutbank mirrors summary judgment principles regarding evidentiary sufficiency and waiver. Just as summary judgment denies relief when no genuine dispute exists over material facts preventing liability or conviction from standing up to scrutiny, appellate review affirms convictions when no genuine dispute exists regarding legal error or factual insufficiency to sustain a different outcome. In *Cutbank*, many of appellant's arguments failed immediately due to a lack of preservation or failure to cite supporting authority, effectively functioning as waived claims similar to those dismissed for lack of evidence in lower-court motions.

Moreover, even where arguments were technically raised but lacked merit—such as challenges to intrinsic evidence or authentication requirements—the appellate court treated them with finality akin to a grant of summary judgment on those specific issues: there was no factual dispute capable of changing the result given the weight of contrary evidence like DNA matches and victim testimony. The opinion underscores that appellate courts will not engage in speculative reversals based on unsupported assertions or unpreserved claims absent a showing of plain error or fundamental injustice. By systematically dismantling each contention through reference to binding circuit precedent and undisputed record facts (e.g., D.F.'s live testimony curing Confrontation Clause concerns), the court reached an affirmative conclusion grounded entirely in the absence of any reversible defect in either trial procedure or sentencing calculation.
