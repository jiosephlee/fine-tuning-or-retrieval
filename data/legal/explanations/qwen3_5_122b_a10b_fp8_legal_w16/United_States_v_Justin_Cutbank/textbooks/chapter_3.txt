Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 3: # Constitutional Protections: Hearsay, Confrontation, and Trial Discretion

When analyzing appellate challenges to a criminal conviction, students must distinguish between errors that fundamentally violate a defendant's constitutional rights and those that fall within the broad discretionary powers of the trial judge. In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the appellant challenged several evidentiary rulings, specifically focusing on the admission of out-of-court statements by witnesses and the introduction of emotionally charged testimony regarding a victim's family tragedy. These challenges implicate the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause, the rules governing hearsay, and Federal Rule of Evidence 403. To understand why the Eighth Circuit affirmed Cutbank's conviction, one must examine how these doctrines interact when witnesses are present at trial and when a district court exercises its gatekeeping role over potentially prejudicial evidence.

## Hearsay Exceptions: Statements Offered for Context Versus Substantive Truth

A foundational concept in evidence law is distinguishing between hearsay offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted and non-hearsay offered for a different purpose. Under the Federal Rules of Evidence, an out-of-court statement is generally inadmissible as hearsay if its sole value lies in establishing that the contents of the statement are true. However, such statements are admissible if they are relevant for another purpose, such as explaining an officer's subsequent conduct or providing context for why police acted in a certain way upon arriving at a scene.

In *Cutbank*, this distinction became central to his challenge against police testimony recounting what victims told them immediately after escaping his custody. Cutbank argued that admitting these accounts violated hearsay prohibitions because they recounted his alleged actions. The government countered that these statements were not introduced to prove Cutbank committed specific acts via the victims' word alone, but rather to establish "the context for police officer actions and their knowledge at the time." For instance, knowing where Cutbank might be located or understanding why he was acting erratically explains why officers pursued him to a specific garage or approached him with heightened caution. By framing the testimony around the officers' perspective and immediate investigative needs rather than as proof of guilt itself, the prosecution navigated around strict hearsay bars. This illustrates a critical litigation strategy: even if a statement contains assertions of fact by an absent declarant (before they take the stand), it may be admissible if it serves a legitimate non-truth purpose that aids in reconstructing the timeline of events leading up to an arrest or search.

## *United States v. Charboneau*: Satisfying the Confrontation Clause Through Live Testimony

Even if an out-of-court statement avoids classification as hearsay due to its contextual use, it often triggers concerns under the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause, which guarantees defendants the right to cross-examine witnesses against them. Historically, courts scrutinize whether introducing such statements deprives a defendant of this fundamental right. However, *United States v. Charboneau* clarifies that when a declarant appears in court and submits to cross-examination regarding their prior statements, any potential confrontation clause violation is cured.

In *Cutbank*, both primary victims—D.F., who was held hostage at gunpoint, and C.F., her housemate—testified at trial regarding their observations and interactions with Cutbank prior to his flight from D.F.'s home. Because these witnesses took the stand under oath and faced direct questioning by defense counsel about their memories and consistency, Cutbank had every opportunity to confront them about their earlier reports to police. As noted in *Charboneau*, "the hearsay declarants... actually appear[] in court and testify[] in person," thereby satisfying constitutional requirements regardless of whether officers repeated their initial accounts on redirect or during narrative explanations of their investigation. Consequently, Cutbank's argument failed because he could not demonstrate that he was denied access to cross-examine his accusers; indeed, he exercised that very right during D.F.'s testimony regarding her fear of guns stemming from her sister's death—a topic explored further below—and inconsistencies regarding her memory of seeing the rifle on the night in question.

## Limiting Instructions: Abuse of Discretion Standard When Instructions Are Delayed Until Closing Argument

Defendants frequently request immediate limiting instructions when potentially problematic evidence is admitted during trial—for example, asking jurors to disregard an out-of-court statement except for its limited purpose (such as explaining police behavior) rather than treating it as proof of guilt. While judges have discretion to grant such requests mid-trial, they are not required to do so immediately provided they offer adequate safeguards elsewhere in proceedings. The standard for reviewing a refusal to give an immediate instruction is "abuse of discretion," meaning appellate courts will only reverse if no reasonable judge would have declined it under similar circumstances (*United States v. Bennett*).

In *Cutbank*, defense counsel asked for an explicit instruction preventing jurors from considering D.F.'s early statements as substantive evidence until she personally testified later on trial days ahead. Instead of issuing an instantaneous order interrupting testimony flow—which risks confusing jurors or disrupting rhythm—the district court opted instead for comprehensive final instructions delivered at closing arguments covering all necessary distinctions between permissible uses of evidence versus impermissible ones relying solely on third-party declarations without corroboration from live witnesses themselves ("if needed").
