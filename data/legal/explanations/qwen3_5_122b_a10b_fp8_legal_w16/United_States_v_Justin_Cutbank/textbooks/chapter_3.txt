Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 3: # Confrontation Clause Rights and Hearsay Limitations

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a cluster of evidentiary challenges raised by the defendant regarding the admission of out-of-court statements and the procedures surrounding witness testimony. These issues sit at the intersection of the Federal Rules of Evidence and the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause. While defendants often argue that admitting prior statements violates their right to cross-examine witnesses or hear only direct evidence, *Cutbank* illustrates how courts distinguish between hearsay offered for its truth versus non-hearsay contextual use, and how live testimony can cure potential constitutional defects. The appellate court's rejection of these claims reinforces the standard that district judges possess broad discretion in managing trial procedure, provided fundamental fairness is maintained and specific constitutional rights are not violated.

## Hearsay Rules: Contextual Use vs. Substantive Evidence

A central distinction in evidentiary law is whether an out-of-court statement is offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (hearsay) or merely to provide context for subsequent police conduct or investigation (non-hearsay). In *Cutbank*, the defendant argued that D.F.'s statements to police upon calling 911 constituted inadmissible hearsay if introduced as substantive evidence of his actions. The government countered that these statements were admissible only to explain why officers went to a specific location and what their state of mind was at the time they encountered Cutbank in the garage. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c), a statement is hearsay only if offered to prove the truth of its contents. If offered for a different purpose—such as establishing the background information available to an investigating officer—it falls outside the definition of hearsay entirely.

The district court accepted this distinction, noting that it did not admit D.F.'s statements as substantive proof of Cutbank's guilt during her absence from the stand initially, but rather as context for officer behavior. This approach prevents juries from treating untested police reports as proof of crime while still allowing officers to recount their investigative steps without sounding like "robotic narrators" devoid of motive. By limiting the purpose of these statements to context, the trial court avoided a hearsay violation, thereby removing one potential barrier to admissibility under Rule 802.

## 'Abuse of Discretion' Standard for Denying Limiting Instructions

Even when evidence is admitted for a limited purpose, such as providing context rather than proving facts, defendants often request specific jury instructions warning jurors not to use that evidence substantively until a key witness takes the stand. In *Cutbank*, defense counsel requested an immediate instruction preventing the jury from considering D.F.'s pre-testimony statements as substantive evidence until she appeared and testified personally. The district court declined this request, relying on established precedent that courts need not issue piecemeal instructions throughout a trial if appropriate limiting instructions are given at closing.

On appeal, Cutbank challenged this denial under an "abuse of discretion" standard, which asks whether the lower court's decision fell outside the range of permissible choices available to a reasonable judge citing *United States v. Bennett*. The Eighth Circuit affirmed that district courts have flexibility in timing their instructions; requiring immediate cautionary notes every time context-based evidence arises would disrupt trial flow unnecessarily so long as jurors receive clear guidance at the end regarding how to weigh such evidence. Because D.F. eventually took the stand and testified fully about her experiences, any risk that jurors might have prematurely relied on her initial out-of-court remarks was mitigated by her subsequent live testimony and cross-examination opportunity. Consequently, denying a mid-trial instruction did not constitute reversible error.

## Satisfaction of Confrontation Clause Requirements through Live Testimony

Perhaps most critically for constitutional due process concerns is whether admitting prior statements violates a defendant's Sixth Amendment right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." The Confrontation Clause generally bars admission of testimonial hearsay unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination (*Crawford v. Washington*). However, in *Cutbank*, no such bar existed because D.F., C.F., and other victims appeared in court voluntarily and testified under oath regarding their observations and interactions with Cutbank on November 19–20, 2020.

The Eighth Circuit emphasized that when declarants actually appear in court and submit themselves to cross-examination regarding their prior statements or current testimony, any potential Confrontation Clause defect is cured (*United States v. Charboneau*). Since D.F.'s initial calls to police were followed by her full courtroom appearance where she recounted holding Cutbank at gunpoint, threatened him with violence, and identified weapons used against her family members, Cutbank had ample opportunity to challenge her credibility directly on those points during cross-examination. Therefore, even if some earlier statements were technically testimonial in nature, their admission did not infringe upon constitutional rights because they were corroborated by live testimony subject to adversarial testing. The mere existence of prior inconsistent statements or out-of-court utterances does not trigger a constitutional violation when the source witness is present and available for questioning at trial.

## (Unpreserved Error): Authentication Standards for Extrinsic Impeachment Evidence

While discussing various evidentiary disputes, *Cutbank* also touches upon procedural nuances regarding unpreserved errors and authentication requirements during impeachment attempts. During Cross-Examination concerning inconsistencies between C.F.'s trial testimony and her earlier comments to investigators, defense counsel sought to read excerpts from a police report into evidence without laying proper foundational groundwork showing C.F.'s authorship or adoption of those specific written records (*United States v. Cumbie*). Although extrinsic evidence—documents outside oral testimony—is sometimes permitted to impeach witnesses via prior inconsistent statements under Rule 613(b), such documents must first be authenticated as reliable representations made by or attributable to that witness before they can be entered into evidence independently (*Fed.R.Evid.* 901).

When defense counsel failed to object properly or lay adequate foundation before attempting introduction via reading aloud portions authored by someone else (likely an officer summarizing interviews), sustaining objections became routine practice rather than abusive discretion (*Sprint/United Mgmt.Co.v.Mendelsohn*).
