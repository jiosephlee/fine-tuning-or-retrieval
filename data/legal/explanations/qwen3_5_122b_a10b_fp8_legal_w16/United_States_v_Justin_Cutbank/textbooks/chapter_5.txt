Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 5: # (Substantive Reasonableness) Review of Sentence Length (§ 3553(a))

While the appellate court often scrutinizes the procedural mechanics of a trial, *United States v. Cutbank* dedicates significant attention to the final stage of the judicial process: the imposition of a sentence. Even when a defendant does not challenge the calculation of the advisory Guidelines range, they may argue that the resulting sentence is substantively unreasonable. In this case, Justin Cutbank conceded that his sentence fell within the properly calculated Guidelines range but contended that imposing a term within that range violated the core statutory command of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Specifically, he argued that a twenty-nine-year sentence was "greater than necessary" to serve the purposes of punishment and public safety. To evaluate this claim, students must understand how appellate courts balance deference to district judges with their duty to ensure sentences are justifiable under federal law.

## Analysis: Harmless Error Standard Applied to Cumulative Trial Errors

Before addressing the specific sentencing argument, it is crucial to understand how the court handled Cutbank's earlier challenges regarding evidentiary and procedural rulings. The defendant raised a series of complaints concerning the admission of testimony about his prior possession of the firearm and cross-examination limitations. However, rather than engaging in deep doctrinal analysis for each claim individually, the Eighth Circuit applied the **harmless error standard** to these potential mistakes collectively. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure and established precedent, an appellate court will not reverse a conviction based on an evidentiary error unless that error had a "substantial influence" on the verdict or left little doubt that it affected the outcome.

The court emphasized that even if one assumes—without deciding—that certain evidentiary rulings were technically incorrect, those errors were rendered harmless by the overwhelming strength of the government's case. Cutbank failed to demonstrate how any single error, or even a combination ("cumulative effect") of errors, substantially swayed the jury against him. The record contained direct physical evidence (DNA matching Cutbank to the sawed-off rifle), corroborating eyewitness testimony from multiple victims who identified him with the weapon on previous occasions, and his own erratic behavior during arrest. Because this evidence was so robust, any minor procedural missteps by the district judge did not deprive Cutbank of a fair trial or alter the result. This illustrates a key lesson for law students: preserving an issue for appeal is distinct from proving reversible error; without showing actual prejudice affecting the verdict's reliability, appellate courts will affirm convictions despite isolated trial imperfections.

## Conclusion: Overwhelming Government Evidence Renders Any Error Non-Reversible

Having resolved that no reversible error tainted Conviction phase I & II proceedings through either individual or cumulative prejudice, the opinion pivots definitively to **substantive reasonableness**. A sentence imposed within a properly calculated advisory Guidelines range benefits from a **presumption of reasonableness** on appeal. For Cutbank to overcome this presumption, he would need to show that his sentence was unreasonably high given his crime and personal characteristics under § 3553(a). The court reaffirmed that it does not require district judges to mechanically recite every factor on record; rather, it suffices if it is clear from the transcript that the judge understood and considered those factors before rendering a decision.

In *Cutbank*, this requirement was easily met. The district judge explicitly acknowledged reviewing Presentence Investigation Reports (PSR), listening to arguments regarding § 3553(a) factors, and ultimately concluding that a within-Guidelines sentence was appropriate given the extreme nature of Cutbank's conduct. The court highlighted specific aggravating circumstances: Cutbank engaged in domestic abuse involving kidnapping and assault in front of children while armed with a sawed-off rifle; he threatened lives over paranoid delusions; and he violently resisted arrest by biting an officer and attempting to seize their weapon. These facts demonstrated a profound danger to society and justified a severe penalty without needing to depart upward from or vary downward from Guidelines recommendations. Consequently, because neither procedural nor substantive errors existed—and because any hypothetical evidentiary flaws were harmless—the Eighth Circuit affirmed both Cutbank's conviction and his twenty-nine-year sentence as legally sound and proportionate to his crimes.
