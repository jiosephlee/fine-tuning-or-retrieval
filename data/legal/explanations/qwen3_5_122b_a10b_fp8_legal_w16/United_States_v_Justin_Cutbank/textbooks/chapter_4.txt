Chapter 4: # Sentencing Enhancement Under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

## Interpretation of "In Connection With" Another Felony Offense

The central sentencing dispute in *United States v. Cutbank* concerns the application of a four-level enhancement under United States Sentencing Guidelines (U.S.S.G.) § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision mandates an increase in the offense level if a defendant "used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense." To understand the Eighth Circuit's affirmation of Cutbank's sentence, one must grasp the specific meaning of the phrase "in connection with." The guideline does not require that the firearm be used to commit the other felony; rather, it requires a nexus where the possession facilitates or furthers the commission of that other crime. In Cutbank's case, the district court found that his possession of the sawed-off rifle was inextricably linked to his commission of domestic violence and kidnapping offenses against D.F. By pointing the gun at her, forcing her into a closet, and threatening her life while possessing the weapon during these acts, Cutbank utilized the firearm to intimidate and control his victims. The court viewed this conduct as satisfying the "in connection with" requirement because the firearm played a distinct role in enabling the underlying felonies to occur and persist. This interpretation aligns with the commentary to § 2K2.1, which clarifies that the enhancement applies broadly whenever a firearm is possessed in relation to another criminal act, regardless of whether that act constitutes a separate count of conviction.

## Necessity of a Separate Conviction or Charge

A critical component of Cutbank's argument was his attempt to distinguish between cases where a defendant is formally charged with multiple crimes versus those where only one charge exists. He implicitly suggested that without a separate indictment or conviction for kidnapping or domestic assault, the enhancement should not apply. However, the text of U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1 explicitly rejects this limitation. The official commentary to Section 2K2.1 states that an additional felony offense need not result in "a criminal charge [being] brought, or a conviction [being] obtained" for the enhancement to attach. The Eighth Circuit relied heavily on this language to reject Cutbank's contention. The policy rationale behind this rule is that sentencing courts must account for all criminal conduct associated with a defendant's behavior, even if prosecutorial discretion resulted in only one charge being filed at trial. Consequently, as long as there is sufficient evidence establishing that another felony occurred and was connected to the firearm possession—as was evident from D.F.'s testimony regarding her confinement and terror—the absence of a second conviction does not bar the application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The focus remains strictly on the factual reality of what transpired during the incident rather than procedural outcomes regarding charges filed by prosecutors.

## Fact-Finding and Reliance on PSR Facts Without Objection

Procedurally, Cutbank challenged how the district court established the existence of these underlying felonies required for the enhancement. During sentencing proceedings, neither party objected to specific factual statements contained within the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) regarding Cutbank's prior convictions and his actions during November 2020. Under Eighth Circuit precedent, specifically *United States v. Dunn*, when factual assertions in a PSR go unobjected to at sentencing, they are deemed admitted for purposes of calculating sentencing enhancements unless they are clearly erroneous on their face—a standard rarely met when undisputed facts support an enhancement like kidnapping or domestic violence supported by trial testimony already entered into evidence at trial. Because Cutbank failed to object to these specific findings before sentencing concluded, he waived any challenge to their accuracy regarding his possession in connection with another felony. The appellate court noted that since no objection was raised regarding these specific facts supporting § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), there was no error in adopting them as true facts upon which to base the four-level increase. This underscores a vital lesson for practitioners: silence at sentencing can effectively concede foundational facts necessary for guideline enhancements, shifting any subsequent appellate review toward plain error or abuse-of-discretion standards rather than de novo fact-finding challenges.
