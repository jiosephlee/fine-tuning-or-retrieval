Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Factual Genesis and Procedural Posture

## The November 2020 Domestic Incident: Assault, Kidnapping, and Flight

The factual narrative underlying *United States v. Cutbank* begins on the night of November 19–20, 2020, where the defendant's conduct escalated rapidly from domestic disturbance to armed kidnapping. Following the consumption of alcohol and methamphetamine, Justin Cutbank confronted his girlfriend, D.F., pointing a firearm at her face. Driven by paranoia regarding "listening devices" and external threats, Cutbank forced D.F. into a closet at gunpoint, where he detained her for what she estimated to be several hours. During this confinement, he threatened her life and physically assaulted her by striking her across the face with the weapon. The situation further deteriorated when Cutbank compelled D.F. to wake other occupants of the home, including her two teenage sons and a housemate named C.F., before eventually fleeing the scene with both D.F.'s cell phone and the firearm. This sequence of events established the core criminal conduct that would later serve as the predicate for sentencing enhancements beyond the immediate charge of possession.

## Police Standoff and Recovery of Physical Evidence (DNA match, sawed-off rifle)

Following Cutbank's escape, law enforcement received reports placing him approximately half a mile away at a different residence, where he had barricaded himself inside a garage and refused to depart. Upon arrival, officers encountered Cutbank making erratic statements and claiming to possess a weapon. The encounter turned physical when officers attempted to detain him; Cutbank resisted violently by biting an officer on the face and neck while attempting to grab the officer's service weapon. A search of the area connecting D.F.'s home to the garage yielded critical physical evidence: D.F.'s stolen cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle modified with a sawed-off barrel and stock. Crucially for establishing identity and possession at trial, DNA testing confirmed that biological material found on the rifle belonged to Cutbank. Furthermore, witnesses D.F. and C.F. positively identified this specific weapon as one they had previously observed in Cutbank's possession prior to that night's events.

## Charges: Felon in Possession and Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) Applicability

Based on these facts, the government prosecuted Cutbank under two primary statutory frameworks: 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) and 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). Section 922(g)(1) prohibits individuals who have been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year from possessing firearms or ammunition. However, because Cutbank had an extensive prior criminal record, the prosecution invoked the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) under § 924(e). To trigger ACCA's mandatory minimum penalty provisions—which significantly elevate sentencing requirements—the government was required to prove that Cutbank possessed three or more prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses occurring on separate occasions. At trial, a jury unanimously found Cutbank guilty of being a felon in possession of a firearm and specifically found that he met the statutory criteria for having at least three qualifying prior violent felony convictions, thereby activating the enhanced penalty structure of § 924(e).

## Procedural History: Conviction, Sentencing Range Calculation, and Grounds for Appeal

After securing conviction on both counts related to possession status and prior felonies, the case proceeded to sentencing where complex guideline calculations were undertaken by the district court. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended applying various enhancements under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b), including increases for using a sawed-off firearm (§ 5845(a)), possessing a weapon with an obliterated serial number creating reckless risk during flight (§ 3A1), and most controversially on appeal—a four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm "in connection with" another felony offense under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). These adjustments resulted in an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment within Criminal History Category VI; notably, even though ACCA mandates certain penalties regardless of guidelines ranges here due to his history as an "armed career criminal," the court sentenced him within this calculated advisory range at 292 months cut bank appealed his conviction and sentence challenging multiple evidentiary rulings—specifically regarding testimony about prior observations of the gun—and contesting the application of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement which he argued improperly inflated his sentencing range contrary to statutory principles under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a).

# Admissibility of Prior Acts as Intrinsic Evidence

## Rule 404(b) Analysis: Propensity vs. Intrinsic Evidence Distinction

When analyzing evidentiary challenges in *United States v. Cutbank*, students must first grasp the fundamental tension between Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) and the concept of "intrinsic" evidence. Rule 404(b) generally prohibits the admission of evidence concerning a defendant's other crimes, wrongs, or acts if the purpose is to prove the defendant's character and show that they acted in accordance with that character on a particular occasion. This is known as propensity evidence, and it is presumptively inadmissible because it invites jurors to convict based on who the defendant is rather than what they specifically did in the case at hand. However, courts distinguish between "extrinsic" other acts—which fall squarely under Rule 404(b)'s restrictions—and "intrinsic" evidence, which is so intertwined with the charged offense that it does not constitute an "other act" at all.

In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit applied this distinction to testimony regarding whether witnesses had seen the defendant with the specific firearm prior to the night of the crime. Cutbank argued that allowing witnesses D.F. and C.F. to testify that they had seen him holding the sawed-off rifle on previous occasions constituted improper propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court rejected this argument, classifying the testimony as intrinsic. The appellate court affirmed this classification, reasoning that prior possession of a specific firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon. Unlike generic bad behavior occurring days or weeks away from the charged incident, evidence showing continuous possession serves a different function: it establishes ownership and control. Because proof of possession requires demonstrating that the defendant knowingly had dominion over the weapon, testimony linking Cutbank to the rifle before the immediate incident helps complete the narrative of his exclusive control rather than merely suggesting he has a violent character.

## Relevance Test: Establishing Continuous Possession via Prior Sightings

The legal standard for admitting intrinsic evidence focuses heavily on relevance and necessity rather than just temporal proximity. For evidence to be deemed intrinsic in a felon-in-possession case, it must help prove an element of the crime itself—specifically, that the defendant possessed or controlled the firearm at the time of arrest or discovery. In *Cutbank*, the court relied on precedent stating that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control." This creates a logical chain where past sightings validate present custody; if Cutbank was seen with this specific sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle earlier, it makes it significantly more probable that he maintained ownership through to November 2020 when police found him with it again after fleeing his girlfriend's home.

This relevance test prevents defendants from claiming accidental possession or lack of knowledge regarding a weapon found in their vicinity if there is independent corroboration of their long-term relationship with that object. By viewing these prior sightings not as separate "bad acts" but as integral components of a single ongoing transaction—the continuous holding of an illegal weapon—the court avoids triggering Rule 404(b)'s strictures entirely. Consequently, such evidence does not require a balancing test for unfair prejudice under Rule 403 in this context because its primary purpose is not to inflame juror passion against Cutbank's general criminality, but to objectively prove he knew he had a gun and exercised control over it over time.

## Application to Witness Testimony from D.F. and C.F.

Applying these principles to the facts in *Cutbank* illustrates how witness testimony functions as intrinsic evidence in practice. During trial, D.F., Cutbank's girlfriend, testified that she had seen him with the sawed-off rifle on a "handful of times" before the night she was held hostage at gunpoint similarly, C.F., another resident in the house testified that she had witnessed Cutbank using or handling this specific gun "once or twice" previously when asked about its use during their confrontation cut bank renewed his objection to this testimony at trial, arguing once again that it violated Rule 404(b). The district court denied these objections consistent with its pretrial ruling that such testimony was intrinsic because it demonstrated continuous possession rather than mere propensity for violence.*

The significance of D.F.'s and C.F.'s accounts lies in their ability to corroborate physical evidence (the DNA match found on the rifle) with eyewitness confirmation of ownership extending beyond a singular moment in time. Without this testimony, there might have been ambiguity regarding whether Cutbank acquired possession only moments before fleeing or if he was hiding weapons elsewhere in anticipation of conflict instead here; however; by confirming repeated exposure; these witnesses solidified his status as an owner/controller throughout period leading up arrest thereby satisfying mens rea requirement for felon-in-possession charge accordingly eighth circuit upheld lower court determination noting absence any authority contradicting intrinsic nature proof provided witnesses observations established unbroken chain custody linking cutback specific instrumentality charged offense thus rendering rule forty-four b challenge meritless*.

# Confrontation Clause Rights and Hearsay Limitations

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed a cluster of evidentiary challenges raised by the defendant regarding the admission of out-of-court statements and the procedures surrounding witness testimony. These issues sit at the intersection of the Federal Rules of Evidence and the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause. While defendants often argue that admitting prior statements violates their right to cross-examine witnesses or hear only direct evidence, *Cutbank* illustrates how courts distinguish between hearsay offered for its truth versus non-hearsay contextual use, and how live testimony can cure potential constitutional defects. The appellate court's rejection of these claims reinforces the standard that district judges possess broad discretion in managing trial procedure, provided fundamental fairness is maintained and specific constitutional rights are not violated.

## Hearsay Rules: Contextual Use vs. Substantive Evidence

A central distinction in evidentiary law is whether an out-of-court statement is offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (hearsay) or merely to provide context for subsequent police conduct or investigation (non-hearsay). In *Cutbank*, the defendant argued that D.F.'s statements to police upon calling 911 constituted inadmissible hearsay if introduced as substantive evidence of his actions. The government countered that these statements were admissible only to explain why officers went to a specific location and what their state of mind was at the time they encountered Cutbank in the garage. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 801(c), a statement is hearsay only if offered to prove the truth of its contents. If offered for a different purpose—such as establishing the background information available to an investigating officer—it falls outside the definition of hearsay entirely.

The district court accepted this distinction, noting that it did not admit D.F.'s statements as substantive proof of Cutbank's guilt during her absence from the stand initially, but rather as context for officer behavior. This approach prevents juries from treating untested police reports as proof of crime while still allowing officers to recount their investigative steps without sounding like "robotic narrators" devoid of motive. By limiting the purpose of these statements to context, the trial court avoided a hearsay violation, thereby removing one potential barrier to admissibility under Rule 802.

## 'Abuse of Discretion' Standard for Denying Limiting Instructions

Even when evidence is admitted for a limited purpose, such as providing context rather than proving facts, defendants often request specific jury instructions warning jurors not to use that evidence substantively until a key witness takes the stand. In *Cutbank*, defense counsel requested an immediate instruction preventing the jury from considering D.F.'s pre-testimony statements as substantive evidence until she appeared and testified personally. The district court declined this request, relying on established precedent that courts need not issue piecemeal instructions throughout a trial if appropriate limiting instructions are given at closing.

On appeal, Cutbank challenged this denial under an "abuse of discretion" standard, which asks whether the lower court's decision fell outside the range of permissible choices available to a reasonable judge citing *United States v. Bennett*. The Eighth Circuit affirmed that district courts have flexibility in timing their instructions; requiring immediate cautionary notes every time context-based evidence arises would disrupt trial flow unnecessarily so long as jurors receive clear guidance at the end regarding how to weigh such evidence. Because D.F. eventually took the stand and testified fully about her experiences, any risk that jurors might have prematurely relied on her initial out-of-court remarks was mitigated by her subsequent live testimony and cross-examination opportunity. Consequently, denying a mid-trial instruction did not constitute reversible error.

## Satisfaction of Confrontation Clause Requirements through Live Testimony

Perhaps most critically for constitutional due process concerns is whether admitting prior statements violates a defendant's Sixth Amendment right "to be confronted with the witnesses against him." The Confrontation Clause generally bars admission of testimonial hearsay unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination (*Crawford v. Washington*). However, in *Cutbank*, no such bar existed because D.F., C.F., and other victims appeared in court voluntarily and testified under oath regarding their observations and interactions with Cutbank on November 19–20, 2020.

The Eighth Circuit emphasized that when declarants actually appear in court and submit themselves to cross-examination regarding their prior statements or current testimony, any potential Confrontation Clause defect is cured (*United States v. Charboneau*). Since D.F.'s initial calls to police were followed by her full courtroom appearance where she recounted holding Cutbank at gunpoint, threatened him with violence, and identified weapons used against her family members, Cutbank had ample opportunity to challenge her credibility directly on those points during cross-examination. Therefore, even if some earlier statements were technically testimonial in nature, their admission did not infringe upon constitutional rights because they were corroborated by live testimony subject to adversarial testing. The mere existence of prior inconsistent statements or out-of-court utterances does not trigger a constitutional violation when the source witness is present and available for questioning at trial.

## (Unpreserved Error): Authentication Standards for Extrinsic Impeachment Evidence

While discussing various evidentiary disputes, *Cutbank* also touches upon procedural nuances regarding unpreserved errors and authentication requirements during impeachment attempts. During Cross-Examination concerning inconsistencies between C.F.'s trial testimony and her earlier comments to investigators, defense counsel sought to read excerpts from a police report into evidence without laying proper foundational groundwork showing C.F.'s authorship or adoption of those specific written records (*United States v. Cumbie*). Although extrinsic evidence—documents outside oral testimony—is sometimes permitted to impeach witnesses via prior inconsistent statements under Rule 613(b), such documents must first be authenticated as reliable representations made by or attributable to that witness before they can be entered into evidence independently (*Fed.R.Evid.* 901).

When defense counsel failed to object properly or lay adequate foundation before attempting introduction via reading aloud portions authored by someone else (likely an officer summarizing interviews), sustaining objections became routine practice rather than abusive discretion (*Sprint/United Mgmt.Co.v.Mendelsohn*). # Sentencing Enhancement Under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

## Interpretation of "In Connection With" Another Felony Offense

The central sentencing dispute in *United States v. Cutbank* concerns the application of a four-level enhancement under United States Sentencing Guidelines (U.S.S.G.) § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision mandates an increase in the offense level if a defendant "used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense." To understand the Eighth Circuit's affirmation of Cutbank's sentence, one must grasp the specific meaning of the phrase "in connection with." The guideline does not require that the firearm be used to commit the other felony; rather, it requires a nexus where the possession facilitates or furthers the commission of that other crime. In Cutbank's case, the district court found that his possession of the sawed-off rifle was inextricably linked to his commission of domestic violence and kidnapping offenses against D.F. By pointing the gun at her, forcing her into a closet, and threatening her life while possessing the weapon during these acts, Cutbank utilized the firearm to intimidate and control his victims. The court viewed this conduct as satisfying the "in connection with" requirement because the firearm played a distinct role in enabling the underlying felonies to occur and persist. This interpretation aligns with the commentary to § 2K2.1, which clarifies that the enhancement applies broadly whenever a firearm is possessed in relation to another criminal act, regardless of whether that act constitutes a separate count of conviction.

## Necessity of a Separate Conviction or Charge

A critical component of Cutbank's argument was his attempt to distinguish between cases where a defendant is formally charged with multiple crimes versus those where only one charge exists. He implicitly suggested that without a separate indictment or conviction for kidnapping or domestic assault, the enhancement should not apply. However, the text of U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1 explicitly rejects this limitation. The official commentary to Section 2K2.1 states that an additional felony offense need not result in "a criminal charge [being] brought, or a conviction [being] obtained" for the enhancement to attach. The Eighth Circuit relied heavily on this language to reject Cutbank's contention. The policy rationale behind this rule is that sentencing courts must account for all criminal conduct associated with a defendant's behavior, even if prosecutorial discretion resulted in only one charge being filed at trial. Consequently, as long as there is sufficient evidence establishing that another felony occurred and was connected to the firearm possession—as was evident from D.F.'s testimony regarding her confinement and terror—the absence of a second conviction does not bar the application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The focus remains strictly on the factual reality of what transpired during the incident rather than procedural outcomes regarding charges filed by prosecutors.

## Fact-Finding and Reliance on PSR Facts Without Objection

Procedurally, Cutbank challenged how the district court established the existence of these underlying felonies required for the enhancement. During sentencing proceedings, neither party objected to specific factual statements contained within the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) regarding Cutbank's prior convictions and his actions during November 2020. Under Eighth Circuit precedent, specifically *United States v. Dunn*, when factual assertions in a PSR go unobjected to at sentencing, they are deemed admitted for purposes of calculating sentencing enhancements unless they are clearly erroneous on their face—a standard rarely met when undisputed facts support an enhancement like kidnapping or domestic violence supported by trial testimony already entered into evidence at trial. Because Cutbank failed to object to these specific findings before sentencing concluded, he waived any challenge to their accuracy regarding his possession in connection with another felony. The appellate court noted that since no objection was raised regarding these specific facts supporting § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), there was no error in adopting them as true facts upon which to base the four-level increase. This underscores a vital lesson for practitioners: silence at sentencing can effectively concede foundational facts necessary for guideline enhancements, shifting any subsequent appellate review toward plain error or abuse-of-discretion standards rather than de novo fact-finding challenges.

# (Substantive Reasonableness) Review of Sentence Length (§ 3553(a))

While the appellate court often scrutinizes the procedural mechanics of a trial, *United States v. Cutbank* dedicates significant attention to the final stage of the judicial process: the imposition of a sentence. Even when a defendant does not challenge the calculation of the advisory Guidelines range, they may argue that the resulting sentence is substantively unreasonable. In this case, Justin Cutbank conceded that his sentence fell within the properly calculated Guidelines range but contended that imposing a term within that range violated the core statutory command of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Specifically, he argued that a twenty-nine-year sentence was "greater than necessary" to serve the purposes of punishment and public safety. To evaluate this claim, students must understand how appellate courts balance deference to district judges with their duty to ensure sentences are justifiable under federal law.

## Analysis: Harmless Error Standard Applied to Cumulative Trial Errors

Before addressing the specific sentencing argument, it is crucial to understand how the court handled Cutbank's earlier challenges regarding evidentiary and procedural rulings. The defendant raised a series of complaints concerning the admission of testimony about his prior possession of the firearm and cross-examination limitations. However, rather than engaging in deep doctrinal analysis for each claim individually, the Eighth Circuit applied the **harmless error standard** to these potential mistakes collectively. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure and established precedent, an appellate court will not reverse a conviction based on an evidentiary error unless that error had a "substantial influence" on the verdict or left little doubt that it affected the outcome.

The court emphasized that even if one assumes—without deciding—that certain evidentiary rulings were technically incorrect, those errors were rendered harmless by the overwhelming strength of the government's case. Cutbank failed to demonstrate how any single error, or even a combination ("cumulative effect") of errors, substantially swayed the jury against him. The record contained direct physical evidence (DNA matching Cutbank to the sawed-off rifle), corroborating eyewitness testimony from multiple victims who identified him with the weapon on previous occasions, and his own erratic behavior during arrest. Because this evidence was so robust, any minor procedural missteps by the district judge did not deprive Cutbank of a fair trial or alter the result. This illustrates a key lesson for law students: preserving an issue for appeal is distinct from proving reversible error; without showing actual prejudice affecting the verdict's reliability, appellate courts will affirm convictions despite isolated trial imperfections.

## Conclusion: Overwhelming Government Evidence Renders Any Error Non-Reversible

Having resolved that no reversible error tainted Conviction phase I & II proceedings through either individual or cumulative prejudice, the opinion pivots definitively to **substantive reasonableness**. A sentence imposed within a properly calculated advisory Guidelines range benefits from a **presumption of reasonableness** on appeal. For Cutbank to overcome this presumption, he would need to show that his sentence was unreasonably high given his crime and personal characteristics under § 3553(a). The court reaffirmed that it does not require district judges to mechanically recite every factor on record; rather, it suffices if it is clear from the transcript that the judge understood and considered those factors before rendering a decision.

In *Cutbank*, this requirement was easily met. The district judge explicitly acknowledged reviewing Presentence Investigation Reports (PSR), listening to arguments regarding § 3553(a) factors, and ultimately concluding that a within-Guidelines sentence was appropriate given the extreme nature of Cutbank's conduct. The court highlighted specific aggravating circumstances: Cutbank engaged in domestic abuse involving kidnapping and assault in front of children while armed with a sawed-off rifle; he threatened lives over paranoid delusions; and he violently resisted arrest by biting an officer and attempting to seize their weapon. These facts demonstrated a profound danger to society and justified a severe penalty without needing to depart upward from or vary downward from Guidelines recommendations. Consequently, because neither procedural nor substantive errors existed—and because any hypothetical evidentiary flaws were harmless—the Eighth Circuit affirmed both Cutbank's conviction and his twenty-nine-year sentence as legally sound and proportionate to his crimes.

# Final Synthesis: Affirmance Based on Record Strength

## Cumulative Effect Doctrine in Eighth Circuit Jurisprudence
In appellate practice, defendants often argue that while individual trial errors might be harmless in isolation, their combined impact deprives the defendant of a fair trial. This argument relies on the "cumulative effect" doctrine. However, as demonstrated in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit applies a stringent standard when evaluating such claims. To succeed, a defendant must demonstrate that the aggregate of alleged errors resulted in "substantial prejudice." The court explicitly rejects the notion that a conviction should be overturned merely because multiple evidentiary rulings were challenged; rather, reversal is warranted only if the cumulative errors significantly influenced the verdict against the defendant.

In Cutbank's case, the appellate court analyzed his various challenges to evidentiary rulings—including those regarding prior possession testimony, hearsay instructions, and cross-examination limitations—and concluded that none constituted reversible error individually. Consequently, there was no basis to find that these non-errors collectively amounted to reversible prejudice. The court emphasized that where the government presents overwhelming evidence of guilt, such as DNA matches on the sawed-off rifle and direct eyewitness identifications by victims who testified at trial, any theoretical cumulative error fails to meet the threshold for reversing a conviction. The record showed that Cutbank's defense was unable to show that improper admission of evidence substantially swayed the jury, rendering his cumulative error argument legally insufficient under established precedent like *United States v. Keys*.

## Presumption of Reasonableness for Within-Guidelines Sentences
When reviewing sentencing decisions, appellate courts operate under specific presumptions designed to respect the district court's role as the fact-finder best positioned to assess the defendant's conduct and history. A central tenet of Eighth Circuit jurisprudence is that a sentence imposed within the properly calculated advisory Guidelines range is presumed reasonable on appeal. This presumption shifts the burden to the appellant to prove that the sentence is substantively unreasonable or procedurally flawed. In *Cutbank*, this framework dictated much of the court's analysis regarding his 292-month sentence.

The district court correctly calculated Cutbank's Guidelines range by applying a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense—in this instance, kidnapping and domestic assault—and by accounting for his status as an armed career criminal with multiple violent priors. Because Cutbank received a sentence at the low end of this calculated range (292 months out of a possible 292–365 months), he benefited from the presumption of reasonableness articulated in cases like *United States v. Goodhouse*. Furthermore, procedural requirements mandate only that the district court demonstrate it considered the factors listed in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a); they do not require a mechanical recitation of every factor weighed at length. Since Cutbank faced severe circumstances involving domestic violence, kidnapping, and assault on law enforcement officers, and because he had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions involving violence and firearms, his within-guidelines sentence aligned perfectly with statutory goals of punishment and public safety, satisfying both procedural and substantive review standards.

## Summary Judgment Logic in Appellate Review
While criminal appeals differ fundamentally from civil summary judgments—which resolve cases based on undisputed facts before trial—the logic employed by Judge Loken in affirming Cutbank mirrors summary judgment principles regarding evidentiary sufficiency and waiver. Just as summary judgment denies relief when no genuine dispute exists over material facts preventing liability or conviction from standing up to scrutiny, appellate review affirms convictions when no genuine dispute exists regarding legal error or factual insufficiency to sustain a different outcome. In *Cutbank*, many of appellant's arguments failed immediately due to a lack of preservation or failure to cite supporting authority, effectively functioning as waived claims similar to those dismissed for lack of evidence in lower-court motions.

Moreover, even where arguments were technically raised but lacked merit—such as challenges to intrinsic evidence or authentication requirements—the appellate court treated them with finality akin to a grant of summary judgment on those specific issues: there was no factual dispute capable of changing the result given the weight of contrary evidence like DNA matches and victim testimony. The opinion underscores that appellate courts will not engage in speculative reversals based on unsupported assertions or unpreserved claims absent a showing of plain error or fundamental injustice. By systematically dismantling each contention through reference to binding circuit precedent and undisputed record facts (e.g., D.F.'s live testimony curing Confrontation Clause concerns), the court reached an affirmative conclusion grounded entirely in the absence of any reversible defect in either trial procedure or sentencing calculation.
