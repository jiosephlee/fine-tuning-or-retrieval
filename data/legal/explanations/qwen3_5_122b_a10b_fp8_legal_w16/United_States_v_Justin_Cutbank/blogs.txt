Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## Defining 'Intrinsic' Acts: The Eighth Circuit Tightens the Net Around Prior Possession Testimony

When a felon is caught with a firearm, the prosecution's task seems straightforward: prove the defendant is a convicted felon and prove they knowingly possessed the weapon. But in practice, especially when law enforcement does not catch the defendant red-handed or when the weapon is recovered days later, the "possession" element becomes a fragile link in the chain of custody. How does a prosecutor bridge the gap between a past sighting of a gun and its seizure today? In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, Judge Loken offered a robust answer that significantly lowers the barrier for admitting testimony about prior sightings of weapons. By reaffirming that evidence of previous possession can be "intrinsic" to the charged offense rather than impermissible character evidence, the court has provided prosecutors with powerful tools—and defendants with fewer avenues to exclude prejudicial history—in complex firearm cases.

The stakes in *Cutbank* were high. Justin Cutbank, already facing an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) enhancement due to his violent history, was convicted of possessing a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle following a harrowing domestic siege where he held his girlfriend hostage at gunpoint before barricading himself in a neighbor's garage. While the physical evidence—DNA matching Cutbank to the rifle found near his escape route—was strong, his defense hinged on excluding testimony from two victims who stated they had seen him with that specific rifle "a handful of times" prior to that fateful night. Cutbank argued this was classic propensity evidence: showing he liked guns so he must have owned this one. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), such evidence is generally barred because it invites jurors to convict based on bad character rather than specific acts.

The district court disagreed, classifying the testimony as "intrinsic," meaning it was part of the immediate story of the crime itself rather than external bad acts used solely to show disposition. The Eighth Circuit affirmed this characterization without hesitation, leaning heavily on its 2014 precedent in *United States v. Battle*. As Judge Loken wrote, "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession... because it helps establish ownership or control." This distinction is legally subtle but practically massive. If prior sightings are treated as separate "other acts," they trigger strict 404(b) scrutiny and require specific intent findings beyond mere possession. If they are intrinsic, they are admissible as direct proof that Cutbank maintained continuous dominion over the weapon leading up to its discovery by police.

What makes *Cutbank* particularly notable is how casually it dismisses the nuance usually required in these analyses. The opinion spends little time wrestling with whether those earlier sightings constituted distinct criminal episodes or merely background noise; instead, it treats them as integral threads in the tapestry of continuous possession. This approach reflects a pragmatic view common among circuit courts handling felon-in-possession charges: without proof of ongoing control between acquisition and seizure, many prosecutions might collapse if there is even a temporal gap between witnessing someone with a gun and finding it on them or nearby. By validating testimony that witnesses saw Cutbank with *that specific* sawed-off rifle repeatedly before November 2020, the court effectively allows juries to infer continuity from repeated observation rather than demanding forensic chains of custody for every moment in between.

Beyond Rule 404(b), *Cutbank* serves as a reminder of how deferential appellate courts remain toward trial judges managing evidentiary chaos during volatile trials involving domestic violence and armed resistance against police. The panel swept aside challenges regarding hearsay instructions and Confrontation Clause concerns with similar alacrity, noting that since the declarant testified live at trial, constitutional hurdles evaporated regardless of whether limiting instructions were given immediately upon admission versus at closing argument. Even more telling was the treatment of emotional testimony regarding why one witness initially struggled to identify details—the revelation that her sister had been killed by gunfire was deemed responsive to cross-examination tactics rather than unfairly prejudicial under Rule 403. The message here is clear: when dealing with defendants accused of holding families hostage at gunpoints while high on methamphetamine, trial courts retain broad latitude to let juries hear the full scope of human drama surrounding an alleged crime scene preparation phase.

However, *Cutbank*'s most enduring impact may lie in its reinforcement of sentencing enhancements alongside its evidentiary rulings. While much attention focuses on whether witnesses can talk about seeing guns beforehand, Cutbank also grappled with whether applying a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)—for possessing a firearm "in connection with" another felony—created double counting problems given ACCA status elsewhere in his file cut off below-the-line arguments about proportionality under § 3553(a). Ultimately rejecting these claims because sentences within guideline ranges carry presumptive reasonableness reinforces another trend: once statutory aggravators like ACCA kick in due to prior violent felonies, additional enhancements for contemporaneous conduct rarely succeed unless procedurally miscalculated outright—a rare occurrence indeed given modern sentencing practices emphasizing thorough presentence reports accepted without objection at hearings themselves anyway!

## Beyond ACAs: Navigating the Tension Between Mandatory Minimums and § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancements

When a defendant faces prosecution under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), the stakes are undeniably high. A conviction triggers a rigid statutory floor of fifteen years, effectively removing judicial discretion regarding the baseline length of incarceration. But does the imposition of a mandatory minimum render further sentencing enhancements superfluous? That was the precise question lurking beneath the surface of *United States v. Cutbank*, decided recently by the Eighth Circuit, where the court reaffirmed that statutory floors and guideline ceilings operate in different lanes—and that a judge remains free to stack aggravating factors even when a mandatory minimum is already in play.

The facts of *Cutbank* paint a grim picture typical of high-stakes federal prosecutions. Justin Cutbank, a career offender with an extensive history of violence, held his girlfriend hostage at gunpoint, threatened her life, struck her with a firearm, and subsequently barricaded himself in a neighbor's garage while assaulting responding officers. His conviction for being a felon in possession of a firearm was bolstered by findings that he qualified as an armed career criminal due to three prior violent felonies. However, Cutbank's appeal turned not on his guilt—which was supported by overwhelming physical and testimonial evidence—but on the arithmetic of his punishment. Specifically, he challenged the district court's application of a four-level upward adjustment under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm "in connection with" another felony offense.

Cutbank's argument was rooted in a theory of redundancy rather than innocence or procedural error. He conceded that ACCA mandated a minimum sentence but contended that layering the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement on top of that framework violated the core directive of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a): that a sentence must be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." His logic suggested that once Congress has dictated a severe mandatory minimum via ACCA, adding further weight to the guidelines calculation serves no purpose other than double-counting his culpability for similar underlying conduct—in this instance, domestic abuse and kidnapping intertwined with firearm possession.

Judge Loken's opinion for the Eighth Circuit swiftly dismantled this line of reasoning, offering a crisp reminder of how federal sentencing mechanics function. The court clarified that ACCA operates as a statutory constraint—a hard lower bound—while the Sentencing Guidelines serve as an advisory framework designed to account for specific nuances of criminal conduct. The fact that an enhancement pushes an advisory range higher does not conflict with § 3553(a); rather, it ensures that every distinct aspect of dangerous behavior is reflected in the final calculus before any variance occurs. As Judge Loken noted, when Cutbank possessed the sawed-off rifle while actively committing kidnapping and domestic assault, those acts constituted separate harms beyond mere status-based possession covered by ACCA's general mandate. To ignore them would be to fail to capture the full scope of his threat to public safety.

From a doctrinal standpoint, *Cutbank* reinforces a critical distinction often blurred in public discourse: statutory mandates versus guideline calculations remain distinct analytical steps in federal sentencing jurisprudence. The Supreme Court has long held (*e.g.*, *Gall*) that judges must start with an accurate Guidelines range before considering whether to vary downward based on § 3553(a) factors. By rejecting Cutbank's claim that overlapping statutes create impermissible duplication, the Eighth Circuit signaled that appellate courts will continue to scrutinize whether specific conduct justified specific enhancements regardless of whether those enhancements ultimately result in a sentence above or below statutory minima—or simply reinforce them within an advisory band. The court emphasized that because Cutbank received a sentence within his properly calculated advisory range (which incorporated both ACCA-triggered levels and the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) bump), there was no presumption against its reasonableness absent clear error in procedure or substance.

## Procedural Minimalism at Sentencing: What *Cutbank* Says About Judicial Consideration of § 3553(a)

The Supreme Court's decision in *Gall v. United States* established a foundational rule for federal sentencing: once a district court calculates the correct Guidelines range, it must "consider" the statutory factors listed in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) before imposing a sentence. Yet, the precise texture of that consideration remains a fertile ground for appellate litigation. How much does a judge need to say? Must they explicitly address every argument raised by defense counsel? Or is a general acknowledgment of the factors sufficient so long as the final sentence falls within the advisory range?

Last week, in *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit offered a stark answer that leans heavily toward efficiency over exposition. Affirming a 292-month sentence for a felon-in-possession conviction involving egregious conduct—domestic terror, kidnapping, and assaulting an officer—the court relied on emerging precedent to hold that when a defendant receives a within-Guidelines sentence, appellate courts should presume the district judge considered all relevant § 3553(a) factors absent explicit evidence to the contrary. While *Cutbank* presents facts where sympathy for the defendant is hard to muster, its reasoning signals a broader shift toward "procedural minimalism," raising uncomfortable questions about whether high-volume dockets are quietly eroding meaningful judicial engagement with individualized mitigation.

### The Facts: A Textbook Case for Severity

To understand the stakes of *Cutbank*, one must first appreciate the gravity of the underlying conduct. Justin Cutbank was not caught with an unloaded pistol in his pocket; he was convicted of holding his girlfriend at gunpoint in her own home for hours, forcing her into a closet while making paranoid delusions about listening devices, and subsequently barricading himself in a neighbor's garage while armed and intoxicated on methamphetamine. During his flight from police, he bit an officer and grabbed their service weapon. The evidence included DNA matching Cutbank to a sawed-off rifle found near the scene and testimony from witnesses who had seen him wield that specific weapon days earlier.

Given these facts—and his status as an Armed Career Criminal with three prior violent felonies—a severe sentence was virtually inevitable under any interpretation of the Guidelines. Indeed, the district court sentenced him to 292 months, sitting comfortably at the bottom of his advisory range of 292 to 365 months. On paper, this looks like an open-and-shut affirmation case. However, Cutbank's appeal turned on a subtle procedural claim: he argued that even though his sentence fell within the Guidelines range, it violated § 3553(a)'s requirement that punishment be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." Implicitly, he suggested that despite his horrific conduct and lengthy rap sheet, there were mitigating circumstances regarding his mental health or addiction history that required specific discussion before such a heavy term could be justified.

### The Presumption of Competence Without Recitation

Judge Loken's opinion rejects this notion with surgical precision, relying on two very recent Eighth Circuit decisions: *United States v. Masood* (decided just last month in this hypothetical timeline) and *United States v. Maluoth*. These cases reinforce a doctrine increasingly common among circuit courts: if the record shows the judge acknowledged receipt of sentencing memoranda and stated awareness of § 3553(a), they need not engage in a "mechanical recitation" of how those factors tipped the scales.

In *Cutbank*, Judge Loken writes plainly: "To uphold a sentence... it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors." Because Cutbank received a within-range sentence based on an accurate calculation—including enhancements for possessing a sawed-off weapon and committing another felony—the burden shifts entirely to him to prove otherwise. The court notes that Cutbank failed to show any indication that the judge ignored specific arguments; rather, silence on specific mitigators is interpreted as implicit rejection based on overwhelming aggravating evidence like domestic abduction and assault on law enforcement personnel.

This approach prioritizes finality and judicial economy. It recognizes that federal judges manage crushing caseloads where dictating twenty minutes of reasoning for every standard within-range sentence would grind proceedings to a halt. If every failure to mention drug addiction or childhood trauma automatically triggered remand workups for new presentence reports or resentencing hearings, system capacity would collapse under its own weight. From this perspective, *Cutbank* serves as a necessary check against defendants attempting to manufacture reversible error out of thin air simply because their lawyer didn't get five extra minutes of oral argument time dedicated solely to mitigation rhetoric.

### The Danger of Silent Judgments

However, while efficient, this posture risks normalizing what might best be termed "silent adjudication" at sentencing—a practice where critical constitutional values are left unspoken even when they matter most. Section 3553(a) exists precisely because Congress recognized that mathematical calculations alone cannot capture human complexity; judges are tasked with looking beyond numbers to see whether prison terms serve rehabilitation or deterrence better than probation or shorter confinement might have served those goals in rare cases.

When circuits adopt presumptions too readily favorable toward prosecutors—assuming everything went right whenever sentences land inside ranges—they inadvertently lower incentives for thorough deliberation below deck levels known only internally by trial judges themselves.
