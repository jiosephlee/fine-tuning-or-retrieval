Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## Intrinsic vs. Propensity: The Eighth Circuit's Broad Definition of 'Continuous Possession' in Firearm Cases

The line between permissible background context and impermissible character evidence is often the most contentious battlefield in federal criminal trials. In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, Judge Loken reinforced a robust, defendant-unfriendly interpretation of what constitutes "intrinsic" evidence in firearm possession cases. The court affirmed a conviction where witnesses testified that the defendant had been seen with the very same sawed-off rifle days or weeks before the charged incident—a fact the defense argued was classic propensity evidence barred by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). While the case involves a particularly egregious set of facts—including domestic terrorism, kidnapping, and an assault on law enforcement—the appellate reasoning offers a critical look at how lower courts are increasingly collapsing the distinction between "prior acts" and "elements of the crime."

The central evidentiary dispute in *Cutbank* hinged on whether testimony from the victim and a housemate regarding prior sightings of the rifle should have been excluded. The defense moved to suppress these statements as improper character evidence designed to show Cutbank's violent disposition. The district court disagreed, labeling the testimony as intrinsic to the charged offense of felon-in-possession. On appeal, Judge Loken agreed, leaning heavily on the circuit's precedent in *United States v. Battle*. The logic is straightforward yet expansive: prior possession of a specific weapon is not merely evidence of bad character; it is direct proof that the defendant maintained continuous control over that weapon up to the moment of arrest. Without establishing this timeline, prosecutors argue they cannot prove "knowing possession" beyond a reasonable doubt if there is a gap between when the defendant last handled the gun and when police recovered it.

This approach represents a significant narrowing of Rule 404(b)'s protective shield in firearm cases. Traditionally, Rule 404(b) serves as a gatekeeper, preventing prosecutors from introducing evidence of uncharged misconduct solely to suggest that because a defendant acted badly once, they likely acted badly again. However, by categorizing prior sightings of the specific instrumentality as "intrinsic," courts like those in the Eighth Circuit effectively bypass this gatekeeper entirely. If an act is deemed intrinsic—meaning it happened before trial but forms part of the same transaction or chain of events—it falls outside Rule 404(b)'s prohibition altogether. In *Cutbank*, this meant that telling jurors Cutbank had used this specific rifle on previous occasions was treated not as showing he was dangerous, but as showing he owned it when he pointed it at his girlfriend's face hours later.

The practical implication of this doctrine is profound for defendants facing felon-in-possession charges involving long gaps between incidents or ambiguous recovery timelines. Under *Battle* and now *Cutbank*, prosecutors are empowered to paint a detailed picture of a defendant's history with a specific weapon without fear that it will be labeled prejudicial propensity evidence. The court reasoned that excluding such testimony would make it nearly impossible to prove possession in cases where physical custody might fluctuate or where weapons are hidden away for periods before resurfacing during an escalation of violence. While this ensures juries receive full context regarding the continuity of criminal conduct, it risks eroding the fundamental premise that past behavior alone should not define present guilt unless directly tied to an element of the crime inextricably linked to possession itself.

Beyond evidentiary procedure, *Cutbank* also touched upon sentencing mechanics under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), rejecting Cutbank's argument that enhancing his sentence for possessing a firearm "in connection with" another felony (the domestic assault) conflicted with statutory sentencing goals under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Given that Cutbank was already subject to mandatory minimum enhancements under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) due to three prior violent felonies, he faced stacked penalties regardless of this guideline adjustment. Nevertheless, Judge Loken's rejection of his substantive reasonableness challenge underscores a broader judicial deference to district courts when imposing within-guidelines sentences for repeat offenders involved in complex multi-faceted crimes involving firearms and violence against family members and police officers alike.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* illustrates a continued trend in federal appellate jurisprudence: prioritizing prosecutorial efficiency and factual completeness over strict adherence to exclusionary rules designed to prevent unfair prejudice. By broadly defining "continuous possession" as an intrinsic element rather than separate misconduct, circuits like the Eighth are signaling that in firearm cases, context is king—even if that context paints a damning portrait of prior behavior that might otherwise be inadmissible elsewhere in federal practice. For defense counsel operating in these jurisdictions, challenging similar evidence will require more than just citing Rule 404(b); they must demonstrate why such testimony does not logically form part of the chain connecting prior control to current possession—a high bar indeed under current precedent.



## When Hearsay Meets Cross-Examination: Reconciling Officer Testimony with the Confrontation Clause

The tension between the need for efficient law enforcement narratives and a defendant's Sixth Amendment right to confrontation is a staple of federal criminal litigation. Yet, in *United States v. Cutbank*, decided by the Eighth Circuit on October 7, 2025, the court offered a crisp reaffirmation of a doctrine that often gets lost in the heat of trial: the "non-hearsay" use of out-of-court statements to explain police conduct remains permissible even when those statements implicate a defendant, provided the original declarant takes the stand. While Justin Cutbank's case involved a harrowing array of facts—including domestic terrorism, kidnapping, and resistance against arresting officers—his most nuanced legal challenge centered on a procedural maneuver that defense attorneys frequently deploy: demanding immediate limiting instructions when officers testify about what victims told them before trial.

Cutbank's conviction stemmed from a chaotic night in November 2020 where he, intoxicated and paranoid, held his girlfriend and her family hostage at gunpoint before fleeing to a nearby garage. During his trial, government agents recounted the initial calls they received from Cutbank's girlfriend and others upon arriving at the scene. These accounts included details about Cutbank brandishing weapons and making threats. Cutbank argued that admitting these officer testimonies violated Rule against Hearsay and his Confrontation Clause rights unless the judge issued an immediate instruction clarifying that the jury could not treat those statements as proof of truth until the victims themselves testified. The district court declined this request, noting that standard practice allows courts to address such nuances with a final instruction at the close of trial rather than piecemeal interruptions during testimony. The Eighth Circuit affirmed this approach without hesitation.

Judge Loken's opinion dissects this procedural dispute by anchoring it in established precedent regarding the Confrontation Clause. The court reiterated that the constitutional guarantee is satisfied not by excluding all out-of-court references, but by ensuring that any declarant whose statements are effectively placed before the jury is present for cross-examination. In *Cutbank*, both D.F., the primary victim, and C.F., a housemate, took the stand and testified extensively about their interactions with Cutbank and their observations of the weapon. Because they were subject to full cross-examination regarding their credibility, memory gaps (such as D.F.'s initial inability to recall specific gun details due to fear or trauma), and consistency with prior statements, the Confrontation Clause was not violated merely because officers relayed their initial reports earlier in testimony.

The court's rejection of Cutbank's demand for an immediate limiting instruction underscores a pragmatic view of federal trial management. As Judge Loken noted citing *United States v. Bennett*, district judges possess broad discretion to decide *when* to instruct juries on how to weigh evidence. The logic is straightforward: interrupting witness testimony with repetitive cautionary instructions can confuse jurors more than it protects them, especially when the ultimate resolution—that these statements are admissible only for context—is clarified in comprehensive closing instructions. This aspect of *Cutbank* serves as a reminder that appellate courts are generally reluctant to second-guess a trial judge's rhythm unless there is clear prejudice or confusion on the record. In this instance, because D.F.'s subsequent testimony corroborated the officers' accounts and addressed inconsistencies directly (explaining her prior silence or vagueness by referencing her trauma regarding gun violence), any theoretical risk of juror misuse was rendered moot by her live appearance in court.

Beyond its immediate application to evidentiary procedure, *Cutbank* carries significant strategic weight for defense counsel navigating complex domestic violence prosecutions where initial 911 calls or victim statements are critical to establishing probable cause or timeline context. Defense teams often seek early limiting instructions hoping to inoculate jurors against treating "hearsay" as fact before they hear from witnesses who might recant or soften their stories years down the line—a common dynamic in intimate partner violence cases where fear or economic dependence affects testimony reliability later on. However, *Cutbank* signals that absent extraordinary circumstances showing actual confusion or prejudice during deliberations, such tactical requests may be viewed as unnecessary friction rather than essential safeguards. The Eighth Circuit emphasized that if an error occurred—and here they found none—it would be deemed harmless given the overwhelming physical evidence (the sawed-off rifle with matching DNA) and independent corroboration by multiple witnesses.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* reinforces a settled but vital principle: The Confrontation Clause guarantees an opportunity for effective cross-examination, not necessarily an isolationist exclusion of background narrative provided by investigating officers so long as that narrative is later tested in open court. For practitioners managing high-stakes felon-in-possession trials involving ACAC enhancements like those triggered here under § 924(e), *Cutbank* suggests that resources might be better spent crafting rigorous cross-examinations of declarants once they take the stand rather than litigating preliminary motions for immediate curative instructions over officer testimony regarding initial victim accounts. The court's affirmation serves as both a validation of current prosecutorial practices and a cautionary note against relying on procedural technicalities when the substance of evidence overwhelmingly supports guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.



## The Overlap Problem: Layering Sentencing Enhancements Under the Guidelines

When a defendant faces prosecution under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), the statutory framework is rigid: a mandatory minimum sentence kicks in once three qualifying predicate offenses are established. But the advisory guidelines that sit alongside these statutes often present a more fluid puzzle, particularly where the factual predicates for a mandatory minimum overlap with conduct that might trigger additional sentencing enhancements. In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided October 7, 2025, the Eighth Circuit addressed a recurring tension in federal sentencing: does applying a four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm "in connection with" another felony violate the principles of proportionality when that "other felony" is already serving as an ACCA predicate? The court's answer is a resounding yes—the guidelines permit this layering, provided the district court properly weighs the totality of circumstances under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a).

The case arises from a harrowing night in November 2020, where Justin Cutbank, armed with a sawed-off rifle and under the influence of drugs, held his girlfriend and her family hostage before fleeing and barricading himself in a neighbor's garage. His conviction for felon in possession triggered ACCA due to his history of violent felonies, mandating a statutory floor. However, at sentencing, the district court also applied U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), adding four levels to his offense because he possessed the firearm "in connection with another felony offense"—specifically, the kidnapping and assault inherent in the hostage situation. Cutbank argued on appeal that this was impermissible double counting; since those same acts formed his ACCA predicates, using them again to boost his guidelines range rendered his sentence excessive and violated § 3553(a)'s mandate that punishment be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary."

Judge Loken's opinion for the Eighth Circuit rejects this argument by drawing a sharp distinction between statutory floors and guideline calculations. The court notes that while ACCA sets a hard minimum based on past convictions, the Guidelines operate differently. They are designed to capture specific aspects of culpability relevant to immediate danger and risk creation. Section 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) targets the *conduct* of combining firearms with other criminal activity—a dangerous synergy that increases societal risk regardless of whether that activity constitutes an ACCA predicate elsewhere in the calculation. As Judge Loken writes, "The Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies," meaning when multiple enhancement paths exist—one via ACCA-related adjustments and another via connection-to-felony adjustments—the system defaults to whichever yields the higher penalty level. This structure implies Congress intended for these calculations to be additive rather than mutually exclusive when they reflect distinct dimensions of criminal behavior.

Perhaps more critical than the mechanics of the calculation is how *Cutbank* reinforces judicial deference regarding substantive reasonableness once a correct guidelines range is established. Cutbank contended that even if technically permissible under the Guidelines manual, imposing an enhanced range based on overlapping facts resulted in an unreasonably high sentence relative to his culpability. The Eighth Circuit shut down this line of attack by reiterating that sentences within an advisory range carry a presumption of reasonableness on appeal. The court emphasized that it is not required for district judges to mechanically recite every § 3553(a) factor on record; it suffices if it is clear from context that they considered them. Given Cutbank's extensive criminal history—nearly a dozen prior felonies—and the egregious nature of holding victims at gunpoint in front of children while resisting arrest with violence against officers, the panel found no abuse of discretion in adopting the PSR's recommendation of 292 months.

Doctrinally, *Cutbank* offers little solace for defendants hoping to use ACCA predicates as a shield against related guideline enhancements. The decision underscores a pragmatic reality in federal sentencing: prosecutors need not choose between charging conduct as an ACCA predicate or as a basis for § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B); they can effectively utilize both tracks simultaneously within their respective frameworks so long as they adhere to procedural requirements like proper fact-finding and consideration of § 3553(a). For defense practitioners operating in circuits following similar logic to *Cutbank*, this suggests that challenges to "double counting" will likely fail unless there is evidence that the enhancement addresses facts already fully accounted for in a way that renders them redundant beyond mere overlap—such as using specific details already captured by a career offender designation where no separate conduct-based enhancement exists.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* serves as a reminder that while ACCA imposes statutory rigidity, the surrounding guidelines ecosystem remains flexible enough to stack enhancements based on complex factual scenarios involving firearms and violence. By affirming that distinct legal functions (statutory minima versus advisory risk assessment) allow for parallel consideration of similar facts, the Eighth Circuit ensures that defendants engaging in compound crimes involving weapons face sentences commensurate with their total threat profile—not just their historical record or their current possession alone separately viewed but synthesized into one comprehensive assessment of danger posed to society and law enforcement alike
