Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## Defining 'Intrinsic' Acts: The Eighth Circuit Tightens the Net Around Prior Possession Testimony

When a felon is caught with a firearm, the prosecution's task seems straightforward: prove the defendant is a convicted felon and prove they knowingly possessed the weapon. But in practice, especially when law enforcement does not catch the defendant red-handed or when the weapon is recovered days later, the "possession" element becomes a fragile link in the chain of custody. How does a prosecutor bridge the gap between a past sighting of a gun and its seizure today? In *United States v. Cutbank*, decided last week by the Eighth Circuit, Judge Loken offered a robust answer that significantly lowers the barrier for admitting testimony about prior sightings of weapons. By reaffirming that evidence of previous possession can be "intrinsic" to the charged offense rather than impermissible character evidence, the court has provided prosecutors with powerful tools—and defendants with fewer avenues to exclude prejudicial history—in complex firearm cases.

The stakes in *Cutbank* were high. Justin Cutbank, already facing an Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) enhancement due to his violent history, was convicted of possessing a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle following a harrowing domestic siege where he held his girlfriend hostage at gunpoint before barricading himself in a neighbor's garage. While the physical evidence—DNA matching Cutbank to the rifle found near his escape route—was strong, his defense hinged on excluding testimony from two victims who stated they had seen him with that specific rifle "a handful of times" prior to that fateful night. Cutbank argued this was classic propensity evidence: showing he liked guns so he must have owned this one. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), such evidence is generally barred because it invites jurors to convict based on bad character rather than specific acts.

The district court disagreed, classifying the testimony as "intrinsic," meaning it was part of the immediate story of the crime itself rather than external bad acts used solely to show disposition. The Eighth Circuit affirmed this characterization without hesitation, leaning heavily on its 2014 precedent in *United States v. Battle*. As Judge Loken wrote, "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession... because it helps establish ownership or control." This distinction is legally subtle but practically massive. If prior sightings are treated as separate "other acts," they trigger strict 404(b) scrutiny and require specific intent findings beyond mere possession. If they are intrinsic, they are admissible as direct proof that Cutbank maintained continuous dominion over the weapon leading up to its discovery by police.

What makes *Cutbank* particularly notable is how casually it dismisses the nuance usually required in these analyses. The opinion spends little time wrestling with whether those earlier sightings constituted distinct criminal episodes or merely background noise; instead, it treats them as integral threads in the tapestry of continuous possession. This approach reflects a pragmatic view common among circuit courts handling felon-in-possession charges: without proof of ongoing control between acquisition and seizure, many prosecutions might collapse if there is even a temporal gap between witnessing someone with a gun and finding it on them or nearby. By validating testimony that witnesses saw Cutbank with *that specific* sawed-off rifle repeatedly before November 2020, the court effectively allows juries to infer continuity from repeated observation rather than demanding forensic chains of custody for every moment in between.

Beyond Rule 404(b), *Cutbank* serves as a reminder of how deferential appellate courts remain toward trial judges managing evidentiary chaos during volatile trials involving domestic violence and armed resistance against police. The panel swept aside challenges regarding hearsay instructions and Confrontation Clause concerns with similar alacrity, noting that since the declarant testified live at trial, constitutional hurdles evaporated regardless of whether limiting instructions were given immediately upon admission versus at closing argument. Even more telling was the treatment of emotional testimony regarding why one witness initially struggled to identify details—the revelation that her sister had been killed by gunfire was deemed responsive to cross-examination tactics rather than unfairly prejudicial under Rule 403. The message here is clear: when dealing with defendants accused of holding families hostage at gunpoints while high on methamphetamine, trial courts retain broad latitude to let juries hear the full scope of human drama surrounding an alleged crime scene preparation phase.

However, *Cutbank*'s most enduring impact may lie in its reinforcement of sentencing enhancements alongside its evidentiary rulings. While much attention focuses on whether witnesses can talk about seeing guns beforehand, Cutbank also grappled with whether applying a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)—for possessing a firearm "in connection with" another felony—created double counting problems given ACCA status elsewhere in his file cut off below-the-line arguments about proportionality under § 3553(a). Ultimately rejecting these claims because sentences within guideline ranges carry presumptive reasonableness reinforces another trend: once statutory aggravators like ACCA kick in due to prior violent felonies, additional enhancements for contemporaneous conduct rarely succeed unless procedurally miscalculated outright—a rare occurrence indeed given modern sentencing practices emphasizing thorough presentence reports accepted without objection at hearings themselves anyway!
