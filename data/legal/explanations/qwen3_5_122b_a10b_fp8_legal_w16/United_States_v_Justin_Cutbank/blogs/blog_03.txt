Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## Procedural Minimalism at Sentencing: What *Cutbank* Says About Judicial Consideration of § 3553(a)

The Supreme Court's decision in *Gall v. United States* established a foundational rule for federal sentencing: once a district court calculates the correct Guidelines range, it must "consider" the statutory factors listed in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) before imposing a sentence. Yet, the precise texture of that consideration remains a fertile ground for appellate litigation. How much does a judge need to say? Must they explicitly address every argument raised by defense counsel? Or is a general acknowledgment of the factors sufficient so long as the final sentence falls within the advisory range?

Last week, in *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit offered a stark answer that leans heavily toward efficiency over exposition. Affirming a 292-month sentence for a felon-in-possession conviction involving egregious conduct—domestic terror, kidnapping, and assaulting an officer—the court relied on emerging precedent to hold that when a defendant receives a within-Guidelines sentence, appellate courts should presume the district judge considered all relevant § 3553(a) factors absent explicit evidence to the contrary. While *Cutbank* presents facts where sympathy for the defendant is hard to muster, its reasoning signals a broader shift toward "procedural minimalism," raising uncomfortable questions about whether high-volume dockets are quietly eroding meaningful judicial engagement with individualized mitigation.

### The Facts: A Textbook Case for Severity

To understand the stakes of *Cutbank*, one must first appreciate the gravity of the underlying conduct. Justin Cutbank was not caught with an unloaded pistol in his pocket; he was convicted of holding his girlfriend at gunpoint in her own home for hours, forcing her into a closet while making paranoid delusions about listening devices, and subsequently barricading himself in a neighbor's garage while armed and intoxicated on methamphetamine. During his flight from police, he bit an officer and grabbed their service weapon. The evidence included DNA matching Cutbank to a sawed-off rifle found near the scene and testimony from witnesses who had seen him wield that specific weapon days earlier.

Given these facts—and his status as an Armed Career Criminal with three prior violent felonies—a severe sentence was virtually inevitable under any interpretation of the Guidelines. Indeed, the district court sentenced him to 292 months, sitting comfortably at the bottom of his advisory range of 292 to 365 months. On paper, this looks like an open-and-shut affirmation case. However, Cutbank's appeal turned on a subtle procedural claim: he argued that even though his sentence fell within the Guidelines range, it violated § 3553(a)'s requirement that punishment be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." Implicitly, he suggested that despite his horrific conduct and lengthy rap sheet, there were mitigating circumstances regarding his mental health or addiction history that required specific discussion before such a heavy term could be justified.

### The Presumption of Competence Without Recitation

Judge Loken's opinion rejects this notion with surgical precision, relying on two very recent Eighth Circuit decisions: *United States v. Masood* (decided just last month in this hypothetical timeline) and *United States v. Maluoth*. These cases reinforce a doctrine increasingly common among circuit courts: if the record shows the judge acknowledged receipt of sentencing memoranda and stated awareness of § 3553(a), they need not engage in a "mechanical recitation" of how those factors tipped the scales.

In *Cutbank*, Judge Loken writes plainly: "To uphold a sentence... it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors." Because Cutbank received a within-range sentence based on an accurate calculation—including enhancements for possessing a sawed-off weapon and committing another felony—the burden shifts entirely to him to prove otherwise. The court notes that Cutbank failed to show any indication that the judge ignored specific arguments; rather, silence on specific mitigators is interpreted as implicit rejection based on overwhelming aggravating evidence like domestic abduction and assault on law enforcement personnel.

This approach prioritizes finality and judicial economy. It recognizes that federal judges manage crushing caseloads where dictating twenty minutes of reasoning for every standard within-range sentence would grind proceedings to a halt. If every failure to mention drug addiction or childhood trauma automatically triggered remand workups for new presentence reports or resentencing hearings, system capacity would collapse under its own weight. From this perspective, *Cutbank* serves as a necessary check against defendants attempting to manufacture reversible error out of thin air simply because their lawyer didn't get five extra minutes of oral argument time dedicated solely to mitigation rhetoric.

### The Danger of Silent Judgments

However, while efficient, this posture risks normalizing what might best be termed "silent adjudication" at sentencing—a practice where critical constitutional values are left unspoken even when they matter most. Section 3553(a) exists precisely because Congress recognized that mathematical calculations alone cannot capture human complexity; judges are tasked with looking beyond numbers to see whether prison terms serve rehabilitation or deterrence better than probation or shorter confinement might have served those goals in rare cases.

When circuits adopt presumptions too readily favorable toward prosecutors—assuming everything went right whenever sentences land inside ranges—they inadvertently lower incentives for thorough deliberation below deck levels known only internally by trial judges themselves.
