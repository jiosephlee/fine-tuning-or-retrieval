Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does the 'pendency' requirement for Exemption 7(A) interact with multi-year investigations involving immigration and potential terrorism charges?
Question:
I am confused about the timeline in this case. The underlying immigration proceedings started in 2002, and the FOIA litigation spans many years. The appellant argued that the passage of time made the claim that proceedings were 'pending or reasonably anticipated' vague and conclusory. How exactly does the D.C. Circuit reconcile the need for current pendency with investigations that span decades? Specifically, does the fact that Farahi is an Iranian national facing terrorism-related allegations automatically extend the 'reasonably anticipated' window indefinitely, or is there a specific factual threshold the agency must meet every few years to keep Exemption 7(A) alive?
Answer:
The D.C. Circuit's decision in *Farahi v. FBI* clarifies that the "pending or reasonably anticipated" requirement for Exemption 7(A) does not impose a rigid temporal limit on investigations, even those spanning decades, nor does it require the agency to meet a new, heightened factual threshold merely because time has passed. Instead, the court reconciles the passage of time through **contextual review** and **deference**, particularly where national security and counterterrorism are involved.

Here is how the opinion addresses your specific questions regarding multi-year investigations:

### 1. Time Does Not Automatically Invalidate Pendency
The court rejects the argument that the mere passage of time renders an assertion of "ongoing" proceedings vague or conclusory. While *CREW v. DOJ* warns against bare assertions, *Farahi* establishes that a declaration confirming an investigation remains open is sufficient **if viewed within the full context of the record**.

In this case, the appellant argued that the 2022 declaration by Agent Bender—which tersely stated investigations were "ongoing"—was insufficient on its own. The D.C. Circuit disagreed, noting that Bender's declaration was not intended to stand alone but served as an **update** to earlier, detailed declarations (by Agent Hardy) that had already established a robust nexus between Farahi and terrorism investigations. The court reasoned that once an investigation is properly shown to be active with specific details (as done in Hardy's ex parte declaration), a subsequent confirmation that "nothing has changed" satisfies the pendency requirement without needing to re-litigate the entire investigative scope every few years.

### 2. No Automatic Extension Based Solely on Nationality or Allegations
The opinion explicitly states that Farahi's status as an Iranian national facing terrorism allegations does **not** create an indefinite extension of Exemption 7(A) protections by operation of law. The protection persists only so long as there is a **"concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding."** (*CREW*, quoted at *Farahi* ¶ IV.B).

However, the court finds that in cases involving complex counterterrorism networks—such as those linking individuals to groups like Al Qaeda or designated Foreign Terrorist Organizations—the likelihood of a continuing investigation is inherently higher than in routine matters. The court notes:
> "The breadth of the FBI's underlying investigation also makes plausible its representation that the investigation remains ongoing."

This suggests that while nationality doesn't trigger automatic immunity from scrutiny, the **nature of the underlying facts** (e.g., links to multiple convicted terrorists, recruitment for banned organizations) supports a finding that a "reasonable anticipation" of future proceedings exists despite years passing. The agency must still demonstrate this connection via affidavit; they cannot rely solely on labels like "terrorist suspect."

### 3. The Role of Deference and Segregability
Two additional doctrinal points from *Farahi* reinforce why long-term investigations survive FOIA challenges:
*   **Deference:** The court emphasizes significant deference to the Executive Branch when national security is implicated (*Center for Nat'l Sec. Stud.*). If an agency asserts an investigation into terrorism is ongoing and explains how disclosure would tip off targets regarding the scope of inquiry, courts will generally accept this absent strong contrary evidence.
*   **Segregability:** Even if some records are old, if exempt information is "inextricably intertwined" with non-exempt information (or if redacting exempt portions leaves no meaningful information), the entire record may be withheld regardless of age (*Mead Data*). The district court found here that meaningful segregation was impossible given the density of sensitive intelligence within Farahi's file.

### Conclusion
To keep Exemption 7(A) alive over decades, an agency does not need to file fresh motions proving pendency every few years with entirely new facts. Rather, they must provide **updated affidavits confirming continuity**, interpreted alongside their original detailed submissions demonstrating a specific link to law enforcement duties and national security risks. As *Farahi* holds, where an investigation involves serious threats like terrorism and has been previously documented with specificity, a simple confirmation of its continued status suffices to satisfy Exemption 7(A)'s pendency requirement.

### What is the legal effect of a district court's prior finding that an agency 'seemingly' met its burden on Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In Part IV(B), the appellate court notes that when the district court first denied summary judgment, it stated the FBI had 'seemingly' met its burden regarding pending proceedings. Later, when reviewing the updated declarations, the appellate court uses this past finding to bolster its conclusion that the new, terser Bender declaration was sufficient. Does a preliminary judicial observation like 'seemingly met its burden' create a form of collateral estoppel or at least shift the evidentiary weight significantly in subsequent motions? Or is this just a rhetorical device to say 'we already believed you once'? I'm struggling to understand how a tentative lower-court finding cures a potentially deficient affidavit.
Answer:
No, the district court's use of the word "seemingly" does not create collateral estoppel, nor does it formally shift the evidentiary burden in a procedural sense. Instead, as the D.C. Circuit explains in *Farahi v. FBI*, that language served a specific contextual function: it allowed the appellate court to treat the subsequent, terser declaration not as a standalone proof of liability, but as a necessary **update** to an already-established factual foundation.

Under FOIA jurisprudence, specifically regarding Exemption 7(A), the agency always retains the burden of proving that enforcement proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated." A preliminary observation that an agency has "seemingly" met its burden is inherently tentative; it indicates that the evidence appeared sufficient at that moment but was insufficient to grant final summary judgment because of external factors—here, the need for updated declarations due to the passage of time and concerns about segregability.

The legal significance of this prior finding lies in how it frames the scope of the *new* evidence required. In Part IV(B), Judge Katsas clarifies that Farahi erred by analyzing Joseph Bender's updated declaration "in isolation." Because the district court had previously determined (even tentatively) that the FBI had established a nexus between the investigation and law enforcement duties via David Hardy's earlier detailed declarations, Bender's role was limited to confirming continuity. The appellate court reasoned that Bender's brief statement—"investigations remain ongoing"—was legally sufficient precisely because it operated as an addendum to Hardy's comprehensive record, rather than a fresh attempt to satisfy the exemption requirements from scratch.

Therefore, the prior finding did not cure a deficient affidavit through preclusion or presumption. Rather, it provided the **contextual backdrop** necessary for a short update to be deemed adequate under *CREW v. DOJ*. If Hardy's original declaration had been rejected entirely, Bender's terse update would likely have failed for being conclusory. Because Hardy's declaration was accepted as establishing the baseline reality of an ongoing investigation, Bender merely needed to attest that nothing had changed since then. The word "seemingly" reflects judicial caution regarding finality before updates were received, not a binding legal conclusion that permanently lowered the agency's burden for future filings.

### Can an agency satisfy segregability requirements solely through ex parte submissions without any public description of why redaction is impossible?
Question:
Under FOIA, agencies must release non-exempt portions of records. Here, Farahi challenges the segregability analysis because it relies heavily on an ex parte declaration (Bender's) that he never saw and couldn't contest effectively. The court says relying on ex parte materials is 'inherent' in national security cases. But doesn't this violate due process or FOIA's transparency goals if the plaintiff cannot even argue *against* specific redactions? What specific level of detail must be provided in the *public* version of a Vaughn Index or declaration to allow a plaintiff to meaningfully challenge an agency's claim that information is 'inextricably intertwined,' especially when thousands of pages are withheld?
Answer:
**Yes.** Under D.C. Circuit precedent, an agency may satisfy FOIA's segregability requirements primarily through *ex parte* submissions, even if the public record lacks a detailed explanation of why specific redactions are "impossible," provided the court independently reviews the *ex parte* material and finds it sufficient.

While FOIA generally requires agencies to provide a "Vaughn index" or similar public justification to allow for meaningful adversarial testing (*Vaughn v. Rosen*, 484 F.2d 820 (D.C. Cir. 1973)), the D.C. Circuit recognizes that this requirement yields when the nature of the exemption—particularly in national security or sensitive law enforcement contexts—makes public disclosure of the rationale itself harmful.

In *Farahi v. FBI*, the court explicitly rejected the argument that reliance on *ex parte* declarations violates due process or FOIA's transparency goals when challenging segregability claims. The court reasoned that:

1.  **The Problem is "Inherent":** The inability of a plaintiff to contest specific *ex parte* assertions is "inherent in those FOIA cases where... an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." *Id.* at 1067 (citing *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC*). If revealing *why* information is inseparable would reveal the exempt information itself, the agency cannot be forced to provide a more detailed public explanation than what is necessary to prove pendency of proceedings generally.
2.  **Judicial Review Suffices:** The safeguard against arbitrary withholding is not full public adversarial debate, but rather independent judicial review of the sealed submissions. In *Farahi*, the district court examined Bender's *ex parte* declaration and found it provided a "detailed justification" for why exempt material was not reasonably segregable. The appellate court deferred to this finding, noting that once a court has reviewed the *ex parte* material and determined it meets the statutory standard, no further public elaboration is mandated by FOIA or due process in this context.
3.  **Deference on Intertwined Material:** The court reaffirmed that records need not be released if non-exempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt ones (*Juarez v. DOJ*). Where an agency demonstrates via affidavit (even if sealed) that non-exempt information is scattered line-by-line throughout documents containing sensitive law enforcement details, releasing fragments would compromise the integrity of the investigation or reveal patterns of inquiry that Exemption 7(A) seeks to protect (*Mead Data Cent., Inc.*).

Therefore, while a plaintiff like Farahi desires a robust public record to challenge categorization and segregation, the D.C. Circuit holds that an agency satisfies its burden if its *ex parte* submissions convince the reviewing court that segregation would be futile or harmful, even absent a parallel public narrative explaining exactly which lines are untouchable and why.

### Does forfeiture occur if a plaintiff fails to specifically attack an agency's document categorization scheme in FOIA litigation?
Question:
In Part IV(C), Farahi objected generally that his documents were categorized too broadly but didn't explain *why* specific categories were overbroad. The court ruled he forfeited this argument under *Khine*. Is this a harsh application of waiver rules? Usually, courts require agencies to justify categories with specificity (*Gallant*). If an agency defines categories like 'Terrorism Related Intelligence,' isn't it up to them to prove those aren't overbroad? Why does the burden shift entirely to Farahi to disprove broad categories rather than staying on the FBI to define them narrowly enough for judicial review?
Answer:
No, this is not a harsh application of waiver rules; rather, it reflects the D.C. Circuit's consistent requirement that a FOIA plaintiff must meaningfully engage with the agency's categorical justifications to preserve an appeal. While you are correct that agencies bear the ultimate burden of proving exemptions apply and must define categories with "reasonable specificity" (*Gallant v. NLRB*), this burden does not absolve a litigant of the duty to articulate *how* those categories fail to meet that standard.

**The Legal Standard: Specificity vs. General Objection**
Under *Gallant*, an agency cannot withhold documents using "boilerplate" descriptions; its categories must be "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied." However, this creates a two-step process:
1.  **Agency Burden:** Define categories with sufficient particularity.
2.  **Plaintiff Duty:** Identify specific flaws in those definitions if they believe them to be overbroad or vague.

As the opinion notes in Part IV(C), citing *Khine v. DHS*, a plaintiff who offers only a "general objection" that categories are too broad—without explaining *why* specific categories are defective—has failed to develop an argument capable of judicial review. The court explicitly states: "Farahi provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." Consequently, the court deemed the argument forfeited.

**Why the Burden Shifts (Procedurally)**
You ask why the burden shifts to Farahi to disprove broad categories rather than staying on the FBI. The distinction lies between the **burden of production/proving validity** and the **preservation of error**.

*   **Initial Burden:** The FBI satisfied its initial burden by submitting declarations defining its categories (e.g., relating to national security and terrorism investigations) and explaining their nexus to law enforcement duties (Part IV(A)-(B)). The court found these declarations provided "considerable detail" regarding the nature of the investigation.
*   **Preservation Requirement:** Once an agency presents a facially adequate categorical framework, a plaintiff cannot simply assert "these are too broad" and expect reversal on appeal without pointing out exactly where the framework collapses. If Farahi believed a category like "Terrorism Related Intelligence" was overinclusive, he needed to argue, for example, that it swept in purely historical data unrelated to pending proceedings or conflated distinct types of intelligence in a way that prevented segregation analysis.

By failing to make these specific attacks below, Farahi deprived both the district court and this appellate court of a factual record necessary to evaluate his claim. As Judge Katsas writes, allowing general objections would permit plaintiffs to bypass rigorous review simply by waiting until appeal to raise new theories unsupported by the trial record.

**Conclusion**
The ruling aligns with established D.C. Circuit precedent (*Khine*) requiring litigants to provide specific reasoning when challenging agency categorizations. The FBI met its obligation under *Gallant* by providing detailed declarations linking its categories to ongoing investigations involving national security threats (including links to Al Qaeda and foreign terrorist organizations). Because Farahi offered only a generalized complaint without identifying specific deficiencies in how those categories were constructed or applied, he forfeited his right to challenge them on appeal. This ensures that FOIA litigation focuses on concrete disputes rather than abstract assertions of overbreadth.

### How does deference differ between general law enforcement investigations and those implicating national security under Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The opinion states twice that deference is 'particularly appropriate' when national security concerns are involved (citing *Campbell* and *CNSS*). However, it also cites *Pratt* and *Clemente* regarding general law enforcement nexus tests. Practically speaking, does this mean the standard for proving an investigation exists is lower for terrorism cases than for, say, white-collar fraud? If so, what constitutes enough evidence in a terrorism case versus a non-terrorism case before a judge will accept an affidavit as non-conclusory? I want to know if there's actually two different standards being applied here.
Answer:
No, the opinion does not establish two different legal standards for proving the existence of an investigation depending on whether the case involves national security or general law enforcement. Rather, it clarifies that while the **legal test** remains uniform, the **degree of judicial deference** afforded to the agency's assertions varies based on the sensitivity of the subject matter.

Under Exemption 7(A), the government must always satisfy a single, unified standard: it must demonstrate that records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" and that their release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." As the *Farahi* opinion reiterates in Part IV(A), even in national security contexts, the FBI's declaration must still establish a "rational 'nexus'" between the investigation and its law enforcement duties, citing *Pratt* and *Clemente*. There is no separate, lowered threshold for terrorism cases where an investigation need not be substantiated; the requirement for a factual connection remains constant.

However, as noted in Part III and applied in Part IV(B), courts apply a heightened level of **deference** when national security is implicated. The opinion explicitly states that while agency decisions generally warrant a "fair measure of deference," such deference is "**particularly appropriate**" when national security concerns are involved (*Campbell*; *CNSS*). This distinction affects how a judge evaluates whether an affidavit is "conclusory," not what elements must be proven.

In practice, this means:
1.  **General Law Enforcement:** Courts scrutinize agency affidavits closely to ensure they provide sufficient detail to prove a concrete link between an individual/incident and a potential violation of law (*Pratt*). A vague assertion that an investigation is "ongoing" may fail if unsupported by specific facts showing why disclosure would harm proceedings (*CREW*).
2.  **National Security:** While the agency must still provide a rational nexus, courts give greater weight to agency expertise regarding threats and investigative methods. In *Farahi*, the court acknowledged that Acting Section Chief Bender's declaration was "surprisingly terse" on its own. Yet, because it was read in conjunction with detailed ex parte declarations regarding terrorism connections (recruiting for foreign terrorist organizations, links to Al Qaeda), and given the sensitive nature of counterterrorism work, the court found the government met its burden. The court reasoned that context—the breadth of allegations involving designated terrorist groups—made it plausible that proceedings remained active, accepting the update as non-conclusory due to this deferential lens.

Therefore, there are not two different standards for *what* constitutes proof of an investigation. Instead, there is one standard where national security cases benefit from a presumption of correctness regarding the necessity and continuity of investigations, allowing agencies to rely more heavily on specialized knowledge and less on granular public disclosure than might be required in routine white-collar fraud cases. The evidence required is still specific enough to show a rational nexus, but the court is more willing to accept agency representations about ongoing risks in terrorism cases without demanding exhaustive public detail.

### Is it permissible for an agency to submit conflicting or evolving declarations across multiple summary judgment motions without explaining discrepancies?
Question:
The FBI submitted declarations from Hardy in 2019/2020 and then from Bender in 2023. The court acknowledges Bender's declaration was 'surprisingly terse' compared to Hardy's detailed ex parte declaration but treats them as consistent updates rather than contradictions. If Bender had said something slightly different about the status of proceedings compared to Hardy three years earlier, would that have been fatal? How do courts handle situations where agencies update declarations years later—do they look at them holistically as part of one continuous narrative, or do they scrutinize each new affidavit independently for internal consistency?
Answer:
Based on the D.C. Circuit's opinion in *Farahi v. FBI*, the short answer is **no**: slight differences or "terse" language in a subsequent declaration are not automatically fatal, provided the court views the new declaration within the full context of the litigation history. Courts generally analyze agency declarations **holistically** rather than scrutinizing each affidavit in strict isolation, especially when the later filing serves as an update to an earlier, more detailed record.

Here is how the court applied this standard in *Farahi*:

### 1. Holistic Review vs. Isolated Scrutiny
The appellant argued that Joseph Bender's 2023 declaration was "surprisingly terse" and insufficient on its own to prove that enforcement proceedings were still pending, citing *CREW* regarding vague assertions. The court explicitly rejected analyzing Bender's declaration in isolation. Instead, it looked at the **"context"** of the entire administrative record.

The court reasoned that Bender's declaration was not intended to re-establish the pendency of proceedings from scratch (which would require high specificity). Rather, it functioned as a procedural update confirming that "nothing had changed" since David Hardy's detailed 2019/2020 declarations. As Judge Katsas wrote: *"The Bender declaration can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document."* Because Hardy had previously provided "considerable detail" about the investigation's scope and nature, Bender's brevity did not create a fatal gap in the agency's proof.

### 2. Deference and Reasonableness
Under Exemption 7(A), courts afford agencies deference regarding their law enforcement judgments (*Campbell*). The key inquiry is whether the agency has shown that disclosure could *reasonably* be expected to interfere with proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated (*CREW*).

If Bender had stated something slightly different about the status of proceedings compared to Hardy three years prior, it would likely only be fatal if those differences undermined the **rational nexus** between the investigation and law enforcement duties or suggested that no concrete proceeding existed anymore. In *Farahi*, even though Bender was brief, his confirmation that investigations remained ongoing was bolstered by:
*   The foundational facts laid out in Hardy's ex parte declaration;
*   Public concessions made by Farahi regarding his links to terrorist organizations; and
*   The logical plausibility that a complex counterterrorism investigation involving high-level figures (like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed associates) would naturally span many years.

### 3. Practical Takeaway for Law Students
The opinion illustrates that while agencies cannot rely solely on conclusory statements (as warned in *CREW*), they are permitted to submit streamlined updates years later without restating every factual predicate established in prior filings.

However, this does not mean agencies can ignore genuine contradictions entirely. If a new declaration actively contradicted a previous one—for example, stating an investigation was closed when Hardy said it was open—the court would likely demand an explanation for the discrepancy before granting deference. In *Farahi*, because there was no contradiction, only a difference in detail level attributable to procedural timing ("update"), the holistic approach saved the agency's motion for summary judgment.

**Conclusion:** Courts treat sequential declarations as parts of a continuous narrative if they serve to update rather than replace prior evidence. Slight variations or terseness are permissible so long as they do not contradict established facts or render the claim of "pending proceedings" implausible when viewed alongside earlier, more detailed submissions.
