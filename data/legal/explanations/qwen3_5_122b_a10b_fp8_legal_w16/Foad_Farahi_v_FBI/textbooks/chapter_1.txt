Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

## Factual Context: Farahi's Immigration Proceedings and Terrorism Allegations
To understand the stakes of *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, one must first grasp the gravity of the underlying circumstances surrounding the appellant. Foad Farahi, an Iranian national residing in the United States since 1993, became the subject of intense scrutiny beginning in 2002 when the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him for a visa overstay. These immigration proceedings were not merely administrative disputes regarding status; they involved serious allegations concerning national security. During these hearings, DHS opposed Farahi's applications for asylum and other forms of relief by presenting evidence that included live testimony from an FBI agent. This agent testified that the Bureau had documented "connections to high-level terrorists." Specifically, public records from parallel litigation reveal that the FBI alleged Farahi acted as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, maintained connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and other Al Qaeda members, linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists including Jose Padilla, and supported charities accused of financing terrorism. These allegations formed the backdrop against which Farahi subsequently sought access to his own FBI file via the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA).

## The Initial FOIA Request and Agency Response Delays
Motivated by these serious accusations made during his removal proceedings, Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. The sheer volume of responsive material was substantial; the FBI located approximately 10,750 pages of documents and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive information. However, before producing these records, the agency failed to meet its statutory deadlines for response, prompting Farahi to initiate suit in federal district court. Following this lawsuit, the agency engaged in a multi-year process of reviewing the responsive records. While they disclosed some materials to Farahi during this period, they ultimately withheld the majority of the documents. In October 2019, having completed its initial review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in favor of withholding most of the file. To support this motion, the agency submitted a declaration from David Hardy, then Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that releasing them would interfere with enforcement proceedings that were either pending or reasonably anticipated at that time. Notably, recognizing the sensitive nature of this information, both parties agreed—without opposition from Farahi—to allow the FBI to file a supplemental version of Hardy's declaration under seal and *ex parte*.

## Procedural Posture: Summary Judgment Motions and Stale Declarations
The procedural journey took a pivotal turn when the district court addressed the government's initial motion for summary judgment in November 2022. By this point, nearly three years had passed since the FBI filed its motion based on Hardy's original assertions. Although the district court initially found that Hardly seemed to have met his burden regarding law enforcement purposes and potential interference with proceedings, it expressed significant concern regarding "stale" declarations. Given the substantial passage of time since 2019, it was unclear whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated as originally claimed. Furthermore, while reviewing Hyde's declaration regarding segregability—the requirement that agencies release non-exempt portions of documents—the court determined it lacked sufficient information to make a ruling on public record without compromising sensitive details. Consequently, rather than granting immediate summary judgment or dismissing the case outright on these grounds alone, the district court denied summary judgment without prejudice. Instead, it issued a critical directive ordering the FBI to update its assessments regarding both the current status of any enforcement proceedings and its ability to segregate non-exempt information from exempt material within their massive cache of records.
