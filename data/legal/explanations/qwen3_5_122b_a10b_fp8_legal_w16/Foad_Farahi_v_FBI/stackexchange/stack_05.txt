Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does deference differ between general law enforcement investigations and those implicating national security under Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The opinion states twice that deference is 'particularly appropriate' when national security concerns are involved (citing *Campbell* and *CNSS*). However, it also cites *Pratt* and *Clemente* regarding general law enforcement nexus tests. Practically speaking, does this mean the standard for proving an investigation exists is lower for terrorism cases than for, say, white-collar fraud? If so, what constitutes enough evidence in a terrorism case versus a non-terrorism case before a judge will accept an affidavit as non-conclusory? I want to know if there's actually two different standards being applied here.
Answer:
No, the opinion does not establish two different legal standards for proving the existence of an investigation depending on whether the case involves national security or general law enforcement. Rather, it clarifies that while the **legal test** remains uniform, the **degree of judicial deference** afforded to the agency's assertions varies based on the sensitivity of the subject matter.

Under Exemption 7(A), the government must always satisfy a single, unified standard: it must demonstrate that records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" and that their release "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings" that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." As the *Farahi* opinion reiterates in Part IV(A), even in national security contexts, the FBI's declaration must still establish a "rational 'nexus'" between the investigation and its law enforcement duties, citing *Pratt* and *Clemente*. There is no separate, lowered threshold for terrorism cases where an investigation need not be substantiated; the requirement for a factual connection remains constant.

However, as noted in Part III and applied in Part IV(B), courts apply a heightened level of **deference** when national security is implicated. The opinion explicitly states that while agency decisions generally warrant a "fair measure of deference," such deference is "**particularly appropriate**" when national security concerns are involved (*Campbell*; *CNSS*). This distinction affects how a judge evaluates whether an affidavit is "conclusory," not what elements must be proven.

In practice, this means:
1.  **General Law Enforcement:** Courts scrutinize agency affidavits closely to ensure they provide sufficient detail to prove a concrete link between an individual/incident and a potential violation of law (*Pratt*). A vague assertion that an investigation is "ongoing" may fail if unsupported by specific facts showing why disclosure would harm proceedings (*CREW*).
2.  **National Security:** While the agency must still provide a rational nexus, courts give greater weight to agency expertise regarding threats and investigative methods. In *Farahi*, the court acknowledged that Acting Section Chief Bender's declaration was "surprisingly terse" on its own. Yet, because it was read in conjunction with detailed ex parte declarations regarding terrorism connections (recruiting for foreign terrorist organizations, links to Al Qaeda), and given the sensitive nature of counterterrorism work, the court found the government met its burden. The court reasoned that context—the breadth of allegations involving designated terrorist groups—made it plausible that proceedings remained active, accepting the update as non-conclusory due to this deferential lens.

Therefore, there are not two different standards for *what* constitutes proof of an investigation. Instead, there is one standard where national security cases benefit from a presumption of correctness regarding the necessity and continuity of investigations, allowing agencies to rely more heavily on specialized knowledge and less on granular public disclosure than might be required in routine white-collar fraud cases. The evidence required is still specific enough to show a rational nexus, but the court is more willing to accept agency representations about ongoing risks in terrorism cases without demanding exhaustive public detail.
