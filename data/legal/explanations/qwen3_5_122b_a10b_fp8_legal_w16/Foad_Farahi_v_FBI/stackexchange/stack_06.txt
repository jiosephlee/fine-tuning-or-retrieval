Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Is it permissible for an agency to submit conflicting or evolving declarations across multiple summary judgment motions without explaining discrepancies?
Question:
The FBI submitted declarations from Hardy in 2019/2020 and then from Bender in 2023. The court acknowledges Bender's declaration was 'surprisingly terse' compared to Hardy's detailed ex parte declaration but treats them as consistent updates rather than contradictions. If Bender had said something slightly different about the status of proceedings compared to Hardy three years earlier, would that have been fatal? How do courts handle situations where agencies update declarations years later—do they look at them holistically as part of one continuous narrative, or do they scrutinize each new affidavit independently for internal consistency?
Answer:
Based on the D.C. Circuit's opinion in *Farahi v. FBI*, the short answer is **no**: slight differences or "terse" language in a subsequent declaration are not automatically fatal, provided the court views the new declaration within the full context of the litigation history. Courts generally analyze agency declarations **holistically** rather than scrutinizing each affidavit in strict isolation, especially when the later filing serves as an update to an earlier, more detailed record.

Here is how the court applied this standard in *Farahi*:

### 1. Holistic Review vs. Isolated Scrutiny
The appellant argued that Joseph Bender's 2023 declaration was "surprisingly terse" and insufficient on its own to prove that enforcement proceedings were still pending, citing *CREW* regarding vague assertions. The court explicitly rejected analyzing Bender's declaration in isolation. Instead, it looked at the **"context"** of the entire administrative record.

The court reasoned that Bender's declaration was not intended to re-establish the pendency of proceedings from scratch (which would require high specificity). Rather, it functioned as a procedural update confirming that "nothing had changed" since David Hardy's detailed 2019/2020 declarations. As Judge Katsas wrote: *"The Bender declaration can reasonably be understood as an update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document."* Because Hardy had previously provided "considerable detail" about the investigation's scope and nature, Bender's brevity did not create a fatal gap in the agency's proof.

### 2. Deference and Reasonableness
Under Exemption 7(A), courts afford agencies deference regarding their law enforcement judgments (*Campbell*). The key inquiry is whether the agency has shown that disclosure could *reasonably* be expected to interfere with proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated (*CREW*).

If Bender had stated something slightly different about the status of proceedings compared to Hardy three years prior, it would likely only be fatal if those differences undermined the **rational nexus** between the investigation and law enforcement duties or suggested that no concrete proceeding existed anymore. In *Farahi*, even though Bender was brief, his confirmation that investigations remained ongoing was bolstered by:
*   The foundational facts laid out in Hardy's ex parte declaration;
*   Public concessions made by Farahi regarding his links to terrorist organizations; and
*   The logical plausibility that a complex counterterrorism investigation involving high-level figures (like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed associates) would naturally span many years.

### 3. Practical Takeaway for Law Students
The opinion illustrates that while agencies cannot rely solely on conclusory statements (as warned in *CREW*), they are permitted to submit streamlined updates years later without restating every factual predicate established in prior filings.

However, this does not mean agencies can ignore genuine contradictions entirely. If a new declaration actively contradicted a previous one—for example, stating an investigation was closed when Hardy said it was open—the court would likely demand an explanation for the discrepancy before granting deference. In *Farahi*, because there was no contradiction, only a difference in detail level attributable to procedural timing ("update"), the holistic approach saved the agency's motion for summary judgment.

**Conclusion:** Courts treat sequential declarations as parts of a continuous narrative if they serve to update rather than replace prior evidence. Slight variations or terseness are permissible so long as they do not contradict established facts or render the claim of "pending proceedings" implausible when viewed alongside earlier, more detailed submissions.
