Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the legal effect of a district court's prior finding that an agency 'seemingly' met its burden on Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In Part IV(B), the appellate court notes that when the district court first denied summary judgment, it stated the FBI had 'seemingly' met its burden regarding pending proceedings. Later, when reviewing the updated declarations, the appellate court uses this past finding to bolster its conclusion that the new, terser Bender declaration was sufficient. Does a preliminary judicial observation like 'seemingly met its burden' create a form of collateral estoppel or at least shift the evidentiary weight significantly in subsequent motions? Or is this just a rhetorical device to say 'we already believed you once'? I'm struggling to understand how a tentative lower-court finding cures a potentially deficient affidavit.
Answer:
No, the district court's use of the word "seemingly" does not create collateral estoppel, nor does it formally shift the evidentiary burden in a procedural sense. Instead, as the D.C. Circuit explains in *Farahi v. FBI*, that language served a specific contextual function: it allowed the appellate court to treat the subsequent, terser declaration not as a standalone proof of liability, but as a necessary **update** to an already-established factual foundation.

Under FOIA jurisprudence, specifically regarding Exemption 7(A), the agency always retains the burden of proving that enforcement proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated." A preliminary observation that an agency has "seemingly" met its burden is inherently tentative; it indicates that the evidence appeared sufficient at that moment but was insufficient to grant final summary judgment because of external factors—here, the need for updated declarations due to the passage of time and concerns about segregability.

The legal significance of this prior finding lies in how it frames the scope of the *new* evidence required. In Part IV(B), Judge Katsas clarifies that Farahi erred by analyzing Joseph Bender's updated declaration "in isolation." Because the district court had previously determined (even tentatively) that the FBI had established a nexus between the investigation and law enforcement duties via David Hardy's earlier detailed declarations, Bender's role was limited to confirming continuity. The appellate court reasoned that Bender's brief statement—"investigations remain ongoing"—was legally sufficient precisely because it operated as an addendum to Hardy's comprehensive record, rather than a fresh attempt to satisfy the exemption requirements from scratch.

Therefore, the prior finding did not cure a deficient affidavit through preclusion or presumption. Rather, it provided the **contextual backdrop** necessary for a short update to be deemed adequate under *CREW v. DOJ*. If Hardy's original declaration had been rejected entirely, Bender's terse update would likely have failed for being conclusory. Because Hardy's declaration was accepted as establishing the baseline reality of an ongoing investigation, Bender merely needed to attest that nothing had changed since then. The word "seemingly" reflects judicial caution regarding finality before updates were received, not a binding legal conclusion that permanently lowered the agency's burden for future filings.
