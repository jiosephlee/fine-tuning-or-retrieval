Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Can an agency satisfy segregability requirements solely through ex parte submissions without any public description of why redaction is impossible?
Question:
Under FOIA, agencies must release non-exempt portions of records. Here, Farahi challenges the segregability analysis because it relies heavily on an ex parte declaration (Bender's) that he never saw and couldn't contest effectively. The court says relying on ex parte materials is 'inherent' in national security cases. But doesn't this violate due process or FOIA's transparency goals if the plaintiff cannot even argue *against* specific redactions? What specific level of detail must be provided in the *public* version of a Vaughn Index or declaration to allow a plaintiff to meaningfully challenge an agency's claim that information is 'inextricably intertwined,' especially when thousands of pages are withheld?
Answer:
**Yes.** Under D.C. Circuit precedent, an agency may satisfy FOIA's segregability requirements primarily through *ex parte* submissions, even if the public record lacks a detailed explanation of why specific redactions are "impossible," provided the court independently reviews the *ex parte* material and finds it sufficient.

While FOIA generally requires agencies to provide a "Vaughn index" or similar public justification to allow for meaningful adversarial testing (*Vaughn v. Rosen*, 484 F.2d 820 (D.C. Cir. 1973)), the D.C. Circuit recognizes that this requirement yields when the nature of the exemption—particularly in national security or sensitive law enforcement contexts—makes public disclosure of the rationale itself harmful.

In *Farahi v. FBI*, the court explicitly rejected the argument that reliance on *ex parte* declarations violates due process or FOIA's transparency goals when challenging segregability claims. The court reasoned that:

1.  **The Problem is "Inherent":** The inability of a plaintiff to contest specific *ex parte* assertions is "inherent in those FOIA cases where... an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." *Id.* at 1067 (citing *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC*). If revealing *why* information is inseparable would reveal the exempt information itself, the agency cannot be forced to provide a more detailed public explanation than what is necessary to prove pendency of proceedings generally.
2.  **Judicial Review Suffices:** The safeguard against arbitrary withholding is not full public adversarial debate, but rather independent judicial review of the sealed submissions. In *Farahi*, the district court examined Bender's *ex parte* declaration and found it provided a "detailed justification" for why exempt material was not reasonably segregable. The appellate court deferred to this finding, noting that once a court has reviewed the *ex parte* material and determined it meets the statutory standard, no further public elaboration is mandated by FOIA or due process in this context.
3.  **Deference on Intertwined Material:** The court reaffirmed that records need not be released if non-exempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt ones (*Juarez v. DOJ*). Where an agency demonstrates via affidavit (even if sealed) that non-exempt information is scattered line-by-line throughout documents containing sensitive law enforcement details, releasing fragments would compromise the integrity of the investigation or reveal patterns of inquiry that Exemption 7(A) seeks to protect (*Mead Data Cent., Inc.*).

Therefore, while a plaintiff like Farahi desires a robust public record to challenge categorization and segregation, the D.C. Circuit holds that an agency satisfies its burden if its *ex parte* submissions convince the reviewing court that segregation would be futile or harmful, even absent a parallel public narrative explaining exactly which lines are untouchable and why.
