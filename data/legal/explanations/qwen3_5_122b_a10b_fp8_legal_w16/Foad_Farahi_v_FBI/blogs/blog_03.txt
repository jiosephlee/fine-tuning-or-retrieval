Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

## 'Interspersed Line-by-Line': Clarifying the High Bar for Reasonable Segregability Under FOIA

The Freedom of Information Act operates on a simple premise: the public has a right to know what its government is doing, unless a specific statutory exception applies. Yet, in practice, the most contentious battles are rarely fought over whether an exemption exists, but rather over what survives after the exemption is applied. This is the doctrine of "segregability." Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b), agencies must release any "reasonably segregable" portion of a record once exempt material is redacted. While this requirement sounds straightforward, it collapses under the weight of complex national security investigations. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided September 30, 2025, the D.C. Circuit offered a crisp reminder of just how high the bar is for forcing agencies to carve out non-exempt slivers from a tapestry of classified intelligence. By reaffirming that information "interspersed line-by-line" need not be released if doing so destroys the integrity of the whole, Judge Katsas's opinion reinforces a functional approach to FOIA that prioritizes operational security over piecemeal disclosure.

The facts of *Farahi* illustrate why segregability is such a fraught issue in counterterrorism contexts. Foad Farahi, an Iranian national facing removal proceedings in which DHS alleged he had ties to high-level terrorists and groups like Al Qaeda, sought his FBI file via FOIA. The FBI produced roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs before withholding nearly everything under Exemption 7(A), which shields records whose release could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated law enforcement proceedings. The core dispute on appeal wasn't whether an investigation existed—the court deferred to the FBI's detailed ex parte declarations confirming ongoing probes into Farahi's associations with designated foreign terrorist organizations—but whether anything could be salvaged from those thousands of pages. Farahi argued that among such a massive volume of records, there must be some trivial or administrative information that could be severed and released without harm. The district court disagreed, and the D.C. Circuit agreed entirely.

Judge Katsas's reasoning rests on a foundational principle established decades ago in *Mead Data Central*: FOIA protects "information," not merely "words which the Government has written down." The appellate court noted that when non-exempt material is randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout a document—common in intelligence reports where every sentence relies on unredacted context—it becomes functionally inseparable from the exempt content. Releasing isolated phrases or sentences risks revealing patterns, sources, or methodologies that would otherwise remain hidden if viewed only as part of the larger narrative. As the court observed in *Farahi*, allowing an applicant to cherry-pick disjointed fragments from a dense network of intelligence would effectively defeat the purpose of Exemption 7(A). If releasing ten percent of a document reveals enough about an ongoing investigation to tip off targets or compromise sources, then none of it should be released at all.

This holding serves as a necessary correction to litigants who view segregability as a mathematical exercise rather than a contextual one. Farahi's argument—that sheer volume implies some releasable content—assumes that documents are static repositories of independent facts rather than dynamic tools of investigation. In modern counterterrorism files, data points are deeply interconnected; removing one piece often alters the meaning or safety profile of another. The court rightly recognized that demanding agencies perform surgical extractions on such materials imposes an unreasonable burden and creates new security vulnerabilities in the process. By citing *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. VA*, Judge Katsas emphasized that courts must look at whether meaningful information can actually be extracted without compromising exempt interests, not whether any string of characters technically qualifies as non-exempt in isolation.

The decision also sheds light on the procedural mechanics that make these determinations possible: ex parte submissions. A significant portion of *Farahi*'s analysis hinged on sealed declarations submitted by FBI agents detailing exactly why certain redactions were necessary and why further segregation was impossible because it would reveal sensitive techniques or sources. While this inevitably handicaps plaintiffs who cannot see their opponent's full evidence base, the court affirmed that this trade-off is inherent to national security litigation under FOIA. Without ex parte review, courts would lack the necessary insight to verify agency claims about how tightly woven exempt and non-exempt information truly are in complex files like Farahi's 10,750-page dossier.

Ultimately, *Farahi* clarifies that Exemption 7(A) does more than just hide names; it protects the structural integrity of law enforcement files themselves. As long as an agency can demonstrate with reasonable specificity that separating exempt from non-exempt material would result in either revealing protected information or rendering remaining text meaningless or dangerous to release, they have satisfied their duty under § 552(b). For litigants hoping to pry open closed files by arguing for incremental disclosure, *Farahi* delivers a sobering message: when intelligence is intertwined line-by-line with secrets, silence remains the only safe option for both transparency and security alike.
