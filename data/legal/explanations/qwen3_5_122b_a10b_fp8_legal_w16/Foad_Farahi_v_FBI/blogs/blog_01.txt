## The Long Shadow of Terror: Reaffirming Deference to the Executive in Enduring Counterterrorism Investigations

The Freedom of Information Act is often framed as a mechanism for transparency, a tool to peel back the veil of secrecy surrounding government operations. Yet, in the realm of national security, that veil is rarely thin. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided last week by the D.C. Circuit, Judge Gregory Katsas reinforced the boundary between public curiosity and the operational necessities of counterterrorism. The case centers on a seemingly straightforward question: Can an FBI investigation remain "pending" enough to trigger Exemption 7(A) decades after initial contacts? The court's answer is a resounding yes, reaffirming that in matters involving foreign terrorist organizations, the timeline for "reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceedings stretches far beyond what ordinary litigants might expect.

Farahi, an Iranian national residing in the United States since 1993, sought access to his FBI file following years of contentious immigration proceedings where DHS cited his alleged ties to high-level terrorists as grounds for opposing his relief claims. While Farahi had previously lost in the Eleventh Circuit on those immigration issues, he turned to FOIA hoping to uncover the evidentiary basis for those allegations. The FBI located over 10,000 pages and dozens of CDs but withheld the vast majority under Exemption 7(A), which shields records whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The crux of Farahi's appeal was temporal: he argued that nearly two decades had passed since many of these interactions occurred, rendering any claim that proceedings were still "pending or reasonably anticipated" implausible and conclusory.

Judge Katsas's opinion dismantles this argument not by ignoring the passage of time, but by contextualizing it within the unique nature of modern counterterrorism investigations. The court acknowledged that in standard FOIA litigation, vague assertions that an investigation is "ongoing" are insufficient to sustain an exemption; agencies must provide concrete specifics. However, the panel distinguished Farahi's situation based on the gravity and complexity of the underlying conduct. The FBI's public declarations noted that Farahi was allegedly connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed, recruited for groups designated as Foreign Terrorist Organizations (FTOs), and linked to charities financing terrorism. Given these stakes—recruitment networks and financial pipelines—the court reasoned that such investigations are inherently fluid and long-tailed. An inquiry into whether someone facilitated terrorism twenty years ago does not necessarily conclude once that person leaves a country or changes their name; it evolves into questions about accomplices, funding chains, and dormant cells that may never fully close until every lead is exhausted or deemed safe from compromise.

This reasoning marks a significant practical expansion of what constitutes a "reasonably anticipated" proceeding in national security contexts. By accepting the FBI's updated declarations—which confirmed that agents were still actively reviewing files as recently as March 2023—the court signaled that the definition of "pending" is functional rather than strictly chronological. If releasing information now would tip off targets regarding the scope of an active probe into terror financing or recruitment networks, then Exemption 7(A) applies regardless of how many years have elapsed since the initial contact. This approach grants agencies substantial latitude to maintain secrecy around investigations that span generations or involve transnational networks where leads can lie dormant for years before becoming actionable again.

The decision also highlights the continued reliance on *ex parte* submissions in high-stakes FOIA cases involving national security. A major hurdle for Farahi was proving that his file contained segregable information because much of the agency's justification rested on sealed declarations detailing specific investigative techniques and current intelligence gaps. Judge Katsas defended this practice as unavoidable: revealing why certain redactions are necessary often reveals exactly what is being hidden. By deferring heavily to these confidential submissions and noting that Farahi failed to contest their admissibility earlier in district court proceedings, the appellate panel effectively insulated the government's factual assertions from rigorous adversarial testing in open court. This dynamic creates a structural asymmetry where courts must trust agency affidavits with little ability for plaintiffs to cross-examine or verify them independently when national security is invoked.

Doctrinally, *Farahi* serves as a potent reminder that while FOIA exemptions are narrow exceptions they become broad shields when coupled with judicial deference on matters touching upon international terrorism. The ruling clarifies that "stale" declarations are not automatically fatal if supplemented by timely updates confirming continuity in investigative efforts, even if those updates themselves are brief so long as they build upon a foundation laid by more detailed prior filings. More importantly, it establishes precedent suggesting that courts should be skeptical of challenges based solely on delay when dealing with subjects linked to FTOs like Al Qaeda or Hamas; such links imply enduring threats rather than historical curiosities worthy only of archival interest rather than active investigation protection.
