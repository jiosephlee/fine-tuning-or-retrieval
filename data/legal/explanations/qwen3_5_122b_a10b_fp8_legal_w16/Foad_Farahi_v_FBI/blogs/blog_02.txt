Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

## The Ex Parte Shield: How Sealed Declarations Shape Segregability Analysis in National Security FOIA Cases

The tension between the Freedom of Information Act's mandate for transparency and the government's need to protect sensitive law enforcement operations is a perennial feature of D.C. Circuit jurisprudence. Yet, few areas illustrate this friction quite like the intersection of Exemption 7(A) and the requirement for reasonable segregation. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided recently by Judge Katsas, the court affirmed a district court's denial of a FOIA request concerning an individual with alleged ties to terrorism. While the substantive holding—that the FBI's records were properly withheld because their release could interfere with ongoing enforcement proceedings—is consistent with decades of precedent, the procedural mechanism driving that result warrants closer inspection. Specifically, *Farahi* underscores how courts are increasingly relying on *ex parte* and under-seal declarations to resolve the critical question of segregability, effectively insulating these determinations from public adversarial testing.

At its core, *Farahi* involves a straightforward application of Exemption 7(A), which shields records compiled for law enforcement purposes if disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The FBI produced nearly 11,000 pages and dozens of CDs responsive to Farahi's request but withheld almost all of them. The crux of the litigation shifted from whether an investigation existed—which Farahi largely conceded given his history in immigration court involving allegations of terrorist recruitment—to whether those records could be redacted to reveal non-exempt information before being released. The district court, grappling with a decade-long delay in litigation and stale agency declarations, ordered the FBI to update its assessments. The resulting *ex parte* submissions from acting section chiefs became the linchpin for both confirming the pendency of investigations and proving that no meaningful information within those files was reasonably segregable.

Judge Katsas's opinion offers a robust defense of this procedural posture, particularly regarding the use of sealed declarations in determining segregability. Under FOIA, agencies bear the burden of demonstrating that they have taken "reasonable steps" to separate exempt from non-exempt material. However, when dealing with national security and counterterrorism investigations, revealing even the structure or context necessary to prove what *can* be released often risks revealing what *must* be withheld. As Judge Katsas notes citing *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep't of Veterans Affs.*, this creates an inherent paradox: "ex parte declaration[s] [are] no doubt handicap[ping] FOIA plaintiffs," yet they are often "the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

The appellate court's treatment of Farahi's challenge to this process is instructive for practitioners navigating similar waters. Farahi argued that he should not be bound by a judge's reliance on a sealed declaration he never saw or contested on its merits regarding segregability. The D.C. Circuit rejected this argument on two fronts: first, because Farahi failed to formally oppose the district court's earlier motion allowing *ex parte* filings, thereby waiving his right to object; and second, because accepting such challenges would force courts into exactly the dilemma FOIA seeks to avoid—publicly debating sensitive investigative techniques in open court. By characterizing Farahi's silence as forfeiture rather than mere lack of opportunity, the court reinforces a gatekeeping function where procedural compliance becomes as vital as substantive proof when national security is at stake.

This approach raises profound questions about the "meaningfulness" standard in segregability analysis when viewed through an opaque lens. The opinion reiterates that agencies need not release isolated words or phrases interspersed throughout heavily classified documents if doing so yields no actionable intelligence or contextually useful information for the requester. In *Farahi*, the court accepted that even if individual lines contained non-exempt facts, extracting them would render them meaningless without exposing sensitive methodologies or sources detailed only in the sealed record. This logic expands significantly on previous precedents like *Mead Data Central*, suggesting that in high-stakes national security contexts, "information" is defined not just by text strings but by contextual utility—a definition that remains entirely within the agency's control via its sealed briefs.

Doctrinally, *Farahi* signals a continued narrowing of judicial oversight in complex national security FOIA cases where time has eroded public confidence in agency assertions but not necessarily their validity regarding current threats. By validating updated declarations that merely confirm prior findings ("nothing has changed") rather than requiring fresh public evidence every year, the circuit acknowledges administrative reality while tightening procedural constraints on litigants who seek discovery beyond what is publicly available elsewhere (such as deportation hearings). This creates a feedback loop where older cases become harder to litigate successfully as time passes; stale declarations require updates that are themselves shielded from scrutiny once admitted under seal.

Ultimately, *Foad Farahi v. FBI* serves as a reminder that while FOIA remains a powerful tool for accountability, its reach recedes sharply when national security investigations are framed as ongoing and pervasive. The decision affirms that courts will trust executive branch expertise regarding interference with enforcement proceedings but places significant weight on procedural adherence regarding how those claims are presented privately to judges rather than publicly tested by adversaries. For transparency advocates and civil liberties groups, this ruling suggests that challenging broad withholdings will increasingly require attacking procedural waivers or arguing against categorization schemes upfront—before any sealed record is ever filed—rather than waiting for a judge to weigh unchallengeable affidavits at summary judgment stage.
