Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## Factual Context: Farahi's Immigration Proceedings and Terrorism Allegations
To understand the stakes of *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, one must first grasp the gravity of the underlying circumstances surrounding the appellant. Foad Farahi, an Iranian national residing in the United States since 1993, became the subject of intense scrutiny beginning in 2002 when the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him for a visa overstay. These immigration proceedings were not merely administrative disputes regarding status; they involved serious allegations concerning national security. During these hearings, DHS opposed Farahi's applications for asylum and other forms of relief by presenting evidence that included live testimony from an FBI agent. This agent testified that the Bureau had documented "connections to high-level terrorists." Specifically, public records from parallel litigation reveal that the FBI alleged Farahi acted as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, maintained connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and other Al Qaeda members, linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists including Jose Padilla, and supported charities accused of financing terrorism. These allegations formed the backdrop against which Farahi subsequently sought access to his own FBI file via the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA).

## The Initial FOIA Request and Agency Response Delays
Motivated by these serious accusations made during his removal proceedings, Farahi filed a FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. The sheer volume of responsive material was substantial; the FBI located approximately 10,750 pages of documents and 80 CDs containing potentially responsive information. However, before producing these records, the agency failed to meet its statutory deadlines for response, prompting Farahi to initiate suit in federal district court. Following this lawsuit, the agency engaged in a multi-year process of reviewing the responsive records. While they disclosed some materials to Farahi during this period, they ultimately withheld the majority of the documents. In October 2019, having completed its initial review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in favor of withholding most of the file. To support this motion, the agency submitted a declaration from David Hardy, then Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that releasing them would interfere with enforcement proceedings that were either pending or reasonably anticipated at that time. Notably, recognizing the sensitive nature of this information, both parties agreed—without opposition from Farahi—to allow the FBI to file a supplemental version of Hardy's declaration under seal and *ex parte*.

## Procedural Posture: Summary Judgment Motions and Stale Declarations
The procedural journey took a pivotal turn when the district court addressed the government's initial motion for summary judgment in November 2022. By this point, nearly three years had passed since the FBI filed its motion based on Hardy's original assertions. Although the district court initially found that Hardly seemed to have met his burden regarding law enforcement purposes and potential interference with proceedings, it expressed significant concern regarding "stale" declarations. Given the substantial passage of time since 2019, it was unclear whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated as originally claimed. Furthermore, while reviewing Hyde's declaration regarding segregability—the requirement that agencies release non-exempt portions of documents—the court determined it lacked sufficient information to make a ruling on public record without compromising sensitive details. Consequently, rather than granting immediate summary judgment or dismissing the case outright on these grounds alone, the district court denied summary judgment without prejudice. Instead, it issued a critical directive ordering the FBI to update its assessments regarding both the current status of any enforcement proceedings and its ability to segregate non-exempt information from exempt material within their massive cache of records.

# Legal Framework: FOIA Standards and Judicial Review

## Statutory Requirements of Exemption 7(A): Purpose and Interference

To understand the holding in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, a student must first grasp the specific statutory architecture of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). While FOIA establishes a presumption that federal agencies must release records upon request, it carves out nine specific exemptions where disclosure is prohibited. Exemption 7(A) is the primary vehicle used by law enforcement agencies to protect active investigations. Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A), an agency may withhold records only if they satisfy two distinct elements: first, the records must have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes," and second, their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings."

These two prongs operate independently but must both be met to sustain a withholding. The first prong acts as a threshold filter; not every document held by an agency like the FBI qualifies merely because the agency exists to enforce laws. The records must have been created or gathered specifically to further law enforcement objectives. The second prong focuses on harm; even if a document is compiled for law enforcement, it cannot be withheld unless releasing it poses a tangible risk to an ongoing or anticipated legal process. In *Farahi*, the court emphasizes that this interference need not be certain, but rather "reasonably expected." This standard allows agencies to prevent disclosure that might tip off subjects of an investigation, reveal investigative techniques, or otherwise compromise the integrity of pending proceedings.

## The 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated' Proceeding Standard

A critical component of Exemption 7(A) is defining what constitutes an "enforcement proceeding" sufficient to trigger the exemption. As articulated in *Farahi* and rooted in prior D.C. Circuit precedent like *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* ("CREW"), such proceedings must be either "pending or reasonably anticipated." This language prevents agencies from invoking Exemption 7(A) as a blanket shield for any historical file; there must be a concrete connection to current or imminent legal action. An ongoing criminal investigation counts as a proceeding under this standard, provided the government can attest to its existence and demonstrate that premature release of evidence would jeopardize the potential case.

The temporal aspect of this requirement often becomes contentious in litigation involving old files, as seen in *Farahi*. Appellants frequently argue that significant passage of time renders an investigation dormant or speculative. However, the court clarifies that an investigation does not cease to be "pending" simply because it has lasted for years, particularly when dealing with complex matters like terrorism where evidence gathering is prolonged. The government satisfies this element by providing affidavits stating that investigations remain ongoing and explaining how disclosure would interfere with them—for example, by alerting targets to the scope of inquiry or allowing them to destroy evidence. In *Farahi*, despite the decade-long gap between some events and the lawsuit, the court found that the FBI successfully demonstrated that investigations into Farahi's alleged terrorist connections remained active and thus qualified as "pending" proceedings capable of being interfered with by disclosure.

## Deference Principles in National Security and Law Enforcement Cases

While courts review FOIA claims independently, they apply varying degrees of deference depending on the nature of the records involved. In *Farahi*, Circuit Judge Katsas highlights a crucial nuance: although judicial review is generally independent ("de novo"), courts afford a "fair measure of deference" to agency determinations regarding whether records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and whether national security concerns are present. This deference stems from institutional competence; federal agencies like the FBI possess specialized knowledge regarding their own operations and security risks that judges lack. Consequently, when an agency asserts that documents involve national security or counterterrorism investigations—such as Farahi's file alleging ties to al-Qaeda—the court accepts these assertions unless they appear arbitrary or capricious on their face.

This deferential stance is particularly robust when Exemption 7(A) intersects with national security issues involving foreign terrorist organizations or intelligence gathering. The D.C. Circuit has consistently reiterated that in such contexts, executive branch judgments about what constitutes a threat or how an investigation proceeds warrant significant respect from reviewing courts. In practice, this means that if an agency submits detailed declarations explaining why releasing specific information would harm an investigation (e.g., revealing sources or methods), courts will rarely second-guess those assessments absent clear evidence of bad faith or logical inconsistency. In *Farahi*, this principle allowed the court to credit both public and ex parte declarations from FBI officials confirming that investigations were ongoing and sensitive enough to preclude disclosure without needing intrusive judicial probing into classified details publicly available only through sealed filings.

## Standard of Review: De Novo Analysis with Burden on the Government

Despite the deference afforded to agencies on factual questions regarding law enforcement needs, the ultimate burden of proof in FOIA litigation rests squarely on the government agency seeking to withhold records. As stated explicitly in *Farahi*, when reviewing a grant of summary judgment—which essentially decides whether there are genuine disputes of material fact—the appellate court conducts a *de novo* analysis. This means reviewing judges examine the record anew without deferring to lower court findings on legal conclusions or mixed questions of law and fact regarding statutory interpretation. However, within this framework, it remains incumbent upon the FBI (or any defendant agency) to prove that each element of Exemption 7(A) is satisfied by reasonable specificity in their affidavits and declarations.

The practical application of this standard requires agencies to provide more than conclusory statements; they must articulate a rational nexus between their activities and legitimate law enforcement duties, detailing how specific disclosures would cause specific harms like interfering with pending proceedings. # Analysis Part I: Establishing Law Enforcement Purposes

## "Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes": The Rational Nexus Test

Before a federal agency can withhold records under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), it must first clear a threshold hurdle: demonstrating that the records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." This requirement, found in Exemption 7(A) of the statute, serves as a gatekeeper to ensure that agencies do not broadly shield administrative or policy documents merely by labeling them as investigative files. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit emphasizes that while courts afford agencies a degree of deference—particularly when they are specialized law enforcement bodies like the FBI—this deference is not unlimited. The agency must still provide a factual foundation that establishes a "rational nexus" between the investigation and one of its legitimate law enforcement duties. Furthermore, there must be a discernible connection between the specific individual or incident in question and a potential security risk or violation of federal law.

This "rational nexus" test prevents agencies from engaging in fishing expeditions where any internal memo could theoretically be classified as law enforcement-related without genuine cause. The court in *Farahi* applied this standard by scrutinizing the FBI's declarations to ensure they did more than make conclusory assertions. Instead, the agency had to articulate how the records linked Farahi to actual violations of national security laws or international terrorism statutes. If the connection is tenuous or speculative, the exemption fails at this initial stage, regardless of whether releasing the documents might later interfere with proceedings. Thus, the first step in analyzing an Exemption 7(A) claim is always to verify that the agency has successfully drawn this logical line between its actions and its statutory mandate to enforce federal law.

## Evaluating Public vs. Ex Parte Declarations Regarding Investigation Scope

To satisfy the burden of proving that records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, agencies often rely on sworn declarations from officials within their organization. In complex cases involving national security, these declarations frequently come in two forms: public versions available to the plaintiff and ex parte submissions made solely to the judge under seal. The *Farahi* opinion illustrates how these two types of declarations function together to meet the agency's evidentiary burden without compromising sensitive operational details. The public declaration from David Hardy, Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section, provided sufficient high-level detail to establish the general nature of the investigation—specifically, inquiries into violations of national security and international terrorism involving Farahi's associations with designated foreign terrorist organizations.

However, public statements alone are sometimes insufficient to prove the depth and specificity required by FOIA jurisprudence when dealing with highly classified matters. Consequently, agencies submit ex parte declarations that elaborate on these points without revealing sensitive sources or methods to the requester. In *Farahi*, Hardy's ex parte declaration supplied "considerable detail" regarding the nature and scope of the investigation that could not be safely disclosed publicly. The appellate court noted that while Farahi could not see these details, his ability to challenge them was limited because he had already conceded key facts in separate litigation (discussed below). The court's approach underscores a critical procedural reality: an agency does not need to expose every detail of an investigation in open court to validate its claims; rather, it needs enough public information to show a rational nexus and enough private information (reviewed by the judge) to confirm that specific threats or violations are being pursued. This dual-track system allows courts to perform their judicial review role while respecting executive branch secrecy obligations essential to ongoing counterterrorism efforts.

## The Role of Appellant Concessions in Validating Agency Claims

A unique feature of *Farahi* is how the appellant's own prior admissions significantly bolstered the FBI's position regarding law enforcement purposes. While FOIA litigation generally places a heavy burden on the government to justify withholding records, an applicant's stipulations or concessions in related legal proceedings can serve as powerful corroborative evidence. In this case, Farahi had previously conceded during his immigration removal proceedings before an Eleventh Circuit panel that he was indeed under investigation by intelligence agencies for ties to organizations designated as foreign terrorist groups and for associations with individuals convicted of terrorism charges. These admissions effectively removed any ambiguity about whether a rational nexus existed between Farahi and federal law enforcement duties concerning national security risks.

By leveraging these concessions, Farahi inadvertently strengthened his opponent's argument on appeal regarding Exemption 7(A). The D.C. Circuit explicitly relied on these prior admissions to state unequivocally that there was "no doubt" that the FBI carried its burden regarding compilation for law enforcement purposes. This highlights an important strategic lesson for litigants: statements made outside the immediate FOIA context can become binding constraints within it once they enter the record through cross-referencing or stipulation agreements with opposing counsel or judicial findings in parallel cases. For students reading opinions like *Farahi*, it is vital to look beyond just what is contained within four corners of the current briefs; understanding what parties have admitted elsewhere can often resolve threshold disputes about whether an investigation actually exists and serves a legitimate law enforcement purpose before even reaching questions about interference with proceedings or segregability.

# Analysis Part II: Interference with Pending Proceedings

## 'Reasonably Expected to Interfere': Tipping Off Targets Prematurely

At the heart of Exemption 7(A) lies the requirement that the release of law enforcement records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit focused heavily on the practical consequences of premature disclosure, specifically the risk of "tipping off" targets. The court recognized a fundamental tension in law enforcement: once an investigation is underway, revealing the scope, methods, or specific leads contained within agency files can compromise the ability of investigators to gather evidence effectively. If a target learns exactly what information the FBI possesses, they may alter their behavior, destroy evidence, flee jurisdiction, or coordinate with accomplices to evade detection.

The court affirmed that this rationale is a "widely accepted justification" for invoking Exemption 7(A). In Farahi's case, the FBI argued that releasing his file would reveal the extent of their knowledge regarding his associations and activities. The district court credited this logic, noting that even if an investigation has been active for years, disclosing its current status or specific intelligence gaps could still derail potential future prosecutions or ongoing investigative strategies. Therefore, the standard does not require proof that interference has already occurred; rather, it asks whether such interference is a reasonable expectation based on the nature of law enforcement work. By accepting the FBI's assertion that disclosure would tip off targets regarding the scope of an ongoing inquiry, the appellate court reinforced that protecting the integrity of an investigation justifies withholding records even before formal charges are filed.

## 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated': Defining Ongoing Investigations

To satisfy Exemption 7(A), an agency must demonstrate that enforcement proceedings are either currently pending or reasonably anticipated. The court clarified that "proceedings" in this context includes ongoing criminal investigations aimed at gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case. It is not necessary for an indictment to be imminent; rather, the government need only attest to the existence of an investigation that continues to collect evidence for a prospective prosecution. In *Farahi*, this definition proved critical because Farahi's file contained decades-old information regarding his immigration history and alleged terrorist ties. Without a clear link to present-day enforcement activity, such historical data might not qualify for exemption merely because it was collected years ago.

The D.C. Circuit emphasized that once an agency establishes a baseline connection between an individual and a violation of federal law—such as Farahi's documented links to designated foreign terrorist organizations—the presumption shifts toward finding that proceedings are pending if the agency asserts otherwise. The court noted that ongoing investigations into national security threats often span long periods due to their complexity and sensitivity. Consequently, an investigation remains "pending" as long as agents are actively working to build a case or assess threats, even if no immediate arrest is scheduled. The key inquiry is whether there exists a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding." In this instance, because Farahi was linked to high-profile figures like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and organizations like Al Qaeda, and because he faced removal proceedings where such evidence was central, it was reasonable to anticipate that these matters could evolve into formal criminal or administrative enforcement actions requiring secrecy until resolution.

## Rebutting Arguments on Passage of Time and Conclusory Assertions

A significant portion of Farahi's appeal rested on the argument that the passage of time rendered any claim of "ongoing" investigations implausible and legally insufficient under D.C. Circuit precedent set in *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*). In *CREW*, the court warned against relying on vague assertions that investigations remain open without specific factual support. Farahi contended that Joseph Bender's declaration—which merely stated in passing that investigations "remain ongoing"—was too conclusory to meet this standard given that years had elapsed since his initial request and previous litigation stages involving different declarations by David Hardy.

The appellate court rejected this argument by refusing to view Bender's declaration in isolation. Instead, it applied a contextual approach consistent with FOIA jurisprudence regarding updated submissions. The court reasoned that Bender's brief statement served only as an update confirming continuity from earlier, more detailed declarations made by Hardy during prior phases of litigation. Those original declarations had already provided substantial detail regarding the nature and scope of Farahi's file and its relation to terrorism investigations. Furthermore, Farahi himself had previously conceded in district court filings that he was under investigation for ties to foreign terrorist organizations and enemy combatants. Given this extensive background record establishing the legitimacy and depth of the investigation initially described by Hardy, Bender's short affirmation acted as a bridge confirming nothing had changed rather than attempting to start from scratch with new evidence alone. The breadth of Farahi's alleged connections—from recruiting for extremist groups to supporting charities financing terrorism—made it highly plausible for investigators to maintain active oversight years later without needing constant re-litigation of every fact every time an update was filed.
