Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## Foad Farahi's Immigration History and Allegations of Terrorist Connections

To understand the stakes of this Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) dispute, one must first appreciate the context surrounding the appellant, Foad Farahi. Farahi is an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993. His presence in the country became legally precarious in 2002 when the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him due to a visa overstay. These proceedings were not merely administrative; they involved serious allegations regarding national security. During various stages of his immigration litigation, DHS opposed Farahi's applications for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure by presenting evidence of his alleged connections to high-level terrorists. Specifically, DHS introduced documents and live testimony from an FBI agent who stated that Farahi had documented links to terrorist networks. This background is critical because it frames the nature of the files Farahi subsequently sought through FOIA: records compiled by an intelligence agency concerning potential threats to national security and law enforcement investigations into terrorism.

## The Initial FOIA Request and the FBI's Production

Driven by these contentious immigration proceedings, Farahi submitted a formal FOIA request seeking access to his FBI file. The scope of the agency's search was substantial; upon reviewing its databases, the FBI located approximately 10,750 pages of documents and 80 compact discs containing potentially responsive information. Following standard procedure, Farahi filed suit after the agency failed to meet its statutory deadline for responding to the request. Over the ensuing years, the FBI conducted a meticulous review of these materials. While the agency ultimately released some records to Farahi, it chose to withhold the vast majority of them. In October 2019, having completed its initial review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in federal district court. To support this motion, the agency submitted a declaration from David Hardy, then Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). In this declaration, Hardy asserted that the withheld records were compiled specifically for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were either pending or reasonably anticipated at that time. Notably, recognizing that certain details regarding national security could not be made public without compromising ongoing operations, the FBI moved—and was granted permission—to file a supplemental version of Hardy's declaration under seal and *ex parte*.

## Procedural Posture: Stale Declarations and Judicial Oversight

The procedural journey through the district court highlights a crucial aspect of FOIA litigation: judicial skepticism toward "stale" agency assertions when significant time has passed since an investigation began or since an affidavit was drafted. In November 2022—years after the initial filing—the district court issued its preliminary ruling on summary judgment. The judge acknowledged that Farahi's file appeared compiled for law enforcement purposes and seemingly agreed that Hardy's original declaration offered sufficient grounds for invoking Exemption 7(A), which protects records whose release could interfere with enforcement proceedings. However, given the significant passage of time between Hardy's declaration and the court's consideration, as well as concerns regarding whether non-exempt information could be separated from exempt material (segregability), the court denied summary judgment without prejudice. Crucially, rather than dismissing the case or ruling immediately against the government, the district court exercised its supervisory power by ordering the FBI to update its assessment. The court required new evidence confirming whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated as of late 2022/early 2023 and demanded more detailed explanations regarding why no portions of these massive documents could be redacted and released separately from sensitive content.

## The Role of Ex Parte Submissions in Updating Investigations

Following this directive from lower courts requiring fresh evidence, the FBI returned with updated documentation in early 2023. The agency secured two new declarations from Joseph Bender, who had succeeded Hardy as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Unlike a simple restatement of old facts Bender provided both public and *ex parte* declarations designed specifically to address the district court's lingering doubts about temporal staleness and segregability. In his public declaration, Bender explained that RIDS had re-contacted agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file; this agent confirmed that investigations into Farahi remained active as recently as March 2023. More significantly for resolving disputes involving national security secrets where public disclosure itself might cause harm, Bender provided an *ex parte* declaration containing granular details about why releasing even partial information would compromise these ongoing efforts regarding counterterrorism probes into figures like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and entities linked to Al Qaeda or Hamas-affiliated charities like Al-Haramain Islamic Foundation (though names vary slightly across different contexts). # Legal Framework: FOIA Exemption 7(A) Standards

## Statutory Elements of Exemption 7(A): Law Enforcement Purpose vs. Interference with Proceedings

To understand the resolution of *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, a student must first grasp the two-pronged statutory test embedded in the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). While FOIA generally mandates that federal agencies release records to the public upon request, it carves out specific exceptions. The case turns entirely on Exemption 7(A), codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). This exemption does not create a blanket privilege for all FBI files; rather, it protects records only if they satisfy two distinct conditions. First, the agency must demonstrate that the records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." Second, even if the records meet that threshold, they remain shielded only if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings."

The opinion clarifies that these are separate inquiries. The "law enforcement purpose" prong focuses on the origin and nature of the documents themselves. Because the FBI is inherently a law-enforcement agency, its determination that a file was compiled for such purposes is entitled to a "fair measure of deference." However, this deference is not absolute; the agency's declarations must still establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of its legitimate law enforcement duties, such as investigating national security threats or violations of federal law. In *Farahi*, the court noted that the FBI successfully met this burden by linking the withheld records to investigations involving terrorism and associations with designated foreign terrorist organizations.

The second prong—"interference with enforcement proceedings"—shifts the focus from the creation of the record to the consequences of releasing it. The statute requires more than a speculative harm; there must be a reasonable expectation that disclosure would disrupt specific legal processes. As Judge Katsas explains in Part IV(B) of the opinion, this interference often manifests as tipping off targets regarding the scope of an investigation or compromising evidence gathering before a case can be brought to trial. If an agency can show that releasing information would jeopardize an ongoing effort to build a criminal case, Exemption 7(A) applies. Thus, students should view Exemption 7(A) not merely as a classification label but as a functional test weighing administrative necessity against public transparency rights.

## The 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated' Requirement for Enforcement Proceedings

A critical limitation on Exemption 7(A) is that it does not protect records indefinitely simply because they relate to past misconduct or general surveillance. The opinion emphasizes that for disclosure to constitute an actionable interference, there must be "enforcement proceedings" that are either currently "pending or reasonably anticipated." This temporal requirement prevents agencies from invoking national security exemptions as a pretext to hide embarrassing historical data unrelated to active justice efforts. As cited in *Farahi* through precedents like *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* ("CREW"), ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings," meaning an agency need not wait until formal charges are filed to claim protection under this exemption.

However, the concept of "reasonably anticipated" imposes a concrete evidentiary hurdle on the government. An agency cannot rely on vague assertions that an investigation might lead somewhere someday; it must demonstrate a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding." In *Farahi*, this distinction became central to the appellate court's analysis of whether time had eroded the validity of the FBI's claim. The appellant argued that because years had passed since his initial removal proceedings and earlier disclosures, any current investigation was merely theoretical or stale. The court rejected this argument by looking at context rather than isolated statements. Even though one declaration appeared terse regarding pendency, it served as an update confirming that earlier detailed findings remained valid. The court reasoned that given Farahi's extensive alleged ties to high-level terrorists—including links to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and involvement with groups like Al Qaeda—it was plausible and legally sufficient for investigators to continue gathering evidence for potential future prosecutions years after initial contacts were made. Therefore, students must recognize that "reasonably anticipated" allows for forward-looking protection but demands specific factual support showing that an investigation has moved beyond mere speculation into active preparation for legal action.

## De Novo Review Versus Deference to Agency Expertise in National Security Matters

Procedurally, understanding how courts review FOIA disputes is essential for analyzing *Farahi*. On appeal, grants of summary judgment are reviewed *de novo*, meaning the appellate court examines the matter anew without deferring to the lower court's conclusions regarding questions of law or mixed questions of fact and law where no credibility determinations were made typically required in bench trials involving classified info). However, within this broad standard lies a nuanced doctrine regarding administrative expertise: judicial deference. While courts rigorously scrutinize whether statutory requirements are met, they afford special weight to executive branch judgments when national security is implicated.

As Judge Katsas articulates in Part III and IV(A), because specialized knowledge distinguishes agencies like the FBI from generalist judges regarding what constitutes a threat or how investigative techniques function, their decisions on invoking Exemption 7 are entitled to deference. This is particularly true in cases involving counterterrorism and intelligence gathering, where revealing operational details could itself compromise safety or efficacy (*Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*). This deference operates within boundaries; agencies cannot simply assert secrecy without providing rational explanations tied to their duties (the "rational nexus"). Yet, once an agency provides affidavits detailing why release would cause harm—such as alerting targets about investigative scope—the court accepts these representations unless shown otherwise by contrary evidence not available in open court (often handled via ex parte submissions). Students should note that while *de novo* review suggests independent judgment by judges, in practice within sensitive FOIA cases involving national security like *Farahi*, this independence is tempered by substantial respect for executive assessments of risk and necessity unless those assessments lack logical grounding or fail statutory criteria altogether outright absent contradictory proof provided openly during litigation process itself! # Analysis: Compiling Records for Law Enforcement Purposes

## Fair Measure of Deference Accorded to FBI Determinations on Law Enforcement Duties

To successfully invoke Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), an agency must satisfy two distinct prerequisites: first, the records must have been "compiled for law enforcement purposes," and second, their disclosure must be reasonably expected to "interfere with enforcement proceedings." This chapter focuses exclusively on the first prong, examining how the D.C. Circuit in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* evaluated whether the withheld files were legitimately created for law enforcement rather than general administrative reasons. The court began its analysis by acknowledging the specialized role of the FBI as a primary federal law enforcement agency. Consequently, when the FBI asserts that its records were compiled for law enforcement duties, its determination is entitled to a "fair measure" of judicial deference. This deference is rooted in the recognition that courts lack the operational expertise possessed by law enforcement agencies regarding the nature and purpose of their investigations. However, this deference is not unlimited or automatic; it does not relieve the government of its ultimate burden to prove that an exemption applies. Instead, it shifts the analytical starting point, allowing the court to accept the agency's characterization unless it is contradicted by evidence or lacks a logical foundation.

Despite this deferential standard, the court emphasized that agency declarations must still provide a factual basis sufficient to demonstrate a connection between the specific records and legitimate law enforcement functions. The court cited precedent establishing that an affidavit must "establish a rational 'nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties.'" Furthermore, there must be a discernible link between an individual or incident and a potential security risk or violation of federal law. In *Farahi*, this requirement meant that David Hardy's declaration could not merely state that the files belonged to an FBI agent; it had to articulate how those files served specific statutory mandates related to national security and terrorism investigations. The public declaration succeeded because it explicitly stated that the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into violations of national security laws and international terrorism, thereby satisfying the threshold requirement that these were not merely routine personnel files or administrative memoranda devoid of investigatory significance.

## Establishing a Rational Nexus Between Investigation and National Security Duties

The core of Exemption 7(A)'s first prong lies in proving this "rational nexus." In *Farahi*, the court scrutinized whether there was a genuine connection between Farahi's file and specific threats to national security or violations of federal law. The FBI met this burden through detailed descriptions in both public and ex parte declarations. The public Hardy declaration noted that the investigation concerned Farahi's associations with individuals directly involved in terrorist activities, linking his file to active inquiries into international terrorism statutes. This level of specificity moves beyond generic assertions about "law enforcement work" and anchors the records in concrete legal duties assigned to the FBI under national security statutes. The court found that describing an investigation into connections with foreign terrorist organizations provided sufficient specificity to show that these records were indeed compiled for a recognized law enforcement objective: preventing terrorism and prosecuting violations related to foreign intelligence gathering or recruitment efforts.

The court reinforced this finding by noting that such deference is particularly appropriate when national security concerns are implicated. Drawing on prior precedents like *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, the opinion highlighted that courts consistently afford greater weight to executive branch judgments when they involve sensitive counterterrorism matters. In *Farahi*, this context allowed the court to trust the FBI's assertion that investigating an individual linked to high-level terror networks constituted a valid law enforcement duty requiring secrecy during compilation. The nexus was established not just by naming laws generally violated but by identifying specific types of conduct—such as recruiting for designated foreign terrorist organizations—that fall squarely within the FBI's mandate to investigate threats against national safety. Thus, even without revealing classified details publicly, the agency successfully demonstrated that every page withheld originated from a targeted effort to uncover potential federal crimes related to terrorism, rather than from casual data collection or general administrative tracking.

## Using Public Concessions: How Farahi's Own Admission Validated FBI Claims

One of the most compelling aspects of *Farahi*'s analysis regarding compendium purposes was how external evidence corroborated internal agency assertions. While agencies often submit sworn declarations detailing their investigative processes, such documents can sometimes appear self-serving if uncorroborated by independent facts available on record. In this case, however, Farahi inadvertently bolstered his opponent's argument through admissions made during separate immigration proceedings before an Eleventh Circuit panel involving his removal case (*Farahi v. U.S. Att'y Gen.*). During those proceedings, DHS presented testimony from an FBI agent documenting Farahi's connections to high-level terrorists, and Farahi did not contest these allegations at trial; instead, he effectively conceded in his opposition briefs before this court that he had been investigated for ties to State Department-designated foreign terrorist organizations and individuals detained as enemy combatants who were subsequently convicted on terrorism charges.

These public concessions served as powerful independent validation for Hardy's claims regarding why his file existed in its current form. By admitting he was under scrutiny for affiliations with groups like Hezbollah or Al-Qaeda affiliates (specifically mentioning Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and Jose Padilla), Farahi confirmed that there was indeed a substantive investigation underway involving serious federal crimes related to terrorism at home abroad . His own acknowledgment transformed abstract claims about "national security risks" into concrete realities acknowledged by litigant himself , effectively closing any gap between what Hardy said internally versus what actually happened externally . This interplay illustrates how collateral litigation can serve evidentiary purposes in FOIA disputes ; when a plaintiff admits facts supporting an exemption elsewhere , those admissions become binding constraints limiting ability argue otherwise later . Here , Farahis concession removed any ambiguity about whether real-world threat assessments justified compiling these particular documents under Exemption 7(A).

# Analysis: Interference with Pending or Anticipated Proceedings

## Defining "Enforcement Proceedings" Under Exemption 7(A)

To successfully withhold records under the Freedom of Information Act's (FOIA) Exemption 7(A), an agency must demonstrate two primary elements: first, that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, and second, that their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit focused heavily on the second element, clarifying the scope of what constitutes an "enforcement proceeding." The court reaffirmed established precedent that such proceedings need not be limited to formal litigation or administrative hearings; rather, they encompass ongoing criminal investigations. As cited in *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* ("CREW"), a proceeding qualifies as "pending or reasonably anticipated" if the government is actively gathering evidence for a potential future criminal case. Consequently, even if no indictment has been issued, an investigation remains a valid "proceeding" so long as it is concrete and prospective. The critical inquiry is whether releasing the information at this stage would jeopardize that future case by compromising the integrity of the investigation or alerting subjects to its scope.

## The 'Staleness' Defense and Temporal Passage

A common defense raised by FOIA litigants is that time renders prior law enforcement justifications "stale," effectively negating the claim that current disclosure would cause interference. Farahi argued precisely this point, suggesting that because significant time had passed since the FBI's initial declarations, any assertion that investigations were still active was inherently suspect. The court acknowledged this concern, noting its prior guidance in *CREW* that vague assertions about ongoing investigations may fail to satisfy Exemption 7(A). However, the appellate court rejected Farahi's application of this rule to his case. The mere passage of time does not automatically invalidate an exemption claim; instead, it triggers a requirement for updated verification. In this instance, the district court had previously denied summary judgment specifically to require the FBI to refresh its assessment of whether proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. By ordering updated declarations nearly four years after the initial filing, the lower court ensured that the government's claims were not relying on historical data alone but reflected the current status of its investigative efforts. Thus, staleness is a procedural hurdle requiring fresh evidence, not a substantive bar to withholding records if such evidence is forthcoming.

## Evaluating Declaration Specificity: Conclusory vs. Contextualized Assertions

The crux of Farahi's appeal rested on his characterization of Joseph Bender's supplemental declaration as impermissibly conclusory because it simply stated that investigations "remain ongoing" without extensive new detail. While courts generally reject bare assertions lacking factual support—such as stating an investigation exists without explaining its nature—the D.C. Circuit emphasized that declarations cannot be evaluated in a vacuum. In *Farahi*, Judge Katsas reasoned that looking solely at Bender's brief language ignored the broader evidentiary record. Bender's declaration served primarily as an update confirming that no material changes had occurred since David Hardy's earlier, highly detailed public and ex parte declarations described the specific nature and scope of the terrorism-related probe. When viewed cumulatively with Hardy's original testimony—which outlined specific leads and threats—and bolstered by judicial findings made months prior regarding the likelihood of interference, Bender's succinct confirmation was deemed sufficient. The lesson for students reading opinions like this is that specificity is judged holistically; a short update suffices if it relies upon and corroborates a foundation of previously established, detailed facts within the same litigation history.

## Corroborative Evidence: Public Records Validating Private Claims

One of the most instructive aspects of *Farahi* is how public knowledge can serve as independent corroboration for classified or sensitive law enforcement assertions far beyond what appears in agency affidavits alone. Although much of Hardy's ex parte declaration detailing specific intelligence sources was sealed from public view to protect national security, Farahi himself had admitted in parallel immigration proceedings before his own judge and counsel regarding his associations with designated foreign terrorist organizations and individuals linked to high-profile terrorism convictions (such as Jose Padilla). The appellate court utilized these public admissions from Farahi's immigration trial transcript to validate the FBI's private claims regarding its investigative focus on terrorism recruitment and financing networks. By cross-referencing public judicial records with private agency declarations, the court found it impossible to conclude that a credible investigation into these serious matters had ceased merely due to time passing. This approach demonstrates how courts can use unsealed parts of a party's own legal filings to assess the plausibility of an agency's claim that an investigation remains active and reasonably anticipated without violating confidentiality protections elsewhere in the record.

## Tipping Off Targets: The Logic Behind Premature Disclosure Harm

Finally, *Farahi* reinforces a fundamental rationale for Exemption 7(A): preventing agencies from inadvertently revealing their hand through premature disclosure. Even if an investigation is real and ongoing, releasing files could harm enforcement by "tipping off" targets regarding what evidence authorities possess and how deeply they are investigating them. As noted in *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, disclosing information allows subjects to alter their behavior, destroy evidence, or flee before charges are brought or negotiations begin. In this case, both Hardy and Bender articulated this standard fear—that releasing Farahi's file would reveal the scope of counterterrorism inquiries directed at him and his associates within groups like Al Qaeda or Hamas-affiliated charities (e.g., Holy Land Foundation). The court accepted this logic as a self-evident reason why disclosure would interfere with proceedings: once targets know what investigators know, they can evade justice more easily than if they # Segregability and Dealing with Ex Parte Submissions

Even when an agency successfully demonstrates that records fall within a specific FOIA exemption, its victory is not absolute. The statute imposes a continuing duty to release any portion of a record that can be reasonably segregated from the exempt material. Furthermore, in cases involving national security or sensitive law enforcement data, the judicial process often relies on evidence that the plaintiff cannot see—*ex parte* submissions. This chapter examines how the D.C. Circuit applied these principles in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, clarifying when withholding entire documents is justified and why sealed affidavits are a necessary component of adjudicating such disputes.

## Reasonable Segregation Standard: When Non-Exempt Material Is 'Inextricably Intertwined'

Under the Freedom of Information Act, agencies must "take reasonable steps" to separate and release nonexempt information contained within otherwise exempt records. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b). This requirement ensures that FOIA does not become a tool for blanket secrecy; rather, it mandates transparency wherever possible without compromising the protected interest. However, the obligation to segregate ends where the effort becomes unreasonable or futile. The D.C. Circuit has long held that an agency may withhold a document in its entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt ones. *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting *Mead Data Cent., Inc.*). In such scenarios, redacting the exempt material would either destroy the meaning of the remaining text or reveal the very information the exemption seeks to protect, rendering the disclosure meaningless or harmful.

In *Farahi*, the appellant argued that given the sheer volume of responsive records—over 10,000 pages—the FBI must have contained some trivial, nonexempt facts that could be released independently. The court rejected this intuition-based argument by emphasizing that FOIA focuses on "information," not merely isolated words or phrases scattered across a page. If nonexempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," attempting to extract it would likely result in a disjointed string of words devoid of context or utility. Drawing on precedent like *Perioperative Servs.*, the court affirmed that where exempt and nonexempt content are so tightly woven together, no meaningful information remains once the protected elements are removed. Consequently, if releasing even small fragments risks exposing investigative methods or sources, or if the remaining text is nonsensical without the excised portions, the agency satisfies its burden by withholding the entire record.

## Line-by-Line Redaction Limitations vs Meaningful Information Release

The distinction between "words" and "meaningful information" is central to understanding why *Farahi*'s request for partial release failed despite his assumption that something must be releasable within thousands of pages. The court clarified that while an appellant might theoretically identify individual words or short phrases that appear harmless in isolation, these snippets do not constitute "reasonably segregable" information under FOIA if they lack coherence or context once separated from their source document. The goal of segregation is to provide useful intelligence to the public; providing fragmented sentences that reveal nothing about government operations serves no statutory purpose and may inadvertently tip off subjects about what investigators know by confirming which lines were deemed safe enough to release versus which were redacted as dangerous clues.

In applying this standard to Farahi's case, the district court relied on detailed justifications provided by the FBI explaining why specific categories of documents could not be broken down further without compromising law enforcement interests. The appellate court deferred to this assessment because Farahi failed to demonstrate exactly how specific lines could be extracted safely across such complex files involving national security terrorism investigations. Without a concrete proposal showing how meaningful information could survive redaction without revealing protected details about ongoing probes into terrorist networks, Farahi's generalized claim that "something must be there" was insufficient to overcome the presumption of validity attached to an agency's segregation determinations in sensitive contexts.
