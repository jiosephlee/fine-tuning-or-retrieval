Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

## The 'Stale' Declaration Problem: How D.C. Circuit Preserves Exemption 7(A) Validity Across Years

In the high-stakes arena of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation, few issues are as persistent—or as procedurally thorny—as the "stale declaration" problem. When a lawsuit drags on for years, as many complex national security cases inevitably do, an agency's initial justification for withholding records risks becoming untethered from reality. A promise made in 2019 that an investigation is "ongoing" carries less weight in 2023 if the agency offers no fresh proof that anything has actually happened in the interim. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided September 30, 2025, the D.C. Circuit grappled with exactly this tension. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Gregory Katsas offered a nuanced solution that balances the need for judicial vigilance against bureaucratic drift with the practical realities of managing decades-long counterterrorism files. The court's ruling reaffirms that while agencies cannot rest on laurels indefinitely, they do not need to reinvent the wheel every time a judge asks for an update; rather, a subsequent, brief confirmation can effectively "renew" the validity of earlier, detailed findings.

The facts of *Farahi* present a textbook scenario for why FOIA litigation often becomes a marathon rather than a sprint. Foad Farahi, an Iranian national facing removal proceedings due to alleged ties to terrorist organizations, sought access to his FBI file under FOIA. The FBI located over 10,000 pages and eighty CDs but withheld nearly everything under Exemption 7(A), which shields records whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The district court initially granted summary judgment to the FBI based on declarations from David Hardy, then Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section. However, recognizing that several years had passed since Hardy's affidavit was sworn—and citing precedent warning that vague assertions can grow stale—the district court denied final judgment without prejudice. It ordered the FBI to provide an updated assessment confirming that enforcement proceedings were indeed still pending or reasonably anticipated as of late 2022 or early 2023.

This is where the procedural drama intensifies and where Judge Katsas's analysis shines. To satisfy the court's order, the FBI submitted new declarations from Joseph Bender, his successor as Acting Section Chief. On its face, Bender's public declaration appeared disappointingly thin; it merely stated that investigations remained "ongoing," lacking the granular detail found in Hardy's original filings. Farahi seized on this terseness, arguing that under *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* ("CREW"), such a bare-bones assertion was insufficient to prove pendency after such a long delay. If taken in isolation, Farahi's argument holds water: *CREW* teaches that courts must demand concrete evidence that an investigation hasn't simply evaporated into administrative limbo over time. A two-sentence declaration risks being just another placeholder for delay tactics disguised as compliance with judicial orders.

However, Judge Katsas declined to view Bender's declaration in a vacuum, instead adopting a holistic approach to reviewing multi-year litigation records. The appellate court reasoned that Bender was not tasked with establishing the existence of an investigation anew; rather, his role was strictly confirmatory—to attest that nothing fundamental had changed since Hardy laid out the detailed factual predicate years prior. By reading Bender's succinct update alongside Hardy's robust original declaration (which included both public and ex parte components detailing specific leads and suspects), the court found that the government had successfully bridged the temporal gap. This distinction is critical: it acknowledges that while agencies cannot rely solely on old data forever once challenged by time-lapse concerns, they also shouldn't be forced to relitigate established facts every time a case enters its fourth year of discovery delays caused by judicial caution or administrative processing backlogs.

The implication of this reasoning extends beyond mere procedural efficiency; it touches on the delicate balance between transparency and national security efficacy in protracted litigation scenarios. In cases involving terrorism and foreign intelligence—where investigations can span decades and involve intricate networks of actors—the expectation that investigators must constantly re-articulate foundational facts in response to routine status updates could place an undue burden on law enforcement resources without necessarily enhancing accuracy or accountability. By allowing a later declarant to piggyback on earlier detailed work product when updating their status report does not contradict prior findings but merely confirms continuity, Judge Katsas preserves the integrity of Exemption 7(A) without inviting abuse by agencies seeking to hide behind vague promises forever.

Nevertheless, this approach invites scrutiny regarding judicial oversight mechanisms in delayed FOIA suits. Critics might argue that accepting terse follow-up declarations creates a loophole where agencies could theoretically let cases languish for years before submitting minimal updates hoping courts won't look closely enough at whether an investigation truly remains active or has quietly stalled until it is mooted by statute or policy shifts away from prosecution plans yet still exists legally speaking only technically speaking now perhaps indefinitely extended because nobody said stop officially anyway somewhere along timeline maybe? ## The Invisibility of Justice: The Expanding Role of Ex Parte Declarations in National Security FOIA Litigation

In the high-stakes arena of Freedom of Information Act litigation involving national security, the balance between public transparency and executive confidentiality is often precarious. The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* reinforces a troubling trend: the judiciary's increasing willingness to accept the government's word—delivered behind closed doors—as the final arbiter of what the public may see. While the case turns on the specifics of a decades-old counterterrorism investigation into an Iranian national, its procedural holding regarding ex parte declarations offers a sobering look at how modern FOIA adjudication operates when "national security" is invoked. By affirming that plaintiffs forfeit challenges to sealed submissions if they fail to object immediately, and by characterizing such secrecy as an unavoidable feature of the system rather than a bug, the court has effectively widened the shield protecting classified enforcement strategies from adversarial testing.

The core facts of *Farahi* are stark enough to justify significant government caution. Farahi, a permanent resident since 1993, had been the subject of intense scrutiny by both immigration authorities and the FBI for alleged ties to high-level terrorist organizations, including connections to figures like Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and Jose Padilla. When Farahi sought his FBI file under FOIA, the agency produced thousands of pages but withheld nearly all content under Exemption 7(A), claiming disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated law enforcement proceedings. The district court, sensing that some original declarations might be stale given the passage of time, ordered updates before granting summary judgment for the FBI. On appeal, Judge Katsas affirmed this outcome, largely deferring to new declarations from FBI officials submitted both publicly and under seal.

What distinguishes this opinion is not merely its application of Exemption 7(A)—which routinely shields ongoing counterterrorism investigations—but its rigorous validation of the procedural mechanisms used to reach that conclusion. The court explicitly endorsed the use of ex parte (one-sided) and in camera (private judicial review) submissions to resolve disputes over segregability and exemption applicability. In doing so, it highlighted a critical asymmetry: while Farahi could read public declarations detailing why his file contained sensitive intelligence, he was barred from seeing—or effectively challenging—the detailed justifications contained in sealed filings until after the judge had already ruled on their sufficiency.

This dynamic creates a profound imbalance in adversarial testing. FOIA litigation relies heavily on "Vaughn indices"—itemized lists describing withheld documents—to allow courts and litigants to assess whether exemptions are being abused. Yet, when national security is at stake, agencies increasingly submit voluminous ex parte declarations that contain specific details about investigative techniques or sources that cannot be revealed even to opposing counsel without compromising those very secrets. *Farahi* signals that once an agency invokes this cloak of secrecy, courts are prepared to treat those submissions as dispositive without demanding that plaintiffs first demonstrate they have been prejudiced by their inability to scrutinize them fully. As Judge Katsas noted, while ex parte reliance "handicaps" plaintiffs, this handicap is "inherent" in cases where revealing the evidence would defeat its purpose. While logically sound in theory, this reasoning risks becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy where courts rarely demand more than a bald assertion from agencies because asking for more seems futile without access to the classified record itself.

Perhaps even more consequential is the court's treatment of forfeiture regarding these sealed proceedings. Farahi argued that he should be able to challenge his reliance on Bender's ex parte declaration regarding segregability because he never saw it during discovery or initial briefing. The appellate panel rejected this argument with decisive clarity: because Farahi did not oppose the district court's earlier motion allowing ex parte filings, he forfeited his right to complain about them later on appeal. This procedural gatekeeping places an immense burden on transparency advocates and civil liberties groups who may lack resources or specialized knowledge early in litigation to anticipate every procedural nuance required to preserve objections against secret evidence collection methods. If a plaintiff fails to object at step one—even if they didn't know what was happening inside room A—they lose their ability to question step two in open court months later.

The doctrinal implications extend beyond *Farahi*'s specific facts into a broader pattern of deference that treats executive assertions about ongoing investigations as virtually unassailable once wrapped in national security concerns. The court emphasized that broad allegations about terrorism recruitment and funding made by DHS in parallel removal proceedings lent credence to the FBI's claim that investigations remained active years later. This suggests that once an individual enters the orbit of counterterrorism designations—even via separate administrative proceedings—it becomes exponentially harder for them to prove that their personal files are no longer relevant to "reasonably anticipated" enforcement actions years down the line. The standard for proving pendency appears lower when supported by historical context suggesting deep-seated suspicion rather than current evidentiary proof alone available on public record.

Ultimately, *Foad Farahi v.* FBI serves as a reminder that in national security FOIA cases, justice often remains invisible not because it doesn't exist, but because it operates entirely out of sight of public scrutiny and meaningful adversarial challenge. By cementing rules that make it difficult for litigants to contest sealed evidence unless they raise objections with perfect timing and foresight before ever seeing it, courts risk insulating agencies from genuine oversight challenges regarding how long investigations truly linger and how broadly exemptions are applied. For transparency advocates fighting for accountability in terrorism-related files, this ruling underscores a shifting landscape where the presumption favors secrecy not just over specific documents, but over entire categories of judicial process itself. Unless Congress intervenes or higher courts recalibrate this deferential posture toward ex parte procedures within FOIA jurisprudence today's rulings suggest tomorrow's denials will come with fewer questions asked—and far fewer answers given—in open courtrooms across America



## 'Interspersed Line-by-Line': Reaffirming the High Bar for Segregability in Counterterrorism Files

The tension between the public's right to know and the government's need to protect sensitive law enforcement operations is a perennial feature of Freedom of Information Act litigation. But few contexts sharpen this conflict quite like requests involving counterterrorism files, where the stakes involve national security, ongoing investigations, and the safety of sources. In *Farahi v. FBI*, decided last month by the D.C. Circuit, Judge Gregory Katsas delivered a robust affirmation of the executive branch's ability to withhold entire investigative files when non-exempt material is inextricably woven into exempt intelligence. While the case turns on familiar Exemption 7(A) principles—whether an investigation is "pending"—its most consequential doctrinal contribution lies in its rigorous reapplication of the segregability requirement under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b). By rejecting the notion that agencies must parse out isolated words or phrases from massive dossiers, the court signals that in complex national security contexts, form often follows function: if you strip away the secrets, you destroy the value of the whole.

The facts of *Farahi* present a stark backdrop for this legal question. Foad Farahi, an Iranian national facing removal proceedings in which DHS alleged ties to high-level terrorists, sought his FBI file via FOIA. The bureau turned up over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs, ultimately withholding nearly everything under Exemption 7(A), which shields records that could reasonably interfere with pending or anticipated enforcement proceedings. The district court initially balked at accepting decade-old declarations as proof that an investigation remained active twenty years after its inception, ordering updates before granting summary judgment. On appeal, Judge Katsas found no error in this process. Crucially, however, he also addressed Farahi's argument that even if most of the file was exempt, surely some portion—perhaps a name or a date—must be severable and releasable.

Judge Katsas's treatment of segregability serves as a reminder that FOIA is not merely a mechanical exercise in redaction but a substantive inquiry into whether disclosure compromises the integrity of law enforcement efforts. The appellant argued that given the sheer volume of responsive records (over ten thousand pages), it was statistically improbable that every single word fell within an exemption; thus, some information must be disclosable. The D.C. Circuit flatly rejected this logic by invoking *Mead Data Central v. U.S. Dept. of Air Force*. Under *Mead*, courts look at whether non-exempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with exempt ones. If releasing even innocuous-sounding data points would reveal patterns, methodologies, or gaps in an investigation, they are not "reasonably segregable."

The opinion leans heavily on the specific phrasing "randomly interspersed line-by-line." This phrase captures the reality of modern intelligence work: raw data rarely exists in neat silos where sensitive names sit alongside benign metadata waiting to be chopped out with a digital knife. Instead, as Judge Katsas notes citing *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. VA*, if non-exempt material is scattered throughout a document such that isolating it would require reconstructing or exposing the very structure the government seeks to protect, no segregation is required. The court emphasized that FOIA focuses on "information," not just "words which the Government has written down." In other words, a list of dates might seem harmless in isolation; but if those dates map out surveillance timelines or communication windows critical to an ongoing counterterrorism probe, releasing them defeats the purpose of Exemption 7(A).

This ruling carries significant weight for future litigants challenging broad withholdings in national security cases. It effectively lowers the barrier for agencies to claim total exemption for complex files by validating a holistic view of documents rather than a granular one. For decades following *Mead*, courts have struggled with how much effort agencies must expend to carve out redactions before declaring a document wholly exempt. Some circuits have pushed for aggressive redaction regimes; others have deferred more readily to agency assertions about contextual harm.*Farahi* clearly aligns with the latter approach when national security is implicated. By noting that Farahi failed to identify specific instances where segregation was feasible—and acknowledging that his own concessions regarding his terrorist ties bolstered the FBI's claim—the court suggests that challengers bear a heavy burden to prove otherwise when dealing with classified or highly sensitive operational files.

Furthermore, *Farahi* underscores the continued reliance on *ex parte* submissions as an indispensable tool in resolving these disputes without compromising secrecy itself. The judge defended the district court's reliance on sealed declarations from FBI officials detailing why segregation was impossible—a practice critics sometimes argue handicaps plaintiffs by preventing them from testing agency claims against specific examples.*Farahi* reaffirms that this handicap is inherent and necessary: revealing exactly what can be cut out often reveals too much about what remains hidden.*Katsas writes plainly that relying on these private submissions does not violate fairness when they are "the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

Ultimately,*Farahi*v.FBIdoes little new ground regarding whether an investigation counts as "pending" after two decades; lower courts have long recognized that long-running terrorism probes fit this definition.*What it solidifies is procedural posture regarding segregability.*It tells us that when an agency asserts that releasing fragments would unravel an entire investigative tapestry,*courts should believe them unless shown concrete evidence to contrary.*In an era where big data analytics make even trivial correlations potentially dangerous,*this deference ensures that FOIA remains flexible enough to accommodate legitimate law enforcement needs without becoming a blunt instrument for transparency at all costs.*For advocates seeking access to intelligence files,*the path forward looks increasingly narrow:*they must now demonstrate not just that some text exists outside exemptions,*but that extracting it poses no conceptual risk whatsoever—a bar set exceptionally high by today's decision.*
