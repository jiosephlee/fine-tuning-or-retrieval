Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) allow private employees to sue for 'average daily wage penalties' when the statute explicitly vests that enforcement power in the Commissioner?
Question:
In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the court affirmed summary judgment against Cohen's MPWA claim because § 181.101(a) states that only the Commissioner can impose and collect penalties after serving a demand for payment. The court noted that while an employee can sue for back wages, they cannot sue for penalties unless the statute explicitly grants them that right (as seen in discharge/quit scenarios under §§ 181.13 and 181.14). I understand the statutory construction argument ('expressio unius'), but does this mean that even if an employer clearly violates the wage timing rules, a private plaintiff is completely barred from recovering statutory penalties under this specific section, regardless of the harm suffered? Is there any exception where a private suit for penalties is permitted under MPWA § 181.032 or elsewhere?
Answer:
Based on the Eighth Circuit's decision in *Cohen v. Consilio*, the short answer is **yes**: under Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) § 181.101, a private employee is completely barred from recovering "average daily wage" penalties, even if the employer clearly violated the statute and caused harm. The right to seek these specific penalties is exclusively vested in the Commissioner of Labor.

Here is the breakdown of the legal reasoning provided by the court:

**1. Exclusive Enforcement Authority Under § 181.101**
The court held that the text of Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) creates a distinct procedural mechanism for penalties that excludes private litigants. While the statute allows an employee to "prosecute a claim for wages," it explicitly limits the imposition and collection of penalties to the Commissioner. Specifically, penalties only attach if:
*   The Commissioner serves a demand for payment on behalf of an employee; AND
*   The employer fails to pay within ten days of that service; AND
*   The Commissioner subsequently charges and collects the penalty.

Because the statute does not contain language granting an individual employee the right to sue for these specific penalties, and because it outlines a specific administrative process involving the Commissioner, private plaintiffs cannot bypass this framework to seek "average daily wage" penalties directly in court under this section (*Cohen*, slip op. at 6).

**2. Application of *Expressio Unius***
The court reinforced this conclusion using statutory construction principles similar to *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*. The Minnesota legislature demonstrated it knows how to grant private rights to seek penalties when it intends to do so. For example, under §§ 181.13 (discharge) and 181.14 (resignation/quitting), the statute explicitly states that an aggrieved employee "may charge and collect a penalty."

By contrast, § 181.101 contains no such authorization for employees regarding late-payment penalties outside of termination contexts; instead, it reserves that power solely for the Commissioner (*Cohen*, slip op. at 6–7). Therefore, absent explicit statutory language granting a private cause of action for penalties under § 181.032 or elsewhere applicable to general wage timing violations, no such right exists for private suits under this specific provision.

**3. Distinction Between Wages and Penalties**
It is important to note what *is* available to private plaintiffs versus what is not:
*   **Private Right:** Employees retain an absolute right to sue for **back wages** (compensatory damages) under § 181.032 or via § 181.77 (civil action for redress). The court confirmed that § 181.77 allows employees to recover damages "provided for in the section violated," but since § 181.101 does not provide a penalty remedy for employees, none can be recovered privately (*Cohen*, slip op. at 5–6).
*   **Exclusive Right:** Only the Commissioner can trigger and collect the statutory penalty portion of an MPWA violation under § 181.032/§ 24-2079 context regarding general untimely payment (§ 24-2079 context implies general wage timing rules).

**Conclusion**
Under *Cohen v. Consilio*, there is no exception allowing a private plaintiff to recover average daily wage penalties under MPWA § 24-2079 or related sections when enforcing general wage timing rules like those in § 24-2079(a). Even with clear proof of violation and resulting harm, the remedy is limited to unpaid wages; any statutory penalty assessment must be pursued administratively by the Commissioner, not through a private civil suit by the employee regarding those specific provisions (*Cohen*, slip op. at 6–7).

### Can a plaintiff maintain Article III standing to seek civil penalties payable to the state if their individual monetary damages (overtime and liquidated damages) have already been fully satisfied via settlement?
Question:
The core of my confusion lies in Part II(B) of the opinion. Cohen stipulated he was paid all overtime and liquidated damages, leaving only civil penalties (payable to the Commissioner) disputed. The appellate court expressed concern about mootness/standing because Cohen lacks a direct financial stake in penalties paid to the government. However, citing *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, civil penalties often serve as a deterrent against future violations, which theoretically gives a plaintiff standing even without personal monetary recovery. Why did the Eighth Circuit seem hesitant here compared to *Laidlaw*? Does the fact that these are Minnesota state law penalties rather than federal environmental fines change the standing analysis regarding 'redressability'?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**  
Yes, a plaintiff can generally maintain Article III standing to seek civil penalties payable to the state even after their individual damages are satisfied, *but* only if they can show a "real and immediate" threat that the violation will recur. The Eighth Circuit's hesitation in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* stems not from the fact that the penalties are state-law based, but from the specific procedural posture: because Cohen stipulated his personal damages were paid and the defendant claimed the policy changed, the court lacked sufficient factual findings to determine if the controversy remained "live." Unlike *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, where ongoing violations were evident, *Cohen* requires a threshold determination of whether voluntary cessation has rendered the claim moot before addressing redressability.

---

### Analysis

#### 1. The General Rule: Penalties as Deterrence Confer Standing
You correctly identify that under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), civil penalties payable to the government can satisfy Article III standing requirements even when they do not compensate the plaintiff directly. The Supreme Court reasoned that such penalties serve a deterrent function, discouraging future violations and thereby providing "redress" to plaintiffs who suffer from ongoing unlawful conduct.

In *Cohen*, the Eighth Circuit explicitly acknowledges this principle in Part II(B):
> "Civil penalties... may 'afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct' because such penalties may 'encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.'" (*Cohen*, citing *Laidlaw*).

Therefore, **the source of the penalty (state vs. federal)** or **who receives it (plaintiff vs. state)** does not automatically destroy standing. The critical inquiry is always whether there is an actual controversy regarding future harm or ongoing illegality.

#### 2. Why *Cohen* Differs from *Laidlaw*: The Mootness Threshold
Your confusion likely arises from why the Eighth Circuit seemed skeptical despite citing *Laidlaw*. The distinction lies in **voluntary cessation** and **mootness**, not redressability itself.

In *Laidlaw*, the defendant continued violating environmental laws during litigation, making it clear that penalties would deter future conduct. In *Cohen*, however, two key facts complicated the standing/mootness analysis:
1.  **Stipulation of Full Compensation:** Cohen stipulated he was paid all overtime and liquidated damages, removing his personal financial stake in those specific remedies.
2.  **Dispute Over Policy Status:** While Cohen argued the policy was still active, Consilio implied it might have changed (or at least argued against liability).

The Eighth Circuit noted:
> "[T]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." (*Cohen*, quoting *Arizonans for Official English*).

Because Cohen had no outstanding personal damages left to recover, his sole basis for standing became his fear of future violations by Consilio under the MFLSA. Under Supreme Court precedent (*United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Export Ass'n*), a claim becomes moot if it becomes **"absolutely clear"** that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur (*Cohen*, citing *Concentrated Phosphate*).

The Eighth Circuit did not rule that Cohen *lacked* standing for penalties per se; rather, it ruled that it could not yet decide if he retained standing because **it was unclear whether Conslilo's policy was still in place.** Without knowing if the violation was ongoing or permanently ceased, the court could not determine if a judgment imposing penalties would actually provide redress (by deterring future acts) or if it would merely be an advisory opinion on past conduct (which is prohibited).

#### 3. Procedural Posture Dictates Outcome
The court's solution highlights this nuance: they **vacated** and **remanded** specifically for the district court to determine jurisdiction based on whether Conslilo's violative policy remains in effect (*Cohen*, Part II.B).

If Conslilo proves on remand that it changed its policy voluntarily and irreversibly ("absolutely clear"), then Cohen loses standing/mootness applies regardless of whether he wants state penalties. Conversely, if Conslilo admits or fails to prove that its old policy persists, then under *Laidlaw*, Cohen regains standing because penalties would serve as a necessary deterrent against recurrence.

### Conclusion
The Eighth Circuit was hesitant not because state-law penalties are inherently insufficient for standing, but because **standing depends on a live controversy regarding future conduct.** Since Cohen conceded all past damages were paid, his entire case hinged on proving Conslilo would violate the law again tomorrow. Until that factual predicate is established on remand, any award of civil penalties risks being an impermissible advisory opinion on mooted conduct.

### What is the legal standard for determining if a claim becomes 'voluntarily ceased' versus 'absolutely clear' that violations will not recur in a state labor law context?
Question:
The court vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalty claim because it wasn't clear if Consilio's overtime policy was still in place or if it was just temporarily stopped during litigation. The opinion cites *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle* stating voluntary cessation doesn't automatically strip jurisdiction, but then asks if it is 'absolutely clear' behavior won't recur. How does a district court practically make this determination on remand? Do they look at internal corporate memos, public statements by management, or just whether the written policy document has been officially revoked? Since this is a class action context (even though it seems to have stalled), does the potential impact on other employees affect whether Cohen has standing to pursue these penalties?
Answer:
To determine whether a claim for statutory penalties is moot due to "voluntary cessation," the district court on remand must apply the heavy burden established in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, which requires the defendant to show that it is **"absolutely clear" that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur**.

**1. The Legal Standard: Voluntary Cessation vs. Absolute Clarity**
As noted in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, citing *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle*, a defendant's mere decision to stop a challenged practice does not automatically render a case moot or strip the court of jurisdiction. A defendant cannot voluntarily cease an illegal practice solely to evade judicial review and then resume it once the case is dismissed. Therefore, the presumption favors continued jurisdiction unless the defendant meets the stringent "absolute clarity" test.

On remand, the district court must evaluate whether Consilio has met this high burden. The inquiry is not merely whether the specific violation has stopped for Cohen individually (since he was already paid), but whether there is any reasonable expectation that Consilio will revert to its pre-litigation overtime policy for current or future employees.

**2. Practical Application: What Evidence Matters?**
While the *Cohen* opinion does not list specific evidentiary requirements for this factual determination, applying general principles of voluntary cessation jurisprudence suggests the court should look beyond simple silence or temporary pauses in enforcement. To prove it is "absolutely clear" violations will not recur, Consilio likely needs to provide objective evidence of permanent structural changes, such as:
*   **Formal Revocation:** Evidence that the 2019 policy eliminating overtime premiums has been formally rescinded and replaced with a compliant policy in writing.
*   **Public Commitments:** Official statements by management or updates to employee handbooks indicating a permanent shift away from the disputed practices.
*   **Internal Governance:** Corporate memoranda or board resolutions demonstrating that the previous policy is permanently abandoned and that compliance mechanisms are now institutionalized.

Conversely, if Consilio argues they simply paused payments during litigation without changing their official stance or policies, this would likely fail the "absolute clarity" test. As stated in *Laidlaw*, courts are skeptical of temporary halts designed to moot claims while leaving the underlying policy intact for post-litigation resumption.

**3. Impact on Class Context and Standing**
Regarding your question about class action implications: While *Cohen* notes Cohen's individual receipt of back pay satisfied his claim for wages and liquidated damages, his standing for **statutory penalties** depends entirely on whether he retains a personal stake in ensuring future compliance (*Town of Chester*). 

Even though Cohen is named as a representative of a putative class, standing analysis generally focuses on whether *this specific plaintiff* has suffered an injury capable of being redressed by a judgment against *this specific defendant*. If it is "absolutely clear" that Consilio will never again violate the MFLSA (e.g., because they have permanently changed their system-wide policy), then Cohen can no longer claim he faces an imminent threat of injury from future violations, regardless of how many other employees might theoretically be affected later. The mootness doctrine prevents courts from issuing advisory opinions on hypothetical future harms; thus, if recurrence is impossible, Cohen lacks standing for prospective relief like injunctions or penalties intended to deter future conduct.

However, if there remains ambiguity—for instance, if Consilio retains discretion to reinstate old policies—the court should find that it is *not* absolutely clear violations won't recur, thereby preserving jurisdiction over Cohen's penalty claim despite his individual compensation being complete.

### Did Cohen waive his MWTA claim regarding failure-to-notify penalties by failing to cite specific statutory authority in his appellate brief?
Question:
Regarding Part II(C), Cohen argued Consilio violated the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) by failing to provide notice of policy changes, yet he didn't point to any specific subsection granting him a right to civil penalties for this specific violation. The court dismissed this as waived under *Milligan*. My confusion is procedural: Did Cohen actually fail to identify *any* authority, or did he argue generally that 'penalties are available' without pinpointing the exact text? If he had cited § 181.032(d)(4) broadly claiming it allows penalties, would that have been sufficient to survive waiver, or was his failure to distinguish between 'wage recovery' and 'civil penalty recovery' fatal regardless of citation?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the short answer is **yes**, Cohen waived his MWTA claim regarding failure-to-notify penalties specifically because he failed to cite or argue any statutory authority supporting that remedy.

Here is the breakdown of the procedural failure based on the court's reasoning in Part II(C):

**1. The Nature of the Waiver**
The court explicitly applied the waiver doctrine from *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000). Under *Milligan*, an issue is waived if a party "does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority."

In Paragraph C of the opinion, the court notes two distinct failures by Cohen:
*   He pointed to **"no statutory authority"** allowing for civil penalties under the MWTA for this specific violation.
*   Consequently, he offered **"no meaningful argument in support of reversal."**

Because Cohen failed to identify *which* subsection of the MWTA authorized a private right of action for civil penalties regarding notice violations (as opposed to just stating penalties were generally available), the appellate court refused to address the merits. The court treated this as a total abandonment of that specific legal theory due to insufficient briefing.

**2. Would a Broad Citation Have Saved It?**
It is highly unlikely that merely citing § 181.032(d)(4) broadly would have saved the claim if Cohen failed to explain *why* that subsection supported civil penalties rather than just wage recovery or administrative enforcement.

The Eighth Circuit's analysis suggests that mere citation without analytical linkage constitutes waiver. In Part II(A), regarding the MPWA claim, the court engaged deeply with statutory text (§ 181.101 vs. § 181.171) because Cohen *argued* that "plain language" authorized penalties and then refuted that argument using specific statutory construction principles (e.g., noting that § 181.101 only empowers the Commissioner).

By contrast, in Part II(C), Cohen did not make this type of argumentative bridge for the MWTA claim. He simply asserted entitlement without pointing to the mechanism in § 181.032(d)(4) that would allow an individual employee to recover civil penalties for a notice violation (as opposed to other remedies like actual damages). Had he cited § 181.032(d)(4) but failed to distinguish between "wage recovery" and "civil penalty recovery"—or failed to explain why that subsection created a private right of action for penalties—the result likely would have remained a waiver under *Milligan*. The court requires counsel to do more than list statutes; they must articulate how those statutes apply to their specific requested relief.

**Conclusion**
Cohen's failure was not just a technical omission of a page number; it was a failure to develop an argument connecting his requested relief (civil penalties) to a specific statutory grant of authority within the MWTA framework. Because he provided neither citation nor analysis distinguishing penalty recovery from other potential remedies under that act, the court deemed the issue waived and declined to review it substantively.

### Is an injunctive relief claim considered waived if raised for the first time in summary judgment briefing rather than through an amended complaint?
Question:
In Part II(D), Cohen tried to shift his requested injunctive relief from his original complaint (which asked for specific orders) to asking simply for an order rescinding the illegal policy during summary judgment motions. The court said this new theory wasn't properly before them because Rule 15 requires amended complaints for new claims/theories. Isn't arguing that an existing policy must be rescinded just applying existing facts to existing law? Why isn't this treated as merely clarifying a prayer for relief based on undisputed facts rather than raising a brand new cause of action? What is the line between 'clarification' and 'new claim' in this context?
Answer:
Based on the Eighth Circuit's decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the short answer is **yes**: attempting to raise a new theory for injunctive relief for the first time in summary judgment briefing, rather than through an amended complaint, results in that claim being deemed not "properly before" the court.

Here is the breakdown of why the court drew this line between "clarification" and a "new claim," and why Rule 15 controls this scenario.

### The Procedural Barrier: Rule 15 vs. Summary Judgment Briefing
The core holding in Part II(D) rests on Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), which governs amendments to pleadings. The court explicitly rejected Cohen's attempt to pivot his request for relief mid-stream. 

In his original complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief (likely aimed at enforcing compliance or preventing future violations). However, during summary judgment briefing, he shifted his position to ask solely for an order **"rescind[ing]"** the illegal overtime policy. The appellate court noted that the district court "understandably... did not consider it." Consequently, the Eighth Circuit ruled that this specific request was not properly before them because **"New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs."** (*InfoDeli, LLC*, cited at 801 n.6).

### Clarification vs. New Claim
You asked why requesting rescission isn't viewed as merely "clarifying a prayer for relief" based on undisputed facts. Under *Cohen*, the distinction lies in **notice and procedural fairness**.

1.  **Substantive Shift:** While both requests involve injunctive relief, changing from general enforcement to a specific order of rescission alters the nature of the remedy sought. More importantly, it introduces a new factual or legal posture regarding what constitutes appropriate relief that was not litigated or framed in the initial pleadings.
2.  **The Function of Pleadings:** The purpose of Rule 15 is to ensure that parties have fair notice of the claims and theories against them *before* significant resources are expended on discovery and motion practice. By waiting until summary judgment briefing to argue for rescission, Cohen deprived Consilio of the opportunity to frame their defense specifically around that remedy or to conduct discovery relevant to whether rescission was necessary or feasible at that stage.
3.  **Judicial Economy:** Allowing parties to inject new theories into their final pre-trial arguments undermines the summary judgment process itself. If every party could redefine their requested relief after discovery closed but before judgment, it would effectively bypass the amendment process entirely.

### Conclusion
The line between clarification and a new claim in this context is drawn at **Rule 15**. A mere clarification adjusts *how* an already pleaded claim is supported; it does not alter *what* is being asked for if that alteration changes the scope of liability or remedy in a way inconsistent with the original pleading's framework. 

Because Cohen attempted to swap his requested remedy (from specific orders generally to a specific order of rescission) without filing an amended complaint, the Eighth Circuit treated this as a new claim raised too late. As stated in Part II(D), such issues are waived for appellate review because they were never properly presented to the trial court via an amended pleading as required by Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).

### How does Justice Kobes's concurrence suggest handling attorney's fees and prejudgment interest when liability itself is uncertain due to mootness concerns?
Question:
Justice Kobes concurred specially noting she wasn't convinced Cohen had standing under *Laidlaw* but joined anyway because she agreed with vacating on mootness grounds. She also mentioned vacating rulings on attorney's fees and prejudgment interest without explaining why those were affected by her doubts about standing/mootness regarding penalties specifically. If a plaintiff loses standing on their primary claim (penalties), do ancillary claims like fees automatically fall too? Or was she suggesting that since we don't know if Cohen has standing at all, we can't award fees until jurisdiction is confirmed on remand?
Answer:
Justice Kobes's concurrence suggests that when the viability of a primary claim (such as statutory penalties) is clouded by potential mootness or standing defects, ancillary claims for attorney's fees and prejudgment interest cannot be adjudicated until the threshold jurisdictional questions are resolved.

Her approach rests on the principle that a federal court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction to award fees or interest if the underlying claim to which those awards attach is potentially moot. While the majority vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA penalties solely to allow the district court to determine if the case remained live under *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw*, Justice Kobes explicitly extended this logic to fee-shifting and interest provisions.

By stating she "also vacate[s] the district court's ruling as to attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest," Justice Kobes indicates that these ancillary remedies are derivative of the primary liability claim. Since neither party briefed the specific standing analysis regarding *Laidlaw* penalties versus MFLSA penalties, and because a finding of mootness would strip the court of power entirely, it would be premature to rule on monetary awards dependent on that liability. Her instruction that "[o]n remand, the district court may decide the mootness question first" confirms her view: jurisdiction must be affirmed before any remedy—including fees—can be calculated or awarded. Until it is "absolutely clear" that wrongful conduct will not recur (or conversely, established that it might), the court cannot exercise its discretion to grant financial relief tied to a claim that may not exist in federal court.
