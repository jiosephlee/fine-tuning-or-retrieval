Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## Procedural Pitfalls: When Waiver and Improper Pleading Sink a Wage Claim

In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided October 6, 2025, the Eighth Circuit delivered a mixed bag for a plaintiff who successfully secured backpay and liquidated damages but ultimately lost his fight for statutory penalties and injunctive relief on appeal. While the case touches on significant state labor law questions regarding the availability of "average daily wage" penalties under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the decision offers perhaps more immediate value to litigators as a cautionary tale on procedural hygiene. Two distinct procedural missteps by the appellant—failing to cite legal authority for a statutory claim and attempting to raise new requests for injunctive relief during summary judgment briefing—resulted in affirmances that bypassed substantive analysis entirely. For practitioners navigating complex employment disputes, *Cohen* underscores that even meritorious underlying facts can be forfeited through inadequate briefing and improper pleading practices.

The court's treatment of Bruce Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) illustrates the harsh reality of appellate waiver. Cohen argued that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice regarding changes to its overtime policy, seeking penalties as a remedy. However, when reviewing this claim, the Eighth Circuit noted that while the district court had offered little discussion on the issue, Cohen's own appellate brief contained "little to no discussion." More critically, Cohen pointed to no statutory authority supporting his assertion that civil penalties were available under the specific subsections of the MWTA he cited. Invoking *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355 (8th Cir. 2000), Judge Kelly wrote that where a defendant fails to support an assertion with argument or legal authority, the issue is deemed waived. Consequently, the court declined to disturb the district court's dismissal of the claim. This outcome serves as a stark reminder that in federal appellate practice, especially within circuits like the Eighth that strictly adhere to *Milligan*, a party cannot expect an appellate court to act as counsel by scouring statutes or constructing arguments sua sponte. A claim unsupported by citation is effectively dead on arrival.

Equally instructive is the court's rejection of Cohen's attempt to pivot his requested relief late in the game regarding injunctive measures. Throughout much of his complaint, Cohen sought broad injunctive relief, yet during summary judgment briefing—a stage typically reserved for arguing existing claims rather than reshaping them—he shifted gears to request a very specific affirmative order: that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy entirely. The Eighth Circuit found this new request was not properly before it because it had never been raised in an amended complaint or through proper pre-trial procedures. Citing Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and recent precedent (*InfoDeli, LLC*), the panel emphasized that new claims or fundamentally altered requests for relief must be introduced via amendment, not buried in opposition briefs filed after discovery has closed and motions are pending. By waiting until summary judgment briefing to articulate this specific demand for policy rescission, Cohen deprived Consilio of fair notice and opportunity to respond, leading the court to dismiss the issue without reaching its merits.

These procedural rulings sit alongside more substantive holdings in *Cohen*. The court affirmed that private individuals cannot seek "average daily wage" penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 because that statute explicitly reserves enforcement power for the Commissioner of Labor; conversely, it vacated and remanded on a separate MFLSA penalty claim due to unresolved Article III standing concerns following partial settlement payments. Yet, it is easy to overlook how easily Cohen could have preserved these arguments had he adhered strictly to procedural norms. The distinction between losing on a technicality versus losing on a substantive interpretation of state labor law is often thin but decisive in outcomes like this one.

For employment litigators drafting complaints and managing discovery timelines, *Cohen* reinforces two fundamental principles: first, every asserted remedy must be anchored by explicit statutory citations and developed legal argumentation in initial filings; second, if your theory of liability shifts or your desired remedy evolves significantly during litigation, do not wait for summary judgment briefings—you must amend your pleadings early enough to give opposing counsel adequate time to adjust their strategy. In an era where courts are increasingly skeptical of judicial efficiency being undermined by last-minute tactical pivots, *Cohen* stands as a clear warning: procedure is not merely administrative; it is often dispositive.
