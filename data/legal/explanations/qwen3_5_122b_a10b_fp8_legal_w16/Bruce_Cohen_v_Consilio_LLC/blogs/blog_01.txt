Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## The 'Know How' Doctrine: Why Minnesota Employees Can't Sue for Average Daily Wage Penalties Under § 181.101

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit delivered a sharp reminder of the perils of relying on broad statutory interpretations in wage-and-hour litigation. While the underlying facts involve a standard dispute over overtime pay for legal document reviewers, the appellate court's resolution turns on a much narrower, yet profoundly consequential, point of statutory construction: Does a private employee have the right to sue for "average daily wage" penalties under Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act (MPWA)? The answer, according to Judge Kelly's opinion, is a resounding no. By deploying a rigorous textualist analysis and leaning heavily on the canon that legislatures "know how" to draft what they intend, the court has effectively bifurcated Minnesota's wage enforcement regime, reserving the hammer of civil penalties for the Commissioner of Labor while leaving individual employees with only their base backpay.

The case arose after Consilio, a national legal document review firm, notified its Minnesota-based attorneys that it was eliminating premium overtime pay effective August 2019. When Cohen sued on behalf of himself and others alleging violations of the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA, Consilio moved to settle the core monetary disputes before trial. They paid Cohen his owed overtime and liquidated damages—totaling just over $3,600 for him personally—and stipulated that these sums satisfied all claims for actual wages and double damages under the MFLSA. However, Cohen pressed forward on two fronts: he sought substantial statutory penalties totaling nearly $258,000 under various state statutes and requested injunctive relief to force Consilio to rescind its new policy.

The Eighth Circuit's handling of Cohen's MPWA claim offers a masterclass in statutory parsing. Cohen argued that § 181.171 allowed him to seek "civil penalties or damages... provided for in the section" violated—specifically pointing to § 181.101 as the source of liability for unpaid wages. The problem for Cohen was that § 181.101 itself contains no mechanism for an employee to trigger those penalties directly against an employer in court. Instead, Judge Kelly noted that § 181.101(a) explicitly vests the power to impose and collect penalties solely in the hands of the Commissioner of Labor following a formal demand process by that agency.

Here is where the court applied what legal scholars often call the "expressio unius" principle or, more colloquially in this context, the "know how" doctrine. The opinion highlights that if the Minnesota legislature intended for private citizens to recover daily wage penalties under § 181.101, it knew exactly how to say so elsewhere in the code. Specifically, §§ 181.13 and 181.14—which govern situations where an employee is discharged or voluntarily quits—explicitly authorize employees to "charge and collect a penalty." In contrast, § 181.032 (governing general failure to pay) lacks this language entirely. Citing *Lagasse v. Horton* and *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, the court reasoned that this disparity cannot be accidental; when one statute grants a right and another omits it despite covering similar ground, silence implies exclusion rather than inclusion by oversight. Consequently, because only the Commissioner can initiate penalty collection under § 181.032/§ 181.032(a), Cohen had no standing as a private litigant to pursue them in federal court based on those specific provisions alone.

While affirming dismissal on MPWA grounds was straightforward due to this textual gap, the court took a more nuanced approach toward Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). Here lies perhaps the most significant procedural twist in *Cohen*. Although Consilio had already paid out all disputed wages and liquidated damages—a move that seemingly mooted Cohen's primary grievance—the statute allows for civil penalties payable not to Cohen, but directly to state authorities via fines imposed by courts or administrative bodies depending on interpretation (though here treated as potentially payable to state coffers).

Judge Kelly expressed deep skepticism about whether federal courts retained jurisdiction over this claim once personal compensation was satisfied but before determining if Consilio's policy remained active enough to threaten recurrence. Citing *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, the court acknowledged that civil penalties can sometimes sustain standing by deterring future misconduct even if past harm is remedied by restitution ("redressability"). However, because it was unclear whether Consilio had permanently abandoned its unlawful overtime policy or merely paused it pending litigation outcomes, declaring mootness immediately would be premature—but granting summary judgment without resolving jurisdictional questions was equally improper given Article III constraints against advisory opinions on hypothetical harms that may never recur. Thus came a rare vacatur-and-remand specifically tasked with answering threshold jurisdictional questions before any merits determination could proceed further down line regarding potential future liabilities tied purely deterrent purposes rather than direct compensation flows back into plaintiff pockets themselves!
