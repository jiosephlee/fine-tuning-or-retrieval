Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Is an injunctive relief claim considered waived if raised for the first time in summary judgment briefing rather than through an amended complaint?
Question:
In Part II(D), Cohen tried to shift his requested injunctive relief from his original complaint (which asked for specific orders) to asking simply for an order rescinding the illegal policy during summary judgment motions. The court said this new theory wasn't properly before them because Rule 15 requires amended complaints for new claims/theories. Isn't arguing that an existing policy must be rescinded just applying existing facts to existing law? Why isn't this treated as merely clarifying a prayer for relief based on undisputed facts rather than raising a brand new cause of action? What is the line between 'clarification' and 'new claim' in this context?
Answer:
Based on the Eighth Circuit's decision in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the short answer is **yes**: attempting to raise a new theory for injunctive relief for the first time in summary judgment briefing, rather than through an amended complaint, results in that claim being deemed not "properly before" the court.

Here is the breakdown of why the court drew this line between "clarification" and a "new claim," and why Rule 15 controls this scenario.

### The Procedural Barrier: Rule 15 vs. Summary Judgment Briefing
The core holding in Part II(D) rests on Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), which governs amendments to pleadings. The court explicitly rejected Cohen's attempt to pivot his request for relief mid-stream. 

In his original complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief (likely aimed at enforcing compliance or preventing future violations). However, during summary judgment briefing, he shifted his position to ask solely for an order **"rescind[ing]"** the illegal overtime policy. The appellate court noted that the district court "understandably... did not consider it." Consequently, the Eighth Circuit ruled that this specific request was not properly before them because **"New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs."** (*InfoDeli, LLC*, cited at 801 n.6).

### Clarification vs. New Claim
You asked why requesting rescission isn't viewed as merely "clarifying a prayer for relief" based on undisputed facts. Under *Cohen*, the distinction lies in **notice and procedural fairness**.

1.  **Substantive Shift:** While both requests involve injunctive relief, changing from general enforcement to a specific order of rescission alters the nature of the remedy sought. More importantly, it introduces a new factual or legal posture regarding what constitutes appropriate relief that was not litigated or framed in the initial pleadings.
2.  **The Function of Pleadings:** The purpose of Rule 15 is to ensure that parties have fair notice of the claims and theories against them *before* significant resources are expended on discovery and motion practice. By waiting until summary judgment briefing to argue for rescission, Cohen deprived Consilio of the opportunity to frame their defense specifically around that remedy or to conduct discovery relevant to whether rescission was necessary or feasible at that stage.
3.  **Judicial Economy:** Allowing parties to inject new theories into their final pre-trial arguments undermines the summary judgment process itself. If every party could redefine their requested relief after discovery closed but before judgment, it would effectively bypass the amendment process entirely.

### Conclusion
The line between clarification and a new claim in this context is drawn at **Rule 15**. A mere clarification adjusts *how* an already pleaded claim is supported; it does not alter *what* is being asked for if that alteration changes the scope of liability or remedy in a way inconsistent with the original pleading's framework. 

Because Cohen attempted to swap his requested remedy (from specific orders generally to a specific order of rescission) without filing an amended complaint, the Eighth Circuit treated this as a new claim raised too late. As stated in Part II(D), such issues are waived for appellate review because they were never properly presented to the trial court via an amended pleading as required by Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a).
