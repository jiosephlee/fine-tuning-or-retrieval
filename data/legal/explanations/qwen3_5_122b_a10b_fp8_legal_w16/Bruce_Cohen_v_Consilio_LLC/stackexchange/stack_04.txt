Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Did Cohen waive his MWTA claim regarding failure-to-notify penalties by failing to cite specific statutory authority in his appellate brief?
Question:
Regarding Part II(C), Cohen argued Consilio violated the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) by failing to provide notice of policy changes, yet he didn't point to any specific subsection granting him a right to civil penalties for this specific violation. The court dismissed this as waived under *Milligan*. My confusion is procedural: Did Cohen actually fail to identify *any* authority, or did he argue generally that 'penalties are available' without pinpointing the exact text? If he had cited § 181.032(d)(4) broadly claiming it allows penalties, would that have been sufficient to survive waiver, or was his failure to distinguish between 'wage recovery' and 'civil penalty recovery' fatal regardless of citation?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the short answer is **yes**, Cohen waived his MWTA claim regarding failure-to-notify penalties specifically because he failed to cite or argue any statutory authority supporting that remedy.

Here is the breakdown of the procedural failure based on the court's reasoning in Part II(C):

**1. The Nature of the Waiver**
The court explicitly applied the waiver doctrine from *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000). Under *Milligan*, an issue is waived if a party "does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority."

In Paragraph C of the opinion, the court notes two distinct failures by Cohen:
*   He pointed to **"no statutory authority"** allowing for civil penalties under the MWTA for this specific violation.
*   Consequently, he offered **"no meaningful argument in support of reversal."**

Because Cohen failed to identify *which* subsection of the MWTA authorized a private right of action for civil penalties regarding notice violations (as opposed to just stating penalties were generally available), the appellate court refused to address the merits. The court treated this as a total abandonment of that specific legal theory due to insufficient briefing.

**2. Would a Broad Citation Have Saved It?**
It is highly unlikely that merely citing § 181.032(d)(4) broadly would have saved the claim if Cohen failed to explain *why* that subsection supported civil penalties rather than just wage recovery or administrative enforcement.

The Eighth Circuit's analysis suggests that mere citation without analytical linkage constitutes waiver. In Part II(A), regarding the MPWA claim, the court engaged deeply with statutory text (§ 181.101 vs. § 181.171) because Cohen *argued* that "plain language" authorized penalties and then refuted that argument using specific statutory construction principles (e.g., noting that § 181.101 only empowers the Commissioner).

By contrast, in Part II(C), Cohen did not make this type of argumentative bridge for the MWTA claim. He simply asserted entitlement without pointing to the mechanism in § 181.032(d)(4) that would allow an individual employee to recover civil penalties for a notice violation (as opposed to other remedies like actual damages). Had he cited § 181.032(d)(4) but failed to distinguish between "wage recovery" and "civil penalty recovery"—or failed to explain why that subsection created a private right of action for penalties—the result likely would have remained a waiver under *Milligan*. The court requires counsel to do more than list statutes; they must articulate how those statutes apply to their specific requested relief.

**Conclusion**
Cohen's failure was not just a technical omission of a page number; it was a failure to develop an argument connecting his requested relief (civil penalties) to a specific statutory grant of authority within the MWTA framework. Because he provided neither citation nor analysis distinguishing penalty recovery from other potential remedies under that act, the court deemed the issue waived and declined to review it substantively.
