Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### How does Justice Kobes's concurrence suggest handling attorney's fees and prejudgment interest when liability itself is uncertain due to mootness concerns?
Question:
Justice Kobes concurred specially noting she wasn't convinced Cohen had standing under *Laidlaw* but joined anyway because she agreed with vacating on mootness grounds. She also mentioned vacating rulings on attorney's fees and prejudgment interest without explaining why those were affected by her doubts about standing/mootness regarding penalties specifically. If a plaintiff loses standing on their primary claim (penalties), do ancillary claims like fees automatically fall too? Or was she suggesting that since we don't know if Cohen has standing at all, we can't award fees until jurisdiction is confirmed on remand?
Answer:
Justice Kobes's concurrence suggests that when the viability of a primary claim (such as statutory penalties) is clouded by potential mootness or standing defects, ancillary claims for attorney's fees and prejudgment interest cannot be adjudicated until the threshold jurisdictional questions are resolved.

Her approach rests on the principle that a federal court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction to award fees or interest if the underlying claim to which those awards attach is potentially moot. While the majority vacated the summary judgment on the MFLSA penalties solely to allow the district court to determine if the case remained live under *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw*, Justice Kobes explicitly extended this logic to fee-shifting and interest provisions.

By stating she "also vacate[s] the district court's ruling as to attorney's fees, costs, and prejudgment interest," Justice Kobes indicates that these ancillary remedies are derivative of the primary liability claim. Since neither party briefed the specific standing analysis regarding *Laidlaw* penalties versus MFLSA penalties, and because a finding of mootness would strip the court of power entirely, it would be premature to rule on monetary awards dependent on that liability. Her instruction that "[o]n remand, the district court may decide the mootness question first" confirms her view: jurisdiction must be affirmed before any remedy—including fees—can be calculated or awarded. Until it is "absolutely clear" that wrongful conduct will not recur (or conversely, established that it might), the court cannot exercise its discretion to grant financial relief tied to a claim that may not exist in federal court.
