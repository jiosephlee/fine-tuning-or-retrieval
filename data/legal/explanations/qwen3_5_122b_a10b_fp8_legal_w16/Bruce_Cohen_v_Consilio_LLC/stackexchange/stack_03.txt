Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What is the legal standard for determining if a claim becomes 'voluntarily ceased' versus 'absolutely clear' that violations will not recur in a state labor law context?
Question:
The court vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalty claim because it wasn't clear if Consilio's overtime policy was still in place or if it was just temporarily stopped during litigation. The opinion cites *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle* stating voluntary cessation doesn't automatically strip jurisdiction, but then asks if it is 'absolutely clear' behavior won't recur. How does a district court practically make this determination on remand? Do they look at internal corporate memos, public statements by management, or just whether the written policy document has been officially revoked? Since this is a class action context (even though it seems to have stalled), does the potential impact on other employees affect whether Cohen has standing to pursue these penalties?
Answer:
To determine whether a claim for statutory penalties is moot due to "voluntary cessation," the district court on remand must apply the heavy burden established in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, which requires the defendant to show that it is **"absolutely clear" that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur**.

**1. The Legal Standard: Voluntary Cessation vs. Absolute Clarity**
As noted in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, citing *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle*, a defendant's mere decision to stop a challenged practice does not automatically render a case moot or strip the court of jurisdiction. A defendant cannot voluntarily cease an illegal practice solely to evade judicial review and then resume it once the case is dismissed. Therefore, the presumption favors continued jurisdiction unless the defendant meets the stringent "absolute clarity" test.

On remand, the district court must evaluate whether Consilio has met this high burden. The inquiry is not merely whether the specific violation has stopped for Cohen individually (since he was already paid), but whether there is any reasonable expectation that Consilio will revert to its pre-litigation overtime policy for current or future employees.

**2. Practical Application: What Evidence Matters?**
While the *Cohen* opinion does not list specific evidentiary requirements for this factual determination, applying general principles of voluntary cessation jurisprudence suggests the court should look beyond simple silence or temporary pauses in enforcement. To prove it is "absolutely clear" violations will not recur, Consilio likely needs to provide objective evidence of permanent structural changes, such as:
*   **Formal Revocation:** Evidence that the 2019 policy eliminating overtime premiums has been formally rescinded and replaced with a compliant policy in writing.
*   **Public Commitments:** Official statements by management or updates to employee handbooks indicating a permanent shift away from the disputed practices.
*   **Internal Governance:** Corporate memoranda or board resolutions demonstrating that the previous policy is permanently abandoned and that compliance mechanisms are now institutionalized.

Conversely, if Consilio argues they simply paused payments during litigation without changing their official stance or policies, this would likely fail the "absolute clarity" test. As stated in *Laidlaw*, courts are skeptical of temporary halts designed to moot claims while leaving the underlying policy intact for post-litigation resumption.

**3. Impact on Class Context and Standing**
Regarding your question about class action implications: While *Cohen* notes Cohen's individual receipt of back pay satisfied his claim for wages and liquidated damages, his standing for **statutory penalties** depends entirely on whether he retains a personal stake in ensuring future compliance (*Town of Chester*). 

Even though Cohen is named as a representative of a putative class, standing analysis generally focuses on whether *this specific plaintiff* has suffered an injury capable of being redressed by a judgment against *this specific defendant*. If it is "absolutely clear" that Consilio will never again violate the MFLSA (e.g., because they have permanently changed their system-wide policy), then Cohen can no longer claim he faces an imminent threat of injury from future violations, regardless of how many other employees might theoretically be affected later. The mootness doctrine prevents courts from issuing advisory opinions on hypothetical future harms; thus, if recurrence is impossible, Cohen lacks standing for prospective relief like injunctions or penalties intended to deter future conduct.

However, if there remains ambiguity—for instance, if Consilio retains discretion to reinstate old policies—the court should find that it is *not* absolutely clear violations won't recur, thereby preserving jurisdiction over Cohen's penalty claim despite his individual compensation being complete.
