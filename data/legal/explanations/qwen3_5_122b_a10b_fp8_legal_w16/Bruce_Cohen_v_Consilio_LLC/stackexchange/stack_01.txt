Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) allow private employees to sue for 'average daily wage penalties' when the statute explicitly vests that enforcement power in the Commissioner?
Question:
In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the court affirmed summary judgment against Cohen's MPWA claim because § 181.101(a) states that only the Commissioner can impose and collect penalties after serving a demand for payment. The court noted that while an employee can sue for back wages, they cannot sue for penalties unless the statute explicitly grants them that right (as seen in discharge/quit scenarios under §§ 181.13 and 181.14). I understand the statutory construction argument ('expressio unius'), but does this mean that even if an employer clearly violates the wage timing rules, a private plaintiff is completely barred from recovering statutory penalties under this specific section, regardless of the harm suffered? Is there any exception where a private suit for penalties is permitted under MPWA § 181.032 or elsewhere?
Answer:
Based on the Eighth Circuit's decision in *Cohen v. Consilio*, the short answer is **yes**: under Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) § 181.101, a private employee is completely barred from recovering "average daily wage" penalties, even if the employer clearly violated the statute and caused harm. The right to seek these specific penalties is exclusively vested in the Commissioner of Labor.

Here is the breakdown of the legal reasoning provided by the court:

**1. Exclusive Enforcement Authority Under § 181.101**
The court held that the text of Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) creates a distinct procedural mechanism for penalties that excludes private litigants. While the statute allows an employee to "prosecute a claim for wages," it explicitly limits the imposition and collection of penalties to the Commissioner. Specifically, penalties only attach if:
*   The Commissioner serves a demand for payment on behalf of an employee; AND
*   The employer fails to pay within ten days of that service; AND
*   The Commissioner subsequently charges and collects the penalty.

Because the statute does not contain language granting an individual employee the right to sue for these specific penalties, and because it outlines a specific administrative process involving the Commissioner, private plaintiffs cannot bypass this framework to seek "average daily wage" penalties directly in court under this section (*Cohen*, slip op. at 6).

**2. Application of *Expressio Unius***
The court reinforced this conclusion using statutory construction principles similar to *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*. The Minnesota legislature demonstrated it knows how to grant private rights to seek penalties when it intends to do so. For example, under §§ 181.13 (discharge) and 181.14 (resignation/quitting), the statute explicitly states that an aggrieved employee "may charge and collect a penalty."

By contrast, § 181.101 contains no such authorization for employees regarding late-payment penalties outside of termination contexts; instead, it reserves that power solely for the Commissioner (*Cohen*, slip op. at 6–7). Therefore, absent explicit statutory language granting a private cause of action for penalties under § 181.032 or elsewhere applicable to general wage timing violations, no such right exists for private suits under this specific provision.

**3. Distinction Between Wages and Penalties**
It is important to note what *is* available to private plaintiffs versus what is not:
*   **Private Right:** Employees retain an absolute right to sue for **back wages** (compensatory damages) under § 181.032 or via § 181.77 (civil action for redress). The court confirmed that § 181.77 allows employees to recover damages "provided for in the section violated," but since § 181.101 does not provide a penalty remedy for employees, none can be recovered privately (*Cohen*, slip op. at 5–6).
*   **Exclusive Right:** Only the Commissioner can trigger and collect the statutory penalty portion of an MPWA violation under § 181.032/§ 24-2079 context regarding general untimely payment (§ 24-2079 context implies general wage timing rules).

**Conclusion**
Under *Cohen v. Consilio*, there is no exception allowing a private plaintiff to recover average daily wage penalties under MPWA § 24-2079 or related sections when enforcing general wage timing rules like those in § 24-2079(a). Even with clear proof of violation and resulting harm, the remedy is limited to unpaid wages; any statutory penalty assessment must be pursued administratively by the Commissioner, not through a private civil suit by the employee regarding those specific provisions (*Cohen*, slip op. at 6–7).
