Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 5: # Waiver Issues Regarding MWTA Claims and Injunctive Relief

## Failure to Argue MWTA Penalty Authority Leads to Waiver Under *Milligan* Standard

The Eighth Circuit's treatment of Bruce Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) illustrates the critical importance of developing a coherent legal argument to avoid waiver. While the district court had provided "little to no discussion" of this specific claim in its summary judgment order, the appellate court declined to reverse based solely on that omission. Instead, the court focused on Cohen's own performance during the appeal process. To succeed on appeal, a party must not only identify an error but also provide a reasoned explanation supported by legal authority demonstrating why that error warrants reversal. In this instance, Cohen conceded that he had been paid all wages owed under the statute but nonetheless asserted entitlement to civil penalties. Crucially, however, he pointed to no statutory text or case law establishing a private right of action for such penalties under the MWTA.

Under established Eighth Circuit precedent, specifically *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355 (8th Cir. 2000), an appellant waives an issue if their brief fails to support their assertions with argument or legal authority. The court emphasized that it is not obligated to construct arguments for a litigant or search through statutes independently when the appellant has remained silent on the legal basis for their claim. By failing to cite any provision within Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032 that authorized civil penalties payable to an individual employee—and noting that unlike other sections of Minnesota employment law where such rights are explicit—the MWTA claim lacked a foundation sufficient to overcome summary judgment. Consequently, the appellate court affirmed the dismissal of this claim without reaching the merits, reinforcing the principle that appellate review is limited to arguments actually presented and supported by authority in the record.

## Procedural Defect in Raising New Requests for Injunctive Relief in Summary Judgment Briefs

A second ground for affirmance arose from Cohen's attempt to alter his requested remedy after the close of pleading stages but before trial. Initially, Cohen's complaint sought specific forms of injunctive relief regarding Consilio's overtime policies. However, during his briefing opposing summary judgment, Cohen attempted to pivot his request toward a different form of equitable relief: an "affirmative injunctive order" requiring Consilio to rescind its illegal overtime policy entirely. The district court correctly refused to consider this new request because it was introduced too late in the litigation process and outside the scope of the original pleadings allowed for adjudication at that stage.

The Eighth Circuit upheld this refusal by highlighting a fundamental distinction between defending against existing claims and introducing new ones. A party cannot use an opposition brief—a document designed to argue why facts do not warrant summary judgment—to effectively amend their complaint and assert novel theories or requests for relief that were never pleaded initially. When a litigant attempts to inject new claims or significantly altered prayers for relief at such a late juncture without following proper amendment procedures, those new matters are deemed forfeited for purposes of immediate judicial consideration. The court noted that allowing such tactics would undermine the orderly administration of justice and prejudice defendants who prepared their defenses based on the original scope of allegations filed years prior in the complaint.

## Rule 15(a) Requirements for Amending Complaints vs. Opposition Briefs

The procedural mechanism required to cure such defects lies squarely within Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a). This rule governs amendments to pleadings and establishes that while leave to amend shall be freely given when justice so requires, it must be sought through formal motions or by filing an amended complaint approved by consent or court order—not merely by asserting new positions within a motion response brief. As cited by the Eighth Circuit referencing *InfoDeli, LLC*, new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs; attempting otherwise constitutes a procedural violation that precludes judicial consideration of those new demands at that time.

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the failure to file a motion for leave to amend under Rule 15(a) meant that Cohen's revised request for injunctive relief remained legally invisible to the district court and unreviewable on appeal as a matter currently before it. The court reiterated that Rule 15 serves as a gatekeeper ensuring both parties have fair notice of what is being litigated and adequate opportunity to gather evidence relevant specifically to those issues. By bypassing this requirement and trying to sneak in a new remedy during summary judgment briefing, Cohen deprived Consilio of its right to respond adequately and prevented the district court from managing its docket efficiently according to procedural norms defined by federal law.
