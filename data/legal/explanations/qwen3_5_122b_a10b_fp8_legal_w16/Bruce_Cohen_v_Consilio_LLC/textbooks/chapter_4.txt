Chapter 4: # Jurisdictional Crisis: Standing, Mootness, and MFLSA Penalties

This chapter examines a pivotal procedural hurdle in *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*: the question of whether a plaintiff retains Article III standing to pursue statutory penalties after their individual economic injuries have been fully remedied. While the Eighth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Cohen's claims under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), it took a different approach toward his claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). The court recognized that even though Consilio paid Cohen all back wages and liquidated damages he was owed, a live controversy might still exist regarding his entitlement to civil penalties. This distinction turns on complex doctrines of standing and mootness, specifically how federal courts evaluate whether "voluntary cessation" of a violation truly ends a case or merely pauses it pending judicial resolution.

## Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Doctrine: Civil Penalties as a Remedy for Ongoing Unlawful Conduct

To understand why Cohen's claim survived where others failed, one must distinguish between compensatory damages and civil penalties. Compensatory damages, such as unpaid overtime wages, are designed to make an individual plaintiff whole for past harms. Once Consilio paid Cohen his owed wages and liquidated damages, Cohen's personal financial injury was cured. However, civil penalties serve a different purpose; they are punitive and deterrent measures intended to punish the defendant and discourage future violations of the law.

The Eighth Circuit relied heavily on the Supreme Court's decision in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000). Under *Laidlaw*, civil penalties can afford "redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The logic is that even if an individual plaintiff has been made whole financially, they retain a stake in ensuring the defendant stops breaking the law to prevent future harm to themselves or others. Therefore, unlike claims for back pay which may be rendered moot once paid out, a claim for civil penalties can keep a case alive if there is a possibility that the defendant's illegal conduct could recur. In *Cohen*, this meant that while Cohen lacked standing to sue for more money he had already received, he potentially retained standing to argue for penalties against Consilio if those penalties served as leverage to stop an ongoing policy violation.

## Town of Chester: Requirement That Standing Must Persist Throughout Litigation

While *Laidlaw* establishes that penalties can sustain jurisdiction, it does not guarantee it indefinitely. The court emphasized the principle derived from *Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433 (2017): "a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought." Crucially, standing is not just a threshold requirement met at the filing of the complaint; it must persist throughout the entire lifecycle of the litigation. The Eighth Circuit noted that "[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)."

If events occur during the litigation that eliminate the plaintiff's personal stake in the outcome—such as full payment of all disputed amounts—the claim becomes moot unless an exception applies. In *Cohen*, because Consilio voluntarily paid all outstanding wages and liquidated damages prior to summary judgment, there was no longer any monetary dispute between Cohen and Consilio regarding past work performed. Consequently, without analyzing whether criminal or civil penalties were still viable under *Laidlaw*, one might assume Cohen had lost his Article III standing entirely because he no longer suffered any concrete injury from Consilio's past actions. The court had to determine if this assumption held true or if specific exceptions preserved jurisdiction over the penalty claim alone.

## Voluntary Cessation Analysis: Is It 'Absolutely Clear' That Consilio's Overtime Policy Will Not Recur?

The central factual dispute preventing immediate dismissal on mootness grounds was whether Consilio had permanently abandoned its allegedly illegal overtime policy or merely paused enforcement while litigation was pending. Federal courts generally do not dismiss cases simply because a defendant promises to stop violating the law; this is known as the "voluntary cessation" doctrine. As cited in *Cohen*, quoting *United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Export Association*, 393 U.S. 199 (1968), a case may only be deemed moot if it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." Mere voluntary cessation does not automatically deprive a federal court of its power to decide legality (*City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle*).
