Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 3: # Statutory Interpretation Under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA)

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the Eighth Circuit faced a critical question of statutory interpretation regarding the scope of remedies available to private employees under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). Specifically, the court had to determine whether an individual employee could sue their employer directly for "average daily wage penalties" after failing to receive overtime compensation. The district court had granted summary judgment to Consilio, concluding that such penalties were unavailable to private litigants under the relevant statutes. The appellate court affirmed this decision, employing a rigorous textualist analysis that focused on the specific language of the MPWA and the structural contrasts within Minnesota's labor laws. This chapter dissects the court's reasoning, illustrating how statutory text dictates the allocation of enforcement powers between state officials and private citizens.

## Textual Analysis of Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171 and § 181.101(a)

The foundation of the court's analysis rests on a close reading of two interconnected provisions: Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171 and § 181.101(a). Cohen argued that § 181.032(f), which incorporates violations of other sections like § 181.101, allowed him to seek penalties because § 181.171 generally permits civil actions for violations of the MPWA and mandates liability for "civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated." The crux of Cohen's argument relied on interpreting "provided for in the section" broadly enough to include average daily wage penalties associated with wage theft.

However, the Eighth Circuit rejected this broad reading by examining the specific text of § 181.101(a), which governs unpaid wages for employees who have not been terminated or resigned—the category into which Cohen fell as an active document reviewer. The court noted that while § 181.032 allows an employee to prosecute a claim for *wages* themselves, it explicitly reserves penalties to a different actor. The statute states that if an employer fails to pay wages within ten days of a demand served by the **Commissioner** (the state official overseeing labor standards), then "the commissioner may charge and collect... a penalty." Crucially, § 181.032 clarifies that nothing prevents an employee from suing for wages, but it does not grant them parallel authority to initiate penalty collection procedures under this specific subsection. By adhering strictly to the "plain and unambiguous meaning" of the text, as required by Minnesota precedent, the court concluded that the statutory mechanism for triggering daily average wage penalties is exclusively administrative and initiated by the Commissioner, not judicially initiated by an aggrieved employee under this section.

## Exclusive Authority: The Role of the Commissioner Versus Private Employees

A central theme in *Cohen* is the distinction between public enforcement authority and private rights of action within statutory schemes designed to protect workers' compensation rights. The Eighth Circuit emphasized that while many labor statutes create hybrid systems allowing both private suits and government intervention, Congress—or in this case, the Minnesota Legislature—must clearly delineate where one ends and the other begins regarding monetary sanctions like penalties versus actual wages owed.

Under § 181.032(a), once a violation occurs involving unpaid wages during employment (as opposed to termination), only the Commissioner has standing to serve a formal demand letter that triggers penalty accrual if payment is withheld beyond ten days following that demand. Any money collected through this process must be paid directly to the employee, effectively making them beneficiaries rather than enforcers in this specific context. The court reasoned that if an employee attempts to bypass this administrative step by filing a civil suit solely for penalties without having gone through (or being able to go through) a Commissioner-led demand process under § 181.032(a), they are attempting to exercise an enforcement power that belongs solely to state authorities at common law under this statute's framework (*Hull v. ConvergeOne*). Consequently, because Cohen was not represented by or acting as an agent of the Commissioner in initiating these specific penalty calculations, his private cause of action failed as a matter of law regarding those specific damages under § 181.032(a).
