Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Factual background: Consilio's role as a document review firm and the 2019 elimination of premium overtime pay

To understand the legal controversy in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, one must first grasp the specific employment relationship at the heart of the dispute. Consilio, LLC operates as a nationwide legal document review company with a presence in Minnesota. The plaintiff, Bruce Cohen, was employed by Consilio beginning in 2018 in the capacity of an hourly licensed attorney tasked with reviewing documents. This distinction is significant because while Cohen held a professional license, his compensation structure was tied to an hourly wage rather than a salary, placing him potentially within the scope of overtime protections under state law.

The conflict arose from a unilateral change in Consilio's compensation policy. In July 2019, the company distributed an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers announcing a new "premium overtime policy" set to take effect in August 2019. This policy marked a decisive shift: it eliminated premium overtime pay entirely. Under the revised terms, employees like Cohen were informed that they would receive only their "base pay rate" for all hours worked, regardless of whether those hours exceeded the standard forty-hour workweek threshold typically triggering overtime eligibility. It was this elimination of premium pay that formed the factual basis for Cohen's allegation that Consilio failed to compensate him and similarly situated employees in accordance with applicable labor laws.

## Statutory framework: Overview of the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims filed by Cohen

In August 2020, following nearly two years under the new policy without receiving premium overtime rates, Cohen initiated litigation against Consilio on behalf of himself and a putative class of similarly situated employees. His complaint relied on three distinct Minnesota state statutes designed to protect workers' wages: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). Specifically, Cohen cited Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101 and 181.171 under the MPWA; §§ 177.25 and 177.27 under the MFLSA; and §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), along with cross-references to other wage provisions under the MWTA.

Cohen's legal theory posited that Consilio's failure to pay premium overtime constituted a violation of these statutes. Consequently, he sought a comprehensive array of remedies intended to make him whole and punish non-compliance: payment of owed overtime wages, liquidated damages (which often serve as doubled damages for willful violations), statutory penalties calculated based on daily wages or fixed amounts per violation, and injunctive relief to stop future violations. By invoking multiple overlapping statutes, Cohen attempted to maximize potential recovery through various mechanisms for calculating damages and penalties available under Minnesota law, setting the stage for complex interpretive disputes regarding what each statute actually permitted private plaintiffs to recover versus what remained reserved for state enforcement agencies.

## Pre-trial developments: The stipulation that Cohen received all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages

A critical development occurred after Cohen filed suit but before the case reached trial or final resolution on its merits regarding penalties: Consilio made substantial payments to satisfy the core economic claims of the lawsuit. Prior to filing its motion for summary judgment, Consilio paid out $256,010.01 specifically designated to cover overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under the MFLSA claim alone. Of this sum, Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overdue overtime wages and $462 in liquidated damages covering his individual losses during the relevant period.

These payments led directly to a crucial stipulation entered into by both parties regarding Cohen's primary economic injuries. The parties explicitly acknowledged that "Cohen has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit." Furthermore, they stipulated that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." This admission effectively removed any genuine dispute over whether Cohen was owed back pay or liquidated damages as individuals claimsant under federal or state fair labor standards principles covered by those specific remedies.
