Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # Standard of Review and Analytical Framework

## De Novo Review and the Summary Judgment Threshold
When an appellate court reviews a district court's grant of summary judgment, it applies a *de novo* standard. This means the Eighth Circuit does not defer to the lower court's legal conclusions or factual findings; instead, it examines the record anew, applying the same standard that governed the district court. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56, summary judgment is appropriate only when "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact" and the moving party is "entitled to judgment as a matter of law." In *Cohen v. Consilio*, LLC, Circuit Judge Kelly emphasized that in conducting this review, the appellate court must view the evidence and draw all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the non-moving party, here, Bruce Cohen. This protective lens ensures that if there is any ambiguity or conflicting evidence regarding a material fact, that conflict must be resolved in favor of proceeding to trial rather than ending the case via summary judgment. However, if the undisputed facts establish that no reasonable jury could find for the non-moving party on a particular claim, summary judgment is proper as a matter of law.

## Statutory Interpretation and Legislative Intent
Once the procedural hurdle of summary judgment is cleared—or determined to have been correctly crossed—the court turns to statutory interpretation to resolve the substantive legal questions. In analyzing Cohen's claims under Minnesota state law, the Eighth Circuit relied heavily on established principles of Minnesota statutory construction. The primary rule applied was that when statutory language is "plain and unambiguous," it must be given effect as written because it is assumed to manifest legislative intent. The court refused to look beyond the text of Minnesota Statutes § 181.101 unless ambiguity existed. This approach prevents courts from importing rights or remedies that the legislature did not explicitly enact. By strictly adhering to the text, the court avoided judicial overreach, focusing instead on what specific powers were granted to whom within the statute itself. This textualist approach allowed the court to distinguish sharply between rights available to private citizens and those reserved exclusively for government officials.

## Distinguishing Employee Remedies from State Penalties
A critical analytical distinction in *Cohen* lies in separating compensatory damages available directly to an employee from civil penalties reserved for enforcement by state authorities. Under Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), specifically § 181.101(a), an employee retains a substantive right to prosecute a claim for unpaid wages themselves. However, when Cohen attempted to claim "average daily wage penalties" under this same section, he encountered a textual barrier: penalties under § 181.101 are triggered only after the Commissioner serves a demand for payment and waits ten days without satisfaction; thereafter, only "the commissioner may charge and collect... a penalty." The court noted that while money collected by the Commissioner must eventually be paid to the employee, the *right* to initiate collection and impose that specific penalty belongs solely to the state agency.

The Eighth Circuit reinforced this limitation by pointing out that if the Minnesota legislature intended for employees to access these specific penalties privately, it knew how to draft such authorization elsewhere in its code—such as in §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14 regarding discharged or resigned employees where individuals can directly "charge and collect a penalty." The absence of similar language in § 181.101 signaled a deliberate legislative choice to centralize penalty enforcement with the Commissioner rather than empowering individual litigants. Consequently, because Cohen lacked standing under this specific subsection to seek penalties independently, he could not survive summary judgment on that portion of his MPWA claim as a matter of law.

## Jurisdictional Considerations: Standing and Mootness
Beyond simple statutory interpretation, *Cohen* highlights how appellate analysis must account for Article III jurisdictional requirements like standing and mootness before addressing merits-based defenses. Even though Cohen stipulated he had been paid all overdue wages and liquidated damages under his Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim—a move meant partly to secure partial victory—he continued pressing for statutory penalties payable directly to the state Commissioner rather than himself. Here, procedural posture became fatal: federal courts cannot adjudicate claims where there is no live controversy between parties with adverse interests capable of redress through judicial intervention. While civil penalties can sometimes sustain standing by deterring future misconduct (*Friends of the Earth*), they require proof that wrongful behavior could reasonably recur or that some concrete injury persists beyond mere monetary compensation already rendered by consent decree or settlement-like stipulations. Because Consilio had arguably ceased its violative policy voluntarily—and since Cohen conceded receipt of full backpay—the viability of his remaining MFLSA penalty claim hinged entirely on whether future recurrence remained possible; absent clarity on this point post-stipulation, dismissal risked violating core jurisdictional limits requiring continuous adversariality throughout litigation lifecycle stages up until final resolution orders issued below level trial courts reviewing motions initially filed pre-discovery completion phases altogether!
