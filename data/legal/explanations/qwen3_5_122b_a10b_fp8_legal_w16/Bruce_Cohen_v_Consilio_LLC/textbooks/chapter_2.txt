Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # Standard of Review and Jurisdictional Thresholds

## De Novo Review Standard for Summary Judgment Grants

Before the Eighth Circuit can evaluate the substantive merits of Bruce Cohen's claims against Consilio, LLC, the appellate panel must first establish the framework for its analysis. When a party appeals a district court's grant of summary judgment, the reviewing court applies a *de novo* standard. This means the appellate court examines the record anew, without deference to the lower court's legal conclusions or factual determinations. As articulated in *Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc.*, 766 F.3d 863 (8th Cir. 2014), and reaffirmed in *Placzek v. Mayo Clinic*, 18 F.4th 1010 (8th Cir. 2021), this standard requires the appellate court to view the evidence and draw all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the non-moving party—in this case, Cohen.

Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56, summary judgment is appropriate only when there are "no genuine issues of material fact" and the moving party is "entitled to judgment as a matter of law." For Cohen to survive this hurdle on appeal, he would need to demonstrate that a factual dispute exists regarding essential elements of his claims or that the district court misinterpreted the governing statutes. However, before reaching these substantive arguments regarding overtime laws and penalties, the court must ensure it possesses the constitutional authority to hear the case at all. The opinion highlights that even when a standard of review favors thorough scrutiny, jurisdictional thresholds act as gatekeepers; if a claim fails on standing or mootness grounds, the court lacks the power to adjudicate the merits regardless of how strong the underlying legal arguments might appear.

## Article III Standing Requirements for Civil Penalty Claims

A foundational principle of federal adjudication is that Article III standing is not merely a procedural formality but a constitutional limitation on judicial power derived from Case or Controversy requirements. In *Cohen v. Consilio*, this doctrine becomes pivotal regarding Cohen's claim for statutory penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). While Cohen had already received compensation for his actual overtime wages and liquidated damages—a stipulation that arguably satisfied his personal economic injury—the nature of his remaining request complicates his standing analysis. The parties acknowledged that any civil penalties awarded under the MFLSA would be payable directly to the state Commissioner, not to Cohen personally.

The Supreme Court precedent cited in *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), suggests that civil penalties can sometimes afford redress to private plaintiffs by deterring future unlawful conduct, thereby satisfying Article III standing even when monetary recovery goes to the government rather than individual pockets. However, as noted in *Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433 (2017), standing is claim-specific and relief-specific; a plaintiff must demonstrate a concrete personal interest for *each* form of relief sought. Consequently, just because Cohen had standing to sue for back pay does not automatically guarantee he retains standing to pursue civil penalties once his direct financial losses have been remediated by settlement payments from Consilio. The Eighth Circuit recognized that if Cohen cannot show a continued personal stake in obtaining penalties payable solely to third parties, he may fail to meet this threshold requirement entirely.

## The Doctrine of Mootness: Voluntary Cessation vs. Absolute Clarity of Non-Recurance

Once standing is established at filing, it must persist throughout the litigation; if events occur during pendency that resolve the controversy completely, the case may become moot under Article III's prohibition on advisory opinions. In *Cohen*, this issue arose because Consilio voluntarily ceased its alleged violation by paying Cohen what he claimed was owed prior to summary judgment proceedings on penalties being finalized. Generally, federal courts recognize that "a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power," citing *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle*.
