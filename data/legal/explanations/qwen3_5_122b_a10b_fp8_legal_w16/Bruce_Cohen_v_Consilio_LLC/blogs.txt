Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## The 'Know How' Doctrine: Why Minnesota Employees Can't Sue for Average Daily Wage Penalties Under § 181.101

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit delivered a sharp reminder of the perils of relying on broad statutory interpretations in wage-and-hour litigation. While the underlying facts involve a standard dispute over overtime pay for legal document reviewers, the appellate court's resolution turns on a much narrower, yet profoundly consequential, point of statutory construction: Does a private employee have the right to sue for "average daily wage" penalties under Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act (MPWA)? The answer, according to Judge Kelly's opinion, is a resounding no. By deploying a rigorous textualist analysis and leaning heavily on the canon that legislatures "know how" to draft what they intend, the court has effectively bifurcated Minnesota's wage enforcement regime, reserving the hammer of civil penalties for the Commissioner of Labor while leaving individual employees with only their base backpay.

The case arose after Consilio, a national legal document review firm, notified its Minnesota-based attorneys that it was eliminating premium overtime pay effective August 2019. When Cohen sued on behalf of himself and others alleging violations of the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA, Consilio moved to settle the core monetary disputes before trial. They paid Cohen his owed overtime and liquidated damages—totaling just over $3,600 for him personally—and stipulated that these sums satisfied all claims for actual wages and double damages under the MFLSA. However, Cohen pressed forward on two fronts: he sought substantial statutory penalties totaling nearly $258,000 under various state statutes and requested injunctive relief to force Consilio to rescind its new policy.

The Eighth Circuit's handling of Cohen's MPWA claim offers a masterclass in statutory parsing. Cohen argued that § 181.171 allowed him to seek "civil penalties or damages... provided for in the section" violated—specifically pointing to § 181.101 as the source of liability for unpaid wages. The problem for Cohen was that § 181.101 itself contains no mechanism for an employee to trigger those penalties directly against an employer in court. Instead, Judge Kelly noted that § 181.101(a) explicitly vests the power to impose and collect penalties solely in the hands of the Commissioner of Labor following a formal demand process by that agency.

Here is where the court applied what legal scholars often call the "expressio unius" principle or, more colloquially in this context, the "know how" doctrine. The opinion highlights that if the Minnesota legislature intended for private citizens to recover daily wage penalties under § 181.101, it knew exactly how to say so elsewhere in the code. Specifically, §§ 181.13 and 181.14—which govern situations where an employee is discharged or voluntarily quits—explicitly authorize employees to "charge and collect a penalty." In contrast, § 181.032 (governing general failure to pay) lacks this language entirely. Citing *Lagasse v. Horton* and *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, the court reasoned that this disparity cannot be accidental; when one statute grants a right and another omits it despite covering similar ground, silence implies exclusion rather than inclusion by oversight. Consequently, because only the Commissioner can initiate penalty collection under § 181.032/§ 181.032(a), Cohen had no standing as a private litigant to pursue them in federal court based on those specific provisions alone.

While affirming dismissal on MPWA grounds was straightforward due to this textual gap, the court took a more nuanced approach toward Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). Here lies perhaps the most significant procedural twist in *Cohen*. Although Consilio had already paid out all disputed wages and liquidated damages—a move that seemingly mooted Cohen's primary grievance—the statute allows for civil penalties payable not to Cohen, but directly to state authorities via fines imposed by courts or administrative bodies depending on interpretation (though here treated as potentially payable to state coffers).

Judge Kelly expressed deep skepticism about whether federal courts retained jurisdiction over this claim once personal compensation was satisfied but before determining if Consilio's policy remained active enough to threaten recurrence. Citing *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, the court acknowledged that civil penalties can sometimes sustain standing by deterring future misconduct even if past harm is remedied by restitution ("redressability"). However, because it was unclear whether Consilio had permanently abandoned its unlawful overtime policy or merely paused it pending litigation outcomes, declaring mootness immediately would be premature—but granting summary judgment without resolving jurisdictional questions was equally improper given Article III constraints against advisory opinions on hypothetical harms that may never recur. Thus came a rare vacatur-and-remand specifically tasked with answering threshold jurisdictional questions before any merits determination could proceed further down line regarding potential future liabilities tied purely deterrent purposes rather than direct compensation flows back into plaintiff pockets themselves!

## Standing in Stalemate: The Mootness Trap When Plaintiffs Are Fully Compensated but Seek Government-Facing Penalties

In the high-stakes arena of employment litigation, a plaintiff's victory is often measured in backpay and liquidated damages. Once an employer writes the check to make the individual whole, the calculus usually shifts toward settlement and closure. But what happens when the plaintiff refuses to drop the case, insisting instead on statutory penalties that flow not into their pocket, but directly into the state treasury? This is precisely the jurisdictional knot tied up by the Eighth Circuit in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*. While the court largely affirmed a summary judgment win for the employer, it issued a striking vacatur and remand on one critical point: whether Cohen retained Article III standing to pursue civil penalties after admitting he had already been paid every dollar of wages and damages he was legally owed.

The case presents a fascinating friction between substantive labor rights and federal jurisdictional limits. Cohen, a licensed attorney working as a document reviewer for Consilio, alleged that his employer illegally eliminated overtime premiums for Minnesota-based staff. After litigation commenced, Consilio paid Cohen his outstanding overtime wages plus liquidated damages totaling nearly $400—a sum stipulated by both sides to fully satisfy Cohen's personal financial injuries. Yet, Cohen pressed forward, seeking substantial statutory penalties under various Minnesota wage laws. Under some of these statutes, such penalties are payable exclusively to the Commissioner of Labor, serving as a public deterrent rather than private compensation. The district court dismissed these claims entirely; the Eighth Circuit agreed with most dismissals but paused at the door of constitutional jurisdiction regarding the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim.

The core of the appellate panel's hesitation lies in the doctrine of mootness. Federal courts are strictly prohibited from issuing advisory opinions; they require a live controversy where the plaintiff maintains a "personal stake" in the outcome throughout the litigation. Typically, once an employer pays off a plaintiff's economic losses, any claim for further monetary relief becomes moot because there is nothing left to recover for that individual. However, Supreme Court precedent in *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* established that civil penalties payable to third parties can sometimes sustain standing if they serve to deter future violations by making continued non-compliance financially painful for the defendant.

Justice Kelly's opinion grapples with whether this exception applies when the underlying violation—the failure to pay overtime—is arguably cured by past performance and there is no evidence that Consilio intends to revert to its old policies. The court noted that while voluntary cessation of wrongdoing does not automatically strip a court of jurisdiction, it does shift the burden heavily onto the plaintiff to prove that "it is absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." Herein lies the trap: if Consilio has permanently abandoned its illegal policy and will never reinstate it without coercion from a penalty system that benefits no one but the state, does Cohen truly suffer an injury-in-fact sufficient to justify judicial intervention? Conversely, if there is even a sliver of doubt about recurrence, denying access to these penalties might leave employers free to violate wage laws with impunity until caught again.

This ambiguity forced an unusual procedural move: vacating summary judgment solely on jurisdictional grounds and sending it back down for a threshold determination on mootness. This signals a rigorous application of Article III constraints even in statutory enforcement contexts where legislatures clearly intend robust deterrence mechanisms. It suggests that federal courts will not simply assume standing exists because Congress or a state legislature authorized penalties; plaintiffs must still demonstrate a concrete connection between their continued presence in court and their ability to secure redress or prevent future harm distinct from general public enforcement interests.

The implications extend beyond Minnesota wage law into broader questions about qui tam-style mechanisms and private attorney general suits across various regulatory fields. When statutes authorize private citizens to sue for fines payable to government entities without granting them any portion of those funds, they create a unique standing paradox. If full restitution moots individual claims but leaves open only "public" penalties payable elsewhere, federal courts face difficult choices: either allow individuals to litigate purely as proxies for state enforcement (potentially expanding Article III beyond its historical bounds) or risk leaving gaps in enforcement where no government agency steps in immediately after partial compliance occurs.

Ultimately, *Cohen* serves as a reminder that winning every legal argument on liability does not guarantee survival through summary judgment if jurisdiction evaporates along with your client's wallet damage claim. For practitioners navigating similar terrain—where clients seek punitive measures that bypass their own coffers—the lesson is clear: proving liability is only half the battle; proving you still have skin in the game when your pockets are full is equally vital. Until lower courts clarify exactly how much uncertainty regarding future recidivism is required to keep such cases alive, plaintiffs seeking state-directed penalties will find themselves walking a tightrope between statutory entitlements and constitutional justiciability limits defined by *Laidlaw* and its progeny.



## Procedural Pitfalls: When Waiver and Improper Pleading Sink a Wage Claim

In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided October 6, 2025, the Eighth Circuit delivered a mixed bag for a plaintiff who successfully secured backpay and liquidated damages but ultimately lost his fight for statutory penalties and injunctive relief on appeal. While the case touches on significant state labor law questions regarding the availability of "average daily wage" penalties under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the decision offers perhaps more immediate value to litigators as a cautionary tale on procedural hygiene. Two distinct procedural missteps by the appellant—failing to cite legal authority for a statutory claim and attempting to raise new requests for injunctive relief during summary judgment briefing—resulted in affirmances that bypassed substantive analysis entirely. For practitioners navigating complex employment disputes, *Cohen* underscores that even meritorious underlying facts can be forfeited through inadequate briefing and improper pleading practices.

The court's treatment of Bruce Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) illustrates the harsh reality of appellate waiver. Cohen argued that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice regarding changes to its overtime policy, seeking penalties as a remedy. However, when reviewing this claim, the Eighth Circuit noted that while the district court had offered little discussion on the issue, Cohen's own appellate brief contained "little to no discussion." More critically, Cohen pointed to no statutory authority supporting his assertion that civil penalties were available under the specific subsections of the MWTA he cited. Invoking *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355 (8th Cir. 2000), Judge Kelly wrote that where a defendant fails to support an assertion with argument or legal authority, the issue is deemed waived. Consequently, the court declined to disturb the district court's dismissal of the claim. This outcome serves as a stark reminder that in federal appellate practice, especially within circuits like the Eighth that strictly adhere to *Milligan*, a party cannot expect an appellate court to act as counsel by scouring statutes or constructing arguments sua sponte. A claim unsupported by citation is effectively dead on arrival.

Equally instructive is the court's rejection of Cohen's attempt to pivot his requested relief late in the game regarding injunctive measures. Throughout much of his complaint, Cohen sought broad injunctive relief, yet during summary judgment briefing—a stage typically reserved for arguing existing claims rather than reshaping them—he shifted gears to request a very specific affirmative order: that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy entirely. The Eighth Circuit found this new request was not properly before it because it had never been raised in an amended complaint or through proper pre-trial procedures. Citing Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and recent precedent (*InfoDeli, LLC*), the panel emphasized that new claims or fundamentally altered requests for relief must be introduced via amendment, not buried in opposition briefs filed after discovery has closed and motions are pending. By waiting until summary judgment briefing to articulate this specific demand for policy rescission, Cohen deprived Consilio of fair notice and opportunity to respond, leading the court to dismiss the issue without reaching its merits.

These procedural rulings sit alongside more substantive holdings in *Cohen*. The court affirmed that private individuals cannot seek "average daily wage" penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 because that statute explicitly reserves enforcement power for the Commissioner of Labor; conversely, it vacated and remanded on a separate MFLSA penalty claim due to unresolved Article III standing concerns following partial settlement payments. Yet, it is easy to overlook how easily Cohen could have preserved these arguments had he adhered strictly to procedural norms. The distinction between losing on a technicality versus losing on a substantive interpretation of state labor law is often thin but decisive in outcomes like this one.

For employment litigators drafting complaints and managing discovery timelines, *Cohen* reinforces two fundamental principles: first, every asserted remedy must be anchored by explicit statutory citations and developed legal argumentation in initial filings; second, if your theory of liability shifts or your desired remedy evolves significantly during litigation, do not wait for summary judgment briefings—you must amend your pleadings early enough to give opposing counsel adequate time to adjust their strategy. In an era where courts are increasingly skeptical of judicial efficiency being undermined by last-minute tactical pivots, *Cohen* stands as a clear warning: procedure is not merely administrative; it is often dispositive.
