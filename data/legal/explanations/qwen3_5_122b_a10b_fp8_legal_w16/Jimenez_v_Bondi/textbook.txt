Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Case Overview, Facts, and Procedural History

## The Petitioners' Profile: Political Affiliation and Professional Roles

The petitioners in *Jimenez v. Bondi* are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. Originally from Bogotá, Colombia, the family operated a furniture factory prior to their departure for the United States in December 2022. Central to their legal claim is their long-standing affiliation with the Colombian Liberal Party, a political organization they joined in 2012. Their engagement extended beyond passive membership; during the 2022 presidential election cycle, they actively campaigned for Federico Gutiérrez, a candidate opposing then-President Gustavo Petro. This activism involved direct voter outreach, including door-to-door canvassing in local neighborhoods.

Parallel to their political activities, Jimenez maintained a significant professional profile within Colombia's legal and governmental sectors. Holding a provisional law license while completing his law studies in 2022, Jimenez served as an intern for the National Prosecutor's Office. His duties included assisting victims of domestic abuse and extortion. This dual identity—as a visible political operative for an opposition party and a representative of state prosecutorial authority—created the complex factual matrix from which their asylum claim arose. The petitioners' ability to articulate these distinct roles was critical to understanding how the court analyzed the motives behind the threats they received.

## Chronology of Harm: From Campaign Threats to Property Dispute Escalation

The timeline of harm begins in late May 2022, shortly after the petitioners intensified their campaign efforts for President-elect Gutiérrez. Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls once or twice weekly specifically referencing his involvement with the Liberal Party. These threats were not isolated; other party leaders faced similar intimidation, culminating in the kidnapping and murder of a colleague who had reported receiving threats. Despite this violence and the palpable danger surrounding them, the petitioners remained undeterred and continued their political work throughout June 2022.

However, the nature of the threat escalated significantly following a separate event unrelated to politics initially: a property dispute involving Jimenez's legal internship work. In June 2022, a client approached Jimenez regarding an unpaid rent issue involving her former husband's tenancy at her property located in San Bernardo. When Jimenez attempted to mediate directly with the tenant—a common practice for lawyers at that stage—he encountered hostility. The tenant warned him not to "look for trouble" and refused negotiation. Through his investigation into the property title and usage, Jimenez uncovered that the tenant was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros ("Don Rafa"), a known leader of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Petro's administration. Furthermore, Jimenez discovered that Don Rafa was using the property as a base for narcotics trafficking and prostitution, operating despite sealed health department violations and previous unsuccessful police raids.

## The 'Don Rafa' Incident: Intersecting Criminal Enterprise and Political Activity

The intersection of Jimenez's professional actions and his political identity created a volatile situation that formed the core of the factual dispute on appeal. Shortly after discovering Don Rafa's identity and informing local authorities about his operations—including speaking with a police officer who advised him to stay away—Jimenez received tangible retaliation that blended accusations of political betrayal with criminal grievances. He first received a threatening brochure labeled "death to snitches from Liberal Party." While he initially dismissed this as generic political harassment, subsequent events suggested otherwise.

While working late at his furniture factory one evening two months later, two armed men confronted Jimenez at gunpoint. They explicitly accused him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office due to his investigations into Don Rafa's property operations but simultaneously linked this accusation to his political standing as someone "in politics." The attackers ordered him to leave his neighborhood under threat of death from Don Rafa himself roughly one month later returned with an associate to assault an employee at Jimenez's factory while searching for the petitioners' whereabouts; although they found no one home at that moment (as Petitioners were traveling), they reiterated that Don Rafa had authorized lethal action if Petitioners were seen again because they were viewed as snitches interfering with criminal enterprise while holding political influence. The tension reached its peak when one attacker physically grabbed Jimenez's young son outside school days later—an act prompting immediate flight—to ensure he understood exactly how serious those combined threats really were! Ultimately though—it wasn't just pure ideology driving these individuals forward anymore either since there existed clear overlap between both spheres where personal vendettas could easily morph into something far more deadly indeed!

# Legal Standards and Burdens of Proof in Asylum Claims

## Jurisdictional Basis: 8 U.S.C. § 1252 Review Authority

Before a federal appellate court can evaluate the merits of an immigration case, it must first confirm it possesses the authority to hear the matter. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit exercises jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, which grants courts of appeals the power to review "final orders of removal." This statute is the gateway for judicial oversight in immigration proceedings, allowing petitioners like Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family to challenge decisions made by the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). Crucially, this jurisdictional grant encompasses a consolidated review of all related claims, meaning the court reviews the asylum and withholding of removal denials together rather than treating them as entirely separate legal silos. However, this review is strictly bounded by the administrative record; the court looks only at the evidence presented during the proceedings before the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the BIA. If a petitioner attempts to introduce new facts or arguments that were not part of this official record, they fall outside the scope of judicial review under § 1252(b)(4)(A). Furthermore, when a single member of the BIA affirms an IJ's decision without issuing a separate opinion, as occurred in *Jimenez*, the reviewing court treats that affirmation as adopting the IJ's reasoning but remains free to consult the IJ's more detailed explanation to fully understand the basis for the lower tribunal's decision.

## Standard of Review: Substantial Evidence vs. De Novo Analysis

Understanding how an appellate court evaluates factual findings is perhaps the most critical skill for reading an immigration opinion. Unlike civil or criminal cases where an appellate court might re-examine facts *de novo* (from scratch), immigration appeals are governed by a highly deferential standard known as "substantial evidence." Under this standard, which applies to all factual findings made by the BIA and IJ, those findings are conclusive unless "any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." This is a demanding threshold for petitioners to overcome. It does not mean that if there is *some* evidence supporting a different conclusion, the agency's decision must be overturned. Instead, as long as there is substantial evidence in support of the agency's conclusion—even if there is conflicting evidence elsewhere in the record—the court must affirm. In *Jimenez*, this standard dictated much of the outcome; because there was substantial evidence supporting the BIA's view that threats alone did not constitute persecution or that political opinion was not a central motive, the Tenth Circuit was bound to accept those findings regardless of how sympathetic Petitioners' story might seem on its face. This deference ensures that fact-finding authority remains with agencies specializing in immigration matters rather than shifting to generalist judges who did not observe witness testimony firsthand.

## Statutory Definition of Refugee and Protected Grounds

At the heart of every asylum claim lies a specific statutory definition derived from both domestic law and international treaties. To qualify for asylum under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1), an applicant must prove they are a "refugee," defined as any person outside their country of nationality who is unable or unwilling to return because they suffer persecution or have a well-founded fear of persecution on account of five specific protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. The phrase "on account of" imposes a strict causation requirement; it is not enough for an applicant to simply possess a protected characteristic while simultaneously suffering harm. The harm must be motivated by that characteristic. In *Jimenez*, this definition framed Petitioners' entire legal strategy: they argued their harassment stemmed from their membership in Colombian Liberal Party leadership (political opinion) and potentially their status as lawyers assisting domestic violence victims (potentially membership in a particular social group). However, because Petitioners failed to properly brief their social group claim and focused exclusively on political opinion, their eligibility hinged entirely on whether their persecutors targeted them specifically because of their political views rather than other motives like financial disputes or personal vendettas arising from Jimenez's legal investigations into drug trafficking operations.

## Elements of Past Persecution: Severity, Nexus, and Government Control

Proving eligibility for asylum often begins with establishing "past persecution," which creates a rebuttable presumption that an applicant also holds a well-founded fear of future persecution if returned home. The Tenth Circuit has articulated three distinct elements required to prove past persecution: (1) an incident or series of incidents rising to the level of persecution; (2) committed on account of one of these statutorily protected grounds; and (3) perpetrated by government agents or forces that government is unable or unwilling to control. Defining "persecution" presents unique challenges because Congress never explicitly defined it in statute; instead, courts have developed jurisprudence stating that persecution involves severe suffering or harm inflicted upon those who differ in ways regarded as offensive by society. Importantly, courts look at incidents cumulatively rather than in isolation; while threats alone are typically insufficient to constitute persecution unless accompanied by physical harm or severe psychological trauma resulting from actual violence, repeated threats combined with other abuses may cross that line. However, mere restrictions on liberty or sporadic verbal threats usually fall short unless they create an atmosphere where life or freedom is genuinely threatened with imminent execution or torture. Additionally, even if severe harm occurs, it must be linked directly ("nexus") to a protected ground; if harm results primarily from criminal activity unrelated to politics—such as Jimenez interfering with drug traffickers' business interests—it fails this element regardless of severity. Finally, private actors can only count as persecutors if government authorities are demonstrably unable or unwilling to intervene effectively after being notified of such threats.

## Distinction Between Asylum (Discretionary) and Withholding of Removal (Mandatory)

While asylum and withholding-of-removal protections share similar substantive requirements regarding who qualifies as endangered abroad, they operate under fundamentally different legal frameworks concerning discretion versus mandate and applicable burdens of proof. Asylum remains purely discretionary relief granted solely at Attorney General discretion once statutory eligibility thresholds are met; conversely, withholding-of-removal constitutes mandatory # Analysis of Past Persecution: Threshold Determinations

## Introduction to the Threshold Inquiry
The first major hurdle in any asylum claim is establishing that the applicant has suffered "persecution." This is a high bar that serves as a gatekeeping mechanism, distinguishing serious human rights violations from generalized violence or isolated criminal acts. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit faced the critical task of determining whether the harms suffered by Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family rose to this statutory level. The court's analysis focuses heavily on the nature of the harm itself, emphasizing that while threats and psychological distress are significant, they must be viewed through the lens of specific legal precedents to determine if they constitute persecution under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The court ultimately concluded that the petitioners' experiences, taken together, fell short of this threshold, a determination rooted in strict adherence to Tenth Circuit case law regarding the insufficiency of threats and the limits of psychological harm claims absent physical injury.

## 'Threats Alone' Doctrine: Why Verbal Warnings Are Insufficient
A foundational principle in Tenth Circuit jurisprudence is that threats, standing alone, are generally insufficient to constitute persecution. The *Jimenez* court reiterated this doctrine explicitly, citing *Karki v. Holder* for the proposition that while threats are relevant factors in assessing a claimant's overall situation, they do not automatically satisfy the definition of persecution. In this case, Petitioners relied heavily on a series of threatening communications: weekly phone calls starting in May 2022, a brochure stating "death to snitches," and verbal warnings delivered at gunpoint ordering them to leave their neighborhood. Despite the terrifying nature of these interactions, the court noted that Petitioners themselves conceded during their merits hearing that they never suffered physical harm at the hands of these individuals prior to fleeing Colombia. Furthermore, they acknowledged in their briefs that no lasting physical injuries resulted from these encounters.

The court reasoned that for harassment or intimidation to cross the line into persecution, it must entail more than mere restrictions on liberty or sporadic threats to life. While acknowledging that physical harm is not an absolute prerequisite for a finding of persecution, the court stressed that without it, the alleged mistreatment must be severe enough to be regarded as "offensive" in a profound sense. Mere verbal warnings or even armed confrontations where no shot is fired and no body is touched often fail this test unless accompanied by additional aggravating factors like prolonged detention or torture. In *Jimenez*, because the petitioners were not physically injured and conceded as much, their reliance solely on verbal and written threats could not sustain a finding of past persecution under existing circuit precedent.

## Assessment of Emotional and Psychological Harm Without Physical Injury
Petitioners argued that even absent physical trauma, their suffering included significant emotional and psychological damage warranting asylum eligibility. Angelica Parada Duarte testified about her inability to sleep or rest due to fear, and they presented evidence that their younger son developed panic attacks and stress reactions following an incident where he was grabbed by an associate of their persecutor. The court acknowledged these hardships but maintained a rigorous standard regarding purely psychological claims. Drawing on prior decisions such as *Brandy v. Holder*, the opinion clarified that while events causing fear can be "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," such feelings rarely suffice as independent grounds for asylum when divorced from physical harm or severe deprivation.

The Tenth Circuit emphasized its historical reluctance to grant asylum based exclusively on threats coupled with emotional distress. The court distinguished between understandable fear resulting from living in a volatile environment versus persecution requiring state-sanctioned or tolerated infliction of severe suffering. In *Jimenez*, despite the tragic nature of seeing one's child threatened and living under constant anxiety, these conditions were deemed insufficient to meet the statutory definition of persecution because they lacked the requisite severity usually associated with physical violence or long-term detention. The court noted that accepting such claims without accompanying physical harm would expand asylum eligibility beyond congressional intent, effectively converting all victims of crime-related fear into refugees regardless of whether their government was willing or able to protect them from similar risks faced by others in society.

## Cumulative Impact Analysis: Aggregating Multiple Incidents
Recognizing that single incidents might sometimes fall short while multiple occurrences could combine to create persecution, courts employ a cumulative impact analysis. This approach allows adjudicators to consider various forms of mistreatment together rather than examining each event in isolation—a method endorsed by cases like *Ritonga v. Holder*. However, aggregation does not mean simply adding up unrelated annoyances; rather, it involves evaluating whether repeated instances collectively reach a level so severe as to constitute persecution under international norms adopted by U.S. law (*Wiransane v. Ashcroft*).

# The Nexus Requirement: Distinguishing Political Motive from Personal Retaliation

## "At Least One Central Reason": The Legal Standard for Mixed-Motive Cases

In immigration law, establishing eligibility for asylum requires more than simply proving that an applicant suffered harm; the applicant must also demonstrate a specific causal link between that harm and a protected characteristic, such as race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. This causal connection is known as the "nexus" requirement. As the Tenth Circuit explains in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the statutory mandate is rigorous: an applicant must prove that their protected ground was "at least one central reason" for the persecution they faced. This standard prohibits applicants from succeeding if their political opinion or other protected trait played only a minor, tangential, or superficial role in the perpetrator's decision to cause harm.

However, courts recognize that real-world conflicts often involve complex motivations. A persecutor may have multiple reasons for targeting an individual, ranging from personal vendettas to ideological hatred. In such mixed-motive cases, the law does not require that the protected ground be the *sole* reason for the harm. Instead, it must be *one* central reason among potentially others. If the protected ground is merely incidental—for instance, if a criminal targets someone primarily for financial gain but happens to dislike their political views—the nexus requirement remains unsatisfied. The petitioner bears the burden of proving that their political opinion was not just present in the background but was a driving force behind the attack. In *Jimenez*, this distinction became the fulcrum upon which the entire appeal turned, as the court had to determine whether Don Rafa's violence stemmed from political rivalry or personal retaliation against a legal obstructionist.

## Timeline Analysis: Separating Early Political Threats from Later Escalated Harassment

One of the most effective tools for analyzing motive is a careful examination of chronology. Courts often look to the sequence of events to discern whether a shift in behavior correlates with a shift in motivation. In *Jimenez*, the timeline revealed two distinct phases of harassment that pointed toward different underlying causes. Initially, beginning around late May 2022, Jimenez received threatening phone calls related to his work campaigning for Federico Gutierrez and supporting the Colombian Liberal Party during a contentious election cycle. These threats coincided with periods of high political tension and were consistent with threats received by other party members and colleagues during that timeframe. Crucially, at this stage of events—including after a colleague was kidnapped and murdered—Petitioners remained undeterred and continued their political activities without fleeing Colombia or ceasing their advocacy.

The character of the threats changed dramatically only after Jimenez intervened in a separate matter unrelated to politics: a property dispute involving his client's former husband and landlord, who was identified as "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficker aligned with President Gustavo Petro's administration. It was only after Jimenez investigated Don Rafa's illicit operations at a rental property and informed authorities about drug trafficking and prostitution on site that the nature of the hostility escalated significantly into direct physical danger and targeted violence against Jimenez personally. The attackers explicitly accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" responsible for police raids on Don Rafa's property—a charge directly tied to his professional duties as an intern prosecutor and lawyer seeking restitution for his client. By isolating these temporal markers, the court determined that while early threats might have been politically motivated (though never proven severe enough to constitute persecution), the subsequent escalation that drove Petitioners out of Colombia was triggered by Jimenez's professional interference with criminal enterprises, not his voting record or campaign work.

# Government Protection/Internal Relocation Feasibility

## Evidence Required to Prove Government Unwillingness/Inability

When an asylum seeker claims that their home government is unable or unwilling to protect them from persecution, they bear the burden of providing concrete evidence to support that assertion. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit emphasizes that this determination relies heavily on the government's actual response—or lack thereof—to specific instances of persecution. The court explains that two primary factors weigh most significantly in this analysis: first, whether the government took steps to stop the persecutors, and second, whether the government offered direct protection to the applicant, such as placing them in protective custody. Crucially, for the government's reaction to be evaluated, the applicant must typically provide notice of the harm. Without evidence that an applicant reported past persecution to authorities, a reviewing court cannot determine how the state would have reacted or whether it possessed the willingness or ability to intervene. Consequently, a failure to report incidents can severely undermine a claim of government inability or unwillingness, although it is not automatically dispositive if the applicant can demonstrate that seeking help would have been futile or dangerous.

## Individual Officer Apathy vs. Systemic State Failure

A critical distinction in asylum jurisprudence is separating isolated incidents of police indifference from systemic state failure. In *Jimenez*, Petitioners argued that their interactions with law enforcement proved the Colombian government was complicit in their plight. Specifically, Jimenez recounted telling two different police officers about the threats he faced; both officers allegedly responded by advising him not to "seek trouble" and suggesting he leave the area rather than offering assistance. However, the court rejects this as sufficient proof of a governmental policy of non-protection. Drawing on precedent from other circuits and Tenth Circuit case law, *Jimenez* clarifies that while police apathy can be indicative of a broader problem, a single conversation with an unhelpful officer does not establish that an entire government is "unable or unwilling" to protect its citizens. To succeed on this element, applicants generally need evidence of systemic failures across various jurisdictions or levels of authority, rather than anecdotal accounts of individual officer negligence or corruption within a specific neighborhood. Because Jimenez and his father—who worked within the justice system—did not attempt to report these crimes through official channels beyond these informal conversations, there was no record for the court to assess regarding a systemic breakdown in protection mechanisms.

## Counter-Evidence: Government Searches Targeting Persecutor Operations

Perhaps most damaging to Petitioners' claim of government inability was the objective evidence regarding the activities of their alleged persecutor, "Don Rafa," and his relationship with state actors. Petitioners contended that Don Rafa was so closely aligned with President Gustavo Petro's administration that any attempt at state intervention would be futile. The court found this argument unsupported by the factual record presented during proceedings. On the contrary, evidence showed that law enforcement had actually identified Don Rafa's property as a hub for narco-trafficking and prostitution and had attempted multiple searches and seizures targeting those criminal operations. This activity directly contradicted Petitioners' assertion that their persecutor operated with total impunity due to high-level political connections. If the government were truly unwilling or unable to control Don Rafa due to political alignment, it would likely have refrained from attempting raids on his premises entirely. The existence of these failed search attempts demonstrated that while crime might persist in certain areas, there was no blanket refusal by the state apparatus to enforce laws against individuals like Don Rafa who were disrupting public order. Therefore, Petitioners could not sustain their burden of proving they lacked access to state protection had they formally sought it through proper legal channels.
