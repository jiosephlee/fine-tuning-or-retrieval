Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Threshold Analysis: Defining Persecution in Fact Patterns Involving Threats

## Introduction to the Persecution Inquiry

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit faced a critical threshold question common in asylum litigation: Did the petitioners' experiences rise to the level of "persecution" required by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA)? Before a court can analyze whether harm was motivated by a protected ground like political opinion, or whether the government is unable to provide protection, it must first determine if the underlying harm constitutes persecution at all. This determination is purely factual but governed by strict legal standards. In *Jimenez*, the court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) conclusion that while the petitioners faced genuine danger and significant anxiety, their specific set of experiences did not cross the high bar established for past persecution. Understanding this distinction is vital for law students reading asylum opinions, as many applicants present credible stories of fear and harassment that nonetheless fail to meet the statutory definition of persecution.

## 'More Than Mere Restrictions': The Requirement that Harm Be Offensive and Severe

The foundational rule articulated in *Jimenez* is that persecution entails more than mere restrictions on liberty or sporadic threats. The court reiterated its longstanding precedent that persecution requires "the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ... in a way regarded as offensive." This language establishes a qualitative threshold; not every instance of hostility or discrimination qualifies. For harm to be persecutory, it must be severe enough to be viewed as an affront to human dignity or fundamental rights. In this case, although the petitioners were subjected to intimidation, the court emphasized that the legal standard demands something more substantial than simple inconvenience or isolated acts of aggression. The Tenth Circuit clarified that while physical harm is not strictly mandatory to constitute persecution, the absence of physical injury makes it significantly harder for an applicant to prove that their suffering meets this "offensive" and severe standard absent other egregious factors like prolonged detention or torture.

## Threats Alone Doctrine: Why Repeated Verbal Threats Do Not Automatically Equal Persecution

A central component of the *Jimenez* decision involves the application of the "threats alone doctrine." The petitioners argued that they suffered past persecution based on a series of escalating threats: weekly phone calls beginning in May 2022, a death-threat brochure delivered to their factory, and multiple face-to-face encounters where men held Jimenez at gunpoint demanding he leave his neighborhood. Despite acknowledging these terrifying events, the Tenth Circuit relied on binding precedent holding that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." 

The court reasoned that while threats are taken seriously during an assessment, they do not automatically transform into persecution without an accompanying act of inflicting harm. In *Jimenez*, despite the severity of holding a petitioner at gunpoint and issuing explicit death threats from a known criminal leader ("Don Rafa"), no actual violence occurred against the petitioners themselves prior to their departure from Colombia. The court noted that even when threats are coupled with minor incidents like robbery or short-term detention—as seen in cited precedents—they often remain insufficient unless they escalate into sustained physical abuse or severe deprivation. Consequently, because Jimenez and his family conceded they were never physically harmed and escaped before any attack was carried out, their experience remained within the category of threatened harm rather than realized persecution.

## Psychological Harm Limitations: Distinguishing Between Understandable Fear and Legal Persecution

The petitioners attempted to bolster their claim by highlighting severe psychological consequences resulting from their ordeal. Angelica Parada Duarte testified she could not sleep or rest due to fear, and her young son developed panic attacks after witnessing an attempt on his life by one of his assailants. While these symptoms represent real trauma and understandable human reactions to danger, *Jimenez* illustrates a rigid limitation in asylum jurisprudence: psychological distress alone rarely suffices to establish past persecution if there is no underlying physical violation or extreme deprivation.

The Tenth Circuit explicitly stated that it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone." The court distinguished between subjective feelings of terror—which are expected when facing death threats—and objective conditions rising to the level of persecution defined by statute. Even though the family's fear was rational given their circumstances (including seeing their son grabbed near school), this emotional suffering did not fill the evidentiary gap created by their lack of physical injury. This aspect of *Jimenez* teaches students that credibility findings regarding mental anguish do not substitute for proof of severe external harm; an applicant cannot manufacture a finding of persecution solely through testimony about post-traumatic stress if they were otherwise untouched physically by their persecutors.

## Cumulative Effect Analysis: How Multiple Incidents Are Weighed Together Yet Still Fall Short

Asylum law recognizes that individual incidents might appear minor in isolation but could collectively amount to persecution through their cumulative effect. The Tenth Circuit acknowledged this principle in *Jimenez*, stating it considers incidents collectively rather than in isolation because "the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." However, applying this test revealed a ceiling on what aggregation can achieve when none of the constituent parts involve actual violence against the applicant's person.

Even when combining phone calls, written threats, armed confrontations where guns were brandished but not fired, and one close call involving their child's abduction attempt (which was thwarted), the totality still fell short. The court reasoned that adding up non-violent (though highly threatening) events does not magically create violent ones if no physical contact resulted in injury or long-term disability for Jimenez or his family members mentioned as victims.
