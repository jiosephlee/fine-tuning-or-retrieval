Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Analysis of Past Persecution: Threshold Determinations

## Introduction to the Threshold Inquiry
The first major hurdle in any asylum claim is establishing that the applicant has suffered "persecution." This is a high bar that serves as a gatekeeping mechanism, distinguishing serious human rights violations from generalized violence or isolated criminal acts. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit faced the critical task of determining whether the harms suffered by Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family rose to this statutory level. The court's analysis focuses heavily on the nature of the harm itself, emphasizing that while threats and psychological distress are significant, they must be viewed through the lens of specific legal precedents to determine if they constitute persecution under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The court ultimately concluded that the petitioners' experiences, taken together, fell short of this threshold, a determination rooted in strict adherence to Tenth Circuit case law regarding the insufficiency of threats and the limits of psychological harm claims absent physical injury.

## 'Threats Alone' Doctrine: Why Verbal Warnings Are Insufficient
A foundational principle in Tenth Circuit jurisprudence is that threats, standing alone, are generally insufficient to constitute persecution. The *Jimenez* court reiterated this doctrine explicitly, citing *Karki v. Holder* for the proposition that while threats are relevant factors in assessing a claimant's overall situation, they do not automatically satisfy the definition of persecution. In this case, Petitioners relied heavily on a series of threatening communications: weekly phone calls starting in May 2022, a brochure stating "death to snitches," and verbal warnings delivered at gunpoint ordering them to leave their neighborhood. Despite the terrifying nature of these interactions, the court noted that Petitioners themselves conceded during their merits hearing that they never suffered physical harm at the hands of these individuals prior to fleeing Colombia. Furthermore, they acknowledged in their briefs that no lasting physical injuries resulted from these encounters.

The court reasoned that for harassment or intimidation to cross the line into persecution, it must entail more than mere restrictions on liberty or sporadic threats to life. While acknowledging that physical harm is not an absolute prerequisite for a finding of persecution, the court stressed that without it, the alleged mistreatment must be severe enough to be regarded as "offensive" in a profound sense. Mere verbal warnings or even armed confrontations where no shot is fired and no body is touched often fail this test unless accompanied by additional aggravating factors like prolonged detention or torture. In *Jimenez*, because the petitioners were not physically injured and conceded as much, their reliance solely on verbal and written threats could not sustain a finding of past persecution under existing circuit precedent.

## Assessment of Emotional and Psychological Harm Without Physical Injury
Petitioners argued that even absent physical trauma, their suffering included significant emotional and psychological damage warranting asylum eligibility. Angelica Parada Duarte testified about her inability to sleep or rest due to fear, and they presented evidence that their younger son developed panic attacks and stress reactions following an incident where he was grabbed by an associate of their persecutor. The court acknowledged these hardships but maintained a rigorous standard regarding purely psychological claims. Drawing on prior decisions such as *Brandy v. Holder*, the opinion clarified that while events causing fear can be "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," such feelings rarely suffice as independent grounds for asylum when divorced from physical harm or severe deprivation.

The Tenth Circuit emphasized its historical reluctance to grant asylum based exclusively on threats coupled with emotional distress. The court distinguished between understandable fear resulting from living in a volatile environment versus persecution requiring state-sanctioned or tolerated infliction of severe suffering. In *Jimenez*, despite the tragic nature of seeing one's child threatened and living under constant anxiety, these conditions were deemed insufficient to meet the statutory definition of persecution because they lacked the requisite severity usually associated with physical violence or long-term detention. The court noted that accepting such claims without accompanying physical harm would expand asylum eligibility beyond congressional intent, effectively converting all victims of crime-related fear into refugees regardless of whether their government was willing or able to protect them from similar risks faced by others in society.

## Cumulative Impact Analysis: Aggregating Multiple Incidents
Recognizing that single incidents might sometimes fall short while multiple occurrences could combine to create persecution, courts employ a cumulative impact analysis. This approach allows adjudicators to consider various forms of mistreatment together rather than examining each event in isolation—a method endorsed by cases like *Ritonga v. Holder*. However, aggregation does not mean simply adding up unrelated annoyances; rather, it involves evaluating whether repeated instances collectively reach a level so severe as to constitute persecution under international norms adopted by U.S. law (*Wiransane v. Ashcroft*).
