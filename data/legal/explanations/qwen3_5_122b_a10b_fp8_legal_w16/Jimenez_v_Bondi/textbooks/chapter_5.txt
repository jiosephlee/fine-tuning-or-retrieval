Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 5: # Government Protection/Internal Relocation Feasibility

## Evidence Required to Prove Government Unwillingness/Inability

When an asylum seeker claims that their home government is unable or unwilling to protect them from persecution, they bear the burden of providing concrete evidence to support that assertion. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit emphasizes that this determination relies heavily on the government's actual response—or lack thereof—to specific instances of persecution. The court explains that two primary factors weigh most significantly in this analysis: first, whether the government took steps to stop the persecutors, and second, whether the government offered direct protection to the applicant, such as placing them in protective custody. Crucially, for the government's reaction to be evaluated, the applicant must typically provide notice of the harm. Without evidence that an applicant reported past persecution to authorities, a reviewing court cannot determine how the state would have reacted or whether it possessed the willingness or ability to intervene. Consequently, a failure to report incidents can severely undermine a claim of government inability or unwillingness, although it is not automatically dispositive if the applicant can demonstrate that seeking help would have been futile or dangerous.

## Individual Officer Apathy vs. Systemic State Failure

A critical distinction in asylum jurisprudence is separating isolated incidents of police indifference from systemic state failure. In *Jimenez*, Petitioners argued that their interactions with law enforcement proved the Colombian government was complicit in their plight. Specifically, Jimenez recounted telling two different police officers about the threats he faced; both officers allegedly responded by advising him not to "seek trouble" and suggesting he leave the area rather than offering assistance. However, the court rejects this as sufficient proof of a governmental policy of non-protection. Drawing on precedent from other circuits and Tenth Circuit case law, *Jimenez* clarifies that while police apathy can be indicative of a broader problem, a single conversation with an unhelpful officer does not establish that an entire government is "unable or unwilling" to protect its citizens. To succeed on this element, applicants generally need evidence of systemic failures across various jurisdictions or levels of authority, rather than anecdotal accounts of individual officer negligence or corruption within a specific neighborhood. Because Jimenez and his father—who worked within the justice system—did not attempt to report these crimes through official channels beyond these informal conversations, there was no record for the court to assess regarding a systemic breakdown in protection mechanisms.

## Counter-Evidence: Government Searches Targeting Persecutor Operations

Perhaps most damaging to Petitioners' claim of government inability was the objective evidence regarding the activities of their alleged persecutor, "Don Rafa," and his relationship with state actors. Petitioners contended that Don Rafa was so closely aligned with President Gustavo Petro's administration that any attempt at state intervention would be futile. The court found this argument unsupported by the factual record presented during proceedings. On the contrary, evidence showed that law enforcement had actually identified Don Rafa's property as a hub for narco-trafficking and prostitution and had attempted multiple searches and seizures targeting those criminal operations. This activity directly contradicted Petitioners' assertion that their persecutor operated with total impunity due to high-level political connections. If the government were truly unwilling or unable to control Don Rafa due to political alignment, it would likely have refrained from attempting raids on his premises entirely. The existence of these failed search attempts demonstrated that while crime might persist in certain areas, there was no blanket refusal by the state apparatus to enforce laws against individuals like Don Rafa who were disrupting public order. Therefore, Petitioners could not sustain their burden of proving they lacked access to state protection had they formally sought it through proper legal channels.
