Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 2: # Legal Standards and Burdens of Proof in Asylum Claims

## Jurisdictional Basis: 8 U.S.C. § 1252 Review Authority

Before a federal appellate court can evaluate the merits of an immigration case, it must first confirm it possesses the authority to hear the matter. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit exercises jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, which grants courts of appeals the power to review "final orders of removal." This statute is the gateway for judicial oversight in immigration proceedings, allowing petitioners like Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family to challenge decisions made by the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). Crucially, this jurisdictional grant encompasses a consolidated review of all related claims, meaning the court reviews the asylum and withholding of removal denials together rather than treating them as entirely separate legal silos. However, this review is strictly bounded by the administrative record; the court looks only at the evidence presented during the proceedings before the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the BIA. If a petitioner attempts to introduce new facts or arguments that were not part of this official record, they fall outside the scope of judicial review under § 1252(b)(4)(A). Furthermore, when a single member of the BIA affirms an IJ's decision without issuing a separate opinion, as occurred in *Jimenez*, the reviewing court treats that affirmation as adopting the IJ's reasoning but remains free to consult the IJ's more detailed explanation to fully understand the basis for the lower tribunal's decision.

## Standard of Review: Substantial Evidence vs. De Novo Analysis

Understanding how an appellate court evaluates factual findings is perhaps the most critical skill for reading an immigration opinion. Unlike civil or criminal cases where an appellate court might re-examine facts *de novo* (from scratch), immigration appeals are governed by a highly deferential standard known as "substantial evidence." Under this standard, which applies to all factual findings made by the BIA and IJ, those findings are conclusive unless "any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." This is a demanding threshold for petitioners to overcome. It does not mean that if there is *some* evidence supporting a different conclusion, the agency's decision must be overturned. Instead, as long as there is substantial evidence in support of the agency's conclusion—even if there is conflicting evidence elsewhere in the record—the court must affirm. In *Jimenez*, this standard dictated much of the outcome; because there was substantial evidence supporting the BIA's view that threats alone did not constitute persecution or that political opinion was not a central motive, the Tenth Circuit was bound to accept those findings regardless of how sympathetic Petitioners' story might seem on its face. This deference ensures that fact-finding authority remains with agencies specializing in immigration matters rather than shifting to generalist judges who did not observe witness testimony firsthand.

## Statutory Definition of Refugee and Protected Grounds

At the heart of every asylum claim lies a specific statutory definition derived from both domestic law and international treaties. To qualify for asylum under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1), an applicant must prove they are a "refugee," defined as any person outside their country of nationality who is unable or unwilling to return because they suffer persecution or have a well-founded fear of persecution on account of five specific protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. The phrase "on account of" imposes a strict causation requirement; it is not enough for an applicant to simply possess a protected characteristic while simultaneously suffering harm. The harm must be motivated by that characteristic. In *Jimenez*, this definition framed Petitioners' entire legal strategy: they argued their harassment stemmed from their membership in Colombian Liberal Party leadership (political opinion) and potentially their status as lawyers assisting domestic violence victims (potentially membership in a particular social group). However, because Petitioners failed to properly brief their social group claim and focused exclusively on political opinion, their eligibility hinged entirely on whether their persecutors targeted them specifically because of their political views rather than other motives like financial disputes or personal vendettas arising from Jimenez's legal investigations into drug trafficking operations.

## Elements of Past Persecution: Severity, Nexus, and Government Control

Proving eligibility for asylum often begins with establishing "past persecution," which creates a rebuttable presumption that an applicant also holds a well-founded fear of future persecution if returned home. The Tenth Circuit has articulated three distinct elements required to prove past persecution: (1) an incident or series of incidents rising to the level of persecution; (2) committed on account of one of these statutorily protected grounds; and (3) perpetrated by government agents or forces that government is unable or unwilling to control. Defining "persecution" presents unique challenges because Congress never explicitly defined it in statute; instead, courts have developed jurisprudence stating that persecution involves severe suffering or harm inflicted upon those who differ in ways regarded as offensive by society. Importantly, courts look at incidents cumulatively rather than in isolation; while threats alone are typically insufficient to constitute persecution unless accompanied by physical harm or severe psychological trauma resulting from actual violence, repeated threats combined with other abuses may cross that line. However, mere restrictions on liberty or sporadic verbal threats usually fall short unless they create an atmosphere where life or freedom is genuinely threatened with imminent execution or torture. Additionally, even if severe harm occurs, it must be linked directly ("nexus") to a protected ground; if harm results primarily from criminal activity unrelated to politics—such as Jimenez interfering with drug traffickers' business interests—it fails this element regardless of severity. Finally, private actors can only count as persecutors if government authorities are demonstrably unable or unwilling to intervene effectively after being notified of such threats.

## Distinction Between Asylum (Discretionary) and Withholding of Removal (Mandatory)

While asylum and withholding-of-removal protections share similar substantive requirements regarding who qualifies as endangered abroad, they operate under fundamentally different legal frameworks concerning discretion versus mandate and applicable burdens of proof.
