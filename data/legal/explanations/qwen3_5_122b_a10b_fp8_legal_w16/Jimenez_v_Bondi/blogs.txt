Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

## The 'Central Reason' Trap: How Mixed Motives Doomed the Jimenez Family's Political Asylum Claim

In the high-stakes arena of asylum litigation, few legal hurdles are as treacherous—or as frequently misunderstood—as the "one central reason" standard. The Tenth Circuit's recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, issued on October 7, 2025, serves as a stark reminder that even a compelling narrative of political danger can collapse if the petitioner cannot disentangle their protected status from the mundane realities of criminal conflict. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Timothy Tymkovich denied relief to a Colombian family whose story oscillated between political campaigning and a gritty property dispute with a narco-trafficker. The ruling offers a masterclass in how courts parse mixed-motive scenarios, reinforcing that political opinion must be more than just background noise; it must be the engine driving the persecution.

At first glance, the Jimenez family's plight reads like a textbook asylum case. Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife were active members of the Colombian Liberal Party, engaging in door-to-door campaigning for a presidential candidate opposed to the incumbent administration. Their efforts were met with escalating hostility: threatening phone calls, the murder of a colleague, and eventually, direct intimidation from armed men who labeled them "snitches." Yet, beneath this veneer of political repression lay a critical complicating factor: Jimenez was also acting in his capacity as a provisional attorney representing a client in an eviction dispute against "Don Rafa," a local crime boss aligned with President Gustavo Petro's party. When Don Rafa realized Jimenez was investigating his illicit operations on the disputed property, the threats shifted from general political rhetoric to specific demands tied to Jimenez's professional interference.

The Tenth Circuit's denial hinges entirely on this distinction. While acknowledging that the petitioners faced genuine terror—including an attempt to abduct their young son—the court concluded that these harms did not rise to the level of "past persecution" sufficient to trigger asylum protections. More damningly for the petitioners' case, Judge Tymkovich found that even if one assumed past persecution occurred, it failed the statutory nexus test under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i). The statute requires that race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion be "at least one central reason" for the persecution. In *Jimenez*, the court determined that Don Rafa's motivation was overwhelmingly rooted in silencing an obstacle to his criminal enterprise and protecting his revenue stream from law enforcement scrutiny. The petitioners' Liberal Party affiliation was deemed "merely tangential and subordinate" to this primary motive.

This reasoning underscores a rigid application of mixed-motive doctrine that practitioners must navigate with precision. The court explicitly rejected the notion that because political activity made Jimenez visible or vulnerable initially (via phone threats from unknown actors), every subsequent act of violence by Don Rafa was automatically connected to his politics. Instead, Judge Tymkovich focused on causation: Did Don Rafa attack them *because* they were Liberals? Or did he attack them *because* they were lawyers interfering with his business? The record showed that Don Rafa specifically cited Jimenez's role as an investigator for the prosecutor's office and his interference with property seizures as the catalyst for escalation. By isolating these specific grievances from their broader political context, the court found that politics played only a minor role—a fatal flaw under current precedent which mandates that protected grounds cannot be incidental or superficial drivers of harm.

Beyond the nexus analysis, *Jimenez* reinforces significant barriers regarding government protection and internal relocation—two pillars often relied upon when past persecution is weak or contested regarding motive. The court scrutinized the petitioners' failure to exhaust remedies within Colombia before fleeing. Despite Jimenez's connections within the legal system and his employment history with government agencies, he never formally reported Don Rafa or sought official intervention until after fleeing to rural Bogotá for three weeks. The Tenth Circuit held that without concrete evidence of systemic futility—such as prior denials of aid or documented corruption linking state actors directly to Don Rafa—an isolated comment by an unhelpful police officer does not prove governmental unwillingness or inability to protect. Furthermore, the court pointed out that because Don Rafa had ordered them out of their neighborhood rather than expelled them from Colombia entirely, and because family remained safe elsewhere in Bogota and beyond, internal relocation was reasonably feasible.

The implications of *Jimenez* extend far beyond this single docket number; they signal a tightening grip on how appellate courts evaluate complex narratives where criminality and politics intersect. For immigration practitioners representing clients caught between drug cartels and political factions—a common scenario in Central America and parts of South America—the decision serves as a cautionary tale against relying solely on broad strokes of political affiliation. If a persecutor has multiple motives (financial gain vs. ideological suppression), counsel must marshal evidence proving that ideology was not just present but essential to the attack strategy. Mere correlation is insufficient; there must be proof that removing the political element would have prevented harm entirely or significantly altered its nature.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates that sympathy for victims of violence does not override statutory text when causation is ambiguous or multifaceted split between protected and unprotected grounds. While tragic circumstances often accompany these cases—as evidenced by Jameson's son being grabbed at school—the Tenth Circuit maintained its focus on legal sufficiency over equitable considerations absent clear proof of protected-ground centrality. As lower courts grapple with increasingly sophisticated narratives involving hybrid criminal-political actors in Latin America, *Jimenez* stands as a definitive guidepost: in asylum law, understanding exactly *why* you were targeted is just as important as surviving it once you have been targeted at all



## Threats Without Harm: Reaffirming the High Bar for 'Past Persecution' in Private Violence Cases

The path to asylum in the United States is paved with stories of profound suffering, yet the legal threshold for qualifying as a "refugee" remains stubbornly high. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, issued by the Tenth Circuit on October 7, 2025, Judge Timothy Tymkovich delivered a stark reminder of that reality. The panel unanimously denied relief to a Colombian family whose harrowing narrative included death threats, an attempted abduction of a child, and severe psychological trauma. While the facts are undeniably disturbing, the court's holding underscores a critical doctrinal boundary: under existing Tenth Circuit precedent, threats and emotional distress—no matter how terrifying—do not automatically constitute "persecution" absent physical harm or significant deprivation of liberty. This decision reinforces the circuit's rigid adherence to the principle that asylum is reserved for those who have suffered actual inflicting of suffering, not merely the fear of it.

The petitioners, Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, presented a complex web of dangers rooted in both their political activism and their professional entanglements with organized crime. Mr. Jimenez was an active supporter of a rival presidential candidate and worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor's office assisting victims of crime. His legal intervention in a property dispute involving "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficker aligned with President Gustavo Petro's administration, seemingly triggered a violent escalation. The family faced repeated threats, saw a colleague kidnapped and murdered, and witnessed an assailant grab their young son at school before fleeing to the United States. Despite admitting credibility before the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), the family lost on all counts: they failed to prove past persecution, failed to establish a nexus between their harm and political opinion, failed to show government inability to protect them, and failed to prove that internal relocation was impossible.

At the heart of *Jimenez* lies the court's rigorous application of the "past persecution" standard. The Tenth Circuit reiterated its longstanding doctrine that while persecution need not involve physical violence per se, it must entail more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats. Relying heavily on precedents like *Karki v. Holder* and *Chhetri v. Rosen*, Judge Tymkovich wrote that threats alone are insufficient to cross the statutory line. The court acknowledged the petitioners' genuine fear and documented psychological toll—including panic attacks in their children—but drew a sharp distinction between suffering that is "psychologically unsettling" and harm that rises to the level of persecution required by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). By emphasizing that no physical harm occurred during these encounters—a concession made by counsel—the court signaled that emotional distress, even when chronicled vividly in testimony, cannot independently sustain an asylum claim in this circuit without accompanying physical violence or total loss of freedom.

Perhaps even more consequential than the threshold analysis was the court's handling of the "nexus" requirement—the necessity that persecution be "on account of" a protected ground like political opinion. Here, *Jimenez* offers a nuanced lesson on mixed-motive cases common in Latin American contexts where criminal gangs often possess political allegiances. The petitioners argued they were targeted for their Liberal Party affiliation; however, the record revealed that while they received generic political threats earlier in 2022 from unknown actors who killed others but never touched them personally, Jimenez only came under direct fire from Don Rafa after interfering with his illicit operations via his legal work. The BIA correctly identified that Don Rafa's motivation was retaliatory against an obstacle to his business interests ("snitching"), rendering Jimenez's political views merely tangential or subordinate to his role as an adversary in a criminal enterprise. As Judge Tymkovich noted, even if political opinion played *some* role, it was not "one central reason" for the specific acts of violence committed by Don Rafa's associates. This distinction effectively insulates many individuals caught between warring factions from asylum relief if their primary danger stems from non-political conflicts like debt collection or territorial disputes within criminal organizations.

The decision also sheds light on how courts evaluate state protection claims when applicants allege collusion between criminals and government officials—a frequent argument in Colombian cases involving figures tied to President Petro's coalition. The petitioners contended that reporting their plight would be futile because Don Rafa enjoyed high-level government connections. However, the Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence contradicting this: law enforcement had actively attempted searches at Don Rafa's property prior to Jimenez's interference, suggesting state capacity exists even if imperfectly executed locally. Furthermore, because Jimenez—who had government contacts—did not formally report these specific escalations beyond informal chats with local officers who advised him to flee rather than file charges, he could not demonstrate futility with sufficient clarity under *Aviles-Gonzalez*. The court refused to accept speculative assertions about systemic corruption without concrete proof that official channels were closed specifically to these petitioners once they engaged them properly.

Doctrinally, *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a reaffirmation of judicial restraint in asylum determinations within the Tenth Circuit. It highlights a persistent tension between evolving human rights understandings—which increasingly recognize psychological trauma as severe harm—and established administrative law principles requiring objective manifestations of persecution for statutory eligibility. For practitioners representing clients facing similar fact patterns involving private actors with ambiguous motives or non-physical intimidation tactics operating alongside legitimate governance structures elsewhere in Colombia or abroad, this ruling is sobering news. It suggests that until Congress amends the definition of refugee or Supreme Court jurisprudence shifts significantly on what constitutes "infliction," circuits will remain hesitant to expand asylum eligibility based solely on credible fear without corroborating evidence of physical injury or total immobilization by state-sanctioned forces. ## The Reporting Imperative: Why Failure to Seek Police Protection Fatalized Claims Against Narco-State Actors

The intersection of organized crime and state power presents one of the most complex challenges in asylum jurisprudence. When a petitioner alleges that their persecutors are intertwined with the very government tasked with protecting them, the burden shifts to demonstrating not just that violence occurred, but that the state is effectively paralyzed or complicit in allowing it. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, decided October 7, 2025, the Tenth Circuit delivered a stark lesson on the procedural pitfalls of making that argument without concrete evidence. By denying asylum to a Colombian family targeted by a narco-trafficker allegedly aligned with the presidency, the court underscored a critical principle: generalized assumptions about state corruption cannot substitute for specific proof of futility when seeking police protection.

The petitioners, Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, presented a harrowing narrative rooted in Colombia's volatile political landscape. As active members of the Liberal Party campaigning against President Gustavo Petro's ally, they faced escalating threats from "Don Rafa," a narco-guerrilla leader who controlled a notorious property in Bogotá. The situation turned deadly serious when Jimenez, acting as a provisional lawyer for a client in a property dispute, inadvertently exposed Don Rafa's illicit operations—a mix of migrant trafficking and prostitution housed within a condemned building. Following Jimenez's investigation and subsequent confrontation with the traffickers' enforcers, the family received death threats, suffered intimidation tactics involving their children, and fled to the United States. Despite these alarming facts, the Tenth Circuit affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) denial of relief, finding that the record failed to satisfy three distinct statutory hurdles: past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, and government inability or unwillingness to protect.

While the court's analysis touched on all three prongs, its discussion regarding government protection offers the most significant takeaway for practitioners navigating similar claims against "narco-state" actors. The petitioners argued that seeking help from Colombian authorities would have been futile because Don Rafa enjoyed high-level political connections. However, as Judge Tymkovich wrote for the panel, this assertion remained largely speculative. The court emphasized that an applicant must provide notice to the government before claiming it was unable or unwilling to act. Without evidence that law enforcement was actually approached and rebuffed—or that such an approach would have been demonstrably dangerous beyond mere speculation—the claim of state acquiescence collapses under scrutiny.

Crucially, the record contained evidence directly contradicting the notion of total state paralysis regarding Don Rafa's operations. The petitioners themselves acknowledged that law enforcement had attempted multiple searches and seizures at the contested property prior to their flight. Furthermore, while Jimenez spoke briefly to two patrol officers who advised him not to "seek trouble," he never filed formal reports or leveraged his professional connections within the National Prosecutor's Office or his father's employment in law enforcement. The court reasoned that isolated instances of police apathy do not equate to systemic government unwillingness absent broader evidence. By failing to document specific attempts at recourse or prove why those attempts were impossible rather than merely risky, the petitioners left themselves vulnerable to a finding that they simply chose not to engage with available state mechanisms.

This ruling reinforces a rigorous evidentiary standard regarding internal relocation and state protection that often catches litigants off guard. In cases involving powerful criminal syndicates with political ties, there is an intuitive temptation for applicants to bypass local authorities entirely out of fear or cynicism toward officialdom. *Jimenez* warns against this reflexive avoidance strategy unless accompanied by robust documentation showing that engagement is objectively impossible or lethally dangerous in every instance—not just convenient for avoiding bureaucracy or perceived corruption. The court noted that even if Don Rafa had some influence over certain officials elsewhere in Bogotá, this did not automatically render all parts of Colombia unsafe or all branches of government impotent against him.

The decision also sheds light on how courts parse mixed-motive harassment scenarios common in politically charged environments like Colombia's recent elections. Here, Jimenez was simultaneously engaged in partisan campaigning and professional legal advocacy against a criminal enterprise operating under political cover. While threats initially stemmed from his political work with the Liberal Party—which might suggest a protected ground—the escalation occurred specifically after he interfered with Don Rafa's business interests by investigating his property lease violations and reporting irregularities to health inspectors and prosecutors. Consequently, the Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the BIA's conclusion that Don Rafa's primary motivation was silencing an obstructionist lawyer rather than punishing a political dissident per se. This distinction is vital; when personal vendettas arising from civil disputes merge with political activity, applicants must clearly delineate which motive drove the persecution if they hope to establish nexus under § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i).

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a cautionary tale about the gap between subjective fear and objective proof in immigration litigation. The tragedy of fleeing violence is undeniable here; yet asylum law demands more than sympathy—it requires a factual architecture proving that no alternative exists within one's home country where safety can be secured through state intervention or geographic displacement. For lawyers representing clients facing similar dynamics where criminal actors wear political masks, *Jimenez* dictates a clear path forward: do not assume futility; document it exhaustively before stepping onto American soil expecting sanctuary based on an untested hypothesis about foreign governance failures
