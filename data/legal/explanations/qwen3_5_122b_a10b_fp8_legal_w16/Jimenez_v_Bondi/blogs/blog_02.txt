Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

## Threats Without Harm: Reaffirming the High Bar for 'Past Persecution' in Private Violence Cases

The path to asylum in the United States is paved with stories of profound suffering, yet the legal threshold for qualifying as a "refugee" remains stubbornly high. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, issued by the Tenth Circuit on October 7, 2025, Judge Timothy Tymkovich delivered a stark reminder of that reality. The panel unanimously denied relief to a Colombian family whose harrowing narrative included death threats, an attempted abduction of a child, and severe psychological trauma. While the facts are undeniably disturbing, the court's holding underscores a critical doctrinal boundary: under existing Tenth Circuit precedent, threats and emotional distress—no matter how terrifying—do not automatically constitute "persecution" absent physical harm or significant deprivation of liberty. This decision reinforces the circuit's rigid adherence to the principle that asylum is reserved for those who have suffered actual inflicting of suffering, not merely the fear of it.

The petitioners, Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, presented a complex web of dangers rooted in both their political activism and their professional entanglements with organized crime. Mr. Jimenez was an active supporter of a rival presidential candidate and worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor's office assisting victims of crime. His legal intervention in a property dispute involving "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficker aligned with President Gustavo Petro's administration, seemingly triggered a violent escalation. The family faced repeated threats, saw a colleague kidnapped and murdered, and witnessed an assailant grab their young son at school before fleeing to the United States. Despite admitting credibility before the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), the family lost on all counts: they failed to prove past persecution, failed to establish a nexus between their harm and political opinion, failed to show government inability to protect them, and failed to prove that internal relocation was impossible.

At the heart of *Jimenez* lies the court's rigorous application of the "past persecution" standard. The Tenth Circuit reiterated its longstanding doctrine that while persecution need not involve physical violence per se, it must entail more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats. Relying heavily on precedents like *Karki v. Holder* and *Chhetri v. Rosen*, Judge Tymkovich wrote that threats alone are insufficient to cross the statutory line. The court acknowledged the petitioners' genuine fear and documented psychological toll—including panic attacks in their children—but drew a sharp distinction between suffering that is "psychologically unsettling" and harm that rises to the level of persecution required by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). By emphasizing that no physical harm occurred during these encounters—a concession made by counsel—the court signaled that emotional distress, even when chronicled vividly in testimony, cannot independently sustain an asylum claim in this circuit without accompanying physical violence or total loss of freedom.

Perhaps even more consequential than the threshold analysis was the court's handling of the "nexus" requirement—the necessity that persecution be "on account of" a protected ground like political opinion. Here, *Jimenez* offers a nuanced lesson on mixed-motive cases common in Latin American contexts where criminal gangs often possess political allegiances. The petitioners argued they were targeted for their Liberal Party affiliation; however, the record revealed that while they received generic political threats earlier in 2022 from unknown actors who killed others but never touched them personally, Jimenez only came under direct fire from Don Rafa after interfering with his illicit operations via his legal work. The BIA correctly identified that Don Rafa's motivation was retaliatory against an obstacle to his business interests ("snitching"), rendering Jimenez's political views merely tangential or subordinate to his role as an adversary in a criminal enterprise. As Judge Tymkovich noted, even if political opinion played *some* role, it was not "one central reason" for the specific acts of violence committed by Don Rafa's associates. This distinction effectively insulates many individuals caught between warring factions from asylum relief if their primary danger stems from non-political conflicts like debt collection or territorial disputes within criminal organizations.

The decision also sheds light on how courts evaluate state protection claims when applicants allege collusion between criminals and government officials—a frequent argument in Colombian cases involving figures tied to President Petro's coalition. The petitioners contended that reporting their plight would be futile because Don Rafa enjoyed high-level government connections. However, the Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence contradicting this: law enforcement had actively attempted searches at Don Rafa's property prior to Jimenez's interference, suggesting state capacity exists even if imperfectly executed locally. Furthermore, because Jimenez—who had government contacts—did not formally report these specific escalations beyond informal chats with local officers who advised him to flee rather than file charges, he could not demonstrate futility with sufficient clarity under *Aviles-Gonzalez*. The court refused to accept speculative assertions about systemic corruption without concrete proof that official channels were closed specifically to these petitioners once they engaged them properly.

Doctrinally, *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a reaffirmation of judicial restraint in asylum determinations within the Tenth Circuit. It highlights a persistent tension between evolving human rights understandings—which increasingly recognize psychological trauma as severe harm—and established administrative law principles requiring objective manifestations of persecution for statutory eligibility. For practitioners representing clients facing similar fact patterns involving private actors with ambiguous motives or non-physical intimidation tactics operating alongside legitimate governance structures elsewhere in Colombia or abroad, this ruling is sobering news. It suggests that until Congress amends the definition of refugee or Supreme Court jurisprudence shifts significantly on what constitutes "infliction," circuits will remain hesitant to expand asylum eligibility based solely on credible fear without corroborating evidence of physical injury or total immobilization by state-sanctioned forces.
