Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

## The Reporting Imperative: Why Failure to Seek Police Protection Fatalized Claims Against Narco-State Actors

The intersection of organized crime and state power presents one of the most complex challenges in asylum jurisprudence. When a petitioner alleges that their persecutors are intertwined with the very government tasked with protecting them, the burden shifts to demonstrating not just that violence occurred, but that the state is effectively paralyzed or complicit in allowing it. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, decided October 7, 2025, the Tenth Circuit delivered a stark lesson on the procedural pitfalls of making that argument without concrete evidence. By denying asylum to a Colombian family targeted by a narco-trafficker allegedly aligned with the presidency, the court underscored a critical principle: generalized assumptions about state corruption cannot substitute for specific proof of futility when seeking police protection.

The petitioners, Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his family, presented a harrowing narrative rooted in Colombia's volatile political landscape. As active members of the Liberal Party campaigning against President Gustavo Petro's ally, they faced escalating threats from "Don Rafa," a narco-guerrilla leader who controlled a notorious property in Bogotá. The situation turned deadly serious when Jimenez, acting as a provisional lawyer for a client in a property dispute, inadvertently exposed Don Rafa's illicit operations—a mix of migrant trafficking and prostitution housed within a condemned building. Following Jimenez's investigation and subsequent confrontation with the traffickers' enforcers, the family received death threats, suffered intimidation tactics involving their children, and fled to the United States. Despite these alarming facts, the Tenth Circuit affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals' (BIA) denial of relief, finding that the record failed to satisfy three distinct statutory hurdles: past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, and government inability or unwillingness to protect.

While the court's analysis touched on all three prongs, its discussion regarding government protection offers the most significant takeaway for practitioners navigating similar claims against "narco-state" actors. The petitioners argued that seeking help from Colombian authorities would have been futile because Don Rafa enjoyed high-level political connections. However, as Judge Tymkovich wrote for the panel, this assertion remained largely speculative. The court emphasized that an applicant must provide notice to the government before claiming it was unable or unwilling to act. Without evidence that law enforcement was actually approached and rebuffed—or that such an approach would have been demonstrably dangerous beyond mere speculation—the claim of state acquiescence collapses under scrutiny.

Crucially, the record contained evidence directly contradicting the notion of total state paralysis regarding Don Rafa's operations. The petitioners themselves acknowledged that law enforcement had attempted multiple searches and seizures at the contested property prior to their flight. Furthermore, while Jimenez spoke briefly to two patrol officers who advised him not to "seek trouble," he never filed formal reports or leveraged his professional connections within the National Prosecutor's Office or his father's employment in law enforcement. The court reasoned that isolated instances of police apathy do not equate to systemic government unwillingness absent broader evidence. By failing to document specific attempts at recourse or prove why those attempts were impossible rather than merely risky, the petitioners left themselves vulnerable to a finding that they simply chose not to engage with available state mechanisms.

This ruling reinforces a rigorous evidentiary standard regarding internal relocation and state protection that often catches litigants off guard. In cases involving powerful criminal syndicates with political ties, there is an intuitive temptation for applicants to bypass local authorities entirely out of fear or cynicism toward officialdom. *Jimenez* warns against this reflexive avoidance strategy unless accompanied by robust documentation showing that engagement is objectively impossible or lethally dangerous in every instance—not just convenient for avoiding bureaucracy or perceived corruption. The court noted that even if Don Rafa had some influence over certain officials elsewhere in Bogotá, this did not automatically render all parts of Colombia unsafe or all branches of government impotent against him.

The decision also sheds light on how courts parse mixed-motive harassment scenarios common in politically charged environments like Colombia's recent elections. Here, Jimenez was simultaneously engaged in partisan campaigning and professional legal advocacy against a criminal enterprise operating under political cover. While threats initially stemmed from his political work with the Liberal Party—which might suggest a protected ground—the escalation occurred specifically after he interfered with Don Rafa's business interests by investigating his property lease violations and reporting irregularities to health inspectors and prosecutors. Consequently, the Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the BIA's conclusion that Don Rafa's primary motivation was silencing an obstructionist lawyer rather than punishing a political dissident per se. This distinction is vital; when personal vendettas arising from civil disputes merge with political activity, applicants must clearly delineate which motive drove the persecution if they hope to establish nexus under § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i).

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as a cautionary tale about the gap between subjective fear and objective proof in immigration litigation. The tragedy of fleeing violence is undeniable here; yet asylum law demands more than sympathy—it requires a factual architecture proving that no alternative exists within one's home country where safety can be secured through state intervention or geographic displacement. For lawyers representing clients facing similar dynamics where criminal actors wear political masks, *Jimenez* dictates a clear path forward: do not assume futility; document it exhaustively before stepping onto American soil expecting sanctuary based on an untested hypothesis about foreign governance failures
