Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Can 'cumulative' threats and psychological distress ever constitute 'persecution' absent physical harm in the 10th Circuit?
Question:
I am struggling with the court's definition of 'past persecution' in Section II(C). The petitioners faced repeated death threats, a colleague was murdered, their child was grabbed, and they suffered significant anxiety and sleeplessness. However, the court states that 'threats alone are insufficient' and cites cases where even armed robberies didn't count. Does the 10th Circuit essentially require actual physical injury or detention to meet the threshold for persecution? If so, how does this align with the statutory language which doesn't explicitly mandate physical harm?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** No, the 10th Circuit does not strictly require physical injury or detention to constitute "persecution," but it sets a very high bar for psychological harm and threats alone. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court reaffirmed that while persecution need not involve physical violence, **threats combined with psychological distress are generally insufficient** unless they are accompanied by severe consequences (like prolonged detention, torture, or forced displacement) that go beyond the anxiety naturally resulting from the threats themselves.

### The Legal Standard: Cumulative Harm vs. Mere Threats
The 10th Circuit applies a "totality of the circumstances" test to determine if past persecution occurred. As stated in *Ritonga v. Holder*, courts must consider incidents collectively because "the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." However, this aggregation has limits.

In *Jimenez*, the court explicitly clarified that **"threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution"** (*Karki v. Holder*). This principle holds true even when:
*   The threats are frequent (weekly calls).
*   There is collateral violence (a colleague was murdered).
*   There is a close call involving a child (being grabbed/held down).
*   The petitioner suffers significant anxiety, sleeplessness, and panic attacks.

### Application to Your Facts in *Jimenez*
You correctly identified that the petitioners faced a disturbing array of events: death threats, a murdered colleague, an armed robbery/assault on an employee at their business, and their son being physically restrained by an assailant. Despite this severity, the 10th Circuit denied the claim for two primary reasons related to your question about physical harm:

1.  **Concession of Non-Physical Harm:** The petitioners expressly conceded they suffered **"no lasting physical harm."** The court emphasized that while physical harm is not mandatory (*Brandy v. Holder*), the alternative harm must be "offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats."
2.  **Psychological Harm Must Be Distinct from Fear:** The court distinguished between the natural fear caused by threats and independent psychological trauma severe enough to equal persecution. Writing for the panel, Judge Tymkovich noted that while events were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," asylum is **"rarely, if ever"** granted based solely on these factors absent other aggravating circumstances like detention or torture (*Brandy*; *Chhetri*).

The court cited *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, where even armed robberies and machete threats were deemed insufficient because they were contingent on compliance with demands. Similarly, in *Jimenez*, the escalation of threats (including the son's abduction attempt) was viewed as part of a continuum of intimidation that fell short of the statutory threshold for "severe suffering" required for asylum eligibility in this circuit when no actual physical injury occurred to the petitioners themselves.

### Why Physical Harm Isn't Explicitly Required But Is Practically Decisive Here
The statutory language of the INA indeed does not mandate physical harm. However, in practice within the 10th Circuit, **physical harm serves as a proxy for severity.** Without it, applicants must prove that their psychological suffering is profound enough to equate to torture or severe deprivation of liberty (e.g., long-term wrongful imprisonment).

In *Jimenez*, the psychological distress described—anxiety and sleeplessness—was deemed insufficient because it was viewed as a direct reaction to the threats rather than an independent inflicting of suffering comparable to torture or severe beatings. The court held that even aggregating all incidents (the murder of a friend, the son's near-capture), the totality did not cross the line from "fear" into "persecution" because:
*   The petitioners were never detained or beaten personally.
*   The harassment did not result in total loss of livelihood or forced exile from their entire region prior to fleeing (they relocated internally for three weeks first).

### Conclusion
The 10th Circuit does not have a per se rule requiring physical injury; however, absent such injury or detention, **psychological distress must be exceptionally severe** to qualify as persecution. In *Jimenez*, the combination of repeated death threats and witnessing violence against others—even involving one's own child—did not meet this heightened standard because the petitioners suffered no direct physical attack and their psychological symptoms were attributed to fear rather than independent inflicting of severe mental anguish akin to torture.

Therefore, while theoretical scenarios exist where non-physical harm constitutes persecution (e.g., systematic interrogation causing psychosis without touch), *Jimenez* illustrates that routine death threats and resulting anxiety/sleeplessness will almost certainly fail in this circuit without accompanying physical assault or unlawful detention.
