Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the 'central reason' test apply when a persecutor has mixed motives involving both political opinion and personal vendetta?
Question:
In Section II(D), the court finds that while Jimenez had a political affiliation, his primary motivation for being targeted was his interference with Don Rafa's criminal enterprise (the property dispute). The court concludes the political opinion was merely 'tangential.' I understand that political opinion need not be the *sole* reason, but how high is the bar for it to be a 'central reason'? If a drug lord hates you because you are a liberal politician AND because you disrupted his business operations, is it legally impossible to satisfy the nexus requirement if the business disruption seems like a stronger motivator?
Answer:
**Answer**

You are asking about the threshold for satisfying the "one central reason" standard in mixed-motive asylum cases, specifically when a persecutor acts out of both political animus and personal or criminal self-interest. Based on *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 2025 WL 2447603 (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025), the short answer is: **No, it is not legally impossible to satisfy the nexus requirement**, but the applicant faces a high evidentiary burden to prove that the political opinion was not merely "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate" to the other motive.

Here is how the Tenth Circuit applies this standard in practice, drawing directly from the *Jimenez* opinion:

### 1. The Legal Standard: "Central Reason," Not "Sole Reason"
As noted in Section II(D) of *Jimenez*, the statute requires that a protected ground (like political opinion) be **"at least one central reason"** for the persecution. The court explicitly acknowledges that a persecutor can have multiple motives (*O.C.V. v. Bondi*). However, if there are mixed motives, the protected ground cannot play a "minor role." It must be central to the decision to harm the applicant.

If a drug lord hates you for being a liberal politician *and* because you disrupted his business, you can still win if you prove that his hatred of your politics was a driving force behind his actions, independent of or equal to his desire to protect his profits.

### 2. How *Jimenez* Applied This Standard
In *Jimenez*, the court found that while Petitioners had a genuine political affiliation (supporting a rival presidential candidate), their political opinion was **"merely tangential"** to Don Rafa's motivations. The court reached this conclusion based on specific factual findings regarding **timing** and **causation**:

*   **Temporal Disconnect:** Before Jimenez intervened in the property dispute involving Don Rafa's illicit rental operation, Petitioners faced generic threats related to their campaigning ("death to snitches"). Crucially, the court noted there was no evidence Don Rafa was behind those initial threats.
*   **Escalation Linked to Business Interference:** The threats escalated significantly only *after* Jimenez investigated Don Rafa's property and attempted to evict him on behalf of a client. When attackers confronted Jimenez, they specifically cited his role as an investigator for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for disrupting Don Rafa's operations ("responsible for searches and seizures").
*   **Predominant Motive:** The court concluded that because the violence and threats correlated directly with Jimenez interfering with Don Rafa's criminal enterprise—and not simply his voting record or campaign activities—the political opinion was subordinate to the business motive.

### 3. Applying This to Your Hypothetical
Your scenario posits a drug lord who targets you because you are a liberal politician **AND** because you disrupted his business operations where he claims business disruption is the "stronger motivator."

Under *Jimenez*, this does not automatically disqualify your claim, but it places a heavy burden on you to distinguish your situation from Jimenez's:

*   **If the disruption is purely financial/operational:** If evidence shows the persecutor would have spared you had you remained silent about your politics but continued disrupting business (or vice versa), or if the attacks occurred exclusively in response to business interference regardless of your political views, then—as in *Jimenez*—the political opinion remains "tangential."
*   **If politics drives even partial action:** You must demonstrate that even absent the business dispute, your political identity would have triggered similar hostility from this specific actor. For example, if Don Rafa had previously targeted other politicians who did not interfere with his business, or if he explicitly linked your activism to your investigation (e.g., calling you a "political snitch"), that might elevate politics back up to a "central reason."

### Summary
The bar is high because courts defer to agency fact-finding under the substantial evidence standard (*Yuk v. Ashcroft*). In *Jimenez*, once the record showed that threats spiked only after business interference began and were accompanied by accusations regarding that interference alone, there was no room for inference that politics was central enough to satisfy § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i).

To succeed in your hypothetical where business disruption seems dominant, you would need evidence showing that Don Rafa viewed your political identity as an inherent threat equal to or greater than your operational threat—a distinction Jimenez failed to make because his political activity predated any conflict with Don Rafa by months without consequence from him until the property dispute arose.
