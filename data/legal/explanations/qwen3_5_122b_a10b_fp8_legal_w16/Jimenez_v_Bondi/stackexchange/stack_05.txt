Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why does failing to file a formal police report create a fatal gap in establishing government inability/unwillingness even when danger seems obvious?
Question:
I am confused by the logic in Section II(E) regarding futility. The petitioners argued it would be futile/dangerous to report because Don Rafa is aligned with President Petro. However, they admitted they never actually tried filing a report beyond talking to random beat cops. The court says without trying, we don't know how police would react. But if there is credible evidence (like Don Rafa's public profile) suggesting high-level alignment, shouldn't the burden shift slightly? Why is the petitioner strictly required to risk themselves by attempting formal reporting before claiming futility?
Answer:
The short answer is that under Tenth Circuit precedent, **speculative fear is not enough to excuse the duty to report**, and the "futility" exception requires concrete evidence that the government itself—not just a rogue officer or a specific criminal—would refuse to act.

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court rejects the petitioners' argument that Don Rafa's alleged alignment with President Petro creates an automatic presumption of futility. Here is the breakdown of why the lack of a formal report remains fatal to their claim:

**1. The Burden of Proof Remains on the Applicant**
As established in Section II(E), the burden is on the petitioner to prove that the government is "unable or unwilling" to protect them. The court explicitly states that while a failure to report is not *per se* fatal, it shifts the evidentiary landscape significantly. Without a formal attempt, there is simply **"no way to know how police would have reacted"** (*Singh*). The court refuses to speculate on hypothetical outcomes based solely on general political rhetoric or rumors of high-level corruption.

**2. Distinction Between Individual Corruption and Systemic Unwillingness**
The petitioners relied on a conversation with a single beat cop who told them to "stay away." The court distinguishes between isolated instances of police apathy and systemic government failure. Citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the opinion notes that **"a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling.'"** To overcome this, petitioners must provide evidence of broader institutional refusal, which they failed to do by limiting their inquiries to informal chats rather than official filings.

**3. Contradictory Evidence in the Record Undercuts Futility**
Crucially, the court finds that the record actually contains evidence contradicting the idea that reporting would be futile. Section II(E) highlights that law enforcement had **"attempted several searches and seizures"** at Don Rafa's property prior to Jimenez's intervention. This factual finding directly rebuts the theory that Don Rafa was so powerful or aligned with the state that authorities could not touch him. If police were actively raiding his compound, it suggests they possessed both the willingness and ability to intervene, making Jimenez's claim that reporting would be pointless unsupported by facts.

**4. Specific Knowledge vs. General Allegations**
Finally, the court emphasizes that Jimenez had unique access and knowledge (as a law student and former prosecutor's intern) yet chose not to utilize formal channels or his professional contacts ("neither... reported wrongdoing"). When an applicant possesses specific avenues for relief but relies only on anecdotal interactions with low-level officers, they cannot later claim futility based on generalized fears of political alignment without first testing those avenues formally.

In summary, *Jimenez* holds that even if high-level corruption exists in theory, an applicant cannot rely on it as a blanket shield against reporting duties unless they can show they tried—and failed—to get help through proper channels, or present objective proof (such as previous police inaction in similar cases) that such attempts would inevitably result in harm or total disregard by the state apparatus.
