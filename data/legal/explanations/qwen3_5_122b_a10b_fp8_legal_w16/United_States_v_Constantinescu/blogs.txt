Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

## Beyond Ciminelli: How 'Pump-and-Dump' Schemes Survive the Right-to-Control Rejection via Fraudulent Inducement

For years, the landscape of federal fraud law seemed poised for a seismic shift following the Supreme Court's rejection of the "right-to-control" theory in *Ciminelli v. United States*. That 2023 decision sent shockwaves through white-collar prosecution, holding that depriving a victim of "potentially valuable economic information"—such as data needed to make informed business decisions—does not constitute the deprivation of a "property interest" under federal fraud statutes. Prosecutors scrambled to recalibrate, fearing that many sophisticated schemes relying on informational asymmetry might slip through the cracks. Now, with the Fifth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, we are seeing just how robustly federal courts can distinguish between the discredited right-to-control theory and the enduring "fraudulent-inducement" doctrine. The case serves as a critical reminder that while abstract information isn't property, money parted with due to lies absolutely is.

In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit reversed a district court's dismissal of indictments against several high-profile social media influencers accused of orchestrating massive "pump-and-dump" schemes. The defendants, leveraging their substantial online followings, allegedly purchased securities and then disseminated false and misleading posts about their trading positions and the stocks' potential value. This misinformation induced followers to buy in, driving up prices so the defendants could sell ("dump") their holdings for millions in profit. The district court had thrown out the charges, reasoning that the indictment failed to allege a deprivation of a traditional property interest. Instead, it viewed the scheme as one designed merely to steal "valuable economic information," which *Ciminelli* explicitly shielded from criminal liability under federal fraud laws.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal turns on a nuanced but vital doctrinal line drawn by the Supreme Court itself in *Kousisis v. United States* (decided in 2025). Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Kurt Engelhardt clarified that *Ciminelli* did not dismantle all theories of fraud based on deception; it only struck down those predicated solely on the loss of informational autonomy. The court emphasized that the indictment against Constantinescu and his co-conspirators did not rely on a right-to-control theory. It did not claim that victims lost something intangible like their ability to make better financial choices. Rather, it alleged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme: defendants used material misrepresentations to trick victims into entering contracts—specifically, purchases of securities—that required them to hand over hard cash or property immediately. As Judge Engelhardt noted, distinguishing these two theories is essential because fraudulent inducement protects tangible assets, whereas right-to-control attempts to protect an abstract right to make informed decisions.

Perhaps even more significant for practitioners is how *Constantinescu* addresses the question of victim harm. The defendants argued that because they provided shares in exchange for money—and thus did not necessarily leave their followers with nothing or less than market value—they lacked the requisite "intent to defraud." They contended that since they aimed only to profit themselves rather than inflict net economic loss on their victims, no fraud occurred. Relying heavily on *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit shut this down decisively. The court reiterated that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that a victim suffered an ultimate pecuniary loss or that the defendant sought specifically to injure them financially. If a defendant uses deception to obtain money or property from a victim, even if they provide something of arguably equivalent value in return (like stock shares), the crime is complete once money changes hands under false pretenses. The motive—to enrich oneself rather than impoverish another—is legally irrelevant to establishing intent under these statutes.

This ruling carries profound implications for modern securities enforcement in an era dominated by retail investor communities and social media influence peddling. It signals that while prosecutors can no longer stretch fraud statutes to cover mere non-disclosure or informational hoarding under a broad "right-to-control" umbrella, they remain free to pursue aggressive cases involving active deception leading to asset transfer. The decision effectively closes off one door while keeping another wide open: you cannot be prosecuted simply for withholding data that might help someone trade better, but you certainly can be prosecuted for lying about your trades to get people to give you their money before you cash out your position yourself.

Doctrinally, *Constantinescu* represents a stabilization rather than an expansion of federal fraud power following *Ciminelli*. By anchoring its analysis firmly in traditional property interests—the actual dollars spent by victims—it avoids the slippery slope concerns that led lower courts initially astray after 2023. However, it also warns defense attorneys that arguments centered on "no net loss" or "informational theft only" are likely dead ends when faced with clear allegations of fraudulent inducement affecting wallet contents rather than just mental processes. As digital markets continue to blur lines between genuine advice and coordinated manipulation, *Constantinescu* ensures that the core prohibition against stealing money through lies remains intact, regardless of whether those lies occur in boardrooms or comment sections.



## Intent to Defraud Without Intent to Harm: The End of the 'Net Loss' Requirement in Securities Fraud

In a decision that sharply narrows the defensive playbook for financial fraudsters, the Fifth Circuit reversed a district court's dismissal of indictments against a group of high-profile social media influencers accused of orchestrating a massive "pump and dump" scheme. Writing for the panel in *United States v. Constantinescu*, Circuit Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt delivered a ruling that does more than just reinstate charges; it clarifies a critical, often misunderstood boundary in federal fraud jurisprudence. The core question before the court was whether the government must prove that defendants intended to leave their victims economically worse off to sustain a conviction for securities fraud. Relying heavily on the Supreme Court's recent guidance in *Kousisis v. United States*, the Fifth Circuit answered with a resounding no. This ruling confirms that under current federal law, the crime of fraud is consummated by the deceptive acquisition of money or property, irrespective of whether the perpetrator intended to inflict net pecuniary loss on the victim.

The facts alleged in *Constantinescu* read like a modern cautionary tale for the digital age. Eight defendants, leveraging their substantial social media followings, allegedly purchased low-priced securities and then disseminated false information regarding their trading positions and the potential value of those stocks. Their goal was to induce their followers to buy into these inflated assets ("pump"), thereby driving up the price so the defendants could sell their holdings at a profit ("dump"). The scheme reportedly generated $114 million in illicit gains. However, when challenged on procedural grounds, the district court dismissed the indictment, adopting what many observers feared was an increasingly rigid interpretation of fraud following recent Supreme Court rulings like *Ciminelli*. The lower court concluded that because the defendants allegedly deprived followers only of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest, and because they sought profit rather than direct harm to victims' wallets, no offense had been stated.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal rests on two distinct but related pillars: first, distinguishing between theories regarding what constitutes "property," and second, dismantling the notion that an intent to harm is a necessary component of criminal intent to defraud. On the property front, Judge Engelhardt carefully navigated around *Ciminelli*, where the Supreme Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory—the idea that possessing accurate information needed for economic decision-making constitutes a protected property interest itself. The Fifth Circuit noted that while *Ciminelli* killed off right-to-control claims, it left intact "fraudulent inducement" theories. In this case, prosecutors did not argue that victims lost their right to information; rather, they alleged that defendants used lies to trick victims into handing over cash in exchange for securities. That transactional transfer of money remains a classic protected property interest under federal statute, placing this conduct squarely outside *Ciminelli*'s protective scope.

However, it is the court's analysis of mental state—specifically regarding "intent"—that carries significant weight for future litigation and regulatory enforcement. The defendants argued that because they intended only to enrich themselves through market manipulation rather than specifically aiming to bankrupt or impoverish their followers (who received stock worth market value at the moment of purchase), they lacked the requisite intent to defraud. This argument mirrors one recently raised and summarily rejected by Chief Justice Roberts writing for a unanimous Supreme Court in *Kousisis*. In *Kousisis*, involving bid-rigging schemes where contractors provided services but lied about compliance requirements, the Court held that fraud statutes do not demand proof that a defendant sought to leave victims with less money than they started with wither than simply proving they obtained money through deception.

Judge Engelhardt applied this logic directly to securities markets in *Constantinescu*. He emphasized that under *Kousisis*, "obtaining" another's money or property via material misrepresentation satisfies both elements of fraud: there is deception, and there is an object (the money) being pursued through that deception. Whether the victim walks away with goods or services of equivalent value at the time of exchange is irrelevant to criminal liability if those goods were procured through lies. By rejecting a "net loss" requirement as an element of mens rea, the Fifth Circuit effectively closes a loophole where sophisticated bad actors might claim innocence simply because their scam involved providing something tangible (even if overvalued) alongside their theft by deception.

The doctrinal implications are profound for how prosecutors approach complex financial crimes in an era where asset values can fluctuate wildly and transactions are instantaneous under social media pressure. Previously, defense attorneys might have hoped to argue that because victims received shares at fair market value during the transaction—or perhaps even made short-term profits before dumping them themselves—the essential element of harm was missing from both fact pattern and intent now seems foreclosed by binding precedent from Washington D.C., though strictly speaking only persuasive authority until adopted by other circuits). Yet courts across jurisdictions are already coalescing around this view; indeed Seventh Circuit cited Kousisis explicitly affirming this principle earlier this year too). 

Moreover, this decision reinforces stability within securities regulation by aligning criminal liability closely with civil antifraud doctrines which long recognized fraudulent inducement regardless subsequent investment performance). If anything it raises stakes slightly higher since now even well-meaning investors who temporarily benefit cannot shield manipulators once deception proven initial inducement occurred). Ultimately however greatest impact likely falls upon white-collar practitioners who can no longer rely upon technical distinctions between seeking profit versus causing loss when crafting arguments challenging sufficiency indictments involving deceptive practices designed extract capital markets). As Judge Engelhardt succinctly put it distinguishing profit motive from harmful intent becomes mere semantics when underlying objective remains securing funds via falsehoods—making clear path forward clear prosecution strategy going forward especially amid rise influencer-driven trading schemes threatening integrity global markets today alike everywhere else world over!



## Pumping, Dumping, and the Right to Control: The Fifth Circuit Draws a Line in the Digital Sand

The intersection of social media influence and federal criminal law has long promised a collision between First Amendment freedoms and the rigid demands of fraud statutes. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, decided on October 2, 2025, the Fifth Circuit provided a timely, if narrow, resolution to that tension. Reversing a district court's dismissal of a high-profile securities fraud indictment, the panel clarified that while the Supreme Court has closed the door on prosecuting the mere deprivation of "economic information," it remains firmly open to prosecutions involving classic "fraudulent inducement"—even when executed by influencers with massive Twitter and Instagram followings.

At first glance, *Constantinescu* appears to be a straightforward application of existing precedent to new facts. Eight defendants, including prominent financial commentators like Tom Cooperman and Stefan Hrvatin, were charged with orchestrating a "$114 million pump-and-dump" scheme. Their alleged modus operandi involved buying low-quality securities, hyping them via social media using false claims about their own trading positions and the stocks' potential, inducing followers to buy in and inflate the price, and then dumping their shares for profit. The district court threw out the indictment, reasoning that under recent Supreme Court jurisprudence, specifically *Ciminelli v. United States*, one cannot be convicted of fraud for stealing someone's "right to control" their assets by withholding or falsifying economic data. Since the followers received securities (albeit ones inflated by lies), the district court viewed this as an informational theft rather than a property crime.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal rests on a crucial distinction that separates *Ciminelli* from *Kousisis v. United States*. Writing for the panel in what is likely a landmark moment for crypto and influencer regulation, Circuit Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt emphasized that *Ciminelli* killed only one specific theory of liability: the right-to-control theory. That theory posits that having accurate information is itself a property interest sufficient to support a fraud charge if withheld or manipulated. However, as Justice Barrett noted in *Kousisis* just months prior (in this hypothetical timeline), there exists another well-established pathway: fraudulent inducement. Here, the government did not argue that defendants stole information; it argued they used lies as bait to trick victims into handing over cash for worthless or overvalued paper assets.

This distinction is more than semantic; it fundamentally reshapes how courts will analyze digital financial advice moving forward. The Fifth Circuit correctly identified that when an influencer says "buy this stock now" while secretly planning to sell their own holdings minutes later, they are not merely depriving followers of data—they are actively manufacturing consent for a transaction that would not otherwise occur. The court rejected the defendants' argument that because they made money rather than causing direct net losses (since followers technically received stock worth something), no intent to defraud existed. Relying heavily on *Kousisis*, Judge Engelhardt reiterated that fraud statutes punish the acquisition of money or property through deception regardless of whether the victim suffers an immediate pecuniary loss or receives something of equivalent nominal value in return. If you lie to get someone to sign a check, it doesn't matter if you give them back change later; the fraud occurred at the moment of extraction based on falsehoods.

From a doctrinal perspective, *Constantinescu* serves as an important corrective against judicial overreach into free speech protections while simultaneously reinforcing traditional boundaries around market integrity. Some might worry this ruling opens Pandora's box for prosecuting every instance of hyperbolic financial opinion shared online—a dangerous expansion of federal power into digital commentary. Yet, reading closely reveals that *Constantinescu* does no such thing. The indictment survived because it alleged specific acts of deception regarding material facts: false statements about trading positions and secret plans to dump shares. These are objective misrepresentations designed to induce capital transfer, not subjective opinions like "this stock looks great." The court explicitly distinguished between providing potentially valuable economic information (protected) and lying to extract capital (punishable). This preserves space for robust financial discourse while drawing a hard line at fabrication designed to manipulate markets for personal gain.

However, the decision invites significant downstream questions regarding enforcement in an era where algorithmic amplification blurs the lines between advisor and entertainer. By affirming that social media posts can serve as valid instruments of "fraudulent inducement," courts must now grapple with how much specificity is required in indictments before speech becomes actionable conduct in highly volatile markets like cryptocurrencies or meme stocks where volatility is often driven by sentiment rather than fundamentals? Furthermore, does this logic extend beyond traditional equity markets? If an influencer lies about their endorsement deal status or product efficacy on TikTok or YouTube to drive sales for affiliate products not covered by SEC regulations but perhaps falling under general wire fraud statutes (*18 U.S.C.* § 1343), does *Constantinescu*'s reasoning hold?

Ultimately, *United States v. Constantinescu* is less about expanding federal reach into digital spaces than it is about refusing to let technology obscure timeless principles of fraud law. It confirms that while our methods of communication have evolved from telephone wires to fiber-optic feeds carrying emojis and tweets, the core elements of fraud remain unchanged: deception used as leverage to obtain money or property constitutes a crime regardless of whether you call it "influencing" or "trading." As regulators increasingly target "fin-fluencers," this ruling provides a sturdy legal foundation for prosecution without requiring courts to stretch definitions of property rights beyond recognition—or conversely, without granting influencers immunity simply because their lies were broadcast rather than whispered in boardrooms.
