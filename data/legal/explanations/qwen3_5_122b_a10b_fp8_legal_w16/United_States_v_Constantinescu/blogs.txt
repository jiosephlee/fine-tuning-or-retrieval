Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

## Beyond Ciminelli: Why 'Pump-and-Dump' Schemes Survive the Right-to-Control Rejection

The Supreme Court's recent decision in *Ciminelli v. United States* sent shockwaves through federal white-collar prosecution, effectively killing the "right-to-control" theory of fraud. By ruling that the deprivation of valuable economic information alone does not constitute a property interest under federal fraud statutes, the Court narrowed the scope of criminal liability for deceptive practices. Yet, just months later, the Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, issued on October 2, 2025, demonstrates that *Ciminelli* is not a get-out-of-jail-free card for sophisticated market manipulators. Instead, the court drew a sharp, crucial line between stealing someone's right to make an informed decision and actually tricking them into handing over their wallet.

In *Constantinescu*, a group of social media influencers with massive followings allegedly orchestrated a classic "pump and dump" scheme. The indictment detailed how these defendants purchased securities, then used their platforms to disseminate false and misleading information about their trading positions and the securities' potential value. This misinformation induced thousands of followers to buy the stocks, artificially inflating the price so the defendants could sell ("dump") their holdings for a reported $114 million profit. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government had failed to allege a deprivation of traditional property rights; in its view, the scheme merely deprived victims of potentially valuable economic information—a fatal flaw under *Ciminelli*. The Fifth Circuit reversed this dismissal, holding that the indictment successfully alleged a traditional fraudulent-inducement theory rather than an impermissible right-to-control claim.

The crux of the Fifth Circuit's analysis rests on distinguishing two distinct theories of fraud that often get conflated in high-stakes financial litigation. As Judge Engelhardt noted for the panel, *Ciminelli* specifically targeted the "right-to-control" theory, which posits that a victim's property interest lies in their ability to control assets based on accurate information. The Supreme Court rejected this because informational autonomy is not a "traditional property interest." However, *Constantinescu* relies on what courts call "fraudulent inducement." Here, the object of the fraud was not simply to withhold data or distort a victim's decision-making process; it was to trick victims into executing transactions that transferred money and property from their pockets to those of the defendants. The court emphasized that while losing access to truthful data isn't enough for fraud under *Ciminelli*, being lied into signing away one's cash is exactly what federal securities laws were designed to punish.

Perhaps even more significant is how *Constantinescu* addresses the element of intent and harm. The defendants argued that because they provided shares (which arguably had market value) in exchange for their followers' money, there was no true "deprivation," especially since they claimed they never intended to leave their victims economically worse off—they just wanted to profit themselves. Relying heavily on another recent Supreme Court term case, *Kousisis v. United States*, published earlier in 2025, Judge Engelhardt dismantled this defense. The court clarified that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that a defendant sought to cause net pecuniary loss or leave a victim with nothing of value. As long as the defendant used deception to induce the transfer of money or property with an intent to obtain it for themselves, a crime has occurred. The fact that victims received stock—which might have been worth something—does not negate the fraud if they bought it based on lies regarding its value or liquidity prospects.

Doctrinally, *Constantinescu* serves as an important corrective for lower courts struggling to apply *Ciminelli*. It signals that while prosecutors can no longer stretch fraud statutes to cover mere regulatory violations or nondisclosure of strategic data where no asset changes hands due to deception, they remain fully empowered to pursue traditional scams involving misrepresentation and transactional manipulation. The decision reinforces that "property" in fraud cases still encompasses money and tangible assets obtained through deceitful bargaining chips like fake investment tips or fabricated trading records.

For practitioners and observers of white-collar law, this split between information-based claims and asset-transfer claims will likely define future litigation strategies in digital finance crimes. As social media continues to become a primary venue for investment advice and market manipulation, regulators will lean heavily on fraudulent-inducement theories rather than trying to shoehorn cases into right-to-control frameworks that are now constitutionally suspect under current precedent. The Fifth Circuit made clear: you cannot steal someone's right to know better without consequence if you use lies as bait; but you certainly cannot escape liability just because you gave them something back—even if what you gave them was worthless compared to what you took—provided you lied about it first.



## Intent to Defraud Without Intent to Harm: The Kousisis Standard in Social Media Fraud

The Fifth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, issued just days ago, marks a critical inflection point in how federal courts define the boundaries of securities fraud in the digital age. At first glance, the case appears to be a straightforward reversal of a district court's dismissal of a "pump-and-dump" indictment involving high-profile social media influencers. However, beneath the surface lies a profound doctrinal clarification regarding the *mens rea* required for fraud convictions. By leaning heavily on the Supreme Court's 2025 decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, the Fifth Circuit has firmly established that prosecutors need not prove a defendant intended to leave their victims financially ruined to secure a conviction. Instead, the focus shifts entirely to whether the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce the transfer of money or property. This ruling effectively closes the door on arguments that a "fair deal"—where victims receive market-value securities despite being misled—is immune from criminal prosecution.

The factual backdrop of *Constantinescu* is all too familiar in modern financial markets: a group of defendants with significant social media followings allegedly purchased low-priced securities, hyped them through deceptive posts claiming false trading positions and inflated price targets, and then sold ("dumped") their holdings at an artificial peak for roughly $114 million in profits. The district court initially threw out the indictment, reasoning that it failed to allege a deprivation of a "traditional property interest." The lower court seemed concerned that because the defendants were arguably depriving followers only of "valuable economic information" rather than cash directly, or perhaps because the followers received stock that might have held some intrinsic value, no fraud occurred. The Fifth Circuit, per Judge Engelhardt, corrected this reading immediately upon review, distinguishing between two competing theories of fraud that have dominated recent jurisprudence: the discredited "right-to-control" theory and the robust "fraudulent-inducement" theory.

The core legal battle in *Constantinescu* turned on how lower courts should apply *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023) and its progeny. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court struck down wire fraud convictions based on a theory that depriving someone of their right to control assets using incomplete information constituted fraud. That "right-to-control" theory had allowed prosecutors to charge individuals simply for withholding data necessary for sound economic decision-making. The district court in *Constantinescu* appeared to conflate this rejected theory with the facts at hand, fearing that charging influencers for hiding their trading strategies was an attempt to punish informational asymmetry rather than property theft. The Fifth Circuit clarified that this case was fundamentally different. Here, the government did not rely on a right-to-control theory; it alleged classic fraudulent inducement. The defendants didn't just withhold information; they actively lied to trick investors into handing over cash to buy specific stocks. As Judge Engelhardt noted, obtaining money through deception protects traditional property interests squarely within the scope of 18 U.S.C. § 1348, regardless of whether information rights are involved as well.

Perhaps more consequential for future litigation is the court's rejection of the defendants' second argument: that there can be no intent to defraud if they did not intend to cause their victims net economic loss. The defendants contended that because they were trying to profit—and perhaps because investors received shares worth what they paid—their conduct lacked the requisite criminal intent to harm another's "bottom line." This argument foundered on *Kousisis*. In that landmark decision earlier this year, the Supreme Court explicitly held that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that a victim suffered an economic loss or that a defendant sought to inflict one. A defendant violates the law if they scheme to obtain money or property by means of false pretenses, even if they provide something of equivalent value in return or even if the transaction leaves no net financial deficit for the victim at closing time.

The application of *Kousisis* in *Constantinescu* represents a significant tightening of liability standards for white-collar crime defendants who try to hide behind technicalities regarding victim harm. By affirming that "obtaining money" is distinct from "causing loss," Judge Engelhardt's opinion signals that the essence of securities fraud is now defined by the mechanism of acquisition rather than the aftermath for the investor. If an influencer lies about their portfolio strategy to convince followers to buy a stock so they can sell their own holdings at a premium, it does not matter if those followers happen to hold onto stocks that eventually rise in value independently or trade at fair market rates during pump-up periods. The crime is completed when false pretenses are used as leverage to extract capital from unsuspecting parties. This interpretation aligns perfectly with Congress's intent in enacting broad anti-fraud provisions designed to maintain market integrity by policing honesty in transactions, not just punishing bad outcomes for investors.

Doctrinally, this ruling serves as a warning shot against attempts to narrow fraud statutes based on subjective assessments of victim welfare or objective calculations of market efficiency moving forward down-stream effects will likely ripple through both civil and criminal enforcement landscapes for crypto-assets and meme-stock manipulators alike). Prosecutors will feel emboldened by this precedent when drafting indictments involving complex financial instruments where valuations are opaque; they no longer need wait until victims actually lose money before intervening criminally). Defense counsel will find themselves harder pressed arguing cases where clients claim good faith belief systems exist alongside clear misstatements since proving actual pecuniary damage becomes irrelevant once materiality and inducement are established). Ultimately,* Constantinescu* reinforces a pragmatic view: if you use lies as your primary tool for wealth extraction from others via securities markets you have crossed into illegal territory regardless whether your victims end up broke or merely disappointed.)



## The Digital Marketplace and Traditional Property: Redefining Fraud in the Age of Influencers

The rise of the social media influencer has fundamentally altered how retail investors discover and evaluate securities, creating a new frontier for financial crime. Yet, as the Fifth Circuit made clear this week in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the medium of deception does not change the nature of the offense. In reversing a district court's dismissal of a massive "pump-and-dump" indictment, the panel clarified that federal fraud statutes still hinge on the deprivation of traditional property interests, rejecting arguments that modern influence-peddling falls outside the scope of criminal liability simply because it occurs online rather than in a boardroom.

The case centers on eight defendants who leveraged their substantial social media followings to orchestrate a scheme that allegedly generated $114 million in illicit profits. By purchasing low-priced securities and then broadcasting false claims about their trading positions and the stocks' potential value, the group induced thousands of followers to buy in, artificially inflating prices before dumping their own shares at a profit. The district court had dismissed the indictment, reasoning that while the defendants misled their audience, they did not deprive victims of a "traditional property interest." Instead, the court suggested they merely stole "valuable economic information," a theory the Supreme Court had previously rejected in *Ciminelli*.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal rests on a crucial distinction between two competing theories of fraud: the "right-to-control" theory and "fraudulent inducement." As Justice Barrett noted in *Kousisis v. United States* (decided earlier this year), federal fraud statutes protect money and property, not abstract rights to information. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court struck down convictions based on the idea that depriving someone of data needed to make an informed decision constituted fraud. However, in *Constantinescu*, Judge Engelhardt emphasizes that this case does not rely on such an amorphous theory. The indictment explicitly alleges that defendants used lies to trick victims into entering contracts—specifically, buying securities—and handing over their cash. That transaction involves a tangible transfer of money and property, squarely fitting within established precedent.

Perhaps more contentious is the court's rejection of the argument that an intent to defraud requires an intent to cause net economic loss to the victim. The defendants contended that because their followers received actual shares (albeit inflated ones) rather than nothing at all, no harm occurred if those shares retained some market value. The Fifth Circuit firmly shut down this logic by leaning heavily on *Kousisis*, which held that fraud is complete upon obtaining money or property through deception, regardless of whether the victim ends up financially worse off than if they had known the truth. As Judge Engelhardt writes, providing something of equal value in return does not negate the fraud; what matters is that money changed hands under materially false pretenses. This interpretation reinforces a long-standing principle in white-collar law: you cannot justify theft by claiming you gave something back later or by arguing your victim didn't lose everything immediately after being swindled.

Doctrinally, *Constantinescu* serves as a significant checkpoint for how lower courts apply recent Supreme Court jurisprudence to digital-age crimes. Since *Ciminelli* narrowed the scope of wire fraud by eliminating right-to-control theories, prosecutors have faced pressure to prove concrete property losses rather than just informational asymmetries. Some defense attorneys might have hoped this ruling signaled a retreat from aggressive prosecution schemes involving complex information markets like crypto or meme stocks. Instead, *Constantinescu* suggests that once information is weaponized to induce specific financial transactions resulting in asset transfers, traditional fraud doctrines remain robustly applicable. The court effectively declared that moving a scam from email chains or cold calls to X (formerly Twitter) or TikTok does not immunize perpetrators from federal liability if they are stealing dollars through deception.

The downstream effects of this decision will likely ripple through enforcement strategies regarding influencer marketing and financial advice online. While some may fear an expansion of federal jurisdiction into every instance of exaggerated stock promotion, *Constantinescu* actually draws a clearer boundary: puffery or bad analysis remains civil territory; deliberate schemes designed to extract capital through falsehoods remain criminal territory.
