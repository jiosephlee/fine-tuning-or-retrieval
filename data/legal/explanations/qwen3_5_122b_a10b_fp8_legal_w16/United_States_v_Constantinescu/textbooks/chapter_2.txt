Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Governing Legal Framework for Securities Fraud

## Statutory Text of 18 U.S.C. § 1348
To understand the Fifth Circuit's analysis in *United States v. Constantinescu*, one must first look to the specific language Congress enacted to criminalize securities fraud. The governing statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, creates two distinct but related prohibitions. First, it makes it unlawful for anyone to knowingly execute "a scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with... any security." Second, and perhaps more critically for the facts in *Constantinescu*, the statute prohibits schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of... any security."

When reading the indictment challenged in this case, it is vital to recognize that the government charged the defendants under both prongs: they allegedly conspired to defraud followers and specifically sought to obtain money and property through deceptive practices tied to stock transactions. The text itself sets a high bar regarding what constitutes the target of the fraud; it explicitly mentions "any money or property" as the object of the scheme when utilizing false pretenses. This textual anchor becomes the focal point when defendants argue that their conduct involved only intangible losses rather than tangible financial theft.

## Interpretative Borrowing from Mail and Wire Fraud Statutes
Because § 1348 is a relatively recent addition compared to older federal fraud statutes, courts face a scarcity of direct precedent interpreting its specific contours within particular circuits like the Fifth Circuit. In *Constantinescu*, the court addresses this gap by looking outward to established jurisprudence surrounding mail and wire fraud (18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 and 1343). The appellate panel notes that § 1348 "borrows key concepts" from these longer-standing statutes. Consequently, legal principles developed over decades in mail and wire fraud cases are routinely applied to analyze securities fraud charges unless there is a compelling reason not to do so.

This methodological approach allows courts to import well-defined tests for what constitutes a "scheme" and an "intent" without reinventing analytical wheels for every new type of financial transaction covered by § 1348. By treating these statutes similarly, the court ensures consistency in how federal fraud laws are applied across different mediums of communication—from physical letters and telephone calls to modern digital social media posts alleged in this case. Therefore, understanding *Constantinescu* requires viewing it through the lens of general federal fraud doctrine as much as through specific securities regulations.

## Element One: Defining a 'Scheme to Defraud'
Under this borrowed framework, establishing liability begins with proving a "scheme to defraud." The Fifth Circuit defines this element as requiring a material misrepresentation intended to deceive another party for the purpose of obtaining something of value from them. Crucially, what that "something of value" is determines whether a valid charge exists under current Supreme Court guidance referenced in *Constantinescu*.

The court distinguishes between two competing theories of what can be stolen via deception: the "right-to-control" theory versus the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. A scheme based on depriving a victim of their right to control assets using missing information—the right-to-control theory—is insufficient after recent Supreme Court rulings like *Ciminelli v. United States*. That decision clarified that valuable economic information alone does not constitute traditional property rights protected by federal fraud statutes. However, if the scheme involves tricking a victim into handing over actual money or property—a fraudulent inducement—it satisfies the statutory definition of a scheme to defraud. In *Constantinescu*, the indictment survives because it alleges defendants used lies not just to withhold data, but actively induce followers into purchasing securities and transferring funds, fitting squarely within the permissible scope of fraudulent inducement rather than impermissible deprivation of informational rights.

## Element Two: Establishing 'Intent to Defraud'
Once a viable scheme is identified, prosecutors must prove the requisite mental state: an intent to defraud. This element breaks down into two components: an intent to deceive and an intent that harm results from that deceit. Students often mistakenly believe that proving intent requires showing that the defendant aimed specifically at causing their victim significant financial ruin or negative balance sheet impact (*net pecuniary loss*). *Constantinescu* corrects this misconception by citing *Kousisis v. United States*, where the Supreme Court held that such proof is unnecessary under federal fraud statutes.

The critical inquiry is whether obtaining money or property was an objective ("object") of the defendant's actions, not whether they succeeded in making their victim poorer overall than before the transaction occurred. Even if a defendant provides goods or services back that arguably match what they took—such as providing shares worth market value while lying about their own position—they still possess criminal
