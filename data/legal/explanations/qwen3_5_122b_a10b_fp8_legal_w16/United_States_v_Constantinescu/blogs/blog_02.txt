Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

## Intent to Defraud Without Intent to Harm: The End of the 'Net Loss' Requirement in Securities Fraud

In a decision that sharply narrows the defensive playbook for financial fraudsters, the Fifth Circuit reversed a district court's dismissal of indictments against a group of high-profile social media influencers accused of orchestrating a massive "pump and dump" scheme. Writing for the panel in *United States v. Constantinescu*, Circuit Judge Kurt D. Engelhardt delivered a ruling that does more than just reinstate charges; it clarifies a critical, often misunderstood boundary in federal fraud jurisprudence. The core question before the court was whether the government must prove that defendants intended to leave their victims economically worse off to sustain a conviction for securities fraud. Relying heavily on the Supreme Court's recent guidance in *Kousisis v. United States*, the Fifth Circuit answered with a resounding no. This ruling confirms that under current federal law, the crime of fraud is consummated by the deceptive acquisition of money or property, irrespective of whether the perpetrator intended to inflict net pecuniary loss on the victim.

The facts alleged in *Constantinescu* read like a modern cautionary tale for the digital age. Eight defendants, leveraging their substantial social media followings, allegedly purchased low-priced securities and then disseminated false information regarding their trading positions and the potential value of those stocks. Their goal was to induce their followers to buy into these inflated assets ("pump"), thereby driving up the price so the defendants could sell their holdings at a profit ("dump"). The scheme reportedly generated $114 million in illicit gains. However, when challenged on procedural grounds, the district court dismissed the indictment, adopting what many observers feared was an increasingly rigid interpretation of fraud following recent Supreme Court rulings like *Ciminelli*. The lower court concluded that because the defendants allegedly deprived followers only of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest, and because they sought profit rather than direct harm to victims' wallets, no offense had been stated.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal rests on two distinct but related pillars: first, distinguishing between theories regarding what constitutes "property," and second, dismantling the notion that an intent to harm is a necessary component of criminal intent to defraud. On the property front, Judge Engelhardt carefully navigated around *Ciminelli*, where the Supreme Court rejected the "right-to-control" theory—the idea that possessing accurate information needed for economic decision-making constitutes a protected property interest itself. The Fifth Circuit noted that while *Ciminelli* killed off right-to-control claims, it left intact "fraudulent inducement" theories. In this case, prosecutors did not argue that victims lost their right to information; rather, they alleged that defendants used lies to trick victims into handing over cash in exchange for securities. That transactional transfer of money remains a classic protected property interest under federal statute, placing this conduct squarely outside *Ciminelli*'s protective scope.

However, it is the court's analysis of mental state—specifically regarding "intent"—that carries significant weight for future litigation and regulatory enforcement. The defendants argued that because they intended only to enrich themselves through market manipulation rather than specifically aiming to bankrupt or impoverish their followers (who received stock worth market value at the moment of purchase), they lacked the requisite intent to defraud. This argument mirrors one recently raised and summarily rejected by Chief Justice Roberts writing for a unanimous Supreme Court in *Kousisis*. In *Kousisis*, involving bid-rigging schemes where contractors provided services but lied about compliance requirements, the Court held that fraud statutes do not demand proof that a defendant sought to leave victims with less money than they started with wither than simply proving they obtained money through deception.

Judge Engelhardt applied this logic directly to securities markets in *Constantinescu*. He emphasized that under *Kousisis*, "obtaining" another's money or property via material misrepresentation satisfies both elements of fraud: there is deception, and there is an object (the money) being pursued through that deception. Whether the victim walks away with goods or services of equivalent value at the time of exchange is irrelevant to criminal liability if those goods were procured through lies. By rejecting a "net loss" requirement as an element of mens rea, the Fifth Circuit effectively closes a loophole where sophisticated bad actors might claim innocence simply because their scam involved providing something tangible (even if overvalued) alongside their theft by deception.

The doctrinal implications are profound for how prosecutors approach complex financial crimes in an era where asset values can fluctuate wildly and transactions are instantaneous under social media pressure. Previously, defense attorneys might have hoped to argue that because victims received shares at fair market value during the transaction—or perhaps even made short-term profits before dumping them themselves—the essential element of harm was missing from both fact pattern and intent now seems foreclosed by binding precedent from Washington D.C., though strictly speaking only persuasive authority until adopted by other circuits). Yet courts across jurisdictions are already coalescing around this view; indeed Seventh Circuit cited Kousisis explicitly affirming this principle earlier this year too). 

Moreover, this decision reinforces stability within securities regulation by aligning criminal liability closely with civil antifraud doctrines which long recognized fraudulent inducement regardless subsequent investment performance). If anything it raises stakes slightly higher since now even well-meaning investors who temporarily benefit cannot shield manipulators once deception proven initial inducement occurred). Ultimately however greatest impact likely falls upon white-collar practitioners who can no longer rely upon technical distinctions between seeking profit versus causing loss when crafting arguments challenging sufficiency indictments involving deceptive practices designed extract capital markets). As Judge Engelhardt succinctly put it distinguishing profit motive from harmful intent becomes mere semantics when underlying objective remains securing funds via falsehoods—making clear path forward clear prosecution strategy going forward especially amid rise influencer-driven trading schemes threatening integrity global markets today alike everywhere else world over!
