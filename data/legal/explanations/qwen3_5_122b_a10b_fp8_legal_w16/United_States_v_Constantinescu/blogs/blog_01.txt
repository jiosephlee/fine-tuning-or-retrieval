Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

## Beyond Ciminelli: Why 'Pump-and-Dump' Schemes Survive the Right-to-Control Rejection

The Supreme Court's recent decision in *Ciminelli v. United States* sent shockwaves through federal white-collar prosecution, effectively killing the "right-to-control" theory of fraud. By ruling that the deprivation of valuable economic information alone does not constitute a property interest under federal fraud statutes, the Court narrowed the scope of criminal liability for deceptive practices. Yet, just months later, the Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, issued on October 2, 2025, demonstrates that *Ciminelli* is not a get-out-of-jail-free card for sophisticated market manipulators. Instead, the court drew a sharp, crucial line between stealing someone's right to make an informed decision and actually tricking them into handing over their wallet.

In *Constantinescu*, a group of social media influencers with massive followings allegedly orchestrated a classic "pump and dump" scheme. The indictment detailed how these defendants purchased securities, then used their platforms to disseminate false and misleading information about their trading positions and the securities' potential value. This misinformation induced thousands of followers to buy the stocks, artificially inflating the price so the defendants could sell ("dump") their holdings for a reported $114 million profit. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government had failed to allege a deprivation of traditional property rights; in its view, the scheme merely deprived victims of potentially valuable economic information—a fatal flaw under *Ciminelli*. The Fifth Circuit reversed this dismissal, holding that the indictment successfully alleged a traditional fraudulent-inducement theory rather than an impermissible right-to-control claim.

The crux of the Fifth Circuit's analysis rests on distinguishing two distinct theories of fraud that often get conflated in high-stakes financial litigation. As Judge Engelhardt noted for the panel, *Ciminelli* specifically targeted the "right-to-control" theory, which posits that a victim's property interest lies in their ability to control assets based on accurate information. The Supreme Court rejected this because informational autonomy is not a "traditional property interest." However, *Constantinescu* relies on what courts call "fraudulent inducement." Here, the object of the fraud was not simply to withhold data or distort a victim's decision-making process; it was to trick victims into executing transactions that transferred money and property from their pockets to those of the defendants. The court emphasized that while losing access to truthful data isn't enough for fraud under *Ciminelli*, being lied into signing away one's cash is exactly what federal securities laws were designed to punish.

Perhaps even more significant is how *Constantinescu* addresses the element of intent and harm. The defendants argued that because they provided shares (which arguably had market value) in exchange for their followers' money, there was no true "deprivation," especially since they claimed they never intended to leave their victims economically worse off—they just wanted to profit themselves. Relying heavily on another recent Supreme Court term case, *Kousisis v. United States*, published earlier in 2025, Judge Engelhardt dismantled this defense. The court clarified that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that a defendant sought to cause net pecuniary loss or leave a victim with nothing of value. As long as the defendant used deception to induce the transfer of money or property with an intent to obtain it for themselves, a crime has occurred. The fact that victims received stock—which might have been worth something—does not negate the fraud if they bought it based on lies regarding its value or liquidity prospects.

Doctrinally, *Constantinescu* serves as an important corrective for lower courts struggling to apply *Ciminelli*. It signals that while prosecutors can no longer stretch fraud statutes to cover mere regulatory violations or nondisclosure of strategic data where no asset changes hands due to deception, they remain fully empowered to pursue traditional scams involving misrepresentation and transactional manipulation. The decision reinforces that "property" in fraud cases still encompasses money and tangible assets obtained through deceitful bargaining chips like fake investment tips or fabricated trading records.

For practitioners and observers of white-collar law, this split between information-based claims and asset-transfer claims will likely define future litigation strategies in digital finance crimes. As social media continues to become a primary venue for investment advice and market manipulation, regulators will lean heavily on fraudulent-inducement theories rather than trying to shoehorn cases into right-to-control frameworks that are now constitutionally suspect under current precedent. The Fifth Circuit made clear: you cannot steal someone's right to know better without consequence if you use lies as bait; but you certainly cannot escape liability just because you gave them something back—even if what you gave them was worthless compared to what you took—provided you lied about it first.
