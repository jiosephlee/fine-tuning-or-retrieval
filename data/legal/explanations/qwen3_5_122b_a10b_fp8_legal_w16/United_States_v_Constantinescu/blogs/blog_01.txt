Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

## Beyond Ciminelli: How 'Pump-and-Dump' Schemes Survive the Right-to-Control Rejection via Fraudulent Inducement

For years, the landscape of federal fraud law seemed poised for a seismic shift following the Supreme Court's rejection of the "right-to-control" theory in *Ciminelli v. United States*. That 2023 decision sent shockwaves through white-collar prosecution, holding that depriving a victim of "potentially valuable economic information"—such as data needed to make informed business decisions—does not constitute the deprivation of a "property interest" under federal fraud statutes. Prosecutors scrambled to recalibrate, fearing that many sophisticated schemes relying on informational asymmetry might slip through the cracks. Now, with the Fifth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, we are seeing just how robustly federal courts can distinguish between the discredited right-to-control theory and the enduring "fraudulent-inducement" doctrine. The case serves as a critical reminder that while abstract information isn't property, money parted with due to lies absolutely is.

In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit reversed a district court's dismissal of indictments against several high-profile social media influencers accused of orchestrating massive "pump-and-dump" schemes. The defendants, leveraging their substantial online followings, allegedly purchased securities and then disseminated false and misleading posts about their trading positions and the stocks' potential value. This misinformation induced followers to buy in, driving up prices so the defendants could sell ("dump") their holdings for millions in profit. The district court had thrown out the charges, reasoning that the indictment failed to allege a deprivation of a traditional property interest. Instead, it viewed the scheme as one designed merely to steal "valuable economic information," which *Ciminelli* explicitly shielded from criminal liability under federal fraud laws.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal turns on a nuanced but vital doctrinal line drawn by the Supreme Court itself in *Kousisis v. United States* (decided in 2025). Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Kurt Engelhardt clarified that *Ciminelli* did not dismantle all theories of fraud based on deception; it only struck down those predicated solely on the loss of informational autonomy. The court emphasized that the indictment against Constantinescu and his co-conspirators did not rely on a right-to-control theory. It did not claim that victims lost something intangible like their ability to make better financial choices. Rather, it alleged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme: defendants used material misrepresentations to trick victims into entering contracts—specifically, purchases of securities—that required them to hand over hard cash or property immediately. As Judge Engelhardt noted, distinguishing these two theories is essential because fraudulent inducement protects tangible assets, whereas right-to-control attempts to protect an abstract right to make informed decisions.

Perhaps even more significant for practitioners is how *Constantinescu* addresses the question of victim harm. The defendants argued that because they provided shares in exchange for money—and thus did not necessarily leave their followers with nothing or less than market value—they lacked the requisite "intent to defraud." They contended that since they aimed only to profit themselves rather than inflict net economic loss on their victims, no fraud occurred. Relying heavily on *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit shut this down decisively. The court reiterated that federal fraud statutes do not require proof that a victim suffered an ultimate pecuniary loss or that the defendant sought specifically to injure them financially. If a defendant uses deception to obtain money or property from a victim, even if they provide something of arguably equivalent value in return (like stock shares), the crime is complete once money changes hands under false pretenses. The motive—to enrich oneself rather than impoverish another—is legally irrelevant to establishing intent under these statutes.

This ruling carries profound implications for modern securities enforcement in an era dominated by retail investor communities and social media influence peddling. It signals that while prosecutors can no longer stretch fraud statutes to cover mere non-disclosure or informational hoarding under a broad "right-to-control" umbrella, they remain free to pursue aggressive cases involving active deception leading to asset transfer. The decision effectively closes off one door while keeping another wide open: you cannot be prosecuted simply for withholding data that might help someone trade better, but you certainly can be prosecuted for lying about your trades to get people to give you their money before you cash out your position yourself.

Doctrinally, *Constantinescu* represents a stabilization rather than an expansion of federal fraud power following *Ciminelli*. By anchoring its analysis firmly in traditional property interests—the actual dollars spent by victims—it avoids the slippery slope concerns that led lower courts initially astray after 2023. However, it also warns defense attorneys that arguments centered on "no net loss" or "informational theft only" are likely dead ends when faced with clear allegations of fraudulent inducement affecting wallet contents rather than just mental processes. As digital markets continue to blur lines between genuine advice and coordinated manipulation, *Constantinescu* ensures that the core prohibition against stealing money through lies remains intact, regardless of whether those lies occur in boardrooms or comment sections.
