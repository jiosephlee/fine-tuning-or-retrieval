Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the precise legal distinction between the 'right-to-control' theory rejected in Ciminelli and the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory accepted here?
Question:
I am struggling to understand why the court distinguishes these two theories so sharply. Both seem to involve lying to get someone to make a financial decision. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court said depriving someone of 'valuable economic information' isn't enough for fraud because it's not a 'traditional property interest.' However, in this case (*Constantinescu*), the court says the indictment alleges defendants lied to induce followers to buy stocks, which constitutes obtaining 'money or property.' If the core mechanism in both cases is deception regarding economic data leading to a transaction, why does one fail as a 'property interest' claim while the other succeeds? Is the key difference simply that *Kousisis* involves an actual transfer of funds (buying stock), whereas *Ciminelli* involved bidding on government contracts where no money changed hands immediately?
Answer:
The distinction you are grappling with turns on **what** the defendant is alleged to have taken from the victim, rather than just the fact that deception occurred. While both *Ciminelli* and *Constantinescu* (relying on *Kousisis*) involve lies about economic data, the Fifth Circuit draws a sharp line between depriving a victim of an abstract "right to control" their assets versus tricking a victim into physically transferring money or property.

Here is the breakdown of the legal distinction based strictly on the opinion provided:

### 1. The Rejected Theory: Right-to-Control (*Ciminelli*)
As explained in Part III(A) of the opinion, the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* rejected the "right-to-control" theory because it attempts to criminalize the deprivation of **"potentially valuable economic information."**
*   **The Defect:** Under this theory, the "property" being stolen is defined as the victim's intangible right to make informed decisions about how to use their assets.
*   **The Holding:** The Court ruled that "the right to valuable economic information... is not a traditional property interest." Therefore, if a prosecution relies *solely* on the idea that the victim was denied information needed to exercise discretion over their money, there is no fraud under federal statutes because no traditional property interest was violated.

### 2. The Accepted Theory: Fraudulent Inducement (*Constantinescu/Kousisis*)
In contrast, the indictment in *Constantinescu* survives dismissal because it alleges a **"fraudulent-inducement"** theory. This theory does not rely on the theft of information; it relies on the theft of **actual funds**.
*   **The Mechanism:** The opinion states that this theory applies when a defendant uses a material misstatement to "trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."
*   **The Property Interest:** Here, the property interest is concrete: cash or securities. By lying about their trading positions and price predictions, defendants allegedly caused followers to voluntarily transfer money to purchase stock.
*   **Why It Works:** The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that this is "not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." Instead of claiming victims lost their *ability* to decide wisely (information), the government claims victims actually *parted with their money*. Money and securities are indisputably "traditional property interests."

### 3. Addressing Your Hypothesis on Fund Transfers
You asked if the key difference is simply that one involved an actual transfer of funds while the other did not. **Yes, but with a specific legal nuance.**

It is not merely that money changed hands; it is that in *Ciminelli*, the government tried to argue that losing access to information *itself* constituted a loss of property rights (the right to control). In *Constantinescu*, the government argues that the deception was merely the *means* used to effectuate a transfer of existing property (cash for stock).

Furthermore, Part III(B) clarifies another critical aspect: **Net Economic Loss.** You noted in your question whether victims suffered harm. The opinion explicitly rejects this as a barrier under fraudulent inducement. Even if followers received stock worth what they paid (no net pecuniary loss), fraud still occurred because they were induced to hand over their money under false pretenses. As Justice Kagan wrote in *Kousisis* (quoted extensively in Part III(B)), fraud statutes prohibit obtaining money or property "regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

### Conclusion
The distinction lies in the object of the scheme:
1.  **Right-to-Control (*Ciminelli*):** Deprives victims of **information** so they cannot make good decisions. This fails because information is not "property."
2.  **Fraudulent Inducement (*Constantinescu*):** Deceives victims into **transferring money/stock**. This succeeds because cash and securities are "traditional property interests," regardless of whether victims retained full value or suffered immediate financial ruin.

As stated in Section III(A): *"Unlike the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory advanced here 'protects money and property.'"*
