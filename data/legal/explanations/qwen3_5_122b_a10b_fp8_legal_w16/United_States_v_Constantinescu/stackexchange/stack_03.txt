Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How does 18 U.S.C. § 1348 incorporate mail/wire fraud precedents like Ciminelli and Kousisis given statutory text differences?
Question:
The opinion states that § 1348 (securities fraud) 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes' (§§ 1341/1343). However, I know that securities fraud has its own specific elements regarding connection with securities transactions. Why did the Fifth Circuit feel compelled to rely so heavily on general wire/mail fraud cases like *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* rather than interpreting § 1348 independently? Are there specific textual hooks in § 1348 that mandate this borrowing, or is this purely judicial interpretation based on historical legislative intent? Could Congress have written § 1348 differently to avoid these interpretations?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**  
The Fifth Circuit relies on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* because **18 U.S.C. § 1348 explicitly borrows "key concepts" from the mail and wire fraud statutes**, creating a deliberate interpretive link that binds courts to apply established fraud doctrines unless Congress specifies otherwise. This is not merely judicial preference but a statutory mandate rooted in the text of § 1348 itself.

---

### **Why Mail/Wire Fraud Precedent Applies to Securities Fraud**
#### **1. Statutory Text Mandates Borrowing**
Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to:  
> *"defraud any person in connection with... any security"* OR *"obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of... any security."*  

While § 1348 adds the "in connection with securities" limitation absent in §§ 1341/1343, it **adopts core fraud elements** ("scheme to defraud," "intent to defraud," "false or fraudulent pretenses") verbatim from mail/wire fraud. As the Fifth Circuit notes in *Constantinescu*:  
> *"§ 1348 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,' so 'courts have given the terms similar treatment.'"*  

This textual borrowing compels courts to apply precedent defining those shared terms (*e.g.*, what constitutes a "scheme to defraud" or "property"), as seen in *Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th at 339 n.6. Congress intentionally aligned securities fraud with broader anti-fraud principles while narrowing its scope to securities transactions.

#### **2. *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* Clarify Shared Doctrinal Boundaries**
The Supreme Court's recent decisions directly address ambiguities inherent in all fraud statutes using identical language:  
- ***Ciminelli*** rejected the "right-to-control" theory (deprivation of intangible economic information alone), holding that **"traditional property interests"** are required for fraud convictions under statutes using terms like "scheme to defraud."  
- ***Kousisis*** clarified that **"intent to defraud"** does not require proving net economic loss to victims; obtaining money/property via deception suffices even if victims receive equivalent value.  

Since § 1348 uses these same phrases, the Fifth Circuit correctly applied these rulings to distinguish between:  
(a) A prohibited scheme depriving victims of **money/property** (permissible under § 1348 per *Kousisis*), versus  
(b) An impermissible claim based solely on deprivation of **economic information** (rejected per *Ciminelli*).  

The indictment survived dismissal precisely because it alleged defendants **fraudulently induced followers to part with money**—a traditional property interest—not just withheld information.

#### **3. Congressional Intent vs. Judicial Interpretation**
Congress could have drafted § 1348 differently—for example, by:  
- Defining "defraud" exclusively through SEC regulations rather than common-law fraud concepts;  
- Explicitly rejecting mail/wire fraud precedents; or  
- Adding unique elements unrelated to deception (e.g., requiring proof of market manipulation beyond misstatements).  

Instead, Congress chose **statutory continuity**: By adopting mail/wire fraud's framework while limiting jurisdictional scope to securities, it signaled reliance on existing case law to define ambiguous terms like "scheme" and "property." The Fifth Circuit's approach honors this design—it applies universal fraud principles (*Ciminelli/Kousisis*) within § 1348's narrower context ("in connection with securities").

---

### **Key Takeaway for Law Students**
When a statute incorporates another's language (*e.g.*, "borrowing key concepts"), courts treat those shared terms consistently unless contextual differences demand divergence. Here, § 1348's text creates a bridge to mail/wire jurisprudence, making *Ciminelli*/*Kousisis* indispensable for resolving disputes over what constitutes actionable fraud—even though § 1348 targets securities specifically. The court didn't ignore securities-specific nuances; it applied general fraud rules *through* the lens of those nuances (*i.e.*, ensuring the deception involved a transaction affecting securities).

> 💡 **Pro Tip**: Always ask: *"Does this statute use identical phrasing as another where courts have already defined it?"* If yes—and no conflicting congressional directive exists—the borrowed definition controls (*see Constantinescu*, III.A-B).
