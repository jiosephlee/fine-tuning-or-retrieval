# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Factual Allegations: The Social Media Pump-and-Dump Scheme

The case of *United States v. Constantinescu* arises from a modern variation of a classic financial crime known as "pump and dump," adapted for the era of social media influence. The government alleged that eight defendants—including Edward Constantinescu, Perry "PJ" Matlock, and several others—leveraged their substantial online followings to execute a coordinated scheme to manipulate securities markets. These individuals held themselves out as skilled stock traders and routinely shared their trading activities on various social media platforms. According to the superseding indictment, the defendants utilized these platforms not merely to share opinions, but to actively manipulate asset prices for illicit profit.

The mechanics of the alleged scheme followed a three-step process. First, the defendants would privately purchase a specific security while keeping their position hidden from the public. Second, they would "pump" the price of that security by disseminating false and misleading information regarding their own trading positions and the potential value of the stock. By presenting fabricated data or optimistic projections designed to induce confidence, they encouraged their followers to purchase the same securities, thereby creating artificial demand that drove up the price. Finally, once the price was inflated by this surge in buying activity driven by misinformation, the defendants would "dump" their shares by secretly selling them at a profit. The indictment asserts that this scheme generated approximately $114 million in profits for the group. Crucially, the core deception lay in hiding their intent to sell; they presented themselves as long-term holders or bullish advocates while simultaneously preparing to liquidate their holdings at an artificially high price created by their own lies.

## The Superseding Indictment: Charges under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 1349

Based on these factual allegations, the grand jury returned a superseding indictment charging the defendants with violations of federal securities laws. Specifically, one count charged conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349. Additionally, multiple substantive counts charged direct commission of securities fraud pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and aiding and abetting liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2. Section 1348 broadly prohibits any scheme or artifice to defraud anyone in connection with the purchase or sale of any security, as well as schemes to obtain money or property through false pretenses in such transactions.

One defendant, Daniel Knight, chose to plead guilty early in the proceedings, effectively admitting to his role in this conduct. However, the remaining seven defendants declined plea deals and instead challenged the legal validity of the charges against them before trial began. Their primary contention was not necessarily that they denied making certain statements or engaging in trades—though they contested specific facts—but rather that even if all allegations in the indictment were taken as true, those facts did not constitute a crime under existing statutory interpretation. This challenge set the stage for a pivotal question regarding what constitutes "property" within the meaning of federal fraud statutes when applied to digital age investment advice and market manipulation via social media algorithms and influencer status.

## District Court Dismissal: The 'Right-to-Control' vs. Traditional Property Interest Distinction

Before reaching this Court of Appeals, the district court granted motions filed by most defendants seeking dismissal of the entire indictment. The district judge concluded that despite detailing sophisticated deceptive practices aimed at manipulating stock prices for personal gain, the indictment failed legally because it did not allege a deprivation of a recognized property interest possessed by victims prior to being duped into purchasing stocks. # Governing Legal Framework for Securities Fraud

## Statutory Text of 18 U.S.C. § 1348
To understand the Fifth Circuit's analysis in *United States v. Constantinescu*, one must first look to the specific language Congress enacted to criminalize securities fraud. The governing statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, creates two distinct but related prohibitions. First, it makes it unlawful for anyone to knowingly execute "a scheme or artifice to defraud any person in connection with... any security." Second, and perhaps more critically for the facts in *Constantinescu*, the statute prohibits schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of... any security."

When reading the indictment challenged in this case, it is vital to recognize that the government charged the defendants under both prongs: they allegedly conspired to defraud followers and specifically sought to obtain money and property through deceptive practices tied to stock transactions. The text itself sets a high bar regarding what constitutes the target of the fraud; it explicitly mentions "any money or property" as the object of the scheme when utilizing false pretenses. This textual anchor becomes the focal point when defendants argue that their conduct involved only intangible losses rather than tangible financial theft.

## Interpretative Borrowing from Mail and Wire Fraud Statutes
Because § 1348 is a relatively recent addition compared to older federal fraud statutes, courts face a scarcity of direct precedent interpreting its specific contours within particular circuits like the Fifth Circuit. In *Constantinescu*, the court addresses this gap by looking outward to established jurisprudence surrounding mail and wire fraud (18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 and 1343). The appellate panel notes that § 1348 "borrows key concepts" from these longer-standing statutes. Consequently, legal principles developed over decades in mail and wire fraud cases are routinely applied to analyze securities fraud charges unless there is a compelling reason not to do so.

This methodological approach allows courts to import well-defined tests for what constitutes a "scheme" and an "intent" without reinventing analytical wheels for every new type of financial transaction covered by § 1348. By treating these statutes similarly, the court ensures consistency in how federal fraud laws are applied across different mediums of communication—from physical letters and telephone calls to modern digital social media posts alleged in this case. Therefore, understanding *Constantinescu* requires viewing it through the lens of general federal fraud doctrine as much as through specific securities regulations.

## Element One: Defining a 'Scheme to Defraud'
Under this borrowed framework, establishing liability begins with proving a "scheme to defraud." The Fifth Circuit defines this element as requiring a material misrepresentation intended to deceive another party for the purpose of obtaining something of value from them. Crucially, what that "something of value" is determines whether a valid charge exists under current Supreme Court guidance referenced in *Constantinescu*.

The court distinguishes between two competing theories of what can be stolen via deception: the "right-to-control" theory versus the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. A scheme based on depriving a victim of their right to control assets using missing information—the right-to-control theory—is insufficient after recent Supreme Court rulings like *Ciminelli v. United States*. That decision clarified that valuable economic information alone does not constitute traditional property rights protected by federal fraud statutes. However, if the scheme involves tricking a victim into handing over actual money or property—a fraudulent inducement—it satisfies the statutory definition of a scheme to defraud. In *Constantinescu*, the indictment survives because it alleges defendants used lies not just to withhold data, but actively induce followers into purchasing securities and transferring funds, fitting squarely within the permissible scope of fraudulent inducement rather than impermissible deprivation of informational rights.

## Element Two: Establishing 'Intent to Defraud'
Once a viable scheme is identified, prosecutors must prove the requisite mental state: an intent to defraud. This element breaks down into two components: an intent to deceive and an intent that harm results from that deceit. Students often mistakenly believe that proving intent requires showing that the defendant aimed specifically at causing their victim significant financial ruin or negative balance sheet impact (*net pecuniary loss*). *Constantinescu* corrects this misconception by citing *Kousisis v. United States*, where the Supreme Court held that such proof is unnecessary under federal fraud statutes.

The critical inquiry is whether obtaining money or property was an objective ("object") of the defendant's actions, not whether they succeeded in making their victim poorer overall than before the transaction occurred. Even if a defendant provides goods or services back that arguably match what they took—such as providing shares worth market value while lying about their own position—they still possess criminal # Distinguishing Right-to-Control Theory from Fraudulent Inducement

## Introduction to the Core Legal Challenge
The central dispute in *United States v. Constantinescu* revolves around the definition of "property" within the context of federal securities fraud. After the district court dismissed the indictment against several high-profile social media influencers, arguing that their conduct involved only the theft of "valuable economic information," the Fifth Circuit was tasked with determining whether the government's allegations met the statutory requirements for a crime. The defendants contended that because they misled followers about their own trading strategies rather than stealing tangible assets directly, no property interest was violated. To resolve this, the appellate court had to navigate recent Supreme Court precedents, specifically distinguishing between two competing theories of fraud: the discredited "right-to-control" theory and the established "fraudulent-inducement" theory. Understanding this distinction is critical, as it determines whether a scheme involving deceptive speech constitutes criminal fraud or merely unethical behavior.

## *United States v. Ciminelli*: Rejection of Valuable Economic Information as Property
To grasp why the district court initially dismissed the indictment, one must first understand the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In *Ciminelli*, the Court addressed whether federal fraud statutes protected a victim's "right to control" their assets—a concept known as the right-to-control theory. Under this theory, a defendant could be convicted if they deprived a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make informed financial decisions, even if no physical money or traditional asset changed hands immediately due to that specific piece of information. The Supreme Court firmly rejected this approach in 2023, holding that federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests." The Court reasoned that while having accurate information helps individuals manage their wealth better, information itself is not a traditional property interest like cash or real estate. Consequently, a prosecution cannot rest solely on an allegation that a defendant withheld data or provided misleading advice that affected a victim's ability to control their assets; there must be an allegation of obtaining money or property itself.

## Why Defendants Argued Their Conduct Was Not Criminal Under *Ciminelli*
Relying heavily on *Ciminelli*, the defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that their "pump and dump" scheme fell squarely outside the bounds of criminal liability. Their defense posited that they never stole money from their followers; instead, they allegedly deprived followers of valuable economic information regarding their trading positions and intentions. According to this logic, when followers bought stocks based on false posts about what stocks Constantinescu and his co-defendants owned or planned to sell, they made investment decisions using incomplete or incorrect data. However, once these purchases were made, the followers received shares of stock—the same type of asset anyone else could buy on the open market—at prevailing market prices. Therefore, defendants claimed they did not scheme to deprive victims of money or property but rather manipulated access to information to enrich themselves while leaving victims with equivalent market-value securities in exchange for their funds. If accepted by courts following strict interpretations of *Ciminelli*, this argument would suggest that manipulating investor sentiment through lies about one's own portfolio does not constitute securities fraud unless it can be shown that victims lost more than just informational advantage.

## **Fraudulent-Inducement Theory**: Tricking Victims into Handing Over Assets
The Fifth Circuit clarified that *Constantinescu* does not involve a prohibited right-to-control claim but rather relies on a distinct and legally sound doctrine known as fraudulent inducement. Unlike right-to-control cases where the focus is on missing data affecting decision-making power, fraudulent inducement occurs when a defendant uses material misstatements to trick a victim into entering a transaction where they voluntarily hand over money or property in reliance on those falsehoods. As reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in subsequent rulings like *Kousisis v. United States*, fraudulent inducement remains a valid basis for conviction because it results in the direct transfer of tangible assets from victim to perpetrator based on deception. In this framework, the core wrong is not simply lying about facts; it is using those lies as leverage to extract money or property from another party who would otherwise withhold those assets had they known the truth. The indictment in *Constantinescu* explicitly alleged this mechanism: defendants purchased securities, spread false narratives via social media to inflate demand ("pumping"), and then sold their holdings ("dumping") after unsuspecting investors poured capital into buying those inflated shares at artificial prices driven by lies about profits and potential gains.

## **Distinction Analysis**: Why This Case Involves Money, Not Just Information Loss
The pivotal moment in understanding this opinion comes down to separating what happened versus what might have happened differently absent deception. While defendants argued they left followers with market-value stock—and thus caused no net economic loss—the Fifth Circuit emphasized that under current law, actual net loss is irrelevant to establishing an intent to defraud when fraudulent inducement is present. The key distinction lies in identifying exactly what was taken away: Did victims lose access to secret tips (information), or did they surrender dollars based on false premises?
