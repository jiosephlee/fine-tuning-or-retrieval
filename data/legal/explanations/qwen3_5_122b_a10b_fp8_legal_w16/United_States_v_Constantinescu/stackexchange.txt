Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the precise legal distinction between the 'right-to-control' theory rejected in Ciminelli and the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory accepted here?
Question:
I am struggling to understand why the court distinguishes these two theories so sharply. Both seem to involve lying to get someone to make a financial decision. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court said depriving someone of 'valuable economic information' isn't enough for fraud because it's not a 'traditional property interest.' However, in this case (*Constantinescu*), the court says the indictment alleges defendants lied to induce followers to buy stocks, which constitutes obtaining 'money or property.' If the core mechanism in both cases is deception regarding economic data leading to a transaction, why does one fail as a 'property interest' claim while the other succeeds? Is the key difference simply that *Kousisis* involves an actual transfer of funds (buying stock), whereas *Ciminelli* involved bidding on government contracts where no money changed hands immediately?
Answer:
The distinction you are grappling with turns on **what** the defendant is alleged to have taken from the victim, rather than just the fact that deception occurred. While both *Ciminelli* and *Constantinescu* (relying on *Kousisis*) involve lies about economic data, the Fifth Circuit draws a sharp line between depriving a victim of an abstract "right to control" their assets versus tricking a victim into physically transferring money or property.

Here is the breakdown of the legal distinction based strictly on the opinion provided:

### 1. The Rejected Theory: Right-to-Control (*Ciminelli*)
As explained in Part III(A) of the opinion, the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* rejected the "right-to-control" theory because it attempts to criminalize the deprivation of **"potentially valuable economic information."**
*   **The Defect:** Under this theory, the "property" being stolen is defined as the victim's intangible right to make informed decisions about how to use their assets.
*   **The Holding:** The Court ruled that "the right to valuable economic information... is not a traditional property interest." Therefore, if a prosecution relies *solely* on the idea that the victim was denied information needed to exercise discretion over their money, there is no fraud under federal statutes because no traditional property interest was violated.

### 2. The Accepted Theory: Fraudulent Inducement (*Constantinescu/Kousisis*)
In contrast, the indictment in *Constantinescu* survives dismissal because it alleges a **"fraudulent-inducement"** theory. This theory does not rely on the theft of information; it relies on the theft of **actual funds**.
*   **The Mechanism:** The opinion states that this theory applies when a defendant uses a material misstatement to "trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."
*   **The Property Interest:** Here, the property interest is concrete: cash or securities. By lying about their trading positions and price predictions, defendants allegedly caused followers to voluntarily transfer money to purchase stock.
*   **Why It Works:** The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that this is "not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." Instead of claiming victims lost their *ability* to decide wisely (information), the government claims victims actually *parted with their money*. Money and securities are indisputably "traditional property interests."

### 3. Addressing Your Hypothesis on Fund Transfers
You asked if the key difference is simply that one involved an actual transfer of funds while the other did not. **Yes, but with a specific legal nuance.**

It is not merely that money changed hands; it is that in *Ciminelli*, the government tried to argue that losing access to information *itself* constituted a loss of property rights (the right to control). In *Constantinescu*, the government argues that the deception was merely the *means* used to effectuate a transfer of existing property (cash for stock).

Furthermore, Part III(B) clarifies another critical aspect: **Net Economic Loss.** You noted in your question whether victims suffered harm. The opinion explicitly rejects this as a barrier under fraudulent inducement. Even if followers received stock worth what they paid (no net pecuniary loss), fraud still occurred because they were induced to hand over their money under false pretenses. As Justice Kagan wrote in *Kousisis* (quoted extensively in Part III(B)), fraud statutes prohibit obtaining money or property "regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

### Conclusion
The distinction lies in the object of the scheme:
1.  **Right-to-Control (*Ciminelli*):** Deprives victims of **information** so they cannot make good decisions. This fails because information is not "property."
2.  **Fraudulent Inducement (*Constantinescu*):** Deceives victims into **transferring money/stock**. This succeeds because cash and securities are "traditional property interests," regardless of whether victims retained full value or suffered immediate financial ruin.

As stated in Section III(A): *"Unlike the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory advanced here 'protects money and property.'"*

### Does the lack of net pecuniary loss for the victim invalidate an intent-to-defraud charge under Kousisis?
Question:
The defendants argued that they couldn't be guilty because they didn't intend to 'harm' their victims financially—they just wanted to profit themselves. The court cites *Kousisis* stating that fraud doesn't require a victim to suffer an economic loss or for the defendant to seek a net loss for the victim. This confuses me: if I lie and sell you a stock at fair market value (so you don't lose money relative to what you paid), but I made money by dumping my shares earlier, how is there an 'injury'? Does *Kousisis* effectively mean that any intentional deception resulting in a transfer of money satisfies the 'harm' element, even if the victim received equivalent value in return? How does this align with traditional common-law notions of fraud requiring damage?
Answer:
**No.** Under *United States v. Constantinescu*, the lack of net pecuniary loss for the victim does **not** invalidate an intent-to-defraud charge. The Fifth Circuit explicitly held that the "harm" element in federal fraud statutes is satisfied by the intentional deprivation of money or property through deception, regardless of whether the victim receives equivalent value in return or suffers a financial deficit.

Here is how the court reconciles this with traditional notions of fraud:

### 1. The Distinction Between "Right-to-Control" and "Fraudulent Inducement"
Your confusion likely stems from conflating two distinct theories of fraud discussed in recent Supreme Court jurisprudence (*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*), which the *Constantinescu* court carefully distinguishes:
*   **Rejected Theory:** The "right-to-control" theory (rejected in *Ciminelli*) criminalizes the deprivation of *informational integrity*. If a defendant lies but leaves the victim's wallet untouched (e.g., providing all necessary data to make a decision), there is no fraud because information itself is not a "traditional property interest."
*   **Accepted Theory:** The "fraudulent inducement" theory (affirmed in *Kousisis*). This applies when a defendant uses a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into parting with **money or property**.

As Judge Engelhardt writes, *"Unlike the right-to-control theory... the fraudulent-inducement theory... protects money and property."* In your hypothetical scenario, even if you receive stock at fair market value, you have still parted with cash based on a lie. That transfer of cash constitutes the deprivation of a protected property interest.

### 2. Profit vs. Harm: A False Dichotomy
The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued exactly as you described: they claimed they intended to profit, not to inflict financial harm ("hurt the victim's bottom line"). The court flatly rejected this distinction as irrelevant to liability.

Relying on *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit stated that a defendant violates fraud statutes when they **"scheme[] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."** (*Constantinescu*, citing *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1392).

The court clarified that:
> "[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. (*Id.* at 1394 n.5).

Therefore, obtaining money via deception is sufficient for conviction even if the defendant simultaneously provided goods or services of equal value (e.g., selling stock at fair market price after lying about its potential). The "injury" required by law is not necessarily an economic loss; rather, it is the **unconsented taking of property rights** achieved through deceit.

### Conclusion
To answer your specific questions:
*   **Is there an injury?** Yes. The injury is the involuntary surrender of money/property caused by reliance on a falsehood, not necessarily a reduction in net wealth.
*   **Does any transfer satisfy "harm"?** Only if that transfer was induced by material deception regarding something else (like trading positions or future value), causing the victim to hand over funds they otherwise might not have spent in that manner.
*   **Alignment with Common Law:** While common law often emphasizes damages, modern federal statutory interpretation (as codified here) prioritizes the protection of property ownership rights against deceptive acquisition methods over strict accounting losses. As noted in *Constantinescu*, there is no "mirror image" requirement where every dollar lost by the victim must equal a dollar gained by the defendant; simply obtaining another's money through lies completes the crime.

### How does 18 U.S.C. § 1348 incorporate mail/wire fraud precedents like Ciminelli and Kousisis given statutory text differences?
Question:
The opinion states that § 1348 (securities fraud) 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes' (§§ 1341/1343). However, I know that securities fraud has its own specific elements regarding connection with securities transactions. Why did the Fifth Circuit feel compelled to rely so heavily on general wire/mail fraud cases like *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* rather than interpreting § 1348 independently? Are there specific textual hooks in § 1348 that mandate this borrowing, or is this purely judicial interpretation based on historical legislative intent? Could Congress have written § 1348 differently to avoid these interpretations?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**  
The Fifth Circuit relies on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* because **18 U.S.C. § 1348 explicitly borrows "key concepts" from the mail and wire fraud statutes**, creating a deliberate interpretive link that binds courts to apply established fraud doctrines unless Congress specifies otherwise. This is not merely judicial preference but a statutory mandate rooted in the text of § 1348 itself.

---

### **Why Mail/Wire Fraud Precedent Applies to Securities Fraud**
#### **1. Statutory Text Mandates Borrowing**
Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to:  
> *"defraud any person in connection with... any security"* OR *"obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of... any security."*  

While § 1348 adds the "in connection with securities" limitation absent in §§ 1341/1343, it **adopts core fraud elements** ("scheme to defraud," "intent to defraud," "false or fraudulent pretenses") verbatim from mail/wire fraud. As the Fifth Circuit notes in *Constantinescu*:  
> *"§ 1348 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,' so 'courts have given the terms similar treatment.'"*  

This textual borrowing compels courts to apply precedent defining those shared terms (*e.g.*, what constitutes a "scheme to defraud" or "property"), as seen in *Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th at 339 n.6. Congress intentionally aligned securities fraud with broader anti-fraud principles while narrowing its scope to securities transactions.

#### **2. *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* Clarify Shared Doctrinal Boundaries**
The Supreme Court's recent decisions directly address ambiguities inherent in all fraud statutes using identical language:  
- ***Ciminelli*** rejected the "right-to-control" theory (deprivation of intangible economic information alone), holding that **"traditional property interests"** are required for fraud convictions under statutes using terms like "scheme to defraud."  
- ***Kousisis*** clarified that **"intent to defraud"** does not require proving net economic loss to victims; obtaining money/property via deception suffices even if victims receive equivalent value.  

Since § 1348 uses these same phrases, the Fifth Circuit correctly applied these rulings to distinguish between:  
(a) A prohibited scheme depriving victims of **money/property** (permissible under § 1348 per *Kousisis*), versus  
(b) An impermissible claim based solely on deprivation of **economic information** (rejected per *Ciminelli*).  

The indictment survived dismissal precisely because it alleged defendants **fraudulently induced followers to part with money**—a traditional property interest—not just withheld information.

#### **3. Congressional Intent vs. Judicial Interpretation**
Congress could have drafted § 1348 differently—for example, by:  
- Defining "defraud" exclusively through SEC regulations rather than common-law fraud concepts;  
- Explicitly rejecting mail/wire fraud precedents; or  
- Adding unique elements unrelated to deception (e.g., requiring proof of market manipulation beyond misstatements).  

Instead, Congress chose **statutory continuity**: By adopting mail/wire fraud's framework while limiting jurisdictional scope to securities, it signaled reliance on existing case law to define ambiguous terms like "scheme" and "property." The Fifth Circuit's approach honors this design—it applies universal fraud principles (*Ciminelli/Kousisis*) within § 1348's narrower context ("in connection with securities").

---

### **Key Takeaway for Law Students**
When a statute incorporates another's language (*e.g.*, "borrowing key concepts"), courts treat those shared terms consistently unless contextual differences demand divergence. Here, § 1348's text creates a bridge to mail/wire jurisprudence, making *Ciminelli*/*Kousisis* indispensable for resolving disputes over what constitutes actionable fraud—even though § 1348 targets securities specifically. The court didn't ignore securities-specific nuances; it applied general fraud rules *through* the lens of those nuances (*i.e.*, ensuring the deception involved a transaction affecting securities).

> 💡 **Pro Tip**: Always ask: *"Does this statute use identical phrasing as another where courts have already defined it?"* If yes—and no conflicting congressional directive exists—the borrowed definition controls (*see Constantinescu*, III.A-B).

### Is 'deprivation of potentially valuable economic information' ever actionable under federal fraud statutes post-Ciminelli?
Question:
The district court dismissed the indictment partly because it felt defendants only deprived followers of information. The appellate court reversed, saying this case wasn't about information deprivation but about obtaining money via fraudulent inducement. My confusion lies in the boundary: At what point does a scheme shift from being merely about withholding/misleading on information (which seems barred by *Ciminelli*) to being about obtaining property? If I tell someone false info about a stock's potential so they buy it, aren't I still primarily depriving them of accurate information? What specific factual allegation transforms this from an informational wrong into a property theft under current precedent?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes, a scheme becomes actionable after *Ciminelli* when the misrepresentation is the mechanism used to **induce the transfer of money or property**, rather than merely interfering with the victim's decision-making process. The critical factual allegation distinguishing a non-actionable "information deprivation" from actionable fraud is that the defendant obtained **traditional property interests (cash/securities)** directly from the victim through reliance on false statements.

Based on *United States v. Constantinescu*, here is how the boundary is drawn:

### 1. The Distinction: Right-to-Control vs. Fraudulent Inducement
The core confusion stems from conflating two distinct theories of liability. As clarified in *Constantinescu*, referencing *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*:

*   **The Barred Theory (*Ciminelli*):** A "right-to-control" theory posits that a victim owns their own decision-making autonomy and that depriving them of information necessary to make that decision constitutes a property loss. The Supreme Court rejected this, ruling that "valuable economic information" is not a traditional property interest under federal fraud statutes.
*   **The Actionable Theory (*Kousisis/Constantinescu*):** A "fraudulent-inducement" theory occurs when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into entering a transaction where they hand over money or property. Here, the "property" is not the information itself, but the cash or securities transferred in reliance on the lie.

### 2. The Specific Factual Allegation That Matters
To cross the line from an informational wrong to property theft, the indictment must allege that the deception caused the **actual transfer of funds**.

In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit reversed the dismissal because the indictment alleged more than just misleading followers; it specifically alleged:
> "...defendants would... **'pump' the price of that security by posting false and misleading information... so that other investors were induced to purchase the security...** and 'dump' by secretly selling..." (Indictment ¶¶ 13–14).

The pivotal fact is **"induced to purchase."**
*   If you simply withhold data about a stock's potential, no crime exists under federal fraud statutes post-*Ciminelli*.
*   However, if you fabricate data (e.g., lying about your trading position or price targets) specifically to trigger a buyer to **part with their money** to buy your shares, you have obtained "something of value" (the buyer's cash).

As Judge Engelhardt wrote, *"Unlike the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory advanced here 'protects money and property.'"* The scheme shifts from informational to proprietary when the object of the fraud is explicitly defined as obtaining money or property via deceptive means.

### 3. Harm Is Not Required for Liability
A common misconception (raised by both defendants in *Constantinescu*) is that if victims received market-value stock and thus suffered no net financial loss ("harm"), there can be no fraud. The court explicitly rejected this under *Kousisis*.

Even if your followers bought stocks at fair market value (meaning they got exactly what they paid for), you are still liable if you used lies to get them to pay you at all. The statute protects against obtaining money via false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim ends up with an asset of equivalent value later. As noted in *Constantinescu*: *"A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"* regardless of net pecuniary loss to the victim.

### Conclusion for Your Hypothetical
If you tell someone false info about a stock's potential so they buy it:
1.  **Non-Actionable:** If your goal was merely to manipulate their understanding without seeking their funds directly (or if no funds changed hands based on that specific lie), it falls under *Ciminelli*'s prohibition on right-to-control theories.
2.  **Actionable:** If your goal was to induce them to send you cash (by buying your inflated stock) based on that lie, you have committed securities fraud under § 1348. The "property" stolen is their investment capital; the "information" was merely the tool used to extract it.

*Citing:* *United States v. Constantinescu*, No. 24-20143 (5th Cir. Oct. 2, 2025); *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S.Ct. 1382 (2025); *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023).

### What is the scope of review for sufficiency of an indictment versus sufficiency of evidence at trial?
Question:
The opinion mentions reviewing sufficiency de novo and taking allegations as true. As a student trying to distinguish procedural stages, I want to confirm: Does this reversal mean the government has now proven its case beyond a reasonable doubt? Or does it simply mean they met a very low threshold at this stage (alleging facts plausibly)? If we assume all allegations are true per this standard, why was there ever doubt about whether they alleged a crime? Specifically, how does this standard protect defendants compared to what would happen if we looked at actual evidence later?
Answer:
This reversal **does not** mean the government has proven its case beyond a reasonable doubt. It simply means the government has cleared the very low threshold required to survive a motion to dismiss an indictment.

To understand why the court reversed despite assuming all allegations were "true," you must distinguish between the **legal sufficiency of the pleading** and the **factual sufficiency of proof**.

### 1. The Standard: Legal Sufficiency vs. Factual Proof
When reviewing an indictment, the appellate court applies a *de novo* standard but operates under a critical constraint: **"take the allegations of the indictment as true."** (*Constantinescu*, citing *Fontenot*).

At this stage, the court asks a purely legal question: *If every fact alleged in the indictment is accepted as true, does that set of facts constitute a crime?* The court explicitly refuses to weigh evidence, assess witness credibility, or determine if the facts are actually provable. Those tasks belong to a jury at trial under the "beyond a reasonable doubt" standard.

The district court originally dismissed the indictment not because it doubted the truth of the allegations, but because it concluded that **even if true**, those facts did not describe a crime. Specifically, the lower court believed that depriving someone of "valuable economic information" (rather than traditional property) fell outside the scope of securities fraud following *Ciminelli*.

### 2. Why Was There Doubt? (The Legal Gap)
You asked: *"If we assume all allegations are true... why was there ever doubt?"*

The doubt existed regarding **legal characterization**, not factual reality. The district court interpreted *Ciminelli v. United States* broadly to suggest that any scheme involving non-traditional property interests (like information rights) was invalid for fraud charges. Therefore, even accepting as true that defendants lied to get people to buy stocks, the lower court ruled this was insufficient because they viewed it as an attempt to steal "information," which is not property under current precedent.

The Fifth Circuit's reversal clarifies that there was no legal bar once the specific nature of the fraud was correctly identified. The Court distinguished two theories:
*   **Right-to-Control Theory:** Depriving a victim of information needed to make economic decisions (rejected in *Ciminelli*).
*   **Fraudulent-Inducement Theory:** Using lies to trick a victim into handing over money or property (upheld in *Kousisis*).

The Court found that because the indictment alleged defendants tricked followers into parting with **money** (buying securities), it fit within *Kousisis*'s permissible "fraudulent-inducement" theory, not *Ciminelli*'s prohibited "right-to-control" theory. Thus, assuming the allegations were true, they legally constituted a crime after all.

### 3. How This Protects Defendants
This standard protects defendants by ensuring they are not forced to face trial unless their conduct clearly falls within statutory prohibitions on paper before evidence is ever presented. It prevents prosecutors from charging crimes based on novel or unsupported interpretations of criminal law without first establishing that their factual narrative fits existing legal elements.

However, this protection stops at filing; it does not guarantee acquittal at trial. By reversing and remanding:
1.  The government still bears the burden of proving every element **beyond a reasonable doubt**.
2.  The assumption that "all allegations are true" vanishes immediately upon trial; witnesses can be cross-examined, and evidence can be excluded or discredited.
3.  The defendants retain their full right to present evidence showing they did not intend to defraud or that no misrepresentations occurred.

In short, today's decision merely says, "Assuming everything you said happened exactly as described in paragraph 13–14 is real, you have technically broken the law." Tomorrow at trial, if those events didn't happen or don't meet that high evidentiary bar, you will likely be acquitted anyway.

### Can a defendant be convicted of securities fraud if they provided something of equal value in exchange for the victim's money?
Question:
*Kousisis* suggests that providing something of equal value doesn't negate fraud if there was deception. In this pump-and-dump scenario, defendants sold real shares (even if manipulated) rather than nothingness. If an investor buys $100 worth of stock for $100, but bought it based on lies about trading volume or insider knowledge, is that legally indistinguishable from stealing $100 cash? How does criminal law treat 'fair exchange achieved through lies' versus 'theft by force,' especially when no net wealth transfer occurred for the buyer?
Answer:
Yes, under current Fifth Circuit precedent interpreting the Supreme Court's recent decision in *Kousisis*, a defendant can be convicted of securities fraud even if they provided something of "equal value" in exchange for the victim's money. The core inquiry in fraud cases is whether the defendant used deception to **obtain** money or property, not whether the transaction resulted in a net economic loss for the victim.

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit reversed a district court's dismissal of a "pump-and-dump" securities fraud indictment precisely because the defendants argued that their scheme lacked an intent to defraud since they were providing actual shares rather than nothingness. The defendants contended that because they intended to profit rather than specifically harm their followers' "bottom lines," no fraud occurred.

The appellate court rejected this argument by relying directly on *Kousisis v. United States*. As explained in *Constantinescu*:

1.  **Fraud Does Not Require Net Economic Loss:** The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* held that the fraud statutes do not require proof that a defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. A conviction stands even when the defendant "provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return."
2.  **The Focus is on Obtaining Property via Deception:** The statutory elements focus on whether the defendant engaged in a scheme to "deceive" another person and whether obtaining that person's money or property was an "object" of the fraud. In *Constantinescu*, the indictment alleged that defendants used false statements about trading positions and price potential to induce followers to purchase securities. By tricking investors into handing over their money based on materially false pretenses, the defendants satisfied the requirements for a "scheme to defraud."
3.  **Distinction from Right-to-Control Theory:** The court clarified that this is distinct from the discredited "right-to-control" theory (which relies solely on depriving victims of valuable information). Here, the charge rests on a "fraudulent-inducement theory": using lies to trick a victim into entering a contract where they hand over money or property. The fact that the victims received shares (property) does not negate the crime if those shares were acquired through deception regarding material facts like volume or insider knowledge.

Therefore, legally speaking, obtaining $100 worth of stock through lies about trading volume is treated as securities fraud just as severely as stealing $100 cash, provided the deception was material and induced the transfer of funds. The law protects against being cheated out of one's choice to enter a transaction; it does not guarantee that every honest-looking transaction results in financial loss for the buyer to constitute a crime. As *Constantinescu* notes, "[A] fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses"—regardless of whether fair value was exchanged afterward.
