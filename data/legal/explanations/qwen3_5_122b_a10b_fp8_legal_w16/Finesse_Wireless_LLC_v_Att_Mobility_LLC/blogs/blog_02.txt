Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## Claim Construction vs. Prosecution History Estoppel: The Limits of 'Three Signals'

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit delivered a sharp reminder to patent litigants: winning a battle over claim construction does not guarantee victory on infringement, especially when the technical reality of the accused device fails to align with the specific arithmetic of the claims. The case, decided by Chief Judge Moore, involved a high-stakes dispute over patents covering Passive Intermodulation (PIM) cancellation technology—a critical function in modern cellular networks where signal interference must be mathematically neutralized to maintain call quality. While the district court had allowed a massive $166 million jury verdict to stand, the Federal Circuit reversed, finding that no reasonable jury could have concluded infringement existed based on the evidence presented.

The decision offers a nuanced lesson in the interplay between claim construction and prosecution history estoppel, specifically regarding numerical limitations in method claims. At first glance, Finesse appeared to have secured a significant interpretive win regarding U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775. The patent claims a method requiring "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to be multiplied in seven specific combinations to generate cancellation signals. During prosecution, Finesse had amended its claims from "two or three signals" to strictly "three signals" to distinguish itself from prior art that utilized only two inputs. AT&T argued this amendment constituted a surrender of coverage for devices using fewer than three unique input signals—a classic prosecution history estoppel argument. However, the Federal Circuit declined to accept this narrow reading of the disclaimer. Relying on *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, the court found that Finesse's amendment was not a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of devices using two unique signals provided those were processed into three "separately identifiable" streams. By accepting the district court's construction that "three signals" did not mandate three *unique* physical inputs, the appellate court left open the door for broader interpretations of signal processing architectures.

Yet, this victory in claim construction proved pyrrhic because it collided head-on with the rigid requirements of literal infringement and evidentiary sufficiency. The crux of the reversal lay not in what the claims *could* cover under an expansive construction, but in what Finesse actually *proved* AT&T was doing. Claim 1[d] of the '775 patent explicitly mandates performing seven specific digital multiplications involving those three signals (e.g., $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$, etc.). Despite having successfully argued that two unique signals could satisfy the "three signals" limitation conceptually, Finesse failed entirely to demonstrate that AT&T's Nokia radios performed all seven required multiplication steps.

The breakdown occurred at the intersection of expert testimony and documentary evidence. Finesse's expert relied on Nokia technical documents that depicted only three distinct multiplication operations involving complex conjugates and moduli ($x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, etc.), rather than the seven specific algebraic combinations mandated by the patent language. When pressed at oral argument, Finesse attempted to pivot by noting that Nokia's documents referenced ten total multiplications somewhere in their architecture but admitted they could not map which subset corresponded to the seven claimed steps or how those ten related back to their earlier theory of three distinct multiplications mapping onto seven claimed ones. The court rightly characterized this gap as fatal: broadening claim scope through construction cannot cure a complete failure of proof regarding specific operational limitations within those claims. As Chief Judge Moore noted, no reasonable jury could infer performance of seven distinct mathematical operations from evidence showing only three distinct operations without additional explanatory mapping—which simply did not exist in this record.

The downstream implications of *Finesse Wireless* are particularly sobering for plaintiffs relying on complex algorithmic or mathematical patents where claim terms invite flexible interpretation but depend on precise execution for validity and infringement determinations. The ruling underscores that while courts may be willing to reject aggressive arguments regarding prosecution history estoppel—allowing plaintiffs some breathing room around numerical qualifiers—they remain unwilling to tolerate speculation when it comes to counting specific steps in a patented method sequence. A plaintiff cannot rely on a favorable claim construction to bridge an evidentiary void; if the specification requires seven multiplications and the accused device performs three (or even ten unspecified ones), and there is no credible link connecting them, infringement cannot be sustained as a matter of law via JMOL regardless of how broadly "signals" might be defined.

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* serves as a cautionary tale about treating claim construction as a silver bullet for weak factual cases. While Finesse managed to preserve its theoretical reach over devices utilizing two unique inputs by avoiding estoppel penalties, it lost its grip on reality when unable to prove those inputs were manipulated exactly as claimed. For practitioners drafting or litigating patents with numerical limitations—whether they involve counts of signals, iterations in loops, or types of filters—the message is clear: ensure your evidence maps every single step with precision before stepping into trial with hopes that broad construction will save you later. In this instance, mathematics won out over rhetoric; if you don't do all seven multiplications, you don't infringe, regardless of how many ways you can define your signals beforehand.
