Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## Seven Multiplications Matter: The Strict Literalism Required for Digital Signal Processing Claims

In the high-stakes world of telecommunications litigation, where patents often cover invisible algorithms rather than tangible hardware, the line between literal infringement and functional equivalence can determine the fate of millions of dollars in damages. The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* serves as a stark reminder that when patent claims specify precise numerical steps or mathematical operations, courts will demand concrete evidence that every single step occurred exactly as described. By reversing a massive jury verdict and granting judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on non-infringement, Chief Judge Moore's opinion underscores a rigorous adherence to the text of digital signal processing claims, rejecting speculative "mapping" theories that fail to account for the arithmetic reality of the accused technology.

The core dispute centered on two patents related to mitigating Passive Intermodulation (PIM), a phenomenon where radio signals combine via physical obstacles to create unwanted interference frequencies. While the court addressed multiple patents, the analysis of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 offers the most instructive lesson for practitioners drafting and litigating algorithm-based patents. Claim 1 of the '775 patent explicitly requires generating interference cancellation signals by "digitally multiplying... S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2..." through a total of **seven** distinct multiplications derived from three signals. The district court had permitted a jury to find infringement based on testimony that the accused Nokia radios performed "equivalent" operations using fewer distinct mathematical steps, provided those steps could be conceptually linked to the seven required by the claim.

The Federal Circuit dismantled this approach with surgical precision. Expert witness Dr. Jonathan Wells attempted to bridge the gap between the accused device and the patent claims by pointing to a Nokia technical document showing three distinct multiplications involving modulus and complex conjugate operations ($x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, etc.). Dr. Wells argued these three operations effectively covered the seven claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit found this logic fatally flawed under substantial evidence standards. As Chief Judge Moore noted, there was simply no evidence in the record that the accused radios performed all seven specific multiplications mandated by limitation 1(d). The expert admitted he could not map his three observed operations onto the seven required ones without engaging in conjecture, and crucially, he failed to explain how operations involving complex conjugates satisfied a claim requiring direct multiplication of specific signal variables.

This rejection highlights a critical distinction in patent law regarding algorithmic limitations: functional equivalence is not a license to ignore numerical specificity in claims. When an applicant chooses to draft a claim requiring "seven multiplications," they are staking their protection on that specific computational complexity or structure. If an accused device achieves a similar result through only three distinct mathematical operations—even if those operations are mathematically sophisticated—the literal terms of the claim are not met unless there is explicit evidence that all seven steps occur within the device's logic flow. The court refused to let Finesse retrofit its theory post-trial; arguments raised for the first time on appeal regarding how ten potential multiplications might map back down to seven were properly ignored because they were never presented to the jury or supported by Dr. Wells' original testimony.

Beyond strict literalism, *Finesse Wireless* provides significant clarity on prosecution history estoppel in software patents, particularly regarding amendments made during examination. Finesse argued that although it had amended its claims from "two or three signals" to strictly "three signals" to overcome prior art (the *Filipovic* reference), this did not amount to surrendering coverage for devices using only two unique input signals (where one signal is duplicated). They contended that "three signals" merely meant "three separately identifiable inputs," allowing for repetition among them.

The Federal Circuit declined to adopt such a narrow reading that would render prosecution history meaningless while simultaneously refusing to apply full estoppel barring two-signal devices entirely based solely on that amendment alone—a nuanced middle ground driven by context—but firmly rejected Finesse's attempt to stretch "three signals" into covering two unique inputs without clear justification from intrinsic evidence or explicit disavowal analysis. More importantly, regardless of whether estoppel applied strictly, the failure remained rooted in fact: even if two unique inputs could theoretically satisfy a broad reading of "three separately identifiable signals," they still could not satisfy the requirement for **seven** specific multiplications if only three actual mathematical operations were occurring in hardware. This reinforces that procedural defenses like estoppel cannot save an infringement case when the fundamental factual predicate—that every element is present—is missing from the record.

The downstream implications for technology companies and patent holders are profound. For defendants like AT&T and Nokia, *Finesse* validates their strategy of scrutinizing expert testimony against claim language line-by-line; if an expert cannot demonstrate every literal element without inventing facts or making unsupported leaps in logic, JMOL remains a powerful tool despite unfavorable jury verdicts at trial levels known for plaintiff-friendly juries like Eastern Texas District Court's East Division (implied by venue). Conversely, for patentees holding software patents defined by specific counts or sequences—whether it be seven multiplications, five iterations of a loop, or four distinct data transformations—the decision warns against over-reliance on expert interpretation that diverges from technical reality. Drafters must ensure their specifications support their claims with enough granularity so that experts can draw direct lines between code execution and claim elements without resorting to "confusing changes of course."

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* restores confidence in objective metrics within patent adjudication. In an era where AI-generated code and automated signal processing make it easy for engineers (and experts) to conflate results with processes, this ruling insists that courts look at what actually happens inside the machine, not just what it achieves functionally when words like "seven" are written into public records as defining boundaries of intellectual property rights.
