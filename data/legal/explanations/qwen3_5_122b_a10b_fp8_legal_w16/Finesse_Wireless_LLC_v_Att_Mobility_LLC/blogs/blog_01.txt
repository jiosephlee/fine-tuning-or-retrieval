Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## The Perils of Expert Confusion: Why Self-Contradiction Cannot Save a Jury Verdict

In the high-stakes arena of patent litigation, juries often serve as the final arbiters of technological truth. They weigh competing narratives, parse dense technical diagrams, and decide whether a defendant's product steps into the protected zone of a patent claim. However, the Federal Circuit recently sent a stark reminder to litigants that a jury verdict is only as strong as the evidence supporting it—and when that evidence collapses under the weight of its own contradictions, no amount of deference to the fact-finder can salvage the outcome. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided September 24, 2025, Chief Judge Moore reversed a multi-million dollar infringement verdict against AT&T and Nokia, holding that "confusing and unclear" expert testimony simply cannot sustain a finding of infringement when the party with the burden of proof fails to offer a coherent theory linking the accused technology to the patent claims.

The case centered on two patents related to Passive Intermodulation (PIM) cancellation in cellular radios—a critical function for preventing signal interference in modern wireless networks. Finesse Wireless had secured a jury verdict finding AT&T and Nokia liable for infringement and awarding over $166 million in damages. The district court subsequently denied judgments as a matter of law (JMOL) on non-infringement. On appeal, however, the Federal Circuit looked past the procedural posture and zeroed in on a fatal flaw in Finesse's case: Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse's primary infringement expert, provided testimony that was internally inconsistent to the point of rendering his opinions legally insufficient.

Chief Judge Moore's analysis focused heavily on the "substantial evidence" standard required to uphold a jury verdict. While appellate courts typically view evidence in the light most favorable to the prevailing party, they cannot ignore when that evidence points so overwhelmingly in one direction that no reasonable juror could reach the opposite conclusion. Here, Dr. Wells' testimony regarding how AT&T's radios operated created an impossible logical gap. To prove infringement of U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134, Finesse needed to show that the accused radios sampled both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Dr. Wells initially mapped these terms directly onto specific paths in Nokia's technical documentation: identifying the "downlink TX reference" as the signal of interest and the "modeled PIM path" as the interference-generating signal.

The problem arose during cross-examination when AT&T's counsel highlighted a fundamental physical impossibility: modeled PIM paths are generated digitally *after* sampling occurs within an FPGA; therefore, they cannot exist at the moment an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) takes its initial sample. Confronted with this contradiction, Dr. Wells attempted to pivot without clarifying his previous statements. He suggested there were actually two signals ("x1" and "x2") on the relevant path but failed to explicitly map these new identifiers back to the specific claim limitations required by law ("signals of interest" vs. "interference generating signals"). Throughout subsequent questioning, he oscillated between his original mapping (which was physically impossible) and vague references to x1 and x2 (which lacked evidentiary linkage).

This is where Chief Judge Moore invoked *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, a seminal precedent establishing that when an expert bears contradictory views on material facts necessary for liability, no reasonable jury can rely on their opinion to find infringement. The Federal Circuit noted that Finesse tried to construct a coherent narrative post-hoc by stitching together disparate quotes from Dr. Wells' deposition spanning ninety pages apart. The court rightly rejected this approach, emphasizing that litigation strategy cannot involve guessing what an expert *might* have meant months later when their live testimony remained muddled and self-defeating at trial. If an expert cannot clearly articulate how an accused device meets every element of a claim during direct or cross-examination, they cannot cure that defect by offering new theories after losing at trial or on appeal motions for JMOL.

The decision extends beyond mere procedural housekeeping; it reinforces a substantive barrier against speculative litigation tactics involving complex software-defined radio architectures where signal flow is invisible without precise modeling tools like simulation code or detailed block diagrams with explicit variable definitions—not just generic legends or ambiguous text descriptions like those found in Nokia's internal documents referenced by Dr.Wells.The court also addressed Finesse's attempt to reframe its position regarding U.S.Patent No.'775,'where Dr.Wells again failed to demonstrate how three distinct multiplications in Nokia's documentation could logically satisfy seven specific mathematical operations required by claim language.Finally,the reversal underscoresa broader principle applicable across all federal circuits:while juries are empowered resolve factual disputes.they are not permittedto fill gaps left by parties who fail meet their burden proof through clear,evidence-based testimony.As Chief Judge Moore wrote,"When party with burden proof,suchas Finesse.rests its case ona expert's self-contradictory testimony.we may conclude evidence insufficient satisfy standard."
