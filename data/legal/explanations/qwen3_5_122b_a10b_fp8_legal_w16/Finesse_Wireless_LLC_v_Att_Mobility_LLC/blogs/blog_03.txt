Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## When Technical Complexity Meets Legal Certainty: Why Documentary Evidence Must Match Claim Language Exactly

In the high-stakes world of telecommunications litigation, a $166 million jury verdict can vanish not because the technology is innocent, but because the bridge between technical reality and legal language collapses under scrutiny. That is precisely what happened in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided last month by the Federal Circuit. While the case centers on Passive Intermodulation (PIM)—a notoriously difficult engineering problem involving signal interference—the appellate court's reversal offers a stark reminder to patent litigants: in federal court, functional similarity is not enough. If your expert witness cannot map the accused device's specific mathematical operations directly onto the precise arithmetic recited in the patent claims, the verdict will not survive Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL).

Chief Judge Moore's opinion serves as a masterclass in why "substantial evidence" requires more than just a plausible theory; it demands a rigorous, step-by-step correspondence between the claim limitations and the documentary record. The Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL for both U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775, effectively wiping out a massive damages award because Finesse Wireless failed to prove that Nokia's radios actually performed the specific operations defined in the patents.

### The Fatal Flaw in Expert Testimony

The core of the dispute hinged on how Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, interpreted Nokia's internal technical documents against the strict requirements of patent claims. In intellectual property trials, experts often rely on diagrams and flowcharts to explain complex systems to juries. However, *Finesse* illustrates that when those diagrams describe mathematical processes that differ even slightly from claim language, reliance becomes perilous.

Take first the '134 patent. The claims required a method where a receiver samples a passband containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Dr. Wells attempted to prove infringement by pointing to a Nokia diagram showing a "modeled PIM path." He initially testified that this modeled path constituted the "interference generating signals." Yet, physics and logic dictated that this modeled signal was generated *after* sampling occurred within an FPGA; it simply could not exist at the moment of sampling by an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). When pressed on cross-examination, Dr. Wells pivoted awkwardly, suggesting that two signals labeled "x1" and "x2" in another part of his analysis were what he meant all along. The Federal Circuit rightly noted that this pivot was unsupported by any clear testimony linking x1 and x2 to the specific claim terms at issue during trial. The expert had created a narrative confusion so profound that no reasonable jury could have inferred infringement from it alone.

The situation regarding the '775 patent was even more instructive regarding documentary evidence. Claim 1 of this patent explicitly required generating cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals ($S_1$, $S_2$, $S_3$) in exactly **seven** distinct multiplications ($S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, etc.). This was not merely a functional requirement; it was an arithmetic one. Finesse pointed to Nokia documents showing calculations involving modulus operations ($|x|$) and complex conjugates ($x'$). Dr. Wells argued these amounted to infringement because they served a similar function—canceling interference—but he failed to demonstrate how these mathematically distinct operations mapped onto the seven specific multiplication steps recited in the claim.

The Federal Circuit refused to accept this leap of faith. As Chief Judge Moore wrote, "Nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications." The court highlighted that substituting $|x_2|$ for $x_2$ changes the fundamental nature of operation from simple multiplication to something else entirely under standard mathematical definitions used in digital signal processing contexts unless explicitly proven otherwise. By failing to show that Nokia performed *exactly* seven multiplications—and specifically those listed—the plaintiff left a gaping evidentiary hole that no amount of general testimony could fill post-trial.

### Doctrinal Implications: The Peril of Functional Equivalence Arguments

This decision reinforces a critical doctrine often glossed over in complex tech trials: literal infringement requires literal compliance with claim elements when those elements are structural or arithmetic constraints defined by integers or specific logical gates (or math ops). Courts are increasingly unwilling to stretch claim construction to cover technologies that achieve similar results via different mathematical pathways if those pathways diverge from the explicit text of the claims at trial.

The ruling also sends a warning shot regarding prosecution history estoppel arguments disguised as interpretation errors here too—though Finesse successfully defended against an argument about surrendering scope for two versus three signals due to lack of clear disavowal—they lost on execution at trial regardless. The lesson is twofold: First, amendments during prosecution matter less than what happens when you try to enforce those claims later; second, once you settle on claiming "seven multiplications," you must prove seven multiplications occurred in your evidence file.
