Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 1: # Case Overview and Procedural Context

## Parties and Technology Background
The dispute in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* centers on the complex engineering challenge of managing signal interference in modern wireless communication systems. The plaintiff-appellee, Finesse Wireless LLC, holds ownership of two patents related to methods for mitigating "intermodulation product" (IMP) interference. The defendants-appellants are AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia), collectively referred to as the Appellants. The core technology at issue involves "passive intermodulation products" (PIM), a phenomenon that occurs when radio signals encounter passive obstacles such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors. While radios typically avoid interference by operating on distinct frequencies, physical imperfections in the transmission environment can cause transmit signals to combine and generate new, unwanted frequencies known as IMPs. When these artifacts arise from passive components rather than active electronics within the device itself, they are classified as PIM. Finesse alleged that AT&T and Nokia infringed its intellectual property by utilizing a specific "PIM cancellation" (PIM-C) feature embedded within Nokia-manufactured radios deployed by AT&T.

## Patent Scope
The litigation focused on two specific patents owned by Finesse: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the "'134 patent") and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the "'775 patent"). Both patents describe methods designed to isolate "signals of interest"—the intended communications—from "interference generating signals," compute an estimate of the resulting intermodulation products, and subsequently cancel those products out of the received data stream. The '134 patent specifically claims a method involving over-sampling a passband of received signals to create a bit stream containing both signal types, isolating them via decimating filters, computing estimates of the intermodulation products from the interference sources, and performing phase and amplitude adjustments in a closed-loop manner using sub-sample phase shifts. The '775 patent claims a similar goal but focuses on a digital method for generating IMP cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three specific input signals (S1, S2, and S3) in seven distinct mathematical combinations to model non-linear processes occurring in passive transmitter components after the high-powered amplifier but before the antenna. These technical limitations formed the bedrock of Finesse's infringement theory against the accused Nokia devices.

## Procedural Posture
The case proceeded through the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas with a jury trial that resulted in a comprehensive victory for Finesse. The jury determined that all asserted claims of both the '134 and '775 patents were valid and infringed by the accused Nokia radios. Furthermore, the jury awarded Finesse $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages covering the remaining life of both patents following their expiration dates relative to filing dates or other relevant terms defined by law. Following this verdict, AT&T and Nokia filed post-trial motions seeking judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement and damages, as well as requesting a new trial on damages grounds under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50(b). They argued that Finesse had failed to present sufficient evidence to prove that the accused devices literally met every element of the asserted claims or supported the massive damage calculation. The district court denied all relief granted by Appellants' motions. Dissatisfied with this outcome, AT&T and Nokia appealed directly to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), challenging both the sufficiency of the evidence supporting infringement and the propriety of the damages award.

## Standard of Review
On appeal to the Federal Circuit, specific standards govern how appellate courts evaluate lower court decisions in patent cases involving JMOL and factual findings. Because infringement is ultimately a question of fact derived from claim construction applied to evidence viewed in light most favorable to the verdict winner, appellate review is bifurcated depending on whether claim construction or evidentiary sufficiency is at issue regarding JMOL denial. Here, applying Fifth Circuit law as mandated by Federal Circuit precedent (*Wi-LAN, Inc.*), denials of JMOL are reviewed *de novo*. This means the appellate court disregards any deference usually given to factual findings unless they involve credibility determinations reserved for juries; instead, it asks whether reasonable jurors could have arrived at any contrary conclusion when viewing all evidence in the light most favorable to Finesse. If evidence points so strongly toward one side that no reasonable juror could rule otherwise—a very high bar—the appellate court must reverse despite a jury verdict against that conclusion (*Johns Hopkins Univ.*). Conversely, if reviewing purely factual findings supporting infringement under a "substantial evidence" standard (*Apple Inc.*), reversal is rare; evidence is considered substantial if it allows a reasonable jury to find for the prevailing party based on what was actually presented at trial (*J.A.* records cited throughout). Finally, claim constructions themselves are reviewed *de novo*, though any underlying factual findings supporting those constructions are reviewed only for clear error (*Wi-LAN*).
