Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # '775 Patent Legal Standards on Signal Identification

## Separately Identifiable vs. Unique: Understanding Claim Construction Boundaries

To understand the Federal Circuit's reversal regarding the '775 patent, one must first grasp the specific claim construction adopted by the district court and the legal standard for surrendering claim scope during prosecution. The central dispute turned on the interpretation of "three signals S1, S2 and S3" within Claim 1. During summary judgment, the district court construed these terms to mean signals that are "separately identifiable," explicitly rejecting an argument that they must be "unique." This distinction is critical: "separately identifiable" allows for a scenario where a single physical signal is used multiple times (e.g., a signal and its mathematical derivative or copy), whereas "unique" would strictly require three physically distinct input sources. The court reached this conclusion by examining intrinsic evidence, which did not limit the signals to uniqueness, and extrinsic evidence, specifically inventor testimony indicating that S1, S2, and S3 represent signals generally and could be identical or different.

However, interpreting claim language is only half of the battle in patent litigation; parties must also navigate the doctrine of prosecution history estoppel. The appellants argued that Finesse had surrendered coverage for devices using fewer than three unique signals. They pointed to amendments made during prosecution where Finesse changed claim language from "two or three signals" to specifically "three signals" to overcome an obviousness rejection based on prior art (the *Filipovic* reference). Under federal patent law, a patentee may disavow or surrender claim scope through arguments or amendments made to secure allowance, provided that disavowal is "clear and unambiguous." The Federal Circuit analyzed whether Finesse's amendment constituted such a surrender. The court noted that while Finesse added the limitation of multiplying "three signals," they simultaneously distinguished the prior art by arguing it addressed *active* intermodulation products rather than *passive* ones. Consequently, the appellate court determined that Finesse did not clearly and unambiguously disclaim products with only two unique input signals. Instead, the amendment merely reinforced the requirement for three separately identifiable signals within the mathematical process, leaving open the possibility that one signal could be used more than once if it remained separately identifiable in the computation. Thus, legally speaking, a device using two unique inputs plus a copy of one might satisfy this specific claim limitation under the court's construction.

## Application to Accused Device: Evaluating Input Signals Against Statutory Requirements

With the legal standard established—that three separately identifiable signals are required but need not be unique—the analysis shifted to applying this rule to the factual record of the accused Nokia radios. Appellants contended that because their devices utilized only two unique physical inputs ($x_1$ and $x_2$), they could not possibly meet a requirement for "three" signals unless Finesse had surrenders scope (which we now know they had not). However, satisfying a claim element requires more than just meeting a theoretical definition; it demands substantial evidence linking every step of the patented method to the accused device's operation. In this case, even assuming arguendo that using $x_1$, $x_2$, and a derived version of $x_2$ satisfied the "three separately identifiable signals" prong, Finesse faced an insurmountable evidentiary hurdle regarding how those signals were processed.

The core failure lay in Finesse's inability to prove that the accused radios performed the specific mathematical operations mandated by Claim 1(d). The claim explicitly requires generating Intermodulation Product Cancellation Signals (ICSs) by digitally multiplying three signals in exactly seven distinct multiplications: $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$, and four other permutations involving combinations of $S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$. Dr. Wells, Finesse's expert witness, attempted to bridge this gap by presenting a Nokia technical document showing how their radios operated. He argued that three distinct multiplications shown in that document corresponded to seven claimed multiplications. However, upon closer scrutiny by both parties and affirmed by the Federal Circuit, Dr. Wells' testimony lacked substance because he failed to provide a coherent mapping between his proposed inputs and outputs against what was actually written in Nokia's documentation.

The fatal flaw in applying these legal standards to the facts was revealed when analyzing Nokia's own technical documents cited at trial. These documents described operations involving modulus functions ($|x|$) and complex conjugates ($'$), resulting in expressions like $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$ or $x_1 \cdot x'_2$. These mathematical forms do not align with the plain text of Claim 1(d), which calls for simple digital multiplication of raw signal variables without such transformations embedded in every term unless explicitly claimed as part of the variable definition itself—which they were not here. Furthermore, Dr. Wells admitted he could only identify three distinct multiplication types occurring in Nokia's system, yet he was tasked with proving seven specific multiplications occurred as required by statute under this construction. Without evidence demonstrating how these three observed operations expanded into seven distinct statutory steps—or how complex conjugates counted toward simple signal multiplications—the jury had no rational basis to find infringement based solely on signal identification theories alone.

## Correction/Refinement: Proof Failure Despite Valid Scope Arguments

This chapter concludes with a crucial lesson on how successful arguments regarding claim scope can still lead to defeat if unsupported by factual proof. While Finesse successfully defended its position against prosecution history estoppel—proving it had not surrendered rights over devices with two unique inputs—it ultimately lost because its expert failed to connect those inputs to the required seven-step multiplication process defined in '775 patent Claim 1(d). The Federal Circuit emphasized that even if two unique inputs plus copies theoretically satisfied the "three separately identifiable" requirement established at summary judgment, infringement could only stand if there was substantial evidence showing those inputs underwent all seven specified multiplications.
