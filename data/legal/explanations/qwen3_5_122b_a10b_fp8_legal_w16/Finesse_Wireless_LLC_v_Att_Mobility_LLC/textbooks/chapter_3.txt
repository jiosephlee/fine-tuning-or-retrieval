Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 3: # Analysis of '775 Patent Infringement Claims and Prosecution History

This chapter focuses on the Federal Circuit's reversal of the jury's infringement verdict regarding U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the "’775 patent"). While the district court had found that AT&T's radios infringed the patent, the appellate court determined that no reasonable jury could have reached that conclusion based on the evidence presented at trial. The analysis centers on two critical failures in Finesse Wireless LLC's case: first, the insufficiency of evidence to prove that the accused devices performed the specific mathematical operations required by the claims, and second, a nuanced application of prosecution history estoppel that allowed for flexibility in signal definition despite claim amendments. Understanding this section requires a close reading of how courts evaluate expert testimony against strict claim limitations and how amendments made during patent prosecution affect later litigation positions.

## Prosecution History Estoppel: Analyzing whether amending 'two or three' to 'three' disclaimed two-signal embodiments

A central defense raised by AT&T and Nokia was that Finesse had surrendered coverage for devices utilizing only two unique input signals when it amended its patent claims during examination. During prosecution, the original claims recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals." However, to overcome an obviousness rejection based on a prior art reference known as *Filipovic*, Finesse amended the claims to specifically require "three signals S1, S2 and S3." The appellants argued this amendment constituted a clear disavowal of any embodiment using fewer than three unique signals, effectively barring them from asserting infringement against a device that utilized only two unique signals (x1 and x2) along with a copy of one of those signals.

The Federal Circuit applied the doctrine of prosecution history estoppel but declined to find that Finesse had disclaimed two-signal embodiments. The court reasoned that while Finesse did amend the language from "two or three" to "three," the purpose of that amendment was primarily to distinguish the invention from *Filipovic* regarding *active* intermodulation products versus *passive* ones, rather than strictly limiting the numerical count of unique physical inputs. The court emphasized that for an amendment to result in a surrender of claim scope under 35 U.S.C. § 252(b), it must be "clear and unambiguous." Here, the specification and prosecution history indicated that "S1, S2, and S3" were intended to represent separately identifiable signals within a mathematical framework, not necessarily three physically distinct sources. Consequently, using two unique signals plus a copy of one still satisfied the requirement for "three separately identifiable signals" under the district court's construction. This distinction highlights an important lesson for litigants: not every narrowing amendment results in an absolute bar; courts examine whether the amendment was truly necessary to distinguish prior art in a way that explicitly excludes other subject matter.

## The Seven Multiplication Requirement: Dissecting Claim 1(d)'s mathematical constraints

Having established that using two unique input signals might technically satisfy the claim structure regarding signal identification, Finesse faced a far more insurmountable hurdle: proving that the accused radios actually performed seven specific digital multiplications as mandated by Claim 1(d). The claim language is highly prescriptive, requiring the generation of intermodulation product cancellation signals by digitally multiplying and filtering specific combinations of three inputs ($S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$, etc.) across seven distinct permutations involving third-order terms where $n$ is an integer. This is not merely a functional requirement; it is a structural limitation describing exactly how many multiplication steps must occur in hardware or software logic.

The court treated this limitation as a rigid constraint on what constitutes infringement. If an accused device performs fewer than seven such multiplications, or if it achieves similar results through fundamentally different mathematical operations (such as using modulus functions instead of direct multiplication), it does not literally infringe. In this case, Finesse bore the burden of proving by substantial evidence that AT&T/Nokia's technology executed these exact seven operations. The failure lay not in interpreting what "multiplication" meant legally—a question already resolved at summary judgment—but in failing to present empirical or documentary proof that those specific seven operations were occurring inside the accused chips. The court reiterated that when a claim recites specific numbers or steps, those elements are essential conditions precedent to liability; skipping even one step breaks the chain of literal infringement unless equivalence can be proven (which was not argued here).

## Mapping Failure: The disconnect between Nokia's technical documentation (showing three distinct ops/modulus) and the seven claimed multiplications

The crux of Finesse's evidentiary failure was its inability to bridge the gap between Nokia's internal technical documents and the rigorous requirements of Claim 1(d). To prove infringement, Finesse introduced a Nokia document depicting how their Passive Intermodulation Cancellation (PIM-C) feature operated. Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse's expert witness, attempted to map this document onto the patent claims by identifying three distinct multiplications shown in Nokia's diagrams: $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, $x_1 \cdot x_1 \cdot x'_2$, and $x_1 \cdot |x_2| \cdot |x_2|$. He argued these three operations somehow satisfied or corresponded to all seven required multiplications listed in Claim 1(d).

However, upon closer inspection by the Federal Circuit, this mapping collapsed under scrutiny for several reasons. First, Nokia's document did not list seven multiplications; it listed complex operations involving modulus ($|\cdot|$) and complex conjugates ($\cdot'$), which are mathematically distinct from simple digital multiplication as defined in standard engineering contexts relevant here. Second—and most critically—the document simply did not contain evidence of repeating any operation enough times to total seven distinct multiplications as required by "limitation 1[d]." Dr. Wells admitted he could not explain how three distinct operations equaled seven claimed operations without providing additional evidence that was never tested at trial. Furthermore, attempting to force-fit these equations revealed logical inconsistencies; for instance, mapping Signal $S_3$ simultaneously onto $x_2$, its complex conjugate $x'_2$, and its modulus $|x_2|$ created contradictions because these are different mathematical entities derived from different processing stages. Without clear testimony establishing how these three displayed equations transformed into seven separate multiplication events satisfying every term in Claim 1(d), no reasonable jury could infer infringement existed based solely on this evidence.

## New Evidence Barred on Appeal: Why Finesse's attempt to introduce new mappings at oral argument was insufficient

In an effort to salvage their position after losing at trial and moving for JMOL (Judgment as a Matter of Law), Finesse attempted to clarify its theory during oral argument before the Federal Circuit. They suggested that Nokia’s documents actually showed ten multiplications being performed ($p(n) = a_0$ through $a_9$) rather than just three or seven, implying there must be overlap covering all required steps. They further hinted at new ways these ten multiplications could map onto their patented method without having previously articulated such theories during discovery or trial testimony.

The court firmly rejected this approach based on fundamental principles of appellate procedure and fairness in litigation parties cannot cure defective proofs at trial by introducing new theories or evidence for the first time on appeal or even at oral argument when addressing post-verdict motions like JMOL or new trials. By trying to construct a new mapping between ten theoretical multiplications and seven claimed ones after closing arguments had long passed—and after Dr. Wells failed to articulate such a connection during his deposition or direct testimony—Finesse violated procedural boundaries designed to ensure both sides have notice and opportunity to address all material facts before a jury decides liability.
