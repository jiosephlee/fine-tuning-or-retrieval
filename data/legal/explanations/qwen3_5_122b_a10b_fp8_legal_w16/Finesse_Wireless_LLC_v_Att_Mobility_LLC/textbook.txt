Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Case Overview and Procedural Context

## Parties and Technology Background
The dispute in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* centers on the complex engineering challenge of managing signal interference in modern wireless communication systems. The plaintiff-appellee, Finesse Wireless LLC, holds ownership of two patents related to methods for mitigating "intermodulation product" (IMP) interference. The defendants-appellants are AT&T Mobility LLC (AT&T) and Nokia of America Corporation (Nokia), collectively referred to as the Appellants. The core technology at issue involves "passive intermodulation products" (PIM), a phenomenon that occurs when radio signals encounter passive obstacles such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors. While radios typically avoid interference by operating on distinct frequencies, physical imperfections in the transmission environment can cause transmit signals to combine and generate new, unwanted frequencies known as IMPs. When these artifacts arise from passive components rather than active electronics within the device itself, they are classified as PIM. Finesse alleged that AT&T and Nokia infringed its intellectual property by utilizing a specific "PIM cancellation" (PIM-C) feature embedded within Nokia-manufactured radios deployed by AT&T.

## Patent Scope
The litigation focused on two specific patents owned by Finesse: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the "'134 patent") and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the "'775 patent"). Both patents describe methods designed to isolate "signals of interest"—the intended communications—from "interference generating signals," compute an estimate of the resulting intermodulation products, and subsequently cancel those products out of the received data stream. The '134 patent specifically claims a method involving over-sampling a passband of received signals to create a bit stream containing both signal types, isolating them via decimating filters, computing estimates of the intermodulation products from the interference sources, and performing phase and amplitude adjustments in a closed-loop manner using sub-sample phase shifts. The '775 patent claims a similar goal but focuses on a digital method for generating IMP cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three specific input signals (S1, S2, and S3) in seven distinct mathematical combinations to model non-linear processes occurring in passive transmitter components after the high-powered amplifier but before the antenna. These technical limitations formed the bedrock of Finesse's infringement theory against the accused Nokia devices.

## Procedural Posture
The case proceeded through the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas with a jury trial that resulted in a comprehensive victory for Finesse. The jury determined that all asserted claims of both the '134 and '775 patents were valid and infringed by the accused Nokia radios. Furthermore, the jury awarded Finesse $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages covering the remaining life of both patents following their expiration dates relative to filing dates or other relevant terms defined by law. Following this verdict, AT&T and Nokia filed post-trial motions seeking judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) regarding noninfringement and damages, as well as requesting a new trial on damages grounds under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50(b). They argued that Finesse had failed to present sufficient evidence to prove that the accused devices literally met every element of the asserted claims or supported the massive damage calculation. The district court denied all relief granted by Appellants' motions. Dissatisfied with this outcome, AT&T and Nokia appealed directly to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), challenging both the sufficiency of the evidence supporting infringement and the propriety of the damages award.

## Standard of Review
On appeal to the Federal Circuit, specific standards govern how appellate courts evaluate lower court decisions in patent cases involving JMOL and factual findings. Because infringement is ultimately a question of fact derived from claim construction applied to evidence viewed in light most favorable to the verdict winner, appellate review is bifurcated depending on whether claim construction or evidentiary sufficiency is at issue regarding JMOL denial. Here, applying Fifth Circuit law as mandated by Federal Circuit precedent (*Wi-LAN, Inc.*), denials of JMOL are reviewed *de novo*. This means the appellate court disregards any deference usually given to factual findings unless they involve credibility determinations reserved for juries; instead, it asks whether reasonable jurors could have arrived at any contrary conclusion when viewing all evidence in the light most favorable to Finesse. If evidence points so strongly toward one side that no reasonable juror could rule otherwise—a very high bar—the appellate court must reverse despite a jury verdict against that conclusion (*Johns Hopkins Univ.*). Conversely, if reviewing purely factual findings supporting infringement under a "substantial evidence" standard (*Apple Inc.*), reversal is rare; evidence is considered substantial if it allows a reasonable jury to find for the prevailing party based on what was actually presented at trial (*J.A.* records cited throughout). Finally, claim constructions themselves are reviewed *de novo*, though any underlying factual findings supporting those constructions are reviewed only for clear error (*Wi-LAN*). # Analysis of '134 Patent Infringement Claims

## Claim Construction Limitation: Requirement to Sample a Passband Containing Both Signal Types Simultaneously

To determine whether the accused devices infringed the '134 patent, the Federal Circuit first examined the specific language of Claim 1, particularly the initial step described in limitation 1[a]. The claim requires a method that begins by "over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream." Crucially, this step mandates that the received signals within that passband must include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," which are defined as signals capable of generating intermodulation products (IMPs) that fall in-band with the signals of interest. This textual requirement establishes a strict condition for literal infringement: at the precise moment the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) performs the sampling operation, it must physically capture a mixture containing both types of signals. If the "interference generating signals" do not yet exist or are not present in the passband being sampled by the receiver hardware, the limitation is not met. The court emphasized that Finesse, as the plaintiff bearing the burden of proof, had to demonstrate that the accused radios actually sampled a passband containing these specific interference-generating inputs alongside their intended target signals.

## Expert Testimony Analysis: Dr. Wells' Identification of 'Modeled PIM Path' Versus Actual Physical Signal Paths

The core evidentiary dispute turned on how Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, interpreted a technical diagram from Nokia to map the claim limitations onto the accused technology. Dr. Wells initially testified that the Radio Frequency ADC (RF ADC) samples two specific pathways shown in red on Nokia's diagram: the "downlink TX reference" and the "modeled PIM path." He explicitly identified these two items as corresponding to the claim terms "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," respectively. However, upon cross-examination, Appellants' counsel exposed a fundamental flaw in this mapping by pointing out that the "modeled PIM path" depicted in Nokia's documentation represents a mathematical simulation generated internally within a Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) *after* the sampling event has already occurred. Dr. Wells conceded that this modeled path is created downstream of the RF ADC. Consequently, his initial identification conflated a digital model created post-processing with a physical signal existing pre-sampling. Despite this concession, Dr. Wells attempted to pivot during his testimony, suggesting there were actually two physical signals ("x1" and "x2") entering the ADC on that red path. Yet, he failed to ever explicitly state that x1 or x2 mapped to either "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals," leaving him oscillating between identifying them as modeled paths and unidentified physical inputs without providing a coherent bridge between his theory and the claim language.

## Temporal Impossibility Argument: Why Interference Signals Cannot Exist at Moment of Sampling by RF ADC

The court accepted Appellants' argument regarding temporal impossibility as fatal to Finesse's case. The physics of Passive Intermodulation (PIM) dictates that these interference products arise when transmit signals interact with passive obstacles or internal components *after* transmission but typically outside or distinct from the immediate reception chain logic described in certain models. More critically regarding this specific patent application, if Dr. Wells correctly identified his evidence as relying on a "modeled PIM path," he was describing a computational construct generated after data conversion. A signal cannot be sampled before it exists; therefore, if the "interference generating signal" is merely a modeled output derived from data already processed by an FPGA subsequent to sampling, it is temporally impossible for that signal to be present within the passband being sampled by an upstream RF ADC. The court reasoned that if Finesse's own expert admitted that his primary evidence—the modeled PIM path—is generated after sampling occurs, then no reasonable fact-finder could conclude that this specific element constitutes an input signal captured during over-sampling. This creates an insurmountable logical gap: claiming infringement based on capturing a signal at Time T when evidence shows that signal is only calculated at Time T+1 renders literal infringement impossible under these facts.

## Evidentiary Insufficiency: How Contradictory Expert Testimony Fails Burden of Proof for Literal Infringement

Ultimately, Federal Circuit precedent holds that when an expert's testimony is self-contradictory and fails to provide clear mapping between claim elements and accused features no reasonable jury can rely upon it to sustain a verdict against Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL). In this instance, Dr. Wells provided conflicting accounts: he first stated unequivocally that he mapped "interference generating signals" to modeled paths created post-sampling; later he suggested unnamed inputs x1 and x2 were responsible; yet throughout questioning he refused to clarify which physical inputs corresponded to which claim term despite clear prompts from opposing counsel. The district court erred in finding substantial evidence existed simply because it inferred Dr. Wells might have been referring to x1 and x2 rather than admitting his confusion was irreconcilable with his earlier admissions about modeled paths. Citing *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, appellate courts may overturn verdicts where an expert's contradictory testimony leaves no room for certainty regarding literal infringement requirements here specifically failing on limitation 1[a]. # Analysis of '775 Patent Infringement Claims and Prosecution History

This chapter focuses on the Federal Circuit's reversal of the jury's infringement verdict regarding U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the "’775 patent"). While the district court had found that AT&T's radios infringed the patent, the appellate court determined that no reasonable jury could have reached that conclusion based on the evidence presented at trial. The analysis centers on two critical failures in Finesse Wireless LLC's case: first, the insufficiency of evidence to prove that the accused devices performed the specific mathematical operations required by the claims, and second, a nuanced application of prosecution history estoppel that allowed for flexibility in signal definition despite claim amendments. Understanding this section requires a close reading of how courts evaluate expert testimony against strict claim limitations and how amendments made during patent prosecution affect later litigation positions.

## Prosecution History Estoppel: Analyzing whether amending 'two or three' to 'three' disclaimed two-signal embodiments

A central defense raised by AT&T and Nokia was that Finesse had surrendered coverage for devices utilizing only two unique input signals when it amended its patent claims during examination. During prosecution, the original claims recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals." However, to overcome an obviousness rejection based on a prior art reference known as *Filipovic*, Finesse amended the claims to specifically require "three signals S1, S2 and S3." The appellants argued this amendment constituted a clear disavowal of any embodiment using fewer than three unique signals, effectively barring them from asserting infringement against a device that utilized only two unique signals (x1 and x2) along with a copy of one of those signals.

The Federal Circuit applied the doctrine of prosecution history estoppel but declined to find that Finesse had disclaimed two-signal embodiments. The court reasoned that while Finesse did amend the language from "two or three" to "three," the purpose of that amendment was primarily to distinguish the invention from *Filipovic* regarding *active* intermodulation products versus *passive* ones, rather than strictly limiting the numerical count of unique physical inputs. The court emphasized that for an amendment to result in a surrender of claim scope under 35 U.S.C. § 252(b), it must be "clear and unambiguous." Here, the specification and prosecution history indicated that "S1, S2, and S3" were intended to represent separately identifiable signals within a mathematical framework, not necessarily three physically distinct sources. Consequently, using two unique signals plus a copy of one still satisfied the requirement for "three separately identifiable signals" under the district court's construction. This distinction highlights an important lesson for litigants: not every narrowing amendment results in an absolute bar; courts examine whether the amendment was truly necessary to distinguish prior art in a way that explicitly excludes other subject matter.

## The Seven Multiplication Requirement: Dissecting Claim 1(d)'s mathematical constraints

Having established that using two unique input signals might technically satisfy the claim structure regarding signal identification, Finesse faced a far more insurmountable hurdle: proving that the accused radios actually performed seven specific digital multiplications as mandated by Claim 1(d). The claim language is highly prescriptive, requiring the generation of intermodulation product cancellation signals by digitally multiplying and filtering specific combinations of three inputs ($S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$, etc.) across seven distinct permutations involving third-order terms where $n$ is an integer. This is not merely a functional requirement; it is a structural limitation describing exactly how many multiplication steps must occur in hardware or software logic.

The court treated this limitation as a rigid constraint on what constitutes infringement. If an accused device performs fewer than seven such multiplications, or if it achieves similar results through fundamentally different mathematical operations (such as using modulus functions instead of direct multiplication), it does not literally infringe. In this case, Finesse bore the burden of proving by substantial evidence that AT&T/Nokia's technology executed these exact seven operations. The failure lay not in interpreting what "multiplication" meant legally—a question already resolved at summary judgment—but in failing to present empirical or documentary proof that those specific seven operations were occurring inside the accused chips. The court reiterated that when a claim recites specific numbers or steps, those elements are essential conditions precedent to liability; skipping even one step breaks the chain of literal infringement unless equivalence can be proven (which was not argued here).

## Mapping Failure: The disconnect between Nokia's technical documentation (showing three distinct ops/modulus) and the seven claimed multiplications

The crux of Finesse's evidentiary failure was its inability to bridge the gap between Nokia's internal technical documents and the rigorous requirements of Claim 1(d). To prove infringement, Finesse introduced a Nokia document depicting how their Passive Intermodulation Cancellation (PIM-C) feature operated. Dr. Jonathan Wells, Finesse's expert witness, attempted to map this document onto the patent claims by identifying three distinct multiplications shown in Nokia's diagrams: $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, $x_1 \cdot x_1 \cdot x'_2$, and $x_1 \cdot |x_2| \cdot |x_2|$. He argued these three operations somehow satisfied or corresponded to all seven required multiplications listed in Claim 1(d).

However, upon closer inspection by the Federal Circuit, this mapping collapsed under scrutiny for several reasons. First, Nokia's document did not list seven multiplications; it listed complex operations involving modulus ($|\cdot|$) and complex conjugates ($\cdot'$), which are mathematically distinct from simple digital multiplication as defined in standard engineering contexts relevant here. Second—and most critically—the document simply did not contain evidence of repeating any operation enough times to total seven distinct multiplications as required by "limitation 1[d]." Dr. Wells admitted he could not explain how three distinct operations equaled seven claimed operations without providing additional evidence that was never tested at trial. Furthermore, attempting to force-fit these equations revealed logical inconsistencies; for instance, mapping Signal $S_3$ simultaneously onto $x_2$, its complex conjugate $x'_2$, and its modulus $|x_2|$ created contradictions because these are different mathematical entities derived from different processing stages. Without clear testimony establishing how these three displayed equations transformed into seven separate multiplication events satisfying every term in Claim 1(d), no reasonable jury could infer infringement existed based solely on this evidence.

## New Evidence Barred on Appeal: Why Finesse's attempt to introduce new mappings at oral argument was insufficient

In an effort to salvage their position after losing at trial and moving for JMOL (Judgment as a Matter of Law), Finesse attempted to clarify its theory during oral argument before the Federal Circuit. They suggested that Nokia’s documents actually showed ten multiplications being performed ($p(n) = a_0$ through $a_9$) rather than just three or seven, implying there must be overlap covering all required steps. They further hinted at new ways these ten multiplications could map onto their patented method without having previously articulated such theories during discovery or trial testimony.

The court firmly rejected this approach based on fundamental principles of appellate procedure and fairness in litigation parties cannot cure defective proofs at trial by introducing new theories or evidence for the first time on appeal or even at oral argument when addressing post-verdict motions like JMOL or new trials. By trying to construct a new mapping between ten theoretical multiplications and seven claimed ones after closing arguments had long passed—and after Dr. Wells failed to articulate such a connection during his deposition or direct testimony—Finesse violated procedural boundaries designed to ensure both sides have notice and opportunity to address all material facts before a jury decides liability. # '775 Patent Legal Standards on Signal Identification

## Separately Identifiable vs. Unique: Understanding Claim Construction Boundaries

To understand the Federal Circuit's reversal regarding the '775 patent, one must first grasp the specific claim construction adopted by the district court and the legal standard for surrendering claim scope during prosecution. The central dispute turned on the interpretation of "three signals S1, S2 and S3" within Claim 1. During summary judgment, the district court construed these terms to mean signals that are "separately identifiable," explicitly rejecting an argument that they must be "unique." This distinction is critical: "separately identifiable" allows for a scenario where a single physical signal is used multiple times (e.g., a signal and its mathematical derivative or copy), whereas "unique" would strictly require three physically distinct input sources. The court reached this conclusion by examining intrinsic evidence, which did not limit the signals to uniqueness, and extrinsic evidence, specifically inventor testimony indicating that S1, S2, and S3 represent signals generally and could be identical or different.

However, interpreting claim language is only half of the battle in patent litigation; parties must also navigate the doctrine of prosecution history estoppel. The appellants argued that Finesse had surrendered coverage for devices using fewer than three unique signals. They pointed to amendments made during prosecution where Finesse changed claim language from "two or three signals" to specifically "three signals" to overcome an obviousness rejection based on prior art (the *Filipovic* reference). Under federal patent law, a patentee may disavow or surrender claim scope through arguments or amendments made to secure allowance, provided that disavowal is "clear and unambiguous." The Federal Circuit analyzed whether Finesse's amendment constituted such a surrender. The court noted that while Finesse added the limitation of multiplying "three signals," they simultaneously distinguished the prior art by arguing it addressed *active* intermodulation products rather than *passive* ones. Consequently, the appellate court determined that Finesse did not clearly and unambiguously disclaim products with only two unique input signals. Instead, the amendment merely reinforced the requirement for three separately identifiable signals within the mathematical process, leaving open the possibility that one signal could be used more than once if it remained separately identifiable in the computation. Thus, legally speaking, a device using two unique inputs plus a copy of one might satisfy this specific claim limitation under the court's construction.

## Application to Accused Device: Evaluating Input Signals Against Statutory Requirements

With the legal standard established—that three separately identifiable signals are required but need not be unique—the analysis shifted to applying this rule to the factual record of the accused Nokia radios. Appellants contended that because their devices utilized only two unique physical inputs ($x_1$ and $x_2$), they could not possibly meet a requirement for "three" signals unless Finesse had surrenders scope (which we now know they had not). However, satisfying a claim element requires more than just meeting a theoretical definition; it demands substantial evidence linking every step of the patented method to the accused device's operation. In this case, even assuming arguendo that using $x_1$, $x_2$, and a derived version of $x_2$ satisfied the "three separately identifiable signals" prong, Finesse faced an insurmountable evidentiary hurdle regarding how those signals were processed.

The core failure lay in Finesse's inability to prove that the accused radios performed the specific mathematical operations mandated by Claim 1(d). The claim explicitly requires generating Intermodulation Product Cancellation Signals (ICSs) by digitally multiplying three signals in exactly seven distinct multiplications: $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$, $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$, and four other permutations involving combinations of $S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$. Dr. Wells, Finesse's expert witness, attempted to bridge this gap by presenting a Nokia technical document showing how their radios operated. He argued that three distinct multiplications shown in that document corresponded to seven claimed multiplications. However, upon closer scrutiny by both parties and affirmed by the Federal Circuit, Dr. Wells' testimony lacked substance because he failed to provide a coherent mapping between his proposed inputs and outputs against what was actually written in Nokia's documentation.

The fatal flaw in applying these legal standards to the facts was revealed when analyzing Nokia's own technical documents cited at trial. These documents described operations involving modulus functions ($|x|$) and complex conjugates ($'$), resulting in expressions like $x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$ or $x_1 \cdot x'_2$. These mathematical forms do not align with the plain text of Claim 1(d), which calls for simple digital multiplication of raw signal variables without such transformations embedded in every term unless explicitly claimed as part of the variable definition itself—which they were not here. Furthermore, Dr. Wells admitted he could only identify three distinct multiplication types occurring in Nokia's system, yet he was tasked with proving seven specific multiplications occurred as required by statute under this construction. Without evidence demonstrating how these three observed operations expanded into seven distinct statutory steps—or how complex conjugates counted toward simple signal multiplications—the jury had no rational basis to find infringement based solely on signal identification theories alone.
