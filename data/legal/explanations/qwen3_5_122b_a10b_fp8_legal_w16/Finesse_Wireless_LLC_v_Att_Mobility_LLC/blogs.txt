Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## The Perils of Confusing Expert Testimony: How Self-Contradiction Can Topple a Jury Verdict

In the high-stakes arena of patent litigation, juries are often tasked with deciphering complex technical concepts that defy lay understanding. They rely heavily on expert witnesses to bridge the gap between abstract claim language and the physical reality of accused devices. However, the Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* serves as a stark reminder that an expert's credibility is not merely a factor in weighing evidence; it is the very foundation upon which a verdict stands. By reversing a nearly $167 million jury verdict on the grounds of self-contradictory expert testimony, Chief Judge Moore has drawn a sharp line: when an expert's theory collapses under cross-examination, no amount of judicial deference to the jury can save the verdict from judgment as a matter of law (JMOL).

The core of the dispute involved Finesse Wireless's patents related to Passive Intermodulation (PIM) cancellation—a critical technology for modern cellular networks to mitigate signal interference caused by environmental obstacles. The jury initially sided with Finesse, finding that AT&T's Nokia-based radios infringed claims in both the '134 and '775 patents. Yet, on appeal, the Federal Circuit dismantled this victory by scrutinizing the testimony of Finesse's sole infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. The court's analysis reveals that while appellate courts typically view evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner, there is a ceiling beyond which "substantial evidence" simply ceases to exist: when the primary proponent of that evidence offers conflicting narratives without clarification.

On appeal regarding U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 ('134), the crux of the reversal hinged on a fatal ambiguity in Dr. Wells' identification of specific signal paths within AT&T's hardware. Claim 1 required the accused device to sample both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." During direct examination, Dr. Wells explicitly mapped these terms to two distinct elements shown in a Nokia technical diagram: he identified the "downlink TX reference" as the signal of interest and crucially labeled the "modeled PIM path" as the interference-generating signal. This mapping created an immediate logical impossibility for infringement; as defense counsel pointed out on cross-examination, modeled PIM signals are generated digitally *after* sampling occurs in an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). Therefore, an ADC cannot physically sample them as claimed input signals.

When confronted with this contradiction, Dr. Wells attempted a pivot rather than a correction. He suggested that there were actually two signals on that red path—"x1 and x2"—implying these might be what he meant all along. However, he never explicitly stated that x1 or x2 corresponded to "signals of interest" or "interference generating signals." Instead, he doubled down on his original mapping while vaguely referencing x1 and x2 as representations on that same path. The district court had accepted Finesse's invitation to infer meaning from scattered remarks separated by dozens of pages in the transcript, effectively rewriting Dr. Wells' testimony to fit a non-infringing narrative. The Federal Circuit rightly rejected this approach. Citing *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, Chief Judge Moore emphasized that when an expert provides contradictory testimony regarding a claim limitation they bear the burden of proving, no reasonable jury could find infringement based solely on that confusion. To allow otherwise would be to let parties cure defective proof through appellate speculation rather than trial presentation.

This reasoning carries profound implications for how litigators prepare and present experts in patent cases involving intricate digital signal processing or software algorithms where diagrams are paramount but easily misunderstood in translation to English text claims (*Finesse*). The court did not rule that two-signal architectures cannot infringe three-signal claims; indeed, they addressed this specific issue separately regarding patent '775 and found no prosecution history estoppel barred such coverage based on amendments distinguishing prior art regarding active vs. passive interference issues (*Finesse*). Rather, they ruled that Finesse failed to prove its case because its own witness could not consistently articulate how its theory mapped onto the accused technology without creating internal contradictions regarding temporal sequence (sampling before generation).

The decision reinforces a practical lesson often lost in post-trial motions practice: experts must be rigorously tested against their own diagrams during discovery and pretrial preparation before ever stepping into courtrooms where judges grant wide latitude to jury interpretations (*Finesse*). If an expert identifies Signal A as Element X during opening arguments but later suggests Signal B is Element X under pressure without clarifying why Signal A is no longer Element X, they have likely destroyed their own ability to support a verdict (*Finesse*). While appellate courts generally hesitate to overturn juries due to their role as fact-finders (*Wi-LAN*), *Finesse* illustrates that when an expert's entire theory relies on an uncorrected logical fallacy exposed during trial, deference becomes impossible (*Moore*, citing *Apple Inc.*).

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T* is less about telecommunications engineering and more about evidentiary discipline (*Moore*). It stands as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs who rest their infringement theories entirely on an expert who appears confident but lacks precision under scrutiny (*Wells*). In future trials involving complex technical limitations requiring precise mapping between claim terms and physical implementations (*Claim Construction*), litigators must ensure their experts can navigate cross-examination without resorting to vague pivots or unexplained shifts in terminology (*Finesse*). Otherwise, they risk seeing massive damage awards evaporate not because the defendant didn't infringe per se, but because they couldn't prove it clearly enough for any rational juror—or judge—to follow (*Substantial Evidence Standard*).



## Seven Multiplications Matter: The Strict Literalism Required for Digital Signal Processing Claims

In the high-stakes world of telecommunications litigation, where patents often cover invisible algorithms rather than tangible hardware, the line between literal infringement and functional equivalence can determine the fate of millions of dollars in damages. The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* serves as a stark reminder that when patent claims specify precise numerical steps or mathematical operations, courts will demand concrete evidence that every single step occurred exactly as described. By reversing a massive jury verdict and granting judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on non-infringement, Chief Judge Moore's opinion underscores a rigorous adherence to the text of digital signal processing claims, rejecting speculative "mapping" theories that fail to account for the arithmetic reality of the accused technology.

The core dispute centered on two patents related to mitigating Passive Intermodulation (PIM), a phenomenon where radio signals combine via physical obstacles to create unwanted interference frequencies. While the court addressed multiple patents, the analysis of U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 offers the most instructive lesson for practitioners drafting and litigating algorithm-based patents. Claim 1 of the '775 patent explicitly requires generating interference cancellation signals by "digitally multiplying... S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2..." through a total of **seven** distinct multiplications derived from three signals. The district court had permitted a jury to find infringement based on testimony that the accused Nokia radios performed "equivalent" operations using fewer distinct mathematical steps, provided those steps could be conceptually linked to the seven required by the claim.

The Federal Circuit dismantled this approach with surgical precision. Expert witness Dr. Jonathan Wells attempted to bridge the gap between the accused device and the patent claims by pointing to a Nokia technical document showing three distinct multiplications involving modulus and complex conjugate operations ($x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, etc.). Dr. Wells argued these three operations effectively covered the seven claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit found this logic fatally flawed under substantial evidence standards. As Chief Judge Moore noted, there was simply no evidence in the record that the accused radios performed all seven specific multiplications mandated by limitation 1(d). The expert admitted he could not map his three observed operations onto the seven required ones without engaging in conjecture, and crucially, he failed to explain how operations involving complex conjugates satisfied a claim requiring direct multiplication of specific signal variables.

This rejection highlights a critical distinction in patent law regarding algorithmic limitations: functional equivalence is not a license to ignore numerical specificity in claims. When an applicant chooses to draft a claim requiring "seven multiplications," they are staking their protection on that specific computational complexity or structure. If an accused device achieves a similar result through only three distinct mathematical operations—even if those operations are mathematically sophisticated—the literal terms of the claim are not met unless there is explicit evidence that all seven steps occur within the device's logic flow. The court refused to let Finesse retrofit its theory post-trial; arguments raised for the first time on appeal regarding how ten potential multiplications might map back down to seven were properly ignored because they were never presented to the jury or supported by Dr. Wells' original testimony.

Beyond strict literalism, *Finesse Wireless* provides significant clarity on prosecution history estoppel in software patents, particularly regarding amendments made during examination. Finesse argued that although it had amended its claims from "two or three signals" to strictly "three signals" to overcome prior art (the *Filipovic* reference), this did not amount to surrendering coverage for devices using only two unique input signals (where one signal is duplicated). They contended that "three signals" merely meant "three separately identifiable inputs," allowing for repetition among them.

The Federal Circuit declined to adopt such a narrow reading that would render prosecution history meaningless while simultaneously refusing to apply full estoppel barring two-signal devices entirely based solely on that amendment alone—a nuanced middle ground driven by context—but firmly rejected Finesse's attempt to stretch "three signals" into covering two unique inputs without clear justification from intrinsic evidence or explicit disavowal analysis. More importantly, regardless of whether estoppel applied strictly, the failure remained rooted in fact: even if two unique inputs could theoretically satisfy a broad reading of "three separately identifiable signals," they still could not satisfy the requirement for **seven** specific multiplications if only three actual mathematical operations were occurring in hardware. This reinforces that procedural defenses like estoppel cannot save an infringement case when the fundamental factual predicate—that every element is present—is missing from the record.

The downstream implications for technology companies and patent holders are profound. For defendants like AT&T and Nokia, *Finesse* validates their strategy of scrutinizing expert testimony against claim language line-by-line; if an expert cannot demonstrate every literal element without inventing facts or making unsupported leaps in logic, JMOL remains a powerful tool despite unfavorable jury verdicts at trial levels known for plaintiff-friendly juries like Eastern Texas District Court's East Division (implied by venue). Conversely, for patentees holding software patents defined by specific counts or sequences—whether it be seven multiplications, five iterations of a loop, or four distinct data transformations—the decision warns against over-reliance on expert interpretation that diverges from technical reality. Drafters must ensure their specifications support their claims with enough granularity so that experts can draw direct lines between code execution and claim elements without resorting to "confusing changes of course."

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* restores confidence in objective metrics within patent adjudication. In an era where AI-generated code and automated signal processing make it easy for engineers (and experts) to conflate results with processes, this ruling insists that courts look at what actually happens inside the machine, not just what it achieves functionally when words like "seven" are written into public records as defining boundaries of intellectual property rights. ## When Technical Complexity Meets Legal Certainty: Why Documentary Evidence Must Match Claim Language Exactly

In the high-stakes world of telecommunications litigation, a $166 million jury verdict can vanish not because the technology is innocent, but because the bridge between technical reality and legal language collapses under scrutiny. That is precisely what happened in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided last month by the Federal Circuit. While the case centers on Passive Intermodulation (PIM)—a notoriously difficult engineering problem involving signal interference—the appellate court's reversal offers a stark reminder to patent litigants: in federal court, functional similarity is not enough. If your expert witness cannot map the accused device's specific mathematical operations directly onto the precise arithmetic recited in the patent claims, the verdict will not survive Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL).

Chief Judge Moore's opinion serves as a masterclass in why "substantial evidence" requires more than just a plausible theory; it demands a rigorous, step-by-step correspondence between the claim limitations and the documentary record. The Federal Circuit reversed the district court's denial of JMOL for both U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775, effectively wiping out a massive damages award because Finesse Wireless failed to prove that Nokia's radios actually performed the specific operations defined in the patents.

### The Fatal Flaw in Expert Testimony

The core of the dispute hinged on how Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, interpreted Nokia's internal technical documents against the strict requirements of patent claims. In intellectual property trials, experts often rely on diagrams and flowcharts to explain complex systems to juries. However, *Finesse* illustrates that when those diagrams describe mathematical processes that differ even slightly from claim language, reliance becomes perilous.

Take first the '134 patent. The claims required a method where a receiver samples a passband containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Dr. Wells attempted to prove infringement by pointing to a Nokia diagram showing a "modeled PIM path." He initially testified that this modeled path constituted the "interference generating signals." Yet, physics and logic dictated that this modeled signal was generated *after* sampling occurred within an FPGA; it simply could not exist at the moment of sampling by an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). When pressed on cross-examination, Dr. Wells pivoted awkwardly, suggesting that two signals labeled "x1" and "x2" in another part of his analysis were what he meant all along. The Federal Circuit rightly noted that this pivot was unsupported by any clear testimony linking x1 and x2 to the specific claim terms at issue during trial. The expert had created a narrative confusion so profound that no reasonable jury could have inferred infringement from it alone.

The situation regarding the '775 patent was even more instructive regarding documentary evidence. Claim 1 of this patent explicitly required generating cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals ($S_1$, $S_2$, $S_3$) in exactly **seven** distinct multiplications ($S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$, etc.). This was not merely a functional requirement; it was an arithmetic one. Finesse pointed to Nokia documents showing calculations involving modulus operations ($|x|$) and complex conjugates ($x'$). Dr. Wells argued these amounted to infringement because they served a similar function—canceling interference—but he failed to demonstrate how these mathematically distinct operations mapped onto the seven specific multiplication steps recited in the claim.

The Federal Circuit refused to accept this leap of faith. As Chief Judge Moore wrote, "Nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications." The court highlighted that substituting $|x_2|$ for $x_2$ changes the fundamental nature of operation from simple multiplication to something else entirely under standard mathematical definitions used in digital signal processing contexts unless explicitly proven otherwise. By failing to show that Nokia performed *exactly* seven multiplications—and specifically those listed—the plaintiff left a gaping evidentiary hole that no amount of general testimony could fill post-trial.

### Doctrinal Implications: The Peril of Functional Equivalence Arguments

This decision reinforces a critical doctrine often glossed over in complex tech trials: literal infringement requires literal compliance with claim elements when those elements are structural or arithmetic constraints defined by integers or specific logical gates (or math ops). Courts are increasingly unwilling to stretch claim construction to cover technologies that achieve similar results via different mathematical pathways if those pathways diverge from the explicit text of the claims at trial.

The ruling also sends a warning shot regarding prosecution history estoppel arguments disguised as interpretation errors here too—though Finesse successfully defended against an argument about surrendering scope for two versus three signals due to lack of clear disavowal—they lost on execution at trial regardless. The lesson is twofold: First, amendments during prosecution matter less than what happens when you try to enforce those claims later; second, once you settle on claiming "seven multiplications," you must prove seven multiplications occurred in your evidence file.
