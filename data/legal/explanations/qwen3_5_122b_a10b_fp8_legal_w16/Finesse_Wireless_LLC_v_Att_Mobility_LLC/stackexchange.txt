Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Does 'confusing' expert testimony automatically preclude a jury verdict from being supported by substantial evidence?
Question:
In the Finesse Wireless case, the Federal Circuit reversed the JMOL denial primarily because Dr. Wells' testimony was 'confusing and unclear,' specifically regarding his mapping of 'signals of interest' versus 'interference generating signals.' The court cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* to say that self-contradictory testimony cannot support a verdict. However, isn't the standard for substantial evidence usually just whether *some* reasonable interpretation of the evidence supports the verdict? Does the mere fact that an expert changes their story or is ambiguous on cross-examination mean there is *zero* substantial evidence, or does it depend on whether there was *any* consistent thread in their testimony that a jury could reasonably rely upon? How do courts distinguish between 'unpersuasive' testimony and legally insufficient testimony?
Answer:
No, "confusing" expert testimony does not *automatically* preclude a finding of substantial evidence in every instance, but under the Federal Circuit's decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, confusion that results in **self-contradictory** or **unclear** testimony regarding essential claim limitations can render the evidence legally insufficient.

The distinction lies between testimony that is merely unpersuasive (which a jury may reject or weigh lightly) and testimony that fails to establish a rational connection between the evidence and the claim elements (which leaves no basis for a verdict).

### The Standard: Self-Contradiction vs. Ambiguity
As noted in your question, the general standard for substantial evidence asks whether "reasonable minds could differ." However, *Finesse* clarifies that when an expert's testimony is internally inconsistent to the point where no single theory of infringement remains viable without resorting to speculation, it ceases to be "substantial."

The Federal Circuit relied on *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, stating:
> "When the party with the burden of proof... rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard." (*Finesse*, slip op. at 12).

In *Finesse*, Dr. Wells' testimony was not just ambiguous; it was fundamentally incompatible with itself regarding the mapping of technical terms to physical signals:
1.  **Initial Position:** He explicitly testified that "signals of interest" were the DL(TX) reference and "interference generating signals" were the modeled PIM path.
2.  **Cross-Examination Confrontation:** Counsel demonstrated that the modeled PIM path physically could not exist at the sampling stage required by the claim (as it was generated downstream).
3.  **Failed Pivot:** Dr. Wells attempted to pivot by claiming there were "two signals" (x1 and x2) on a specific path, yet he **never** clarified which signal corresponded to which claim term ("signals of interest" vs. "interference generating"). He continued to assert his original mapping despite acknowledging his potential error regarding x1/x2.

### Why Confusion Became Insufficiency
The court distinguished this from a situation where a jury might simply choose one version of events over another. Here, there was no "consistent thread" for a jury to latch onto because:
*   The expert offered **no clarification** linking x1/x2 to the specific statutory requirements after being challenged.
*   Finesse had to piece together his theory by combining testimony separated by **ninety pages** in the record, rather than relying on clear statements made at trial (*Finesse*, slip op. at 13).
*   The resulting inference required jurors to guess what the expert *meant* rather than deciding what he *said*.

The court held that allowing a verdict based on such testimony would permit parties to rest their case on **"speculation"** rather than evidence (*id.*). If an expert cannot define what constitutes an element of infringement due to internal contradiction, there is effectively zero evidence supporting that element, regardless of how much other material exists in the record.

### Conclusion: Unpersuasive vs. Legally Insufficient
To answer your final question directly: Courts distinguish between "unpersuasive" and "legally insufficient" based on whether a reasonable juror could rationally connect the dots without filling gaps left by the witness's own contradictions.

*   **Unpersuasive:** An expert gives shaky testimony, but maintains a coherent theory (even if weak) that maps facts to claims. A jury may still find for them if they believe that specific thread is credible enough compared to opposing evidence.
*   **Legally Insufficient (per *Finesse*):** An expert's core mapping collapses under cross-examination, and they fail to offer a corrected, coherent alternative within their direct or redirect examination (leaving only post-hoc appellate arguments or disjointed snippets). In this scenario, no reasonable jury could find infringement because there is no evidentiary foundation for any single theory of liability.

Therefore, while ambiguity alone does not always destroy substantial evidence, **self-contradictory ambiguity regarding critical claim mappings**, as seen in Dr. Wells' inability to reconcile his initial mapping with physical realities without offering a clear correction, creates a gap too wide for any reasonable jury verdict to bridge.

### How does prosecution history estoppel apply when a patentee amends claims to specify a number (e.g., 'three') without explicitly disavowing lower numbers?
Question:
Regarding the '775 patent, the appellants argued that Finesse surrendered coverage for devices using only two signals because they amended their claims from 'two or three signals' to just 'three signals' to overcome a prior art rejection involving two inputs. The court rejected this surrender argument, stating that merely adding the word 'three' wasn't a 'clear and unambiguous disavowal.' I'm struggling with the threshold for disclaimer via amendment. If an applicant narrows a claim to specifically recite a number to avoid prior art that uses fewer elements, why doesn't that inherently imply they gave up the narrower scope? What specific language or reasoning in the prosecution file would be required to trigger estoppel in this scenario compared to cases where amendments were deemed disclaimers?
Answer:
Based on the Federal Circuit's decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2024 WL [Case Number], the threshold for triggering prosecution history estoppel via claim amendment is exceptionally high. Merely narrowing a claim to specify a number (e.g., changing "two or three" to "three") to distinguish from prior art does **not** automatically constitute a surrender of the broader scope unless the amendment represents a "clear and unambiguous disavowal" of that scope.

Here is how the court applied this standard in *Finesse* regarding the '775 patent:

### 1. The Standard: "Clear and Unambiguous Disavowal"
The court reiterated that prosecution history estoppel requires more than a mere implication or logical inference that an applicant gave up certain subject matter. Citing *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), the court held that an amendment only surrenders coverage if it constitutes a "clear and unambiguous disavowal."

In *Finesse*, the appellants argued that by amending claims from "two or three signals" to simply "three signals," Finesse implicitly surrendered any device using only two signals. The court rejected this, noting that while the amendment narrowed the literal scope of the claim, it did not explicitly state that devices with fewer than three signals were excluded from protection forever.

### 2. Context Matters: The Reason for Amendment
The court looked closely at *why* the amendment was made to determine if it operated as a disclaimer.
*   **The Prior Art:** The examiner had rejected the original claims as obvious over *Filipovic*, which taught generating cancellation signals using **two** inputs (*J.A.* 1006–07).
*   **The Applicant's Argument:** Finesse distinguished *Filipovic* not solely by adding the number "three," but by arguing that *Filipovic* addressed **active** intermodulation products (IMPs), whereas Finesse's invention addressed **passive** IMPs (*J.A.* 985).
*   **The Holding:** Because Finesse successfully distinguished the prior art based on the *type* of interference (active vs. passive) rather than solely relying on the numerical count, the addition of "three signals" was viewed as defining the specific embodiment necessary to solve the problem, not as a renunciation of all embodiments with fewer inputs.

As Chief Judge Moore wrote: "Although Finesse also amended claims to add the limitation of digitally multiplying 'three signals S1, S2 and S3'... that does not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope over products... with two unique input signals." (*Opinion* at Section I.B).

### 3. What Would Trigger Estoppel?
To trigger estoppel in this scenario, the prosecution history would need to contain language indicating that the applicant understood and intended to give up coverage for fewer elements entirely because they were essential to avoiding prior art or because they were inconsistent with the invention's core concept.

Under *Finesse*, an amendment triggers estoppel if:
1.  **Explicit Disclaimer:** The applicant states, "We amend to require exactly three because anything less is invalid/unpatentable," thereby tying validity directly to that specific limitation in a way that precludes equivalents.
2.  **Reasoning Tied Exclusively to Scope:** If Finesse had argued, "*Filipovic* uses two inputs; therefore, our invention is strictly limited to three inputs because two-input systems are fundamentally incapable of achieving our result," without offering an alternative distinction (like active vs. passive IMPs), this might have been seen as a clear surrender of two-input systems.

However, because Finesse provided an alternative ground for patentability (the nature of the IMPs), the numerical change was treated as a definition of their preferred embodiment rather than a permanent waiver of other configurations that might still work for passive IMP mitigation but utilize fewer unique source signals (provided those three multiplicands can be derived from fewer unique inputs).

### Conclusion
In *Finesse*, specifying "three" did not act as a disclaimer for "two" because:
1.  The amendment lacked explicit language abandoning lower numbers; and
2.  The rationale for overcoming rejection rested on distinguishing active vs. passive interference, leaving open the possibility that two unique input signals could theoretically generate three separately identifiable signals required by the claim construction ("S1, S2 and S3").

Therefore, absent an express statement or unavoidable implication in the prosecution file that lower numbers are impossible or invalid for reasons unrelated to other distinguishing features, courts will not infer a surrender of claim scope solely from numerical limitations added during examination.

### Can a jury infer missing claim limitations from technical documents if the expert witness fails to explicitly map them?
Question:
In section B of the opinion concerning the '775 patent, the court noted that Dr. Wells identified three multiplications in a Nokia document but failed to explain how those mapped to the seven specific multiplications required by the claim (S1×S1×S2, etc.). The court stated that Finesse's attempt to clarify this mapping on appeal was too late because it wasn't presented to the jury. My confusion lies in the boundary between expert opinion and lay inference: Could a technically savvy juror look at a diagram showing three operations and logically deduce it covers seven mathematical steps if no one explicitly told them so? Or is it strictly forbidden for a jury to bridge gaps in technical evidence without explicit expert testimony connecting every single claim element to an accused feature?
Answer:
No, a jury cannot infer missing claim limitations from technical documents when the expert witness fails to explicitly map those elements, particularly in complex technical fields requiring specialized knowledge.

Under the Federal Circuit's standard for substantial evidence, a jury verdict of infringement must be supported by evidence that connects every claim limitation to an accused feature. While juries are permitted to draw reasonable inferences from the evidence presented, they are not authorized to act as experts themselves to fill critical gaps in technical testimony.

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) specifically because Dr. Wells failed to establish this necessary link for the '775 patent. The court highlighted that while Dr. Wells identified three distinct multiplications in a Nokia technical document ($x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, $x_1 \cdot x_1 \cdot x_2'$, and $x_1 \cdot |x_2| \cdot |x_2|$), he **never testified** how these three operations mapped onto the seven specific mathematical combinations required by Claim 1(d) ($S_1\times S_1\times S_2$, etc.).

The court explicitly rejected the notion that a "technically savvy juror" could bridge this gap:

> "But the document... does not show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications... Moreover, Dr. Wells did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications."

The opinion clarifies that post-trial arguments attempting to supply this mapping are inadmissible because they were "never presented to the jury." The court emphasized that without expert testimony connecting the specific operations shown in the document to the specific language of the claim, there is no substantial evidence for a jury to find infringement. The inability of a layperson (even a technically inclined one) to deduce that three displayed operations equate to seven specific statutory requirements demonstrates why expert guidance is mandatory in such contexts. Without that explicit testimony, any finding of infringement is speculative and unsupported by law.

### What is the practical effect of vacating damages after reversing JMOL on non-infringement vs. remanding for retrial?
Question:
The opinion states: 'Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement... we vacate the damages award.' Since JMOL means no reasonable jury could have found infringement based on existing evidence, why didn't the court simply affirmatively rule that damages are zero rather than just vacating? Is there any scenario where vacating allows for new damages calculations on appeal (perhaps based on different theories not raised at trial), or does this procedural step essentially guarantee that no damages will ever be collected unless Finesse files a completely new lawsuit with new experts?
Answer:
The short answer is that once an appellate court reverses a jury's finding of liability via Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL), **vacating** the damages award is the necessary procedural consequence because the legal basis for *any* damages has been extinguished. The court cannot "affirmatively rule damages are zero" in this context because doing so would imply a final adjudication on the merits of damages without a proper factual foundation, whereas vacatur simply restores the parties to their pre-verdict status regarding money.

Here is the breakdown of why this happens and what it practically means for Finesse Wireless LLC:

### 1. The Logical Necessity of Vacatur
Damages in patent cases are inherently derivative; they exist only if liability exists. In *Finesse*, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL on non-infringement for **all** asserted claims of both the '134 and '775 patents. The opinion explicitly states: *"Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement... we vacate the damages award."*

If no infringement occurred (as established by the reversal), then legally, there were no compensable injuries. Therefore, the $166 million award lacks any legal anchor. An appellate court generally does not enter a judgment for "$0" against a plaintiff who lost on liability because:
*   **Jurisdiction/Scope:** The appeal was taken from the denial of JMOL and new trial motions. Once liability is removed, there is no longer a "damages dispute" to resolve on appeal; there is simply no case left for damages.
*   **Finality:** Entering a specific judgment for zero might inadvertently trigger different post-judgment interest calculations or res judicata effects compared to a simple vacatur, which wipes the slate clean regarding that specific monetary award.

### 2. Does Vacatur Allow New Damage Calculations?
No. You asked if vacating allows for new damage calculations based on different theories not raised at trial. The answer is definitively **no**.

The purpose of JMOL is that **"no reasonable jury could have found"** infringement based on the evidence *already presented*. As Chief Judge Moore writes, Finesse rested its entire case on Dr. Wells' testimony, which was found to be "confusing," "self-contradictory," and insufficient to link the accused devices to the claim limitations (specifically regarding sampling "interference generating signals" and performing "seven multiplications").

Because JMOL finds that **substantial evidence was lacking**, it acts as a gatekeeper preventing further litigation on those specific claims *based on that evidence*.
*   **New Theories?** If Finesse tries to argue a new theory of infringement or damages now, they would likely face immediate dismissal or another successful JMOL motion because they already had their day in court and failed to produce admissible evidence linking their theory to reality.
*   **New Experts?** Introducing new experts or new data at this stage would effectively be asking for a retrial on liability, which contradicts the holding that no reasonable jury could have found infringement *on the record as it stood*.

### 3. Practical Effect: Retrial vs. New Lawsuit
You asked if this guarantees no damages will ever be collected unless Finesse files a completely new lawsuit with new experts.

*   **Retrial on Liability:** Generally impossible here. The Federal Circuit has determined that based on current technology and available evidence described in the record, infringement cannot be proven under these claim constructions. Unless Finesse discovers entirely new facts about AT&T/Nokia's internal architecture that were previously unknown and undisclosed (which raises serious discovery sanction issues), they cannot retry these specific claims successfully in this forum.
*   **Dismissal with Prejudice:** While not explicitly stated in this short opinion, when an appellate court reverses JMOL of non-infringement for all claims, it often results in entry of judgment for defendants with prejudice regarding those claims in lower courts (or remand with instructions to enter such judgment).
*   **New Lawsuit:** If Finesse wishes to pursue AT&T again, they would need to file a fresh complaint alleging infringement under potentially different claim constructions (if applicable) or against different products/evidence not previously litigated. However, filing suit over the exact same patents and accused products immediately after losing on JMOL due to lack of evidence would likely violate judicial estoppel or collateral estoppel principles depending on how strictly the district court enters judgment upon remand.

**Conclusion:**
Vacating the damages award here is not merely procedural window dressing; it reflects that without infringement, there are no dollars owed. It does not open a door for Finesse to re-calculate damages using new theories; rather, it confirms that their failure to prove infringement was fatal to their entire recovery effort in this action. Practically speaking, barring some extraordinary development (like discovering hidden prior art invalidating their own position or new evidence of entirely different conduct), Finesse will collect nothing from AT&T regarding these patents unless they start an entirely new legal proceeding with fundamentally different facts—a high bar given JMOL standards require them to show they missed something critical despite having full discovery and trial access previously used by Dr. Wells' failed testimony.

### How does claim construction regarding 'separately identifiable' signals interact with physical reality in digital signal processing?
Question:
The district court construed 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' as needing to be 'separately identifiable' but not necessarily unique (i.e., duplicates allowed). The Federal Circuit upheld this construction but still found non-infringement because only two unique physical inputs existed. I'm trying to understand how software-defined radio handles this conceptually. If my code takes Input A and Input B, creates Copy C of Input A, and multiplies them together (A*B*C), are those considered three 'separately identifiable' signals in light of physics (since A and C are identical waveforms) or logic (since they occupy different memory registers)? Why did the court feel comfortable letting two physical sources count as three logical inputs for construction purposes but then flip-flop when analyzing infringement facts?
Answer:
You have correctly identified a tension between the *legal* definition of "separately identifiable" established during claim construction and the *physical* reality required to prove infringement. However, the Federal Circuit's decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2024-1039 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025), does not actually "flip-flop." Rather, it demonstrates that satisfying a claim construction requirement is merely the first step; the plaintiff must still present substantial evidence that the accused device performs **every** specific limitation of the claim, including quantitative steps that go beyond mere signal identification.

Here is how the court reconciles the doctrine with the facts:

### 1. Claim Construction vs. Fact Application
The distinction lies between defining the scope of a term and applying it to specific evidence.
*   **Claim Construction:** The district court, affirmed by the Federal Circuit, correctly construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean **"separately identifiable,"** explicitly rejecting a requirement that they be "unique" physical sources (*Opinion* at Section I.B). Legally, this means that if a system generates Signal A and then creates a digital copy of Signal A (Signal C), these can constitute two "separately identifiable" signals if they are processed as distinct entities within the algorithm (e.g., occupying different variables or memory registers).
*   **Application to Facts:** The reversal occurred not because the court changed its mind on what constitutes "three signals," but because Finesse failed to prove that AT&T's radios performed the **seven specific mathematical multiplications** required by Claim 1(d) of the '775 patent using those signals (*Opinion* at Section I.B).

### 2. The Fatal Flaw: Missing Evidence of Seven Multiplications
Under your hypothetical scenario (Input A, Input B, Copy C), you might successfully argue that three "separately identifiable" signals exist logically ($A$, $B$, and $Copy(A)$). However, Claim 1(d) of the '775 patent requires more than just having three inputs; it mandates a specific computational sequence: *"digitally multiplying... S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2... [and five others]"* totaling **seven distinct multiplication operations**.

The Federal Circuit reversed because Finesse could not bridge the gap between their theoretical interpretation and the actual operation of AT&T's hardware:
*   **Insufficient Mapping:** Finesse's expert, Dr. Wells, testified that AT&T's radios performed only **three** distinct multiplications based on Nokia documentation ($x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, etc.), rather than the seven required by the claim (*Opinion*).
*   **No Substantial Evidence:** The court noted that even if one assumes two unique physical inputs ($x_1$ and $x_2$) could satisfy the "three separately identifiable signals" limitation (by treating $x_2$ as appearing twice in different forms), there was absolutely no evidence showing that these were multiplied in all seven combinations required by the claim language (*Opinion*). The Nokia document showed modulus and complex conjugate operations that did not map cleanly to the plain text of Claim 1(d).
*   **Burden of Proof:** Because Finesse bore the burden of proving literal infringement by clear and convincing evidence (or preponderance depending on context, but here JMOL standards applied), their failure to explain how three distinct multiplications equated to seven claimed steps was fatal. As Chief Judge Moore wrote, "no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications" (*Opinion*).

### 3. Addressing Your Specific Scenario
In your software-defined radio example:
> *"If my code takes Input A and Input B, creates Copy C of Input A... are those considered three 'separately identifiable' signals?"*

**Yes.** Based on *Finesse*, if your code treats `A`, `B`, and `Copy_A` as separate variables passed into a function requiring three arguments (`func(S1=S_A, S2=S_B, S3=S_CopyA)`), they likely meet the legal standard for "three separately identifiable signals." They are distinguishable by their role in memory/logic even if their waveform content is identical at generation time.

**However,** this would only lead to infringement if your code subsequently executes **all seven** specific multiplication permutations listed in Claim 1(d) using those three variables. If your code only performs three multiplications (as AT&T allegedly did), you would fail to infringe regardless of whether you had one or three unique physical inputs.

### Conclusion
The court did not hold that two physical sources cannot equal three logical inputs; it held that **even assuming they do**, Finesse provided no evidence that AT&T performed the requisite seven mathematical operations derived from those inputs. The reversal was driven by a lack of substantial evidence regarding the *process steps*, not a retraction of the *claim construction*. To prevail in litigation involving such claims, one must prove both:
1.  That enough distinct logical channels exist to satisfy "S1, S2, S3"; AND
2.  That every single multiplication permutation mandated by the claim text is physically or digitally executed by the accused device.

### Did Finesse waive its argument about x1/x2 mapping by failing to object during trial?
Question:
Finesse argued on appeal that Dr. Wells was actually referring to x1 and x2 as his target signals all along, despite testifying he meant DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM paths earlier. They claimed he was laboring under a misimpression corrected later. The district court had accepted this narrative ('Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2'). Did Finesse preserve any objection when Dr. Wells first gave contradictory answers? If an attorney realizes mid-trial that their own expert has confused themselves into admitting non-infringement, what procedural mechanisms exist to fix it before closing arguments? Was there a missed opportunity here for Finesse to move for mistrial or request curative instructions instead of hoping an appellate court would accept their post-hoc reconstruction of intent?
Answer:
Based on the Federal Circuit's opinion in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, **yes, Finesse effectively waived its ability to rely on the "x1/x2" mapping theory to cure the insufficiency of its evidence.** While the term "waiver" technically refers to the intentional relinquishment of a known right, the practical outcome here is that Finesse forfeited the argument because it failed to preserve the issue through proper trial procedure and attempted to reconstruct its case post-hoc without evidentiary support.

**The Procedural Failure: Lack of Preservation**
Under Federal Circuit precedent, specifically regarding Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL), a party must present sufficient evidence at trial to survive a Rule 50(b) motion. The opinion highlights that Dr. Wells' testimony was "confusing and unclear." When confronted with the contradiction that the "modeled PIM path" could not be sampled before generation, Dr. Wells mentioned "x1 and x2" but explicitly **failed to map them** to the claim terms ("signals of interest" and "interference generating signals").

The opinion notes: *"Dr. Wells did not testify that x1 and x2 map onto the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals'... He continued to testify 'the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.'"* Because Finesse's counsel allowed this ambiguous testimony to stand without seeking clarification or correction during cross-examination or redirect examination, they failed to establish a factual record linking x1/x2 to the required claim elements. By resting their case on this contradictory testimony, they provided no substantial evidence for the jury's verdict.

**Missed Trial Mechanisms**
You correctly identified that several procedural mechanisms existed to address this mid-trial confusion, which were not utilized:
1.  **Redirect Examination:** Upon realizing Dr. Wells was conflating terms, Finesse's counsel had an obligation on redirect to force a clear statement mapping x1/x2 to the specific claim limitations before closing arguments.
2.  **Curative Instructions:** Had Finesse recognized the error immediately after Dr. Wells' pivot, they could have requested a limiting instruction from the judge clarifying that any mention of x1/x2 should not be taken as an admission regarding specific claim mappings unless explicitly stated otherwise.
3.  **Motion for Mistrial:** If the confusion rendered the expert's entire testimony unreliable regarding infringement, a motion for mistrial might have been appropriate, though less likely granted than a curative measure.

Instead of taking these steps, Finesse chose to gamble on an appellate reconstruction of intent years later.

**The Appellate Rejection: Substantial Evidence Standard**
The core reason Finesse lost on this point is that appellate courts do not rewrite trial records or infer missing links in logic that were never articulated by witnesses or counsel at trial. The Federal Circuit applied the **substantial evidence standard**, stating: *"When the party with the burden of proof... rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient."*

The court explicitly rejected Finesse's attempt to piece together testimony from "ninety pages apart in the record" (*J.A.* 30) to construct a coherent theory that Dr. Wells meant something other than what he said at every turn when asked directly. The court noted that even if such an inference were possible, it was contradicted by Dr. Wells' repeated explicit statements identifying DL(TX) and modeled PIM paths as his targets. As established in *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, contradictory expert testimony cannot support a jury verdict when no reasonable juror could resolve it in favor of infringement based solely on speculation about what was *meant* rather than what was *said*.

**Conclusion**
Finesse did not strictly "waive" an argument by failing to object (as there was no adverse ruling requiring an objection), but they **failed to carry their burden of production**. By allowing their expert's contradictory testimony to go uncorrected and failing to elicit clear mapping during trial, they left no substantial evidence for a jury verdict. Consequently, their post-hoc attempt on appeal to reinterpret Dr. Wells' confusing remarks as a latent correct theory was properly rejected as insufficient grounds for affirming liability under Rule 50 standards.
