Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Can an automated email trigger a statutory 'receipt' requirement for policy cancellation before any human agent at the insurer sees the document?
Question:
The core factual dispute here is timing: GoAuto's computer system receives an API call/email from APAC and immediately cancels the policy, while a human employee doesn't physically handle or sign the paperwork until hours later. The court ruled that an automated web service interaction counts as 'receipt' under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Does this interpretation align with traditional agency principles where 'receipt' implies actual knowledge by an authorized representative? Is it legally sound to say an insurer has 'taken possession' of a legal notice when only its server acknowledged it, especially given the strict liability nature of these statutes?
Answer:
Yes, under the holding in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, an automated system acknowledgment constitutes valid "receipt" under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), even before a human agent reviews the document. The Fifth Circuit explicitly rejected the argument that "receipt" requires actual human knowledge or physical handling by an authorized representative prior to cancellation.

**The Legal Standard: Possession vs. Human Awareness**
The court grounded its analysis in the statutory text and standard definitions rather than traditional agency principles requiring subjective awareness. Relying on Black's Law Dictionary, the court defined "receipt" as "taking physical possession" or coming into "possession... from some outside source." Crucially, the court interpreted this definition functionally within a modern commercial context: **possession is achieved when the data enters the insurer's controlled system.**

Judge Southwick reasoned that because the statute is a "modern statute set in current times," corporate computers perform functions previously done by individuals. When APAC's system made an API call to GoAuto's "listener" web service, and GoAuto's system accepted the call and executed the cancellation logic, GoAuto had technically taken "custody" of the notice. The court stated plainly: *"Once the e-mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto's computer system, that computer system cancels the policy... GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."*

**Rejection of Traditional Agency Arguments**
You asked if this aligns with traditional agency principles where receipt implies actual knowledge by an authorized representative. *Williams* suggests that while those principles govern common-law agency, they yield to specific statutory construction regarding automated business processes in this context. The plaintiffs argued that without a human signing or seeing the document first, there was no true receipt. The court dismissed this by noting:
1.  **Statutory Silence:** The statute does not explicitly require a signature or human review before cancellation occurs; it only requires "receipt" of the notice and statement.
2.  **Corporate Identity:** The statute mandates certification by "the insurance premium finance company," not necessarily a specific named employee at the moment of transmission (*Benitez v. Elsayed*). Similarly, receipt is attributed to the entity (GoAuto), which acts through its systems just as it once acted through filing clerks.
3.  **Strict Adherence Limits:** While Louisiana law demands "strict adherence" to cancellation statutes (*Delatte v. Lemotte*), this prevents adding extra-judicial hurdles (like requiring manual signatures) rather than preventing technological efficiency. As Judge Southwick noted, strict adherence is *"not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures."*

**Conclusion on Strict Liability**
Regarding your concern about strict liability: The court affirmed that strict adherence serves two purposes—fixing an exact time/date of cancellation and providing a cure period (*Britten v. Reavis*). By accepting automated receipt as valid, the court actually reinforces these goals by creating a precise, timestamped digital record of when possession transferred, eliminating ambiguity about when a human might have eventually seen a paper file hours later. Therefore, under *Williams*, it is legally sound to say an insurer has "taken possession" when its server acknowledges an API call, regardless of subsequent human involvement in signing or filing paperwork.
