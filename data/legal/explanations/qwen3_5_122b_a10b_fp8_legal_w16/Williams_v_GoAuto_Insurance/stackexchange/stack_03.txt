Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Is a corporate entity capable of 'certifying' facts under La. R.S. 9:3550 without an individual employee signing or identifying themselves on the notice?
Question:
The Plaintiffs argued that because no specific employee signed or was named on APAC's cancellation notice, there was no valid 'certification' required by the statute. The court rejected this, noting that the statute says 'the insurance premium finance company' certifies, not an individual. Given that *Benitez* suggested missing signatures could be fatal if other elements weren't met, how does this decision reconcile with general evidence rules requiring authentication? Doesn't allowing unsigned, anonymous corporate certifications undermine the purpose of 'strict adherence,' which is meant to ensure clarity and accountability in cancellation dates?
Answer:
Based on the Fifth Circuit's opinion in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, a corporate entity **is** capable of "certifying" facts under La. R.S. 9:3550 without an individual employee signing or explicitly identifying themselves on the initial notice.

The court reconciles this conclusion with the doctrine of "strict adherence" and prior case law like *Benitez* through three primary lines of reasoning:

**1. Textual Interpretation of the Statute**
The court emphasized that the plain language of La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a) assigns the duty of certification to **"the insurance premium finance company,"** not an individual agent or officer. Because the statute does not explicitly mandate a signature or a named preparer, imposing such a requirement would constitute adding to the statutory procedure rather than adhering strictly to it. As Judge Southwick noted, "Strict adherence... is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures."

**2. Distinction from *Benitez***
While *Benitez v. Elsayed* invalidated a cancellation partly due to a lack of signature, the Fifth Circuit distinguished that case factually and legally. In *Benitez*, the notice failed to identify any employee, lacked a signature, and crucially, **did not purport to "certify"** any statements at all. In contrast, the notices in *Williams* explicitly stated: "**APAC certifies that...**" followed by the four statutory elements required by § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The court reasoned that because the corporate entity itself made the certification statement, the absence of a human signature did not render the certification invalid under this specific statute.

**3. Corporate Authentication vs. Individual Liability**
Regarding your concern about undermining accountability and evidence rules, the court viewed the corporation as a distinct legal actor capable of making binding representations through its automated systems and authorized processes (such as computer-generated emails on letterhead). The purpose of strict adherence—to fix an exact time of cancellation and provide cure periods—was served by GoAuto's automated system receiving and acting upon APAC's certified email immediately upon transmission. The subsequent physical stamping by an employee occurred only *after* cancellation had already been effected electronically, reinforcing that the corporate act (the email certification) was sufficient to trigger statutory rights and duties before individual manual intervention occurred.

In short, under *Williams*, "certification" under § 9:3550 is an act performed by the corporate entity itself via its authorized channels; unless Louisiana law specifically demands an individual's attestation for every corporate communication (which it does not here), anonymity regarding the specific employee preparing the document does not invalidate the notice so long as the entity clearly asserts compliance with all statutory prerequisites.
