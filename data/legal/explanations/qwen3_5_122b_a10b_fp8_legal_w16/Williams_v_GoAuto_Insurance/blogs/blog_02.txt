Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

## The Signature Dilemma: Can a Corporation Certify Without a Human Hand?

In the high-stakes world of insurance administration, precision is usually paramount. Nowhere is this more true than in Louisiana, where the legislature has mandated "strict adherence" to statutory procedures for cancelling financed auto insurance policies. Yet, in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, decided just last week by the Fifth Circuit, the line between rigorous statutory compliance and bureaucratic formalism blurred significantly. The case presents a deceptively simple question that strikes at the heart of modern corporate governance: Does a corporation "certify" a fact only when a human hand signs a document before the deadline, or can the entity itself speak through automated systems? The Fifth Circuit's answer—a resounding yes to the latter—suggests that while Louisiana demands strictness, it does not demand obsolescence.

The factual backdrop of *Williams* involves a classic friction point in consumer protection: the cancellation of an insurance policy due to nonpayment of premiums facilitated by a third-party finance company. Here, American Premium Assistance Company (APAC) paid GoAuto Insurance directly on behalf of insureds like Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, collecting monthly installments instead. When payments stalled, APAC exercised its power of attorney to trigger cancellation. Under Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3), APAC was required to send GoAuto a notice containing four specific certifications regarding the validity of the finance agreement and the status of the default. The crux of the dispute lay in timing and formality. APAC's system automatically emailed GoAuto's computer servers with these certifications early in the morning, prompting an immediate automatic cancellation by GoAuto's software. It was only later that morning that an APAC employee affixed a signature stamp to hard copies of those same documents.

The plaintiffs argued that this sequence violated Louisiana law. Their logic was rooted in a traditional understanding of certification: for a statement to be certified, it must bear the signature of an authorized agent *before* it becomes effective. By their account, allowing an algorithm to cancel coverage based on unsigned digital data created a dangerous gap where policies could be terminated without genuine human oversight or verification at the critical moment. They pointed to state precedent emphasizing that any defect in this process renders cancellation ineffective, arguing that a missing pre-cancellation signature was precisely such a defect.

Judge Southwick's opinion for the Fifth Circuit rejects this formalistic reading, offering a pragmatic interpretation of corporate agency in the digital age. The court began by noting that while Louisiana courts insist on strict adherence to cancellation statutes, they do not invite judges to rewrite them or insert requirements that do not exist on paper. Crucially, Judge Southwick observed that neither La. R.S. § 9:3550 nor controlling state case law explicitly mandates a handwritten or even digitally signed signature prior to transmission. Instead, the statute speaks of "the insurance premium finance company" certifying information. In doing so, he reasoned that when Congress—or in this case, the state legislature—assigns an act to an entity rather than an individual officer, it acknowledges that corporations act through mechanisms other than pen-on-paper signatures every single time they operate.

The court drew a sharp distinction between *Benitez v. Elsayed*, another Louisiana appellate decision cited by plaintiffs which criticized notices lacking identification or signatures, and the facts at bar. In *Benitez*, the notice failed entirely to identify who prepared it or purport to certify anything; here, APAC's automated email explicitly stated it was certifying compliance with all four statutory prongs. The absence of a named individual did not negate the certification because the statute empowers the *company* to act as the certifier. As Judge Southwick noted, accepting plaintiffs' argument would essentially allow courts to add procedural hurdles—like mandatory pre-signatures—that legislators deliberately omitted when drafting laws designed for efficiency alongside protectionism.

Furthermore, the court tackled the issue of "receipt." Plaintiffs argued that GoAuto never truly "received" the request because no human employee opened an envelope or read an email before their computer system acted on it; they contended "receipt" implies physical possession by a person first rejecting this notion as incompatible with modern commerce accepted definitions from Black's Law Dictionary confirmed that taking possession includes electronic delivery via web services where one system calls another ("listens") and executes instructions immediately.
