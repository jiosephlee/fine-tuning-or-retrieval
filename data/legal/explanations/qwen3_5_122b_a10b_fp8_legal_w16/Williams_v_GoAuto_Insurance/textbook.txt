Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Factual Context and Procedural History

## The Tripartite Relationship: Insureds, GoAuto, and APAC
To understand the dispute in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, one must first grasp the unique three-party structure at play. The plaintiffs, Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, along with a third insured involved in a separate accident (Nicholas Jenkins), held auto insurance policies issued by GoAuto Insurance Company. However, these insureds did not pay their premiums directly to GoAuto on a monthly basis. Instead, they entered into financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC ("APAC"). In this arrangement, APAC acted as an intermediary that paid the entire annual premium upfront to GoAuto on behalf of the insureds. In exchange, the insureds agreed to repay APAC through monthly installment payments. This created a tripartite relationship where GoAuto remained the insurer of record, APAC functioned as the premium finance company extending credit to cover the cost of coverage, and the plaintiffs served as borrowers obligated to reimburse APAC.

## Mechanics of Premium Financing and Power of Attorney Provisions
The critical legal mechanism enabling this financing model was the inclusion of specific provisions within each financing agreement between the insureds and APAC. These agreements explicitly granted APAC a "power of attorney." This legal instrument authorized APAC to act on behalf of the insureds to cancel their insurance policies if they failed to meet their financial obligations under the loan agreement. Specifically, if an insured defaulted by failing to make timely installment payments to APAC, this power of attorney empowered APAC to instruct GoAuto to terminate the underlying insurance policy immediately. This provision is central to Louisiana's statutory framework for premium finance companies, allowing them to mitigate their own financial risk by ensuring that collateral (the insurance policy) is withdrawn when payments cease.

## The Automatic Cancellation Process Triggered by Non-Payment
When an insured failed to submit a payment by its due date, a specific automated sequence was triggered under the contracts between APAC and its clients. First, APAC would send a notice to the insured warning that their policy would be cancelled unless payment was made within ten days. If no payment was received by midnight on that tenth day, the process moved from manual notification to automated execution. At that moment, APAC's computer system automatically generated an email containing two key components: a copy of the "Ten Day Notice of Cancellation" sent to the insured and a formal request for cancellation directed at GoAuto. This email included certifications stating that a valid power of attorney existed, that the agreement was in default without cure, and that notices had been sent to all interested parties including government agencies or lienholders. Upon receipt of this digital communication via email or web service interface during early morning hours, GoAuto's internal computer systems were programmed to automatically effectuate the cancellation of the policy without human intervention at that precise moment. Only later in the morning would an APAC employee physically sign or stamp printed copies of these documents for administrative records.

## Litigation Timeline: From State Court Class Action to Federal Removal
Following multiple accidents involving vehicles whose policies had been cancelled due to non-payment—most notably an accident involving plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins—the affected former insureds initiated legal proceedings in Louisiana state court. Williams and Jenkins originally filed a class action lawsuit naming both GoAuto and APAC as defendants. Their core allegations were twofold: first, that GoAuto had not effectively cancelled their policies because its procedures violated Louisiana statutes; and second, that GoAuto had failed to act in good faith and fair dealing throughout this process. Felita Wright joined as an additional plaintiff when an amended complaint was filed shortly thereafter rather than initially joining suit alongside Williams and Jenkins seeking damages for injuries sustained while uninsured due to these alleged improper cancellations. # Governing Legal Framework: Louisiana Statutory Requirements

## Overview of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3): Cancellation by Finance Companies

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the central legal dispute turns on the interpretation of a specific Louisiana statute designed to regulate the intersection between insurance carriers and premium finance companies. When an insured party finances their insurance premiums through a third-party lender rather than paying the insurer directly, the cancellation process becomes more complex because the lender holds a financial stake in keeping the policy active until paid off, while simultaneously possessing the power to terminate coverage if payments cease. To manage this relationship, Louisiana enacted La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3), which establishes a precise mechanism for how a premium finance company may request the cancellation of an insurance policy due to nonpayment.

Under subsection (G)(3)(a), a premium finance company may exercise its power of attorney to cancel a policy only by transmitting a formal request to the insurer. This transmission can occur via mail, private carrier, or electronic mail. Crucially, this transmission must include two distinct components: a copy of the notice of cancellation sent to the insured and a written statement "certifying" four specific factual conditions regarding the default and notification process. These certifications serve as the bridge between the finance company's internal records and the insurer's obligation to act; they confirm that the underlying financing agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the agreement is currently in default without cure, that proper notice was sent to the insured specifying dates, and that copies of said notice were distributed to all interested third parties, such as lienholders or government agencies. In *Williams*, GoAuto relied entirely on this statutory framework to defend its automated cancellation procedures initiated by its partner, American Premium Assistance Company (APAC).

## The Doctrine of 'Strict Adherence' in Louisiana Insurance Law

To understand why *Williams* required such granular analysis of APAC's email timing and signature stamps, one must grasp the doctrine of "strict adherence" that governs Louisiana insurance cancellations involving premium finance agreements. The Fifth Circuit explicitly noted that Louisiana courts demand strict compliance with these statutory requirements when a finance company attempts to exercise its power of attorney. This is not merely a suggestion for best practices; it is a mandatory standard where "any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy."

The rationale for this rigidity stems from the high stakes involved in insurance coverage and property rights. Because cancellation terminates protection immediately upon nonpayment, potentially leaving an insured vulnerable at critical moments, the legislature intended for there to be zero ambiguity regarding when and how that termination occurs. Consequently, courts refuse to infer compliance or excuse minor deviations from the text of § 9:3550(G)(3). In *Williams*, this doctrine framed every argument made by both sides: if APAC's automated email lacked a specific element mandated by statute—such as a human signature before processing—the entire cancellation could be deemed void ab initio under Louisiana law. The court emphasized that "strict adherence" prevents insurers and finance companies from creating their own informal shortcuts; they must follow the legislative script exactly as written.

## Statutory Elements Required for Valid Certification

The core analytical task in *Williams* was determining whether APAC's electronic communications satisfied the four certification elements listed in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's procedures failed because APAC's computer system automatically triggered cancellation based on an unsigned email certification, which they claimed did not constitute valid "certification" under state law. Specifically, they contended that without a human signature applied prior to or simultaneous with the electronic transmission, there was no valid statement certifying compliance with subsection (i) through (iv).

The court analyzed these elements by looking closely at the statutory language rather than importing external requirements not found in text. While one lower court decision (*Benitez*) had criticized a notice for lacking an employee identification or signature alongside other defects, no binding precedent established that a signature was an absolute prerequisite for certification under § 9:3550(G)(3). The Fifth Circuit reasoned that since the statute assigns responsibility to "the insurance premium finance company" generally—not specifically an individual officer—a corporate entity can satisfy its duty to certify through its authorized systems without necessarily attaching an individual's handwritten or digital signature at every step. Therefore, for purposes of this opinion, APAC's email containing explicit assertions that it possessed valid powers of attorney and had notified all required parties constituted sufficient "certification," even though it arrived via automated means before an employee manually stamped paper documents later in the day.

## Purpose of Strict Compliance: Fixing Time and Allowing Cure Periods

Why does Louisiana insist on such rigid procedural hurdles? The court clarified that strict adherence serves two primary functional goals essential to public policy: first, it provides a clear and indisputable method for fixing the exact time and date of cancellation; second, it guarantees a minimum period during which an insured may cure their default before losing coverage permanently. By mandating specific steps—sending notice to the insured first, then notifying interested parties via certified channels—the statute ensures there is no confusion over when coverage ends and allows borrowers time to rectify missed payments before facing total loss protection status changes or vehicle repossession triggers tied to lapsed insurance.

In *Williams*, understanding these purposes helps explain why GoAuto's automated timeline mattered so much legally versus practically. Even though APAC sent notices ten days prior and emails were processed instantly upon receipt by GoAuto's servers, any gap between these events had to align perfectly with statutory mandates regarding when notice was deemed "sent" versus when "cancellation" occurred relative to those notices. If strict adherence were relaxed—for example, allowing cancellations based on incomplete certifications—it would undermine these protections by obscuring exactly when coverage ceased or eliminating opportunities for cure periods guaranteed by law. Thus, every detail in APAC's workflow was scrutinized against these twin objectives of temporal clarity and borrower protection.

# Analysis I: The Requirement of Physical Signature vs. Corporate Certification

## Plaintiffs' Argument: Necessity of Pre-Cancellation Human Signature

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the central dispute regarding the validity of the policy cancellations hinged on the timing and nature of the authentication required by Louisiana law. The Plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's automated cancellation procedures violated the strict adherence standard mandated by La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). Their primary contention focused on the specific moment when the premium finance company, APAC, "certified" the default to the insurer. Under GoAuto's system, when an insured failed to cure a default within ten days, APAC's computer system automatically generated and transmitted an email containing a notice of cancellation and a statement certifying four statutory conditions to GoAuto's web service. Crucially, at the precise moment this electronic transmission occurred and triggered GoAuto's automatic policy termination, no human had signed the document. An APAC employee applied a signature stamp to printed copies of these documents only later in the morning, well after the computer systems had already executed the cancellation based on the unsigned digital message. The Plaintiffs interpreted this sequence as fatal to the cancellation's validity, arguing that a human signature must precede or accompany the act of certification to satisfy the statutory requirement that a valid notice be "sent" before an insurer may proceed to cancel. They posited that without a pre-cancellation signature identifying a specific individual responsible for the certification, the statutory safeguard was bypassed, rendering the cancellation ineffective as a matter of law.

## Statutory Interpretation of 'Certifying': Textual Analysis Absence of Signature Mandate

To resolve whether a human signature is legally requisite before automation can trigger cancellation, Circuit Judge Southwick engaged in a textual analysis of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). The court noted that while Louisiana jurisprudence demands "strict adherence" to statutory cancellation procedures—ensuring clarity on timing and providing insureds an opportunity to cure defaults—the statute itself does not explicitly mandate a handwritten or stamped signature on the notice sent by the finance company. The operative language requires that a premium finance company send "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" specific facts regarding default and notice delivery. The court reasoned that if the legislature intended to require a physical signature as a condition precedent to cancellation, it could have easily included such language in § 9:3550(G)(3). Instead, by using only the verb "certifying," Congress left open whether such certification could be performed by corporate entity actions rather than individual human acts alone. The Fifth Circuit emphasized that "strict adherence" serves as a guardrail against arbitrary deviations from established procedures but is not an invitation for courts to insert additional requirements into clear statutory text that are not expressly demanded by its words. Consequently, because the statute lacks an explicit signature mandate, imposing one would exceed judicial authority and disrupt legislative intent regarding efficient administrative processes in insurance financing.

## Review of Lower Court Opinions Regarding Signatures (*Benitez* Distinction)

The Fifth Circuit scrutinized existing Louisiana appellate case law to determine if judicial precedent had implicitly filled this gap in statutory text by requiring signatures. While several cases reiterated that defects in the cancellation process render cancellations ineffective, none squarely addressed whether an unsigned notice invalidates a transaction where all other substantive elements are met. In analyzing *Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So. 3d 572 (La. App. Cir.), which involved similar issues, Judge Southwick drew a critical distinction between *Benitez* and *Williams*. In *Benitez*, the court criticized a notice for failing to identify any employee who prepared it, lacking both a printed name and a signature, and crucially noting it did not even purport to "certify" any statements contained within it. By contrast, in *Williams*, every notice sent by APAC explicitly stated that it was certifying compliance with all four statutory subsections; it merely lacked an attached individual name or signature at the moment of transmission. The court concluded that *Benitez* did not establish per se rule that an absence of a signature automatically disqualifies a cancellation if other elements are present and if those elements are clearly certified by corporate authority rather than silence or ambiguity regarding responsibility for accuracy. Thus, previous case law provided no binding support for striking down GoAuto's procedure solely due to post-transmission signing practices when electronic certification was otherwise complete and accurate at inception.
