Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The Tripartite Relationship: Insureds, GoAuto, and APAC
To understand the core dispute in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, one must first grasp the complex contractual web connecting three distinct entities: the policyholder (insured), the insurance carrier (GoAuto), and the premium finance company (APAC). In a standard insurance transaction, the insured pays the insurer directly. However, in this case, the plaintiffs—Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright—along with a non-party insured involved in an accident with Nicholas Jenkins, utilized a financing arrangement to pay their premiums. Instead of paying GoAuto directly, they entered into agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC ("APAC"). Under this structure, APAC paid the full premium amount to GoAuto upfront on behalf of the insureds. In exchange, the insureds made monthly installment payments to APAC. This tripartite relationship created a dependency where the continuity of coverage relied not just on the insured's ability to pay APAC, but on APAC's willingness and ability to maintain payment to GoAuto. When an insured defaulted on their obligation to APAC, it triggered a chain reaction affecting their standing with GoAuto, setting the stage for a dispute over who controlled the timing and mechanics of policy termination.

## The Financing Mechanism and Power of Attorney Provisions
The critical legal mechanism enabling this financing model was a "power of attorney" embedded within each financing agreement between the insureds and APAC. These provisions explicitly authorized APAC to act as an agent for the insured specifically regarding policy cancellation in events of financial default. Consequently, if an insured failed to make timely payments to APAC, APAC possessed the unilateral authority to request that GoAuto terminate the insurance policy immediately. This power was not merely administrative; it was a substantive right derived from contract that bypassed direct communication between GoAuto and the insured at the moment of cancellation initiation. The statute governing these transactions required strict adherence to specific notification protocols when such a power was exercised. Specifically, Louisiana law mandated that before an insurer could cancel a policy based on nonpayment by a finance company, that finance company had to send a certified notice containing four specific declarations: that a valid power of attorney existed, that default occurred and remained uncured, that notice was sent to the insured specifying dates, and that copies were sent to interested third parties like lienholders. The plaintiffs' subsequent claims hinged entirely on whether APAC's automated processes met these rigorous statutory requirements before GoAuto actually pulled the plug on coverage.

## The Accident Trigger and Initiation of Class Action Litigation
The abstract legal conflict transformed into concrete litigation following a traffic accident involving Nicholas Jenkins. Jenkins was injured in a collision with another driver who held a financed GoAuto policy through APAC—a driver who was not named as a plaintiff in this suit but whose status as an uninsured motorist at the time of impact precipitated the legal action. Following this incident, Jenkins joined forces with Williams and Wright to file a class-action lawsuit against both GoAuto Insurance Company and APAC in Louisiana state court. Their central allegation was twofold: first, that GoAuto had failed to follow effective statutory procedures for cancelling their policies due to nonpayment; and second, that by allegedly failing those procedures while simultaneously claiming they were cancelled, GoAuto had engaged in misrepresentation and breached duties of good faith and fair dealing. Essentially, they argued that because procedural defects might have rendered their cancellations invalid under Louisiana law technically speaking their policies should still have been active coverage at various points or that the process surrounding their termination violated statutory mandates designed to protect consumers from abrupt loss of protection.

## Removal to Federal Court and Summary Judgment Motions
After filing in state court, GoAuto invoked its right under federal diversity jurisdiction statutes to remove the case from Louisiana state court to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Once in federal court, both sides recognized that resolving any factual disputes regarding damages or liability would be premature without first settling a threshold question of law: Did GoAuto's internal procedures for processing cancellation requests satisfy Louisiana's statutory framework? Accordingly, neither side pursued extensive discovery regarding damages or bad faith conduct initially; instead, they focused exclusively on motions for summary judgment concerning statutory compliance. # Governing Legal Framework: Louisiana's Strict Adherence Standard

## Overview of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the central legal dispute turns on the interpretation of a specific Louisiana statute designed to regulate how insurance policies are cancelled when premiums are paid through a financing arrangement. When an insured borrows money to pay an insurance premium, they enter into a "premium finance agreement" with a third-party lender rather than paying the insurer directly. These agreements typically grant the lender a power of attorney to cancel the policy if the borrower defaults on payments. However, Louisiana law recognizes that such cancellations carry significant consequences for the insured, potentially leaving them without coverage at critical moments. Consequently, the legislature enacted La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3) to impose a rigorous framework on this process.

Under subsection (G)(3)(a), a premium finance company may request cancellation of a policy only after sending specific documentation to the insurer. The statute mandates that this request must include a copy of the notice of cancellation and a formal statement "certifying" four distinct facts. First, the finance company must certify that the underlying premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney authorizing such action. Second, it must certify that the agreement is indeed in default and that this default has not been cured within any applicable timeframe. Third, it must confirm that a notice of cancellation was previously sent to the insured as required by law, including the specific date such notice was dispatched. Finally, the certificate must verify that copies of this notice were sent to all other interested parties listed in the agreement, such as lienholders or government agencies, providing their names and addresses as appropriate. This statutory scheme establishes a multi-step verification process intended to ensure that cancellations are not arbitrary but are grounded in documented financial delinquency and proper notification history.

## The Doctrine of Strict Adherence in Cancellation Procedures

While many areas of contract law allow for substantial performance or cure minor technical defects, Louisiana courts apply a far more demanding standard when reviewing insurance cancellations initiated by premium finance companies under § 9:3550(G)(3). As articulated by the Fifth Circuit in *Williams*, relying on precedent from Louisiana appellate courts such as *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, this standard is known as "strict adherence." The doctrine dictates that any deviation from the statutory requirements renders the cancellation ineffective, regardless of whether the insured actually received notice or whether they were truly in default in substance rather than form.

The rationale behind this rigid approach is rooted in public policy concerns regarding consumer protection and legal certainty within the insurance market. Because cancellation strips an individual of their coverage, often exposing them to immense liability if an accident occurs immediately thereafter, Louisiana jurisprudence treats compliance with § 9:3550(G)(3) as jurisdictional in nature regarding the validity of the termination act itself. In *Williams*, the plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's automated procedures failed to meet this high bar due to timing discrepancies regarding signatures and physical receipt of documents. The court emphasized that "strict adherence" is not merely a guideline but a mandatory rule; if even one element of the statutory checklist is missing or improperly executed, the attempted cancellation is void ab initio. This means that unless every step outlined in paragraph (G)(3) is followed precisely as written by both state statutes and judicial interpretations thereof, no valid cancellation has occurred legally speaking, regardless of how reasonable or efficient those steps might appear operationally.

## Statutory Purpose: Fixing Cancellation Time and Allowing Cure Periods

Understanding why Louisiana imposes such strictures requires examining the dual legislative purposes served by § 9:3550(G)(3). According to citations provided in *Williams* referencing cases like *Britten v. Reavis*, these rigorous requirements serve two primary functions essential to maintaining stability in insurance relationships involving financing arrangements. First, strict adherence provides an unequivocal method for fixing the exact time and date when coverage terminates. # Legal Issue I: The Requirement of Physical Signatures on Notices

## Plaintiffs' Argument: Signature as a Prerequisite for Valid Certification

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the central dispute regarding the validity of the policy cancellations hinged on the mechanics of the "certification" required by Louisiana law. The Plaintiffs, former insureds whose policies were cancelled due to nonpayment, argued that GoAuto's automated cancellation process failed to satisfy the statutory mandate. Their specific contention focused on the timing and nature of the signature on the notice sent by American Premium Assistance Company ("APAC"), the premium finance company. Under APAC's procedure, when an insured defaulted, APAC's computer system automatically emailed a notice of cancellation and a certification statement to GoAuto's computer system, triggering an immediate automatic cancellation of the policy. Crucially, no human signature appeared on these documents at the moment of transmission; instead, an APAC employee applied a signature stamp to printed copies later in the morning, after the policy had already been cancelled.

The Plaintiffs interpreted this sequence as fatal to the cancellation's validity. They argued that for a premium finance company to legally "certify" compliance with the statute—specifically regarding the existence of a valid power of attorney, the status of default, and prior notice to insureds—a human agent must sign or authenticate that certification *before* it is transmitted to the insurer. In their view, allowing an unsigned, machine-generated email to trigger a cancellation deprived them of statutory protections designed to ensure that cancellations were deliberate and verified actions taken by authorized personnel. Consequently, they urged the Fifth Circuit to find that GoAuto's reliance on an unsigned electronic message constituted a failure to strictly adhere to Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3)(a).

## Statutory Text Analysis: Does 'Certifying' Require a Signature?

To resolve this dispute, Circuit Judge Southwick began with a textual analysis of Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The statute explicitly mandates that a premium finance company effectuate cancellation "by sending... a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four specific factual predicates. However, as Judge Southwick noted, the text contains no explicit reference to a signature. The legislative drafters chose the verb "certifying" rather than phrases like "signed by" or "executed under oath." The court reasoned that if Congress or the Louisiana Legislature intended for a physical or digital signature from a named individual to be a prerequisite for validity, they would have included such language in the statute itself.

The court emphasized that statutory interpretation generally precludes judges from reading requirements into laws that are not expressly written there. While Plaintiffs argued that "certifying" inherently implies personal authentication via signature, the Fifth Circuit rejected this implication in favor of plain meaning. The statute assigns this duty to "the insurance premium finance company" as an entity. Therefore, when APAC sends its automated email containing statements asserting its compliance with statutory conditions (e.g., confirming default and prior notice), it is acting as the corporate entity required by law. The court concluded that without explicit statutory direction demanding a signature at every step of communication between entities, imposing such a rigid requirement would exceed judicial authority and rewrite the legislature's intent for insurance cancellations in an increasingly digital era.

## Review of Louisiana Case Law on Unsigned Notices (Benitez v. Elsayed et al.)

Since Louisiana state statutes did not provide definitive clarity on whether "certifying" necessitates a signature, Judge Southwick examined existing Louisiana appellate case law for guidance. The review revealed a distinct lack of direct precedent addressing this specific procedural nuance in recent years. Most prior cases merely paraphrased the statutory requirement as needing "a statement certifying compliance," without elaborating on authentication methods (*KMJ Servs., Inc.*; *Hodges*). Similarly, other opinions simply stated that cancellation proceeds "[u]pon receipt of such notice," leaving open whether those notices required signatures (*Eaglin*; *Lewis*).

The Plaintiffs relied heavily on *Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So. 3d 572 (La. App. Cir.), where one court noted deficiencies in a notice because it lacked identification of any specific employee preparing it and was not signed by any individual who purportedly certified its contents. However, Judge Southwick carefully distinguished *Benitez* from the facts before him in *Williams*. In *Benitez*, while noting these defects alongside others, the court did not hold that an absence of signature alone invalidated a cancellation if all other substantive elements were met; rather, it listed multiple failures in documentation quality and content specificity (*Benitez* at 579). By contrast, in *Williams*, although APAC's emails did not identify specific employees or bear immediate signatures like those criticized in *Benitez*, they unequivocally contained statements explicitly certifying all four statutory requirements mandated by § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Because no binding Louisiana precedent established that an unsigned corporate certification is per se invalid when otherwise compliant with statutory substance, there was insufficient authority to support overturning GoAuto's procedure based solely on missing signatures at transmission time.

## 'Strict Adherence' vs. Judicial Addition of Requirements

A critical component of Pennsylvania jurisprudence cited throughout these discussions—and adopted here—is that courts must exercise "strict adherence" when reviewing insurance-cancellation procedures involving premium finance agreements (*Delatte v. Lemotte*; *Britten v. Reavis*). Strict adherence ensures certainty regarding exactly when coverage terminates and guarantees debtors adequate opportunity to cure defaults before losing protection against liability claims (*Britten* at 1154). However, Judge Southwick clarified that "strict adherence" means faithfully following what is actually written in the statute; it does not grant courts license to invent new procedural hurdles or add requirements that legislators omitted from enactment history or text (*Britten* at 1154). 

If courts were permitted to imply unstated prerequisites—such as mandating manual signatures before electronic transmissions—they would undermine legislative flexibility and potentially disrupt modern business practices without clear justification rooted in statutory purpose or harm prevention principles underlying consumer protection laws themselves ("strict adherence"). Instead serves dual purposes: fixing precise timing dates plus providing minimum curing periods—not creating arbitrary barriers preventing efficient execution among sophisticated commercial actors # Legal Issue II: Defining 'Receipt' in an Automated Digital Environment

## Receipt vs. Delivery: Statutory Interpretation Challenges

The core of the dispute in *Williams v. GoAuto* regarding the timing of cancellation centers on the statutory requirement that an insurer may proceed to cancel a policy only "upon receipt" of a notice and certification from a premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). In this case, the plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's automated processes violated the statute because the insurance company's computer system processed the cancellation request and terminated coverage before any human employee at GoAuto physically took possession of a document or verified the request manually. The plaintiffs contended that "receipt" implies a deliberate act of taking physical possession by a person, suggesting that the statutory language required a human hand to receive the notification before the legal effect of cancellation could trigger. This argument highlights a classic tension in statutory interpretation when applying older legislative frameworks to modern technological realities: does the legislature's use of traditional terminology like "receipt" constrain insurers to pre-digital methods of communication, or does it encompass electronic transmission? The Fifth Circuit had to determine whether the statutory mandate for "receipt" was satisfied when a server accepted data, or if it demanded physical interaction by an employee.

## Applying Black's Law Dictionary Definitions to Electronic Data Transfer

To resolve this ambiguity, the court turned to standard legal definitions, specifically consulting *Black's Law Dictionary* (12th ed.), as neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor previous appellate decisions had explicitly defined "receipt" within this specific statutory context. The dictionary defines "receipt" as "[t]he act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession," and "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." The court reasoned that while these definitions mention physical possession, they fundamentally focus on the transfer of control or custody from one entity to another rather than exclusively on biological humanity performing the act. By accepting these definitions, the court concluded that "receipt" occurs once an entity—whether human or corporate—takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request. Consequently, when GoAuto's computer system successfully ingested the email containing the cancellation notice and certification from APAC (American Premium Assistance Company), GoAuto as an organization had technically taken "possession" of that information from an outside source. The court rejected the plaintiffs' narrow view that only a human handshake could constitute receipt, noting that statutes enacted in modern times must account for contemporary business practices where computers routinely perform tasks previously done by people.

## The Functionality of Web Services as Agents for Receipt

A critical component of GoAuto's defense was explaining how its automated system functioned as an effective agent for receiving communications. The record revealed that APAC's computer system made a direct call to a GoAuto web service—a program designed essentially as a "listener"—to request cancellation. This web service was programmed to constantly monitor for incoming calls; upon recognizing its own name and understanding the parameters of the request (specifically that it pertained to a policy scheduled for cancellation), it immediately performed the activity: canceling the policy. No human intervention occurred at this stage; no employee opened an email inbox or reviewed a file folder until hours later in the morning. The Fifth Circuit treated this web service not merely as passive storage but as an active functional equivalent of an employee receiving mail. Just as an employee might open an envelope and place it on their desk upon arrival, GoAuto's web service accepted and processed the data packet representing the cancellation notice. Therefore, at the moment the server acknowledged and acted upon APAC's digital signal, GoAuto had achieved "receipt" under the statute because it came into possession of the certified notice through its authorized digital channel.

## Timing Mismatch: System Auto-Cancellation Before Human Signature Stamping

The plaintiffs placed significant weight on a chronological discrepancy in GoAuto's workflow: their computer system canceled policies based on digital input before any signature was affixed to physical paperwork later in the day. Specifically, after APAC sent its automated email triggering immediate cancellation via GoAuto's server, an APAC employee would subsequently print documents and apply a signature stamp during morning hours. Furthermore, since 2017, these stamped documents were physically delivered by an APAC employee to a shared filing cabinet managed by GoAuto staff members who stamped them "received." The plaintiffs argued this sequence proved invalidity because cancellation happened before any formalized human verification or signing occurred at GoAuto headquarters. However, the Fifth Circuit determined this timeline was irrelevant to the statutory requirement because "strict adherence" does not mean adhering to outdated manual rituals if those rituals are unnecessary under current technology. Since valid receipt occurred digitally when the web service processed APAC's message—which included all required certifications—the subsequent physical handling and stamping were administrative formalities rather than prerequisites for legal effectiveness. The court emphasized that adding extra steps like mandatory human signatures would contravene legislative intent unless explicitly required by statute text, which it was not.

## Holding: Electronic Delivery to Server Constitutes Valid Receipt

Ultimately, holding firmly against expanding judicially created hurdles beyond statutory text, Fifth Circuit affirmed that electronic delivery directly to an insurer's server constitutes valid receipt under La. Rev. Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The court ruled that because GoAuto took possession via its web service immediately upon receiving APAC's email—and because such emails contained all necessary certifications regarding default status and prior notice—the statutory condition precedent for cancellation was met instantly upon digital ingestion. There was no requirement for physical delivery first or human acknowledgment prior to cancellation; indeed, insisting otherwise would ignore how modern businesses operate efficiently using integrated software systems capable of acting autonomously within defined parameters.
