Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does a 'partial stay' of a preliminary injunction mean the lower court's order remains effective for the non-stayed portions, or is the entire injunction voided until the appellate court rules?
Question:
I'm reading *Pacito v. Trump* and the Ninth Circuit says they are staying the district court's preliminary injunctions "in their entirety except as specified." However, they explicitly carve out an exception where they direct the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for existing refugees. If the appellate court stays an injunction, doesn't that usually freeze everything? How can the district court's order still be partially operative (regarding reinstatement) while being stayed otherwise? Is this a unique procedural mechanism for mixed rulings on statutory vs. constitutional claims?
Answer:
**A partial stay functions exactly as you suspect: it suspends the effectiveness of the lower court's order only to the extent specified, leaving the remainder fully operative.**

There is no special procedural mechanism required for mixed statutory versus constitutional claims to allow this; rather, it is a fundamental feature of how appellate courts exercise their equitable power to grant stays. A stay is not an automatic "voiding" of the district court's judgment; it is a temporary suspension of its enforceability. When an appellate court issues a **partial stay**, it is exercising discretion to balance the equities (the four-factor test from *Nken v. Holder*) differently across distinct parts of the relief granted below.

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit applied this logic by distinguishing between the President's authority to suspend refugee *admissions* and his obligation to provide *resettlement services* to those already admitted.

### 1. The Mechanics of a Partial Stay
The Court's language—"stayed in their entirety except as specified"—confirms that the district court's injunctions are frozen everywhere **except** where the appellate court explicitly carves out an exception.
*   **Stayed Portions:** The injunctions prohibiting the suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) are stayed. Consequently, the Executive Order suspending admissions is allowed to proceed despite the district court's contrary ruling.
*   **Non-Stayed Portions:** The directive requiring the reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees already in the U.S. remains active. Because this portion was *not* stayed, the government must comply with it immediately while the appeal on other issues continues.

### 2. Why This Happens: Discrete Legal Standards
The Court did not need a unique procedural vehicle because different legal standards apply to different parts of the district court's order, leading to different outcomes on the "likelihood of success on the merits" prong of the stay analysis (*Nken*).

*   **Admissions Suspension (§ 1182(f)):** The majority found that under *Trump v. Hawaii*, the President has broad, deferential authority under § 1182(f). They concluded the government was "likely to prevail" on this specific claim, warranting a stay of that portion of the injunction to prevent irreparable harm to national security interests during litigation.
*   **Resettlement Services (§ 1522):** Conversely, regarding reception and placement services for refugees already admitted, the majority analyzed 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and concluded that statutory language ("shall," "insure") created a mandatory duty, not merely discretionary authorization (contrary to Judge Lee's dissent). Finding that "the government is not likely to prevail" on this APA/statutory interpretation claim, they refused to stay this portion of the injunction.

### Conclusion
A partial stay does not render a lower court order void; it renders it **partially ineffective**. In *Pacito*, this means:
1.  The Executive Order banning new entries is valid and enforceable (because that part of the injunction is stayed).
2.  The State Department must reinstate cooperative agreements for existing refugees (because that part of the injunction remains in force).

This approach allows appellate courts to preserve their jurisdiction and manage complex cases without forcing all-or-nothing outcomes when different legal questions within a single case present distinct equities and likelihoods of success on appeal.

### How does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 reconcile 'authorization' language with mandatory duties when Congress appropriates specific funds for refugee services?
Question:
Judge Lee's dissent argues that because § 1522 is titled "Authorization" and uses phrases like "is authorized," it gives the Executive Branch discretion not to fund refugee resettlement. However, the majority points to subsections using "shall" (e.g., "shall... make available sufficient resources") and notes that Congress appropriated billions for this purpose. In statutory interpretation, does a section titled "Authorization" automatically override explicit "shall" mandates found within that same section, especially when specific appropriations exist? Does the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" turn a mandatory duty into a conditional one?
Answer:
No. A section titled "Authorization" does not automatically override explicit "shall" mandates contained within that same section, nor does the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" transform a mandatory duty into a purely discretionary one when Congress has appropriated specific funds.

Under the principles of statutory construction applied in *Pacito v. Trump*, the text of the statute controls over its title. While Judge Lee's dissent correctly notes that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs..." and uses permissive language ("is authorized") in several subsections, the majority rightly identifies that specific operative clauses within the same section impose mandatory duties using the imperative verb "shall."

**1. Titles Do Not Override Operative Text**
The court rejects the premise that a section heading can negate clear statutory commands found in the body of the law. As noted in the discussion, § 1522(a)(1)(A) explicitly directs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to "make available sufficient resources" and "insure that cash assistance is made available." These are not merely aspirational goals; they are commands triggered by the existence of appropriations. The use of "shall" creates a presumptive obligation, whereas titles serve only as descriptive guides and lack independent legal force to contradict substantive mandates.

**2. The Effect of "To the Extent of Available Appropriations"**
The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" functions as a limitation on *capacity*, not a grant of *discretion* to ignore the duty entirely once funds exist. The opinion highlights that for Fiscal Year 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance (later reduced by $800 million via rescission). Because specific funds were allocated by Congress for this exact purpose, the condition precedent for the mandatory duty—"available appropriations"—was satisfied.

Consequently, once Congress appropriates money for a specific statutory purpose, an agency cannot rely on an "authorization" label or internal budgetary preferences to refuse execution of a "shall" command funded by those very dollars. The dissent's argument that § 1522(a) only sets conditions for funding if the government *chooses* to act ignores that Congress has already chosen to act by passing an appropriation bill specifically designated for these services.

**3. Mandatory Duties Remain Intact**
The opinion emphasizes that while § 1522(b)–(e) authorize specific types of assistance (like project grants), § 1522(a) imposes mandatory conditions on how those funds must be used if provided—and since funds *were* provided, those conditions became binding duties. Furthermore, subsections regarding unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and interim support (§ 1522(e)(7)(A)) reinforce this mandatory framework by using "shall" in conjunction with terms like "necessary," indicating that where need exists and funds are present, executive discretion yields to statutory obligation.

In summary, *Pacito* confirms that when Congress appropriates funds and simultaneously enacts statutes using mandatory language ("shall"), the Executive Branch is legally bound to expend those funds to fulfill the statutory mission, regardless of whether the section header labels it an "Authorization." The availability of appropriations converts potential authority into actual duty.

### Can an appellate court grant a stay based on likelihood of success on statutory grounds while simultaneously denying relief on constitutional/INA grounds without issuing a full opinion yet?
Question:
The panel orders states they are granting a stay because the government is likely to succeed on challenging the suspension of admissions (citing *Trump v. Hawaii*), but they deny a full stay because the government might lose on the APA/statutory claim regarding resettlement services (§ 1522). They admit an opinion on merits will come later. Procedurally, how can they weigh these conflicting likelihood-of-success determinations to issue a narrow stay? Do they essentially split their decision-making process between two distinct legal theories before fully briefing either?
Answer:
Yes, an appellate panel can issue a "split" stay—granting relief on one legal theory while denying it on another—even before issuing a full merits opinion. This procedure is not only permissible but often necessary when different claims present distinct standards of review or factual uncertainties that can be resolved independently during the interlocutory phase.

### The Legal Standard for Stays
Under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), a court evaluating a stay pending appeal weighs four factors:
1.  Likelihood of success on the merits;
2.  Irreparable harm absent a stay;
3.  Injury to other parties; and
4.  Public interest.

Crucially, courts apply these factors to the **claims** presented, not necessarily to the entire lawsuit as a monolithic block. If a plaintiff raises multiple independent causes of action (e.g., a constitutional/statutory challenge to an executive order's existence versus an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge to the execution of funding), the court may find that the moving party is likely to succeed on one claim but unlikely to succeed on another.

### Application in *Pacito v. Trump*
In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit panel explicitly engaged in this bifurcated analysis without waiting for a full opinion on the merits. The panel treated the government's challenge as two distinct inquiries:

1.  **The Admission Suspension (Likely Success):** Regarding the suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the panel applied the high deference standard from *Trump v. Hawaii*. They concluded the government was "likely to prevail" because the President had merely found that entry would be "detrimental," satisfying the text of § 1182(f). Consequently, they stayed the portion of the district court's injunction blocking this suspension.
2.  **The Resettlement Funding (Unlikely Success):** Conversely, regarding the termination of cooperative agreements for resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the panel found the government was **"not likely to prevail."** Their reasoning relied on specific statutory language ("shall... make available sufficient resources") and Congressional appropriations, suggesting a mandatory duty exists regardless of discretionary funding levels. Because they did not believe they would uphold this part of the government's appeal, they refused to stay that portion of the district court's injunction (specifically ordering reinstatement of agreements).

### Procedural Justification
You asked how they weigh conflicting likelihoods without fully briefing both theories yet. The answer lies in the nature of **interlocutory review**:

*   **Threshold Determination:** A stay is not a final judgment on liability; it is an equitable remedy preserving the status quo pending final resolution. Courts are permitted—and expected—to make provisional assessments based on current briefings and oral arguments to prevent irreparable harm *now*.
*   **Issue-Specific Analysis:** As seen in *Pacito*, if Claim A (admission suspension) clearly favors one side under existing precedent (*Hawaii*), but Claim B (statutory funding duties) turns on ambiguous statutory interpretation requiring deeper analysis, the court can resolve Claim A immediately while reserving Claim B for full briefing and argument later in time or in a subsequent opinion section.
*   **Avoiding Premature Finality:** By narrowing their stay order, the panel avoided granting a blanket victory to either party prematurely. They preserved their ability to issue a comprehensive opinion later that addresses why § 1522 creates a mandatory duty (as suggested by Judge Lee's dissent regarding statutory construction) while acknowledging that § 1182(f) grants broad presidential discretion today.

### Conclusion
The *Pacito* order demonstrates that appellate panels frequently parse complex litigation into discrete legal theories for procedural purposes. When one theory presents a clear path to reversal under binding precedent (like *Trump v. Hawaii*) and another presents significant statutory ambiguity, issuing a tailored stay that reflects these divergent probabilities is procedurally sound and legally required by the balancing test in *Nken*. The "full opinion" serves to flesh out the nuanced reasoning for these split conclusions, but it is not a prerequisite for making an interim assessment based on probable outcomes.

### What is the legal distinction between terminating 'cooperative agreements' versus suspending 'funding,' and why does it matter for standing under the APA?
Question:
The background mentions that after Feb 25, the government terminated cooperative agreements with NGOs, even though they claimed to be just suspending funding. Later, Judge Lee argues about whether § 1522 mandates funding or just authorizes it. Why did the plaintiffs sue under both the Refugee Act and the APA? Specifically, if Congress didn't *mandate* funding (as Lee suggests), does terminating a contract violate any statute if no money was technically promised in perpetuity? Or is the violation purely procedural under APA because they changed policy without notice/comment?
Answer:
The core legal distinction you are identifying lies between **substantive statutory mandates** and **procedural requirements for changing existing commitments**, and how these interact with Article III standing under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).

Based on the Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump*, here is the breakdown of why the plaintiffs sued under both statutes and how the "termination vs. suspension" distinction impacts the analysis.

### 1. The Dual Legal Theory: Substantive vs. Procedural
The plaintiffs pursued two distinct avenues because they attack the government's actions on different grounds:

*   **The Refugee Act Claim (Substantive):** This claims that Congress created a **mandatory duty** to provide "reception and placement" services once a refugee is admitted. If true, the government cannot simply stop providing these services regardless of policy changes; doing so would violate the statute itself.
*   **The APA Claim (Procedural/Contractual):** This claims that even if Congress only *authorized* funding (did not mandate it), the government cannot arbitrarily terminate existing contractual relationships ("cooperative agreements") without following proper procedure (notice-and-comment) or violating reliance interests.

### 2. The Distinction Between Terminating Agreements and Suspending Funding
You asked why it matters if they called it a "suspension" but acted like a "termination."

Under the facts presented in *Pacito*:
*   **"Suspension"** implies a temporary pause with an intent to resume, often preserving the underlying legal relationship or funding stream for future activation.
*   **"Termination"** of a cooperative agreement is a permanent severance of the contractual bond between the State Department and the NGO partners.

**Why this matters for Standing:**
If the government merely "suspended" funding temporarily, an organization might lack standing to sue for damages because their contract remains intact and could be reactivated instantly. However, by **terminating** every cooperative agreement (as happened on February 26), the government destroyed the plaintiffs' current operational capacity and financial expectations immediately. This creates an **"irreparable injury"** necessary for standing and injunctive relief: the NGOs lost their ability to function *now*, not just potentially later. The court notes that despite claiming to prepare new requests for proposals, the government admitted uncertainty about when or if replacements would exist, cementing the injury caused by termination rather than mere suspension.

### 3. Addressing Judge Lee's Concurrence: Mandate vs. Authorization
Judge Lee argues that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is purely **authorizing**, not mandating, citing phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations" and "is authorized." He contends that if Congress didn't promise money in perpetuity, there is no substantive statutory violation in stopping payments; it is merely an exercise of executive discretion over budget execution.

However, even accepting Judge Lee's reading that § 1522 does not create an absolute right to perpetual funding, **it does not grant carte blanche to ignore procedural constraints.**

This explains why an APA claim survives even if Lee's interpretation holds:
1.  **Reliance Interests:** Once cooperative agreements are signed, they create binding legal obligations between agencies and grantees. Terminating them constitutes a change in agency policy or action affecting rights/obligations.
2.  **Arbitrary and Capricious Review:** Under APA § 706(2)(A), an agency cannot change its mind or terminate contracts without examining relevant data or articulating a satisfactory explanation connecting facts to choice (*Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm*). By terminating agreements while simultaneously claiming they were just "suspending" them—and then failing to show any plan for replacement—the government risks acting arbitrarily, regardless of whether § 1522 mandates future funding levels generally.

### Conclusion
The plaintiffs sued under both acts because they cover different gaps:
*   If § 1522 is a **mandate** (Majority view), terminating services violates substantive law directly, rendering any procedural defense irrelevant regarding past-due obligations for admitted refugees (§ 1522(a)).
*   If § 1522 is only an **authorization** (Lee's view), then terminating specific contracts without notice or a reasoned explanation violates the **APA**. The violation isn't necessarily that money was "promised forever," but that existing valid contracts were dismantled through improper process, causing immediate harm to refugees relying on those specific service providers during their transition period in the U.S.

The Ninth Circuit's order reflects this nuance: while deferring heavily on immigration entry powers (§ 1182(f)), they found enough likelihood of success on at least one APA claim regarding § 1522 services to deny a full stay, specifically directing reinstatement of agreements necessary to meet statutory service requirements currently being funded by appropriations ($3+ billion).

### If Congress reduces appropriations via a Rescissions Act, does that trigger an automatic exemption from statutory mandates like 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)?
Question:
The opinion notes that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion but then passed a Rescissions Act reducing it by $800 million. The majority cites § 1522(a)'s phrase "to the extent of available appropriations." If Congress cuts funds specifically targeting these programs, does that legally excuse the Executive Branch from complying with mandatory directives like making cash assistance available or providing medical services? Or does "available appropriations" refer only to funds currently in existence at any given moment, meaning if funds are rescinded mid-year, the mandate simply becomes impossible to fulfill rather than optional?
Answer:
No, a reduction in appropriations via a Rescissions Act does not create an automatic exemption from the statutory mandates found in 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a); rather, it defines the *scope* of the Executive Branch's obligation. The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" operates as a limitation on the quantum of service required, not as a conditional trigger that renders the entire mandate optional when funds are cut.

Based on the Ninth Circuit's discussion in *Pacito v. Trump*, the distinction lies between a lack of authority to act and a limitation on the amount of resources required to act. The court explicitly identifies § 1522(a) as imposing mandatory duties ("make available," "insure that cash assistance is made available") that are qualified only by the availability of funds. The opinion notes that Congress initially appropriated $3.178 billion and subsequently reduced it by $800 million via the Rescissions Act of 2025. Crucially, despite acknowledging this reduction, the panel did not treat the remaining balance as insufficient to satisfy any legal duty or as grounds to terminate existing cooperative agreements entirely. Instead, recognizing that "funds... were reduced," the court still held that "the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522."

This holding rejects the interpretation that a mid-year rescission transforms a mandatory directive into an optional one or creates impossibility that excuses compliance with all aspects of the statute. If "available appropriations" meant only funds existing at any arbitrary moment without regard to statutory intent, a rescission would automatically void all obligations under § 1522. However, *Pacito* demonstrates that even with reduced funding, the Executive Branch retains an affirmative duty to utilize whatever funds remain to fulfill the core statutory requirements (such as cash assistance and medical services) for refugees already admitted.

Judge Lee's dissent argues for reading § 1522 as purely permissive based on its title ("Authorization"), but this view was rejected by the majority's operative order. The majority treated "to the extent of available appropriations" as a cap on expenditure rather than an escape hatch from liability. Therefore, under *Pacito*, a Rescissions Act reduces the *amount* of money available to meet mandates but does not legally excuse the Executive Branch from attempting to meet those mandates within the new budgetary constraint. The mandate remains; only its execution is scaled to match current appropriations.

### Does *Nken v. Holder*'s four-factor test allow an appellate court to balance irreparable harm differently for different classes of plaintiffs within a single case?
Question:
In previous orders mentioned in this text (March/April/May), the panel carved out exceptions for people already conditionally approved before Jan 20. Now in this September order, they seem to be balancing harm differently again: allowing suspension of new entries but mandating service for current residents. How does *Nken* handle cases where one group suffers irreparable harm (new entrants blocked) while another group suffers financial/administrative harm (existing refugees losing services)? Can a court find irreparable harm exists for Group A but not Group B in a single consolidated appeal?
Answer:
Yes, under *Nken v. Holder*, an appellate court can—and frequently must—balance irreparable harm differently for distinct classes of plaintiffs within a single consolidated appeal, resulting in a stay that applies partially rather than universally.

The Supreme Court in *Nken* established that the four-factor test for a stay pending appeal requires courts to weigh "whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay" against "where the public interest lies." While *Nken* treats these factors holistically, it does not mandate a binary outcome for every plaintiff in a multi-party suit. Instead, the analysis is inherently fact-specific to the nature of the injury alleged by each movant or affected class. If one group faces irreparable constitutional or statutory deprivation (such as being barred from entry) while another faces merely financial or administrative disruption (such as loss of funding mechanisms), the court is free to find that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of relief for one group but not the other.

The September 12, 2025 order in *Pacito v. Trump* demonstrates exactly this application. The Ninth Circuit panel applied the *Nken* factors to two distinct groups of plaintiffs arising from Executive Order 14163:
1.  **New Entrants:** For refugees seeking entry outside the U.S., the court found the government was likely to succeed on the merits based on broad presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Consequently, the court determined that suspending their entry did not constitute irreparable harm warranting judicial intervention against valid executive power, leading to a full stay regarding admission bans.
2.  **Current Residents:** Conversely, for refugees already admitted to the United States, the panel identified a separate legal challenge regarding 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority concluded there was a likelihood of success on the merits regarding whether this statute mandates "reception and placement" services. Because terminating these services would deprive admitted refugees of statutorily required care (cash assistance, medical services, etc.), the court found irreparable harm existed for this specific subgroup.

The result was a tailored remedy: the court vacated its prior stays and reinstated district court injunctions *only* to compel the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide services to currently admitted refugees ("Group B"), while simultaneously staying all other aspects of the injunctions related to new admissions ("Group A").

This approach confirms that *Nken* permits courts to dissect consolidated appeals when different classes face qualitatively different harms supported by different legal theories. As seen in *Pacito*, a court may grant relief where irreparable harm is imminent and legally cognizable (statutory violation affecting current residents) while denying it where no such harm exists or where executive deference precludes intervention (admission bans). The dissent by Judge Lee reinforces this distinction; even she agreed with staying the ban on new entries but dissented only because she believed § 1522 was permissive rather than mandatory, illustrating that the divergence turned entirely on how irreparable harm and legal merit were assessed for each specific class within the same docket numbers (Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939).
