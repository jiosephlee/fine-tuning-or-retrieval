# Factual Background and Procedural History

## Issuance of Executive Order 14163 under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)
The litigation in *Pacito v. Trump* originates from a significant shift in federal immigration policy initiated by the Executive Branch on January 20, 2025. On that date, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." This order relied explicitly on the authority granted by Congress in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a statute that empowers the President to suspend the entry of any alien or class of aliens whenever he determines that such entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Exercising this broad statutory delegation, the President found that continued entry under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) posed such a detriment and subsequently directed a suspension of all refugee entries under USRAP pending further findings. This invocation of § 1182(f) places the dispute squarely within the realm of presidential power over border control and national security, areas where courts traditionally afford substantial deference to executive determinations.

## Suspension of USRAP Funding via Executive Order 14169
Compounding the suspension of admissions, the administration took further steps to dismantle the operational infrastructure of the refugee program just days later. On January 24, 2025, pursuant to a separate directive, Executive Order No. 14169 ("Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid"), the Department of State suspended all funding for USRAP. Crucially, this suspension extended beyond mere processing fees; it halted funding for domestic resettlement services intended for refugees already admitted to or approved for admission into the United States. While EO 14163 addressed who could enter, EO 14169 targeted what happened after entry or approval, effectively cutting off financial support for reception, placement, and care services that had historically been administered through partnerships with non-profit organizations.

## Identity of Plaintiffs: Refugees and resettlement organizations
The lawsuit was brought by a diverse group of plaintiffs directly affected by these executive actions: individual refugees and three non-governmental organizations (NGOs). The individual plaintiffs fall into two categories: those who had already been admitted to the United States as refugees but faced disruption in their support services due to the funding cuts, and those who had received conditional approval for resettlement but remained outside U.S. borders awaiting their arrival due to the suspension on admissions. The organizational plaintiffs consisted of entities holding cooperative agreements with the Department of State specifically designed to facilitate overseas processing for asylum seekers and provide essential resettlement services domestically. These organizations argued that they suffered direct harm from the sudden termination of their contractual relationships with the government and from being unable to fulfill their missions due to lack of funding and clarity regarding future operations.

## District Court Preliminary Injunctions (February 28 and March 24, 2025)
In response to these executive orders, plaintiffs filed suit in February 2025 in the United States District Court for the Western District of Washington. They alleged violations of both the Refugee Act of 1980 regarding EO 14163 and various provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) concerning EO 14169's defunding measures. The district court acted swiftly to halt potential irreparable harm to vulnerable populations. # Legal Framework: Presidential Authority vs. Statutory Mandates

## Presidential Power under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): "Detrimental to Interests" Standard

The core of the majority's reasoning regarding the suspension of refugee admissions rests on the expansive authority granted to the President by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). This statute serves as the primary legal justification for Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended the entry of refugees into the United States. The text of § 1182(f) establishes a low threshold for presidential action: whenever the President finds that the entry of any alien or class of aliens would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States," he may suspend their entry or impose restrictions. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit panel emphasized that this standard is purely subjective; it requires only that the President make a finding, without demanding that courts scrutinize the factual basis or wisdom behind that determination. The court noted that "the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry... would be detrimental." Because President Trump explicitly made this finding in his executive order, concluding that refugee entry was detrimental to national interests, the statutory condition was satisfied on its face. Consequently, under this specific provision, judicial review is extremely narrow, focusing almost exclusively on whether the President followed the procedural steps required by the statute rather than evaluating the substantive merits of his policy choice.

This interpretation aligns with and relies heavily on Supreme Court precedent, particularly *Trump v. Hawaii*. The *Pacito* panel quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* to illustrate that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" and vests the President with "ample power" to restrict entry beyond what might otherwise be enumerated in immigration laws. When analyzing whether an executive order exceeds statutory limits under § 1182(f), courts generally refrain from engaging in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." Instead, they accept facial compliance with the statutory text as sufficient grounds for upholding the restriction. In this case, because Executive Order No. 14163 did not exceed any textual limit on presidential authority—merely invoking a blanket suspension based on a finding of detriment—the majority concluded that the government was likely to succeed on its defense against challenges to this portion of the order. This high level of deference creates a significant barrier for plaintiffs attempting to block entry suspensions solely under INA § 1182(f).

## Statutory Duties under 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Reception and Placement Obligations

While presidential power over entry is broad and discretionary, the court identified a contrasting legal landscape regarding post-entry services governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, part of the Refugee Act of 1980. Unlike § 1182(f), which focuses on preventing entry, § 1522 outlines specific responsibilities for assisting refugees already admitted to or en route to the United States. The majority distinguished between general policy discretion and mandatory statutory duties by pointing to specific language within § 1522(a)(1)(A), which mandates that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettling "shall"—to the extent of available appropriations—make resources available for employment training, English language acquisition, cash assistance, and ensure equal opportunities for women. The use of imperative language like "shall" suggests a congressional command rather than mere suggestion. Furthermore, subsection (d)(2)(B) imposes a direct obligation regarding unaccompanied minors, stating that during transit or interim periods before placement, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)." These provisions create a framework where Congress has not merely authorized but legally compelled specific actions once refugees enter or are in transit toward admission.

The tension in this case arises from how these statutory mandates interact with funding mechanisms and administrative execution. Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) permits these services to be delivered through contracts or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies rather than directly by federal employees alone. Historically, this has resulted in a system reliant on third-party organizations like those represented by organizational plaintiffs in *Pacito*. However, unlike other parts of immigration law where funding might be purely discretionary based on annual appropriations bills alone here interacts with specific statutory directives requiring service delivery regardless of whether new contracts are immediately signed if existing ones are terminated arbitrarily without replacement capacity being secured. The court noted Congress appropriated billions for migration assistance yet reduced funds via rescission acts; nevertheless, where statutes say "shall provide," those commands persist even amidst budgetary fluctuations unless explicitly repealed by Congress itself—a nuance crucial when assessing whether terminating all cooperative agreements violates federal law independent from political policy shifts driving suspension orders themselves!
