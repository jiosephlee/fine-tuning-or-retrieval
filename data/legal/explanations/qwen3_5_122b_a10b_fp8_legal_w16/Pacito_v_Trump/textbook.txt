Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Procedural History and the Emergency Stay Context

## Issuance of Executive Order No. 14163 (Suspension of Entry) and Executive Order No. 14169 (Defunding)

The litigation in *Pacito v. Trump* arose from a rapid sequence of executive actions taken shortly after President Trump assumed office on January 20, 2025. On January 30, 2025, the President issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." Relying explicitly on the authority granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the Order declared that the entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Consequently, Section 3(a) of the Order suspended all entries into the United States for refugees under USRAP pending further findings. This statute provides broad presidential power, allowing the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens whenever he makes such a finding of detriment to national interest.

Simultaneously, on January 24, 2025—four days prior to the first Order but issued simultaneously with related policy shifts—the Department of State suspended all funding for USRAP pursuant to a separate directive, Executive Order No. 14169 ("Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid"). This defunding was not merely a pause on new admissions; it extended to domestic resettlement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. These twin executive actions created an immediate legal crisis: they halted future refugee arrivals and severed the financial lifeline required to support those who had already arrived or were awaiting resettlement, setting the stage for a constitutional and statutory challenge by affected parties.

## Plaintiffs' Standing: Refugees, Applicants Abroad, and Resettlement Organizations

The plaintiffs in this action represented two distinct categories of aggrieved parties: individual refugees and organizational partners of the federal government. The individual plaintiffs included refugees who had already been admitted to the United States as well as those who had received conditional approval for resettlement but remained outside U.S. borders waiting for travel arrangements. Their presence in this lawsuit demonstrates that standing can attach not only to those currently inside a jurisdiction but also to those with finalized approvals whose ability to enter has been abruptly revoked by executive fiat.

Complementing these individual claims were three organizational plaintiffs that held cooperative agreements with the Department of State. These organizations were tasked with providing essential overseas processing for asylum seekers and delivering resettlement services—such as housing, employment training, and cultural orientation—to refugees once they arrived in America. By suing alongside individual refugees, these organizations highlighted a dual harm: they faced direct injury through the termination of their contractual relationships with the government, while their beneficiaries faced separation from family members abroad or destitution upon arrival without support services. Together, these plaintiffs alleged that Executive Order No. 14163 violated the Refugee Act of 1980 (incorporated into the Immigration and Nationality Act) and that the subsequent defunding violated procedural requirements under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).

## District Court Actions: Oral Preliminary Injunction (Feb 25) and Written Orders (Feb 28 & Mar 24)

The pace of judicial intervention was exceptionally fast following the filing of suit in February 2025 in the Western District of Washington. Just weeks after receiving notice of potential terminations, plaintiffs secured relief from a federal district judge who acted with unusual speed given typical civil procedure timelines. On February 25, relying on his assessment that irreparable harm was imminent if enforcement continued unchecked during litigation proceedings, Judge orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of Sections 3(a), (b), (c), and (d) of Executive Order No. 14163 regarding entry suspensions. Three days later, on February 28, responding to government concerns about ambiguity regarding funding cuts already underway—or threatened—the court issued a written order defining the scope of its earlier oral ruling; notably, this clarification explicitly prohibited any suspension of USRAP funding intended for domestic resettlement services.

# Legal Standards Governing a Stay Pending Appeal

## The Four-Factor Test from *Nken v. Holder*

When a party appeals a district court's preliminary injunction, the appellate court must decide whether to "stay" that injunction while the appeal is pending. A stay effectively pauses the lower court's order, allowing the status quo to revert to what it was before the injunction issued. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit utilized this mechanism to determine whether the district court's preliminary injunctions—which halted the suspension of refugee admissions and required the reinstatement of funding for resettlement services—should remain in effect during the appellate process. The court did not create a new standard for this decision; rather, it relied on the Supreme Court's definitive guidance in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009). According to *Nken*, a court considering a stay pending appeal must evaluate four distinct factors: (1) whether the applicant has made a strong showing that they are likely to succeed on the merits of their appeal; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury if the stay is denied; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. These factors are not mere suggestions but represent a rigorous balancing test designed to prevent irreparable harm while preserving judicial resources until a final judgment can be rendered on substantive claims.

## 'Strong Showing' Requirement for Likelihood of Success on Merits

The first factor in the *Nken* analysis often proves dispositive: whether the moving party has demonstrated a "strong showing" that they are likely to win their underlying appeal. This threshold is higher than simply raising a serious question or demonstrating that victory is merely possible; it requires a persuasive demonstration that success is probable. In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit engaged in this assessment separately for two distinct legal theories raised by the government: one challenging the suspension of refugee admissions and another challenging the defunding of resettlement services. Regarding the suspension of admissions under Executive Order No. 14163, which relied on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the court found that the government satisfied this heavy burden. The panel reasoned that under controlling precedent like *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), courts afford significant deference to presidential determinations regarding national security and immigration policy. Because § 1182(f) requires only that the President find entry "detrimental" to U.S. interests—a finding explicitly made in this case—the government showed it was highly unlikely that a reviewing court would overturn that determination without engaging in an impermissible "searching inquiry." Consequently, on this specific claim, the government met its obligation to show likelihood of success.

However, regarding the second claim—that suspending funding for resettlement services violated administrative law principles—the analysis yielded a different result, preventing an automatic full stay of all district court orders. The court acknowledged potential merit in the plaintiffs' argument that Congress, through 8 U.S.C. § 1522, imposed mandatory duties on federal officials to provide reception and placement services once refugees were admitted or even while they were in transit. Unlike broad presidential discretion over entry bans, statutory mandates regarding specific appropriations and service delivery often carry less deference if they contain clear "shall" language creating affirmative obligations. By recognizing that there were genuine legal questions about whether § 1522 constituted an authorization or a mandate, particularly concerning unaccompanied minors and recent arrivals, the court # Likelihood of Success Regarding Suspension of Refugee Admissions

## Statutory Scope of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): 'Detrimental to Interests'

In evaluating the government's likelihood of succeeding on the merits regarding the suspension of refugee admissions, the Ninth Circuit focused its analysis squarely on the text and scope of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). This statute serves as the primary legal authority invoked by President Trump in Executive Order No. 14163, which suspended entry into the United States for refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). The core legal question addressed by the panel was whether the President's determination that such entry "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" satisfied the statutory requirements to trigger his suspension powers. The court concluded that it did, emphasizing that the language of § 1182(f) sets a low threshold for presidential action. Specifically, the statute provides that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States," he may suspend their entry or impose restrictions deemed appropriate. By framing this as a finding based on national interest rather than a specific factual predicate requiring judicial verification, Congress intended to grant the Executive Branch significant latitude in managing border security and foreign policy concerns related to alien entry.

The court's reading of § 1182(f) underscores that the statute operates as a conditional grant of power: once the President makes a finding of detriment, the authority to suspend entry activates automatically without further legislative approval or judicial vetting of that finding's substantive correctness. In *Pacito v. Trump*, this interpretation meant that Executive Order No. 14163 fell comfortably within the statutory boundaries because it explicitly stated that entry under USRAP would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The panel rejected any notion that courts possess an independent role in determining whether a particular class of aliens actually poses a threat or detriment; instead, their role is limited to verifying that a formal finding exists on record. This approach treats "detrimental to the interests" not as a standard requiring objective proof akin to probable cause, but as a subjective judgment call reserved for political branches charged with protecting national sovereignty.

## **Trump v. Hawaii** Precedent: Deference to Presidential Proclamations

Central to the Ninth Circuit's reasoning was its reliance on *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which established a robust framework for judicial deference when reviewing presidential proclamations under § 1182(f). In *Pacito*, citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court reiterated that this specific statutory provision "exudes deference" toward the President and vests him with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." This precedent dictates that when Congress delegates such broad authority, courts must accord substantial weight to presidential determinations regarding national security and foreign relations. The Supreme Court in *Trump v. Hawaii* held that judicial scrutiny must be highly circumscribed because immigration control is deeply intertwined with diplomatic relations and national safety—areas where judges lack expertise and constitutional primacy compared to elected officials and appointed administrators. Consequently, when assessing whether Executive Order No. 14163 was lawful, the Ninth Circuit applied this same high level of deference, refusing to second-guess the administration's rationale for suspending refugee admissions even if plaintiffs argued policy disagreements or humanitarian concerns outweighed security risks.

The influence of *Trump v. Hawaii* extended beyond mere citation; it shaped the entire analytical structure used by panel members writing for majority opinion regarding admissions suspensions. By adopting *Hawaii*'s stance, they signaled that challenges based on alleged bad faith or insufficient evidence are generally foreclosed unless they directly contradict explicit textual limits in the statute itself—a bar rarely cleared given how open-ended § 1182(f) remains after decades of litigation history involving similar executive actions including bans targeting specific countries during previous administrations like Obama era travel policies too! # Mandatory Duties vs. Discretionary Funding: The APA Challenge

## Distinguishing Immigration Control from Post-Admission Assistance

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit draws a critical legal distinction between the President's power to control the border and his authority to manage the welfare of individuals already within the United States. This bifurcation drives the court's mixed ruling on the stay pending appeal. Regarding the suspension of refugee admissions, the panel relies heavily on the Supreme Court's precedent in *Trump v. Hawaii*. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the President possesses "ample power" to suspend the entry of any class of aliens if he finds it detrimental to U.S. interests. The court emphasizes that judicial review in this context is extremely narrow; courts are prohibited from engaging in a "searching inquiry" into the President's justifications. Because Executive Order No. 14163 explicitly contains a finding that suspending refugee entry is detrimental to national interests, the court concludes that the statutory prerequisites for an entry ban are met. Consequently, the government is likely to succeed on its claim that it can legally halt future admissions, and any injunction blocking this suspension must be stayed.

However, the analysis shifts dramatically when addressing the termination of funding for refugees already admitted or those in transit. While § 1182(f) governs *entry*, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 governs *resettlement*. Here, the court identifies potential mandatory statutory duties that constrain executive discretion even during a policy shift. Unlike § 1182(f), which grants broad deference, § 1522 outlines specific responsibilities for receiving and placing refugees, including employment training, English language acquisition, cash assistance, and care for unaccompanied minors. The majority opinion suggests that these provisions are not merely aspirational guidelines but create affirmative obligations on the federal government once Congress has appropriated funds for these purposes. The government's sudden termination of all cooperative agreements with resettlement agencies raises a question separate from immigration control: did the Executive Branch violate its statutory duty to provide "reception and placement" services required by law? This distinction allows the court to grant relief regarding post-admission services while simultaneously staying relief regarding entry bans.

## The Role of Administrative Procedure Act (APA) Challenges in Defunding Cases

The legal vehicle used to challenge the defunding of resettlement services is primarily the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), specifically its prohibition against agency actions that are "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law." While an APA challenge might fail against a pure policy change like an entry ban—due to the sweeping deference granted under § 1182(f)—it becomes potent when an agency acts contrary to explicit statutory mandates found elsewhere in code sections like § 1522. In this case, plaintiffs argued that terminating cooperative agreements violated various provisions of the APA because it failed to adhere to congressional directives regarding refugee care.

The court's discussion highlights how an APA challenge functions as a check on executive overreach when statutes impose specific conditions on funding usage. The majority notes that under § 1522(a)(1)(A), Congress has mandated that funds be used for specific outcomes, such as achieving economic self-sufficiency and providing English training "to the extent of available appropriations." Furthermore, subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) utilize mandatory language ("shall") regarding legal responsibility for unaccompanied minors and interim support projects for recent arrivals. By terminating all agreements without ensuring these statutory requirements could still be met through other means or new contracts, the government risks acting "not in accordance with law." The court indicates that unlike immigration entry decisions, where policy preferences suffice, defunding existing programs may require adherence to procedural and substantive constraints imposed by Congress through appropriation laws and specific service mandates embedded in § 1522.

## Reviewability: When Agency Action Exceeds Statutory Authority vs. Mere Policy Shifts

A central theme in *Pacivo* is determining when an agency's action crosses the line from a permissible policy shift into unlawful conduct exceeding statutory authority. Justice Lee's dissent illustrates one side of this debate by characterizing § 1522 as purely permissive legislation—a mere "authorization" rather than a mandate—which would render any decision not to fund resettlement services immune from judicial review under standard administrative law principles. If § 1522 were interpreted solely as authorization, then terminating contracts would simply be an exercise of executive discretion allowed by Congress, making it largely unreviewable except perhaps for bad faith or constitutional violations unrelated to statutory text.

Conversely, the majority panel treats certain portions of § 1522 as creating binding legal obligations that restrict executive choice once appropriations exist ($3.178 billion was appropriated for FY 2025). They reason that when Congress appropriates money and directs its use via mandatory verbs like "shall," agencies cannot simply ignore those directives without violating their statutory authority under the APA. The court points out that while Congress gave itself flexibility through phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations," it did not grant unlimited freedom to abandon core functions entirely if funds are present but unused due to contract terminations. The uncertainty expressed by government counsel during oral arguments—that they were unaware if new agencies were being secured—further supports a finding that current actions might exceed statutory limits rather than merely shifting policy direction temporarily until alternatives arise reviewable by courts under arbitrary-and-capricious standards rather than broad deference doctrines applied to national security claims under § 1182(f).

# Interpreting Mandatory Obligations Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522

This chapter examines the Ninth Circuit's analysis of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the specific statutory provision governing "reception and placement" services for refugees already admitted to the United States. While the court deferred to the President's broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend future refugee *entries*, it reached a different conclusion regarding the government's duties toward refugees who had already been processed and admitted. The majority opinion turns on a nuanced interpretation of statutory text, distinguishing between the President's power to halt admissions and Congress's command that certain support services be provided once admission occurs. This distinction formed the basis for the court's decision to partially lift its earlier stays, requiring the government to reinstate cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations despite the executive order suspending funding.

## Statutory Construction of Section 1522: From 'Director Shall Make Available Resources' to 'Shall Assume Responsibility'

The core legal dispute in this portion of the opinion centers on whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a mandatory obligation or merely an authorization for discretionary action. The majority rejected the government's argument that § 1522 is purely permissive, focusing instead on the imperative language used throughout the statute. The court highlighted that while some subsections describe what the Director is "authorized" to do, key provisions utilize the mandatory term "shall." Specifically, § 1522(a)(1)(A) directs that, "to the extent of available appropriations," the Director "shall... make available sufficient resources" for employment training, English language acquisition, cash assistance, and gender-equal participation opportunities. The court interpreted this phrasing as imposing a binding duty on the executive branch: if funds exist—as they did in FY 2025 via congressional appropriation—the agency must deploy them for these specific purposes.

The majority further anchored its reading in subsection (d)(2)(B), which addresses unaccompanied minors. This provision states that during any interim period before placement, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child." By emphasizing this explicit assignment of liability and care, alongside similar mandates in subsection (e)(7)(A) regarding interim support for refugees under thirty-six months old, the panel concluded that Congress intended to create a safety net that operates automatically upon admission. This construction stands in stark contrast to Judge Lee's dissenting view in his partial concurrence/dissent, which argued that because § 1522 is titled "Authorization for programs," and many subsections use permissive language like "is authorized," the entire scheme remains discretionary unless explicitly mandated otherwise. However, for purposes of understanding the majority's holding, it is crucial to recognize their reliance on specific instances where Congress chose mandatory verbs ("shall") coupled with concrete operational requirements ("make available," "assume responsibility"), thereby creating an enforceable statutory right for admitted refugees regardless of shifting political priorities or executive orders aimed at future entries.

## Historical Context of Cooperative Agreements with Resettlement Organizations

To understand why terminating funding constituted a violation of § 1522 rather than simply ending a voluntary partnership, one must appreciate the historical mechanism through which these statutory mandates are fulfilled. The opinion notes that while § 1522 places responsibility on federal officials like the Director of Refugee Resettlement within HHS or representatives at State Department levels, it explicitly permits—and historically relies upon—a delegation model. Specifically, § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) allows these services to be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. For decades prior to January 2025, this system functioned through a network of organizations that held formal cooperative agreements with the Department of State to manage overseas processing and domestic reception and placement services. These were not ad-hoc arrangements but institutionalized partnerships essential for executing the complex logistics required by statute: transporting refugees from points abroad to local communities in America and connecting them immediately with housing, job training, and medical care as mandated by §§ 1522(a) and (e). When analyzing this part of *Pacito*, students should view these cooperative agreements not as optional grants from goodwill but as the primary operational vehicle Congress envisioned for satisfying its mandatory spending directives in § 1522 without requiring direct federal employees to perform every service task personally.

## The Government's Uncertainty in Oral Argument: Failure to Secure New Providers

The factual predicate for finding an imminent violation of statutory duties emerged during oral argument before the Ninth Circuit panel following extensive briefing by both sides. Although Executive Order No. 14163 suspended refugee admissions and cut off funding streams effective January 30, 2025—and subsequent actions terminated all existing cooperative agreements by late February—the government initially represented to lower courts that it was actively seeking replacements. On March 10, counsel informed the district court they were preparing requests for proposals (RFPs) to secure new agencies capable of providing reception and placement services. However, when pressed during September oral arguments regarding whether any progress had actually been made toward securing these new providers since March or July dates mentioned in previous filings, government counsel conceded uncertainty about whether any replacement agencies had been identified or contracted. This admission was pivotal; it demonstrated a gap between statutory necessity and administrative reality. If no entities existed under contract or grant agreement capable of performing these duties within days or weeks of arrival (or even transit), then thousands of refugees who had legally been admitted—or were conditionally approved before suspension—would face immediate destitution without access to housing or food assistance guaranteed by law under § 1522(d) & (e). The lack thereof indicated either negligence or deliberate abandonment contrary to legislative intent embedded within appropriations statutes passed just months earlier ($3+ billion allocated minus rescissions). Thus failure wasn't just bureaucratic delay—it created actual impossibility unless corrective judicial intervention occurred promptly enough prevent harm flowing directly from non-compliance with federal mandate itself!

# Concurring-Dissenting Views: Limits on Judicial Power over Budgetary Decisions

## Judge Lee's Interpretive Framework: 'Authorization' vs. 'Mandate' in Statutory Titles

Judge Lee's partial concurrence and partial dissent in *Pacito v. Trump* hinges on a fundamental disagreement with the majority regarding the nature of the statutory duties imposed by 8 U.S.C. § 1522. While the majority concludes that the government is legally obligated to reinstate cooperative agreements to provide "reception and placement" services to refugees already admitted, Judge Lee argues that the statute merely authorizes such actions without mandating them. Her analysis begins with a close reading of the statute's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Relying on standard principles of statutory construction, she emphasizes that the term "authorization" is inherently permissive rather than obligatory. By citing Black's Law Dictionary, she defines authorization as "permission to do something," contrasting it sharply with a command or mandate. For Judge Lee, this linguistic signal at the very outset of the provision suggests that Congress intended to grant the Executive Branch discretion to determine whether and how to fund these programs, rather than stripping that discretion away by creating an affirmative legal duty to spend money regardless of policy shifts or administrative priorities. This interpretive starting point frames her entire objection to the majority's order directing the reinstatement of specific contracts.

## Analysis of 'Shall' Terms Qualified by 'To Extent of Available Appropriations'

A central pillar of Judge Lee's dissent addresses the majority's reliance on specific clauses within § 1522 that utilize the imperative verb "shall." The majority points to provisions stating that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettling "shall" make resources available or ensure cash assistance is provided as evidence of a mandatory obligation. Judge Lee contends that this reading ignores critical qualifying language embedded within those same sentences. She focuses specifically on phrases such as "to the extent of available appropriations." In her view, this qualification transforms what might otherwise appear as a mandatory directive into a conditional one tied strictly to legislative funding decisions. She argues that § 1522(a), titled "Conditions and considerations," operates only if the government has already chosen to exercise its discretionary power under subsections (b) through (e). Consequently, when Congress writes that officials "shall" act within these funded programs, they are setting conditions for *how* funds must be used once spent, not creating an independent duty to secure or expend those funds in the absence of explicit appropriation mandates. Furthermore, she notes that even provisions containing stronger language like "necessary decisions" for unaccompanied minors are circumscribed by terms like "if necessary," which she interprets as preserving significant executive latitude rather than imposing rigid judicial-enforceable requirements.

## Deference in Spending Decisions: Is Court Overstepping into Legislative Function?

Underlying Judge Lee's statutory interpretation is a robust concern regarding separation of powers and the proper role of the judiciary in budgetary matters. She posits that because § 1522 functions primarily as an authorization scheme, it leaves the actual decision-making regarding funding levels and program existence within the realm of executive discretion, subject ultimately to Congress's power of the purse. When a court orders an agency to reinstate specific cooperative agreements and provide services funded by appropriations, Judge Lee argues it risks usurping both executive policy choices and legislative spending authority. Her dissent highlights that even when Congress appropriates billions for migration assistance—as seen in FY 2025—it retains broad discretion over how those funds are allocated across competing national needs. By interpreting vague statutory language as creating binding mandates for specific expenditures, she warns against courts effectively rewriting legislation or compelling spending patterns that reflect judicial preferences rather than congressional intent or executive judgment. This perspective aligns with her broader view that immigration policy involves complex diplomatic and resource-allocation judgments best left to political branches unless Congress has spoken with unmistakable clarity—a clarity she finds lacking in § 1522.

## Impact on Separation of Powers if Court Orders Specific Contract Renewals

The practical implications of Judge Lee's view extend directly to her critique of the remedy imposed by both the district court and her colleagues on this panel: ordering the reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements with private resettlement organizations. If § 1522 is indeed merely permissive, then any contractual relationship between the State Department and these agencies exists solely at government pleasure during periods when funds are allocated and policies permit continuation thereof. Termination of such agreements represents a valid exercise of administrative discretion under her reading; therefore, judicial intervention forcing their renewal constitutes an impermissible intrusion into executive management functions. Moreover, she questions whether courts possess institutional competence to supervise ongoing contract negotiations or monitor compliance with service delivery standards required under these arrangements—a task traditionally reserved for agency oversight mechanisms or legislative review processes outlined in reporting statutes like § 1523.
