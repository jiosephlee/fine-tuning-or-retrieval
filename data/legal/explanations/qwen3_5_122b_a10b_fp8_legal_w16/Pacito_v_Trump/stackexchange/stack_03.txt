Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Can an appellate court grant a stay based on likelihood of success on statutory grounds while simultaneously denying relief on constitutional/INA grounds without issuing a full opinion yet?
Question:
The panel orders states they are granting a stay because the government is likely to succeed on challenging the suspension of admissions (citing *Trump v. Hawaii*), but they deny a full stay because the government might lose on the APA/statutory claim regarding resettlement services (§ 1522). They admit an opinion on merits will come later. Procedurally, how can they weigh these conflicting likelihood-of-success determinations to issue a narrow stay? Do they essentially split their decision-making process between two distinct legal theories before fully briefing either?
Answer:
Yes, an appellate panel can issue a "split" stay—granting relief on one legal theory while denying it on another—even before issuing a full merits opinion. This procedure is not only permissible but often necessary when different claims present distinct standards of review or factual uncertainties that can be resolved independently during the interlocutory phase.

### The Legal Standard for Stays
Under *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), a court evaluating a stay pending appeal weighs four factors:
1.  Likelihood of success on the merits;
2.  Irreparable harm absent a stay;
3.  Injury to other parties; and
4.  Public interest.

Crucially, courts apply these factors to the **claims** presented, not necessarily to the entire lawsuit as a monolithic block. If a plaintiff raises multiple independent causes of action (e.g., a constitutional/statutory challenge to an executive order's existence versus an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge to the execution of funding), the court may find that the moving party is likely to succeed on one claim but unlikely to succeed on another.

### Application in *Pacito v. Trump*
In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit panel explicitly engaged in this bifurcated analysis without waiting for a full opinion on the merits. The panel treated the government's challenge as two distinct inquiries:

1.  **The Admission Suspension (Likely Success):** Regarding the suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the panel applied the high deference standard from *Trump v. Hawaii*. They concluded the government was "likely to prevail" because the President had merely found that entry would be "detrimental," satisfying the text of § 1182(f). Consequently, they stayed the portion of the district court's injunction blocking this suspension.
2.  **The Resettlement Funding (Unlikely Success):** Conversely, regarding the termination of cooperative agreements for resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the panel found the government was **"not likely to prevail."** Their reasoning relied on specific statutory language ("shall... make available sufficient resources") and Congressional appropriations, suggesting a mandatory duty exists regardless of discretionary funding levels. Because they did not believe they would uphold this part of the government's appeal, they refused to stay that portion of the district court's injunction (specifically ordering reinstatement of agreements).

### Procedural Justification
You asked how they weigh conflicting likelihoods without fully briefing both theories yet. The answer lies in the nature of **interlocutory review**:

*   **Threshold Determination:** A stay is not a final judgment on liability; it is an equitable remedy preserving the status quo pending final resolution. Courts are permitted—and expected—to make provisional assessments based on current briefings and oral arguments to prevent irreparable harm *now*.
*   **Issue-Specific Analysis:** As seen in *Pacito*, if Claim A (admission suspension) clearly favors one side under existing precedent (*Hawaii*), but Claim B (statutory funding duties) turns on ambiguous statutory interpretation requiring deeper analysis, the court can resolve Claim A immediately while reserving Claim B for full briefing and argument later in time or in a subsequent opinion section.
*   **Avoiding Premature Finality:** By narrowing their stay order, the panel avoided granting a blanket victory to either party prematurely. They preserved their ability to issue a comprehensive opinion later that addresses why § 1522 creates a mandatory duty (as suggested by Judge Lee's dissent regarding statutory construction) while acknowledging that § 1182(f) grants broad presidential discretion today.

### Conclusion
The *Pacito* order demonstrates that appellate panels frequently parse complex litigation into discrete legal theories for procedural purposes. When one theory presents a clear path to reversal under binding precedent (like *Trump v. Hawaii*) and another presents significant statutory ambiguity, issuing a tailored stay that reflects these divergent probabilities is procedurally sound and legally required by the balancing test in *Nken*. The "full opinion" serves to flesh out the nuanced reasoning for these split conclusions, but it is not a prerequisite for making an interim assessment based on probable outcomes.
