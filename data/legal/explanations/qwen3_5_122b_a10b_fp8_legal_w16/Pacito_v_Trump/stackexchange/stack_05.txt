Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### If Congress reduces appropriations via a Rescissions Act, does that trigger an automatic exemption from statutory mandates like 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)?
Question:
The opinion notes that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion but then passed a Rescissions Act reducing it by $800 million. The majority cites § 1522(a)'s phrase "to the extent of available appropriations." If Congress cuts funds specifically targeting these programs, does that legally excuse the Executive Branch from complying with mandatory directives like making cash assistance available or providing medical services? Or does "available appropriations" refer only to funds currently in existence at any given moment, meaning if funds are rescinded mid-year, the mandate simply becomes impossible to fulfill rather than optional?
Answer:
No, a reduction in appropriations via a Rescissions Act does not create an automatic exemption from the statutory mandates found in 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a); rather, it defines the *scope* of the Executive Branch's obligation. The phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" operates as a limitation on the quantum of service required, not as a conditional trigger that renders the entire mandate optional when funds are cut.

Based on the Ninth Circuit's discussion in *Pacito v. Trump*, the distinction lies between a lack of authority to act and a limitation on the amount of resources required to act. The court explicitly identifies § 1522(a) as imposing mandatory duties ("make available," "insure that cash assistance is made available") that are qualified only by the availability of funds. The opinion notes that Congress initially appropriated $3.178 billion and subsequently reduced it by $800 million via the Rescissions Act of 2025. Crucially, despite acknowledging this reduction, the panel did not treat the remaining balance as insufficient to satisfy any legal duty or as grounds to terminate existing cooperative agreements entirely. Instead, recognizing that "funds... were reduced," the court still held that "the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522."

This holding rejects the interpretation that a mid-year rescission transforms a mandatory directive into an optional one or creates impossibility that excuses compliance with all aspects of the statute. If "available appropriations" meant only funds existing at any arbitrary moment without regard to statutory intent, a rescission would automatically void all obligations under § 1522. However, *Pacito* demonstrates that even with reduced funding, the Executive Branch retains an affirmative duty to utilize whatever funds remain to fulfill the core statutory requirements (such as cash assistance and medical services) for refugees already admitted.

Judge Lee's dissent argues for reading § 1522 as purely permissive based on its title ("Authorization"), but this view was rejected by the majority's operative order. The majority treated "to the extent of available appropriations" as a cap on expenditure rather than an escape hatch from liability. Therefore, under *Pacito*, a Rescissions Act reduces the *amount* of money available to meet mandates but does not legally excuse the Executive Branch from attempting to meet those mandates within the new budgetary constraint. The mandate remains; only its execution is scaled to match current appropriations.
