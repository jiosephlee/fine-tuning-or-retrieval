Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the legal distinction between terminating 'cooperative agreements' versus suspending 'funding,' and why does it matter for standing under the APA?
Question:
The background mentions that after Feb 25, the government terminated cooperative agreements with NGOs, even though they claimed to be just suspending funding. Later, Judge Lee argues about whether § 1522 mandates funding or just authorizes it. Why did the plaintiffs sue under both the Refugee Act and the APA? Specifically, if Congress didn't *mandate* funding (as Lee suggests), does terminating a contract violate any statute if no money was technically promised in perpetuity? Or is the violation purely procedural under APA because they changed policy without notice/comment?
Answer:
The core legal distinction you are identifying lies between **substantive statutory mandates** and **procedural requirements for changing existing commitments**, and how these interact with Article III standing under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).

Based on the Ninth Circuit's order in *Pacito v. Trump*, here is the breakdown of why the plaintiffs sued under both statutes and how the "termination vs. suspension" distinction impacts the analysis.

### 1. The Dual Legal Theory: Substantive vs. Procedural
The plaintiffs pursued two distinct avenues because they attack the government's actions on different grounds:

*   **The Refugee Act Claim (Substantive):** This claims that Congress created a **mandatory duty** to provide "reception and placement" services once a refugee is admitted. If true, the government cannot simply stop providing these services regardless of policy changes; doing so would violate the statute itself.
*   **The APA Claim (Procedural/Contractual):** This claims that even if Congress only *authorized* funding (did not mandate it), the government cannot arbitrarily terminate existing contractual relationships ("cooperative agreements") without following proper procedure (notice-and-comment) or violating reliance interests.

### 2. The Distinction Between Terminating Agreements and Suspending Funding
You asked why it matters if they called it a "suspension" but acted like a "termination."

Under the facts presented in *Pacito*:
*   **"Suspension"** implies a temporary pause with an intent to resume, often preserving the underlying legal relationship or funding stream for future activation.
*   **"Termination"** of a cooperative agreement is a permanent severance of the contractual bond between the State Department and the NGO partners.

**Why this matters for Standing:**
If the government merely "suspended" funding temporarily, an organization might lack standing to sue for damages because their contract remains intact and could be reactivated instantly. However, by **terminating** every cooperative agreement (as happened on February 26), the government destroyed the plaintiffs' current operational capacity and financial expectations immediately. This creates an **"irreparable injury"** necessary for standing and injunctive relief: the NGOs lost their ability to function *now*, not just potentially later. The court notes that despite claiming to prepare new requests for proposals, the government admitted uncertainty about when or if replacements would exist, cementing the injury caused by termination rather than mere suspension.

### 3. Addressing Judge Lee's Concurrence: Mandate vs. Authorization
Judge Lee argues that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is purely **authorizing**, not mandating, citing phrases like "to the extent of available appropriations" and "is authorized." He contends that if Congress didn't promise money in perpetuity, there is no substantive statutory violation in stopping payments; it is merely an exercise of executive discretion over budget execution.

However, even accepting Judge Lee's reading that § 1522 does not create an absolute right to perpetual funding, **it does not grant carte blanche to ignore procedural constraints.**

This explains why an APA claim survives even if Lee's interpretation holds:
1.  **Reliance Interests:** Once cooperative agreements are signed, they create binding legal obligations between agencies and grantees. Terminating them constitutes a change in agency policy or action affecting rights/obligations.
2.  **Arbitrary and Capricious Review:** Under APA § 706(2)(A), an agency cannot change its mind or terminate contracts without examining relevant data or articulating a satisfactory explanation connecting facts to choice (*Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. State Farm*). By terminating agreements while simultaneously claiming they were just "suspending" them—and then failing to show any plan for replacement—the government risks acting arbitrarily, regardless of whether § 1522 mandates future funding levels generally.

### Conclusion
The plaintiffs sued under both acts because they cover different gaps:
*   If § 1522 is a **mandate** (Majority view), terminating services violates substantive law directly, rendering any procedural defense irrelevant regarding past-due obligations for admitted refugees (§ 1522(a)).
*   If § 1522 is only an **authorization** (Lee's view), then terminating specific contracts without notice or a reasoned explanation violates the **APA**. The violation isn't necessarily that money was "promised forever," but that existing valid contracts were dismantled through improper process, causing immediate harm to refugees relying on those specific service providers during their transition period in the U.S.

The Ninth Circuit's order reflects this nuance: while deferring heavily on immigration entry powers (§ 1182(f)), they found enough likelihood of success on at least one APA claim regarding § 1522 services to deny a full stay, specifically directing reinstatement of agreements necessary to meet statutory service requirements currently being funded by appropriations ($3+ billion).
