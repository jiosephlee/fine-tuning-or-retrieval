Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does *Nken v. Holder*'s four-factor test allow an appellate court to balance irreparable harm differently for different classes of plaintiffs within a single case?
Question:
In previous orders mentioned in this text (March/April/May), the panel carved out exceptions for people already conditionally approved before Jan 20. Now in this September order, they seem to be balancing harm differently again: allowing suspension of new entries but mandating service for current residents. How does *Nken* handle cases where one group suffers irreparable harm (new entrants blocked) while another group suffers financial/administrative harm (existing refugees losing services)? Can a court find irreparable harm exists for Group A but not Group B in a single consolidated appeal?
Answer:
Yes, under *Nken v. Holder*, an appellate court can—and frequently must—balance irreparable harm differently for distinct classes of plaintiffs within a single consolidated appeal, resulting in a stay that applies partially rather than universally.

The Supreme Court in *Nken* established that the four-factor test for a stay pending appeal requires courts to weigh "whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay" against "where the public interest lies." While *Nken* treats these factors holistically, it does not mandate a binary outcome for every plaintiff in a multi-party suit. Instead, the analysis is inherently fact-specific to the nature of the injury alleged by each movant or affected class. If one group faces irreparable constitutional or statutory deprivation (such as being barred from entry) while another faces merely financial or administrative disruption (such as loss of funding mechanisms), the court is free to find that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of relief for one group but not the other.

The September 12, 2025 order in *Pacito v. Trump* demonstrates exactly this application. The Ninth Circuit panel applied the *Nken* factors to two distinct groups of plaintiffs arising from Executive Order 14163:
1.  **New Entrants:** For refugees seeking entry outside the U.S., the court found the government was likely to succeed on the merits based on broad presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Consequently, the court determined that suspending their entry did not constitute irreparable harm warranting judicial intervention against valid executive power, leading to a full stay regarding admission bans.
2.  **Current Residents:** Conversely, for refugees already admitted to the United States, the panel identified a separate legal challenge regarding 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority concluded there was a likelihood of success on the merits regarding whether this statute mandates "reception and placement" services. Because terminating these services would deprive admitted refugees of statutorily required care (cash assistance, medical services, etc.), the court found irreparable harm existed for this specific subgroup.

The result was a tailored remedy: the court vacated its prior stays and reinstated district court injunctions *only* to compel the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide services to currently admitted refugees ("Group B"), while simultaneously staying all other aspects of the injunctions related to new admissions ("Group A").

This approach confirms that *Nken* permits courts to dissect consolidated appeals when different classes face qualitatively different harms supported by different legal theories. As seen in *Pacito*, a court may grant relief where irreparable harm is imminent and legally cognizable (statutory violation affecting current residents) while denying it where no such harm exists or where executive deference precludes intervention (admission bans). The dissent by Judge Lee reinforces this distinction; even she agreed with staying the ban on new entries but dissented only because she believed § 1522 was permissive rather than mandatory, illustrating that the divergence turned entirely on how irreparable harm and legal merit were assessed for each specific class within the same docket numbers (Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939).
